# Neural Radiance Fields: Volumetric Rendering, Positional Encoding, and Real-Time Splatting Variants

## Abstract

We survey the theoretical foundations and algorithmic descendants of neural radiance fields, the volumetric scene representation that recast novel-view synthesis as differentiable optimization of a continuous five-dimensional function. Beginning with the classical volume rendering equation and its quadrature approximation, we derive the NeRF training objective, the role of Fourier positional encodings in overcoming the spectral bias of multilayer perceptrons, and the hierarchical sampling scheme that concentrates computation near surfaces. We then analyze four decisive refinements: integrated positional encoding for anti-aliasing in mip-NeRF; multiresolution hash encodings that reduce training from days to seconds in Instant-NGP; explicit sparse voxel grids with spherical harmonics in Plenoxels; and anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives with tile-based differentiable rasterization that achieve real-time rendering. We synthesize published empirical results across standard benchmarks, sketch convergence and differentiability arguments for the rendering operators involved, and delineate open limitations including dynamic scenes, unbounded illumination, and editability.

## 1. Introduction

Novel-view synthesis — rendering photorealistic images of a scene from viewpoints never observed by any camera — is among the oldest ambitions of computer vision and graphics. Classical image-based rendering approached it by interpolating between captured photographs, while multi-view stereo reconstructed explicit geometry first and textured it second. Both traditions struggled with view-dependent appearance: specular reflections, transparency, and fine geometric detail resisted compact representation.

In 2020, Mildenhall et al. proposed a radical simplification: represent the scene itself as a single continuous function, parameterized by a multilayer perceptron (MLP), mapping a five-dimensional coordinate — spatial position **x** = (x, y, z) and viewing direction **d** = (θ, φ) — to volume density σ and view-dependent emitted radiance **c** = (r, g, b) [1]. Rendering an image then reduces to marching camera rays through this field and compositing the queried values with classical volume rendering. Because every step is differentiable, the network is optimized end-to-end from posed photographs alone, with no 3D supervision whatsoever. The result is a compact, continuous scene representation — roughly five megabytes of weights — capable of synthesizing strikingly photorealistic novel views.

The impact was immediate and enormous. NeRF ignited a research program spanning anti-aliasing, training acceleration, explicit representations, dynamic scenes, and generative modeling. Yet the original formulation carried three conspicuous costs: *aliasing* under multi-scale observation, *training times* measured in days, and *rendering* far from real-time. This article traces how the community systematically dismantled each cost. We derive the volumetric rendering machinery from first principles, explain why positional encoding is mathematically necessary, and then examine four landmark variants — mip-NeRF's integrated encoding, Instant-NGP's hash grids, Plenoxels' explicit voxels, and 3D Gaussian Splatting's rasterized primitives — as successive answers to the question of how a radiance field should be represented, sampled, and rendered.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The volume rendering equation

NeRF rests on the emission–absorption model of radiative transfer. Consider a camera ray **r**(t) = **o** + t**d** for t in [t_n, t_f]. If σ(**r**(t)) is the differential probability per unit length that the ray terminates at t, and **c**(**r**(t), **d**) is the radiance emitted there toward the camera, the observed color is

C(**r**) = ∫_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t) σ(**r**(t)) **c**(**r**(t), **d**) dt,

where the *transmittance*

T(t) = exp(−∫_{t_n}^{t} σ(**r**(s)) ds)

is the probability that the ray travels from t_n to t without being absorbed. This is the continuous volume rendering integral: each point contributes its emitted color weighted by local density and by the probability that light from that point actually reaches the camera. The formulation is old — it underlies medical visualization and participating-media rendering — but NeRF's insight was to make σ and **c** the outputs of a neural network and differentiate *through* the integral [1].

Numerically, the integral is approximated by quadrature. Partition the ray into N intervals with endpoints t_i and widths δ_i = t_{i+1} − t_i, assume σ and **c** constant within each interval, and obtain

Ĉ(**r**) = Σ_{i=1}^{N} T_i (1 − exp(−σ_i δ_i)) **c**_i,  with  T_i = exp(−Σ_{j<i} σ_j δ_j).

The term (1 − exp(−σ_i δ_i)) is the *alpha* of segment i, and T_i α_i forms a proper set of compositing weights summing to at most one. Every operation — exponentials, cumulative products, weighted sums — is smooth in the network parameters, so gradients from a photometric loss flow backward through the entire ray march.

### 2.2 Positional encoding and spectral bias

A plain MLP mapping raw coordinates to density fails to reproduce fine detail. The reason is *spectral bias*: neural networks with standard initializations learn low-frequency functions far more readily than high-frequency ones, and the mapping from a low-dimensional coordinate to a high-frequency radiance field is precisely the hard case. The remedy adopted in NeRF is to lift the input into a higher-dimensional Fourier feature space before the first linear layer [1]:

γ(p) = (sin(2^0 πp), cos(2^0 πp), …, sin(2^{L−1} πp), cos(2^{L−1} πp)).

Applied coordinate-wise, with L = 10 for spatial position and L = 4 for viewing direction, this encoding gives the MLP direct access to a bank of sinusoids at exponentially spaced frequencies. The network no longer needs to *synthesize* high-frequency variation from smooth activations; it merely *selects and combines* the provided harmonics. The same principle — that the choice of input encoding largely determines the effective hypothesis class — recurs in every successor we study: mip-NeRF integrates the encoding over ray footprints, and Instant-NGP replaces fixed sinusoids with learned hash-table features.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Architecture and ray marching

The canonical NeRF network is an MLP with eight fully connected layers of 256 units and ReLU activations. The positionally encoded spatial coordinate γ(**x**) passes through these layers to produce density σ and a 256-dimensional feature vector; the feature vector is concatenated with the encoded viewing direction γ(**d**) and passed through one additional layer to yield view-dependent RGB. Crucially, σ depends only on position, enforcing the physical constraint that geometry is view-independent while appearance may vary with viewpoint.

Rendering proceeds by casting one ray per pixel, drawing N_c = 64 samples via stratified sampling — one uniform sample per evenly spaced bin, guaranteeing coverage while injecting the stochasticity that aids optimization — evaluating the network, and compositing with the quadrature formula above. The entire pipeline is implemented in a few dozen lines:

```python
import torch

def composite_ray(densities, colors, deltas):
    """Quadrature of the emission-absorption volume rendering integral."""
    alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-densities * deltas)        # per-segment opacity
    transmittance = torch.cumprod(1.0 - alpha, dim=0)   # T_i prefix products
    weights = transmittance * alpha                    # compositing weights
    return torch.sum(weights.unsqueeze(-1) * colors, dim=0)
```

### 3.2 Hierarchical sampling

Uniform sampling wastes the majority of network evaluations in empty space. NeRF therefore trains two networks: a *coarse* model evaluated at the 64 stratified samples, and a *fine* model evaluated at 128 additional samples drawn from the piecewise-constant distribution induced by the coarse weights w_i = T_i α_i. Regions the coarse model deems dense attract more fine samples, concentrating representational effort near surfaces. Both models are supervised by the same photometric objective, the squared error between rendered and observed pixel colors summed over a batch of rays:

L = Σ_{**r** ∈ R} (‖Ĉ_c(**r**) − C(**r**)‖² + ‖Ĉ_f(**r**) − C(**r**)‖²).

Optimizing this loss with Adam for several hundred thousand iterations — historically one to two days on a single GPU — yields the celebrated results: sharp geometry, faithful specularities, and view synthesis quality far beyond prior neural rendering [1].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Anti-aliasing with integrated positional encoding: mip-NeRF

NeRF casts an infinitesimal ray per pixel, so when training or test images observe the same content at different distances, the model is asked to represent multiple scales with a single point-sampled field — producing both blurring and shimmering aliasing. The brute-force remedy, supersampling with many rays per pixel, multiplies the already heavy cost of hundreds of MLP evaluations per ray. Barron et al. instead render *conical frustums* rather than rays [2].

The key observation is that positional encoding under a Gaussian distribution admits a closed form. If a conical frustum segment is approximated by a multivariate Gaussian with mean μ and covariance Σ, then the expected Fourier features have an analytic expression:

γ(μ, Σ) = E_{**x** ∼ N(μ, Σ)}[γ(**x**)],

where each sinusoid's expectation is attenuated by a factor depending on its frequency and the covariance — high frequencies are automatically damped exactly when the footprint is large, mirroring classical prefiltering. This *integrated positional encoding* (IPE) replaces the point encoding throughout the network at negligible extra cost. The resulting mip-NeRF, named by analogy with mipmapping, reduces average error by 17% on the original NeRF dataset and by 60% on a challenging multiscale variant, while being 7% faster and half the size of NeRF, and matches brute-force supersampled NeRF while running 22× faster [2].

### 4.2 Multiresolution hash encodings: Instant-NGP

The dominant cost in NeRF is the large MLP: every sample requires a full forward pass through eight wide layers. Müller et al. inverted the design — shrink the network to almost nothing and move capacity into a *learned input encoding* [3]. Their multiresolution hash encoding maintains L independent grids (typically 16) at geometrically increasing resolutions. Each grid vertex stores a short trainable feature vector (F = 2 dimensions) in a hash table of fixed size T; a query point is encoded by hashing its surrounding vertices at every level, looking up their vectors, trilinearly interpolating, and concatenating the results. The spatial hash

h(**x**) = (x_1 π_1 ⊕ x_2 π_2 ⊕ x_3 π_3) mod T,

with large prime multipliers π_i and bitwise xor, scatters vertices pseudo-randomly so that collisions — inevitable once the grid outgrows the table — occur between distant points and are disambiguated by the multiresolution structure and the network itself.

The concatenated encoding feeds an MLP of merely two hidden layers with 64 units. Implemented in fully fused CUDA kernels that keep the working set in registers and shared memory, the whole system trains a NeRF-quality scene in *seconds* — a fox in under five seconds on a single GPU — and renders 1920×1080 frames in tens of milliseconds [3]. Instant-NGP demonstrated that the encoding, not the network depth, is the true locus of representational power, and its hash-grid idea became the default substrate for nearly all subsequent fast NeRF variants.

### 4.3 Explicit voxel grids without neural networks: Plenoxels

If the encoding carries the capacity, perhaps the network can be discarded entirely. Fridovich-Keil, Yu et al. showed exactly this with Plenoxels: a *sparse voxel grid* in which each occupied voxel directly stores an opacity value and view-dependent color as spherical harmonic coefficients — 9 coefficients per channel (degree 2) for 27 numbers per voxel — with trilinear interpolation providing continuity between voxels [4].

Optimization is direct gradient descent on voxel values with total-variation regularization to encourage spatial smoothness, plus a coarse-to-fine schedule that prunes empty voxels. With no neural network at all, Plenoxels trains in roughly eleven minutes on a single GPU while matching NeRF's view-synthesis quality on standard benchmarks [4]. The result is conceptually clarifying: volume rendering never required deep learning per se — it required a *differentiable, optimizable volumetric representation*, and a regularized explicit grid qualifies. The trade-off is memory: explicit voxels scale with scene extent rather than scene complexity, motivating the hybrid hash structures of Instant-NGP and, later, factorized tensor decompositions.

### 4.4 Anisotropic 3D Gaussians and differentiable rasterization

Kerbl et al. abandoned ray marching altogether. 3D Gaussian Splatting represents the scene as a set of anisotropic 3D Gaussians, initialized from the sparse point cloud produced by structure-from-motion camera calibration [5]. Each Gaussian carries a position μ, a covariance parameterized as Σ = R S Sᵀ Rᵀ (rotation R and per-axis scale S, guaranteeing positive semi-definiteness during optimization), an opacity α, and spherical harmonic coefficients for view-dependent color.

Rendering projects each 3D Gaussian to a 2D screen-space splat using the affine approximation of the projective transform, Σ′ = J W Σ Wᵀ Jᵀ, where W is the viewing transformation and J the Jacobian of the projection — the classic EWA splatting formulation made differentiable. A custom tile-based rasterizer sorts Gaussians front-to-back within each 16×16 tile and alpha-composites them, achieving the visibility-aware blending of volume rendering at rasterization speed. During optimization, an adaptive density controller *clones* small Gaussians in under-reconstructed regions, *splits* over-large ones, and *prunes* transparent primitives, growing the set from thousands to typically a few million.

The payoff is dramatic: state-of-the-art visual quality matching the best NeRF variants, with training times competitive with the fastest prior methods and — decisively — real-time novel-view synthesis at 1080p resolution exceeding 30 frames per second, where no previous radiance field method had achieved real-time display rates [5]. Gaussian splatting reframed the field's central trade-off: explicit primitives plus a differentiable rasterizer can preserve the optimization benefits of volumetric fields while escaping the per-pixel ray-marching cost entirely.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

Published results across the standard benchmarks tell a coherent story of compounding improvements. The table below summarizes representative figures reported by the original papers on the synthetic NeRF dataset and real captures:

| Method | Training time | Render speed | Quality note |
|---|---|---|---|
| NeRF [1] | 1–2 days (1 GPU) | ~30 s / frame | PSNR ≈ 31.0 on synthetic scenes |
| mip-NeRF [2] | ~1 day | ~7% faster than NeRF | −17% avg. error; −60% on multiscale |
| Instant-NGP [3] | seconds–minutes | tens of ms @ 1080p | Matches NeRF quality at 1000× speedup |
| Plenoxels [4] | ~11 minutes | real-time-ish ray marching | Competitive with NeRF, no network |
| 3D Gaussian Splatting [5] | tens of minutes | ≥ 30 fps @ 1080p | SOTA quality, real-time |

For unbounded real-world captures, where cameras orbit freely and content exists at any distance, Barron et al. extended mip-NeRF with a contracted scene parameterization, online distillation from a proposal sampler, and a distortion regularizer that penalizes "floaters" by encouraging compact weight distributions along each ray [6]. Their mip-NeRF 360 reduces mean-squared error by 57% relative to mip-NeRF on 360° captures and produces detailed depth maps for intricate outdoor scenes [6].

Beyond benchmarks, the core operators admit clean analytical guarantees:

> **Theorem:** *(Quadrature convergence and differentiability.)* Let σ and **c** be Lipschitz continuous along each ray. Then the composite estimator Ĉ(**r**) converges to the continuous volume rendering integral C(**r**) as the maximum segment width max δ_i → 0, with error O(max δ_i). Moreover, Ĉ(**r**) is differentiable with respect to every sampled (σ_i, **c**_i), and gradients ∂L/∂σ_i decompose into a direct term through α_i and an indirect term through all downstream transmittances T_j, j > i.

*Proof sketch.* Convergence follows from standard Riemann-sum analysis: on each interval the constant approximation errs by O(δ_i²) under Lipschitz continuity, and summing N intervals with max width δ gives O(δ) total. Differentiability is immediate since Ĉ is a composition of exponentials, products, and sums. The gradient structure follows from the chain rule applied to T_j = exp(−Σ_{k<j} σ_k δ_k): perturbing σ_i scales every later transmittance multiplicatively, which is precisely why density errors near the camera dominate the loss and why hierarchical sampling's concentration of samples at the first surface crossing is so effective. ∎

The theorem explains two practical phenomena: why increasing sample counts yields diminishing returns once δ is small relative to scene detail, and why the coarse-to-fine schedule is not merely an acceleration but a variance-reduction strategy aligned with the gradient's structure.

## 6. Limitations

Despite this progress, fundamental limitations persist:

- **Static scenes.** The canonical formulations assume a frozen world; extending to dynamics requires either time-conditioned fields, deformation models, or per-frame Gaussians, each reintroducing heavy costs.
- **Per-scene optimization.** Every method above fits one scene from scratch. Generalizable variants exist but lag in fidelity, and the dream of a feed-forward "NeRF in a forward pass" remains imperfectly realized.
- **Illumination and materials.** Radiance fields bake lighting into emitted color; relighting, material editing, and physically based decomposition demand inverse-rendering extensions that are far less robust.
- **Memory scaling.** Hash tables collide under extreme detail, and Gaussian splatting scenes routinely require millions of primitives — gigabytes of parameters for city-scale captures.
- **Geometry extraction.** Density fields yield noisy surfaces; extracting clean meshes for downstream simulation or fabrication remains an active, unsolved interface problem.
- **Editability.** Implicit and splat-based representations resist intuitive artistic manipulation compared with meshes, limiting adoption in content-creation pipelines.

## 7. Conclusion

In six years, radiance fields traveled from a days-long optimization curiosity to real-time rendering primitives. The through-line is a single equation — the volume rendering integral — combined with a single realization: that *how* one encodes coordinates matters more than the size of the network that consumes them. Positional encoding overcame spectral bias; integrated encoding tamed aliasing; hash encodings collapsed training to seconds; explicit voxels removed the network; and Gaussian splatting removed ray marching itself. Each step preserved the differentiability that makes the whole enterprise optimizable while attacking a different term in the cost equation. The remaining frontiers — dynamics, relighting, generalization, and scale — are where the next generation of representations will be forged, but they will almost certainly still be rendered, in the end, by compositing weighted colors along rays.
