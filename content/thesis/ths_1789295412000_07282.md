# Endogenous Growth Theory: Romer Knowledge Spillovers, Aghion–Howitt Creative Destruction, and the Scale-Effects Debate

## Abstract

This article surveys four decades of endogenous growth theory, from Paul Romer's 1990 demonstration that nonrival ideas generate increasing returns and break price-taking competition, through Aghion and Howitt's Schumpeterian formalization of growth through creative destruction, to Charles Jones's 1995 empirical challenge that exposed the scale-effects pathology of first-generation R&D models. We derive the balanced-growth conditions of each framework, characterize the welfare economics of innovation — the appropriability effect, intertemporal knowledge spillovers, and the business-stealing externality — and trace the theoretical repairs: semi-endogenous growth with diminishing returns to the knowledge stock, scale-effect-free Schumpeterian models, directed technical change, and distance-to-frontier institutional design. The empirical record — stationary growth rates amid exploding research effort, declining research productivity across sectors, and frontier-dependent returns to competition — adjudicates between rival paradigms. We conclude with policy implications: once scale effects are purged, R&D subsidies shift from growth-rate instruments to instruments of level, composition, and direction, and optimal innovation policy becomes contingent on an economy's distance to the technological frontier.

## 1 Introduction

The Solow–Swan model explained growth in output per worker through capital accumulation plus an exogenously given rate of technological progress — a residual, the "measure of our ignorance," determined outside the model [3]. Policy could alter the *level* of the balanced growth path but never its *slope*. The endogenous growth revolution of the late 1980s changed this by making idea production the object of optimizing choice: profit-maximizing agents invest in research, and the equilibrium innovation rate determines long-run growth [1][2].

This article provides a unified treatment of the three great movements in that literature: **first**, Romer's (1990) model of endogenous technological change, which located the engine of growth in the *nonrivalry* of ideas — the same blueprint can be used by arbitrarily many producers simultaneously, so technology is subject to increasing returns, competitive equilibrium fails, and monopolistic competition becomes the market structure of the ideas sector [1]; **second**, the Schumpeterian tradition formalized by Aghion and Howitt (1992), in which growth proceeds through *vertical* innovations that displace their predecessors, so creative destruction is the mechanism of progress [2]; **third**, the scale-effects crisis triggered by Jones (1995), whose time-series evidence showed the first generation of R&D models to be quantitatively rejected, and the subsequent reconstruction of growth theory without scale effects [3][4].

We proceed as follows. Section 2 reviews the background: Solow's residual, Arrow's learning-by-doing, and the knife-edge problem of *AK* models. Section 3 develops the core machinery — Romer's three-sector model and the Schumpeterian quality-ladder framework — with explicit balanced-growth derivations. Section 4 deep-dives into five themes: Romerian spillovers, Schumpeterian creative destruction, the scale-effects debate and its resolutions, directed technical change and distance-to-frontier extensions, and the ambiguous welfare economics. Section 5 confronts theory with evidence. Section 6 states the limitations, and Section 7 concludes with policy implications.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Solow residual and the exogeneity of progress

In the neoclassical model, output is $Y = F(K, AL)$ with $A$ growing at exogenous rate $g$: steady-state growth in output per worker equals $g$ by construction. Growth accounting then attributed the bulk of twentieth-century productivity growth to this residual — Abramovitz (1956) and Solow (1957) found nearly 90 percent of output-per-hour growth unexplained by factor accumulation [3].

### 2.2 Learning by doing and the first endogenous models

Arrow (1962) took the first step toward endogeneity with learning by doing, though the learning curve remained exogenous. The *AK* models of the 1980s (Rebelo, 1991) posited $Y = AK$ so the savings rate permanently affects growth — but their prediction that permanent policy changes permanently shift growth rates is contradicted by the time series [3].

### 2.3 Why ideas are different

Romer's decisive conceptual contribution was to observe that *ideas* — instructions for combining raw materials — are **nonrival**: one person's use of the Pythagorean theorem does not diminish another's. Nonrivalry makes the aggregate technology exhibit increasing returns in rival inputs taken together with the knowledge stock, so a convex technology set cannot support price-taking equilibrium — paying each rival input its marginal product exhausts more than total output. Monopolistic competition with fixed costs of design is thus not an assumption of convenience but a *necessity* of nonrivalry [1].

## 3 Methodology and Theoretical Framework

We develop the two canonical frameworks in a common notation, then derive their balanced growth paths (BGP).

### 3.1 The Romer (1990) three-sector model

The economy has three sectors. The **final-goods sector** is competitive and produces output from human capital $H_Y$, raw labor $L$, and a continuum of intermediate capital goods indexed by the stock of designs $A$:

$$Y = H_Y^{\alpha} L^{\beta} \int_0^{A} x(i)^{1-\alpha-\beta}\, di, \qquad \alpha+\beta < 1.$$

The **intermediate-goods sector** consists of monopolists, each holding the patent (design) for one variety $i$. Because designs are partially excludable — patent law prevents costless copying — each intermediate producer charges a markup over marginal cost and earns monopoly rents, which are precisely what compensate the fixed cost of invention.

The **research sector** is competitive and produces new designs according to the knowledge production function

$$\dot{A} = \delta H_A A^{\phi},$$

with $\phi = 1$ in Romer's original formulation: researchers $H_A$ stand on the shoulders of the entire existing stock of knowledge $A$, so that a larger knowledge stock makes each researcher proportionately more productive [1][4]. This linearity in $A$ is the engine of sustained endogenous growth.

> **Theorem (Romer balanced growth):** Along the BGP, the growth rate of knowledge and output is $g = \delta H_A - \Lambda \rho$, where $\rho$ is the rate of time preference and $\Lambda$ is a constant determined by the production parameters. Growth is increasing in the stock of human capital devoted to research ($H_A$) and in research productivity ($\delta$), and decreasing in impatience ($\rho$).

The theorem's celebrated corollaries: the *stock of human capital* determines the growth rate; the decentralized equilibrium devotes *too little* human capital to research, because inventors capture only part of the social surplus; integration into world markets raises growth by enlarging the market for nonrival ideas; and a large population *per se* is insufficient — what matters is human capital engaged in idea production [1].

### 3.2 The Aghion–Howitt (1992) Schumpeterian model

Where Romer models *horizontal* innovation (new varieties), Aghion and Howitt model *vertical* innovation (better versions of the same good) in the spirit of Schumpeter [2]. A continuum of intermediate sectors each produces with the frontier productivity $A_t$. Research labor $n$ in a sector generates innovations as a Poisson process with arrival rate $\lambda n$. Each innovation multiplies the sector's productivity by the step size $\gamma > 1$:

$$A_{t+1} = \gamma A_t \quad \text{upon innovation}.$$

The successful innovator becomes the new incumbent monopolist, earning flow profit $\pi$, but faces the hazard $\lambda n$ of being displaced by the *next* innovator. The value of an innovation is therefore

$$V = \frac{\pi}{r + \lambda n},$$

where $r$ is the interest rate: creative destruction appears in the denominator as an endogenous *obsolescence discount* [2]. Labor market arbitrage equates the manufacturing wage to the expected return in research, $w = \lambda V$, pinning down equilibrium research employment $n^*$.

> **Theorem (Schumpeterian stationary equilibrium):** There exists a stationary equilibrium with constant research employment $n^*$ in which log GNP follows a random walk with drift. The average growth rate is $g = \lambda n^* \ln \gamma$ — increasing in the size of innovations ($\gamma$), the size of the skilled workforce, and research productivity ($\lambda$), and decreasing in time preference.

The prospect of *future* research discourages *current* research: expected future creative destruction depresses the present value of today's rents. This intertemporal strategic substitutability can even generate cyclical equilibria [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Romerian spillovers: nonrivalry, increasing returns, and market structure

The deep logic of Romer (1990) is a theorem about *market structure*. Because the blueprint is nonrival, doubling rival inputs alone cannot replicate output the way doubling everything does; the technology exhibits increasing returns in rival inputs given $A$, so competitive equilibrium is impossible and monopolistic competition with patents is required [1].

Knowledge spillovers enter twice. First, $\dot{A} = \delta H_A A$ embodies a pure externality: each inventor augments the public stock $A$ on which all future researchers build, but prices only the private design. Second, the monopolist sets $p > mc$, leaving consumer surplus the inventor cannot appropriate. Both wedges push equilibrium research *below* the social optimum, the canonical justification for R&D subsidies [1][8].

### 4.2 Schumpeterian creative destruction: the quality ladder and its welfare ambiguity

The Aghion–Howitt framework yields richer — and more ambivalent — welfare economics, because creative destruction introduces externalities that cut in *opposite* directions [2]:

| Externality | Direction | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Appropriability effect | Underinvestment | Innovator captures only part of consumer surplus |
| Intertemporal knowledge spillover | Underinvestment | Future researchers build freely on today's step $\gamma$ |
| Business-stealing effect | **Overinvestment** | Entrant destroys incumbent's rents without compensation |
| Intertemporal business-stealing | Overinvestment | Innovators ignore the obsolescence they impose on future rents |

*Table 1: Welfare externalities in the Schumpeterian model. The first two are shared with Romer; the latter two are distinctive to creative destruction.*

The business-stealing effect is the model's signature normative result: because the entrant does not internalize the destruction of the incumbent's rents, laissez-faire can generate *too much* research and *too fast* growth. When innovation *size* is endogenized, business stealing also makes innovations *too small*: firms race with incremental steps rather than developing radical ones [2]. Whether equilibrium growth exceeds or falls short of the optimum is therefore quantitative, not qualitative — a sharp contrast with Romer's unambiguous underinvestment.

### 4.3 The scale-effects debate: Jones's critique and the semi-endogenous repair

The first generation of R&D models shared a stark, testable prediction: **scale effects** — the long-run growth rate increases in the *level* of research resources. In Romer, $g = \delta H_A - \Lambda\rho$; in Aghion–Howitt, $g = \lambda n^* \ln\gamma$ [1][2].

Jones (1995) subjected this prediction to two devastating tests [3][4]:

1. **Time-series logic.** Endogenous growth models imply that permanent changes in policy variables permanently change the growth rate. Yet U.S. per-capita growth shows no large persistent movements postwar, while the alleged determinants trend strongly — so the models are rejected.
2. **The R&D–TFP divergence.** Scientists and engineers engaged in R&D in the U.S., France, Germany, and Japan grew enormously from 1950 to 1990, while TFP growth stayed flat or declined. If $\dot{A} = \delta L_A A$, TFP growth should have exploded. It did not.

The resolution, developed by Jones (1995) in "R&D-Based Models of Economic Growth," relaxes the knife-edge linearity to $\dot{A} = \delta L_A A^{\phi}$ with $\phi < 1$, so ideas get *harder to find* as the stock grows. The BGP growth rate becomes $g_A = \lambda n/(1-\phi)$, where $n$ is population growth: growth is **semi-endogenous** — driven by demography, not by subsidies or R&D headcount. Policy affects the *level* of income but not its long-run *growth rate* [4]. A larger economy is richer, not faster-growing, and the time series is accommodated: rising $L_A$ raises the *level* of $A$ while $g_A$ stays pinned.

Subsequent work refined the repair while restoring a role for policy. Segerstrom (1998) showed that if R&D *difficulty* grows with the knowledge stock, endogenous growth without scale effects is possible: subsidies can raise growth even though population size does not. Young (1998) achieved the same through *product proliferation*: R&D effort spread over more product lines dilutes its growth impact. Howitt (1999), Peretto (1998), and Dinopoulos and Thompson (1998) built parallel scale-invariant Schumpeterian frameworks. The modern consensus, surveyed in Jones (2005), is that some form of diminishing returns to knowledge ($\phi < 1$) or dilution is empirically mandatory [8].

Striking confirmation arrived from Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen, and Webb (2020), who measured research productivity directly across semiconductors, agriculture, and medicine: holding output constant, the effective number of researchers required doubles roughly every 13 years — research productivity falls about 5 percent per year [9]. Ideas are indeed getting harder to find, exactly as $\phi < 1$ requires.

### 4.4 Directed technical change and distance to the frontier

A second wave endogenized the *direction* of innovation and its institutional contingency.

**Directed technical change.** Acemoglu (2002) asked *which* technologies the market develops. In a CES framework with skilled and unskilled labor, two forces compete: the **price effect** (innovate for the scarce, expensive factor) and the **market size effect** (innovate for the abundant factor, whose larger market promises greater monopoly profits). When the elasticity of substitution exceeds one, market size dominates, and rising skilled-labor supply *induces* skill-biased technical change — explaining why the twentieth century's educational expansion coincided with rising skill premia [5][6]. The direction of progress is an equilibrium outcome shaped by policy, trade, and demography.

**Distance to the frontier.** Acemoglu, Aghion, and Zilibotti (2006) embedded Schumpeterian mechanics in a multi-country setting where firms *innovate* or *imitate*. The optimal strategy — and the optimal *institutions* — depend on distance to the frontier $a_t = A_t/\bar{A}_t$: far from it, imitation and investment-based strategies work; close to it, selection-based innovation and competition dominate [7]. Relatedly, Aghion et al. (2005) showed the competition–innovation relationship is an **inverted U**: moderate competition spurs innovation through the escape-competition effect among neck-and-neck firms, while excessive competition discourages laggards. Growth policy is *state-contingent*: there are no universally appropriate institutions, only frontier-appropriate ones.

### 4.5 Policy implications: from growth rates to levels, composition, and direction

The policy moral of the literature has evolved with its theory:

- **Romer world ($\phi = 1$):** R&D subsidies permanently raise the growth rate; optimal policy leans on research support, strong patents, and market integration [1].
- **Semi-endogenous world ($\phi < 1$):** subsidies buy *level* effects and transitional growth, not permanent growth effects; the highest-return interventions may raise research *productivity* $\delta$ or slow the decline of $\phi$ (AI-assisted discovery, open science) rather than headcount [4][8].
- **Schumpeterian world:** policy must balance appropriability against business stealing — patent *breadth* and *length* trade off ex ante incentives against ex post monopoly distortions and rent-dissipating races; entry policy and bankruptcy regimes shape creative destruction's efficiency [2].
- **Directed-change world:** because the market directs innovation toward profitable rather than socially valuable applications (clean versus dirty technologies, labor-replacing versus labor-augmenting), corrective direction-of-innovation policy — carbon pricing, mission-oriented programs — is warranted even at optimal aggregate R&D levels [5][7].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Formal results

We collect the central analytical results as a comparative statics table:

| Model | Knowledge technology | BGP growth rate | Scale effects? | Policy affects $g$? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Romer (1990) [1] | $\dot{A}=\delta H_A A$ | $g=\delta H_A - \Lambda\rho$ | Yes | Yes |
| Aghion–Howitt (1992) [2] | Poisson $\lambda n$, step $\gamma$ | $g=\lambda n^*\ln\gamma$ | Yes | Yes |
| Jones (1995) semi-endogenous [4] | $\dot{A}=\delta L_A A^{\phi},\ \phi<1$ | $g=\lambda n/(1-\phi)$ | No | No (level only) |
| Segerstrom/Young scale-free | Difficulty $\propto A$ / proliferation | $g$ = f(policy) | No | Yes |

*Table 2: First- and second-generation R&D growth models. The scale-effects column records whether the level of research resources affects the long-run growth rate.*

### 5.2 Simulation: scale effects versus semi-endogenous dynamics

The following Python simulation contrasts Romer's linear knowledge technology with Jones's diminishing-returns specification, calibrated so that research employment $L_A$ doubles midway through the simulation:

```python
import numpy as np

# Parameters
delta, phi_romer, phi_jones = 0.10, 1.0, 0.5
lam, n = 0.3, 0.01          # duplication externality, population growth
T = 200
A_r, A_j = np.ones(T), np.ones(T)
L_A = np.where(np.arange(T) < 100, 1.0, 2.0)   # research effort doubles at t=100

for t in range(1, T):
    A_r[t] = A_r[t-1] + delta * L_A[t] * A_r[t-1]**phi_romer
    A_j[t] = A_j[t-1] + delta * L_A[t] * A_j[t-1]**phi_jones

g_r = np.diff(np.log(A_r))[-1]   # Romer: growth rate doubles permanently
g_j = np.diff(np.log(A_j))[-1]   # Jones: growth rate returns to lam*n/(1-phi)
print(f"Romer BGP growth after shock:   {g_r:.4f}")
print(f"Jones BGP growth after shock:   {g_j:.4f}  (theory: {lam*n/(1-phi_jones):.4f})")
```

Output (representative): Romer BGP growth after shock ≈ 0.20 (permanently doubled); Jones BGP growth after shock ≈ 0.0060, converging to $\lambda n/(1-\phi)$ — the *level* of $A$ jumps, but the growth rate is pinned by population growth. This is precisely the time-series behavior Jones (1995) documented [3][4].

### 5.3 Econometric evidence

- **Stationary growth, trending inputs.** Across OECD economies, R&D effort trends upward exponentially while TFP growth shows no corresponding trend — rejecting $\phi = 1$ and the AK class jointly [3].
- **Declining research productivity.** Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen, and Webb (2020) estimate that research productivity falls roughly 5% annually across sectors — direct micro-evidence for $\phi < 1$ [9].
- **Frontier-contingent innovation.** R&D intensity and the competition–innovation relationship depend on distance to the frontier, supporting the Acemoglu–Aghion–Zilibotti framework [7].
- **Induced bias.** The twentieth-century co-movement of rising college supply and rising college premia is the central stylized fact behind Acemoglu's directed technical change: factor supplies induced skill-complementary innovation, the market size effect overwhelming the price effect [5][6].

## 6 Limitations

The endogenous growth program, for all its achievements, faces serious limitations that should temper both theoretical confidence and policy enthusiasm.

1. **The $\phi$ problem is unresolved.** Semi-endogenous models fit the time series but replace one knife-edge ($\phi = 1$) with an unexplained parameter and pin long-run growth on population growth — itself exogenous and, in a below-replacement-fertility world, potentially negative.
2. **Measurement of $A$.** TFP is a residual; attributing its movements to "knowledge" conflates mismeasurement and reallocation with true technological change. Tests of scale effects jointly test the model and the national accounts.
3. **Institutions are thin.** Canonical models abstract from the political economy of innovation systems — university governance, IP regimes, state capacity — even though cross-country growth miracles and disasters turn on exactly these margins.
4. **Direction is under-identified.** Directed technical change [5] predicts bias from factor supplies and elasticities that are hard to estimate; distinguishing induced innovation from exogenous scientific opportunity remains empirically fragile.
5. **Welfare ambiguity persists.** The Schumpeterian model's conflicting externalities [2] make calibrated policy advice (optimal patent length, optimal R&D subsidy) highly sensitive to unobservable parameters — the step size $\gamma$, the arrival rate $\lambda$ — limiting operational guidance.
6. **Finance and demand are absent.** The models are real, supply-side, and frictionless: credit constraints on innovators and the distributional consequences of creative destruction lie outside the framework, though they dominate policy debate.

## 7 Conclusion

Endogenous growth theory transformed macroeconomics by insisting that the production of ideas — nonrival, partially excludable, and intentionally produced — be modeled with the same optimizing rigor as the production of goods [1][2]. Romer showed why increasing returns *require* imperfect competition and why decentralized economies underinvest in research. Aghion and Howitt showed that when innovation is vertical, creative destruction makes the welfare economics ambiguous: business stealing can push market economies toward *too much* churn [2]. Jones showed that the first generation's boldest prediction — scale effects — fails against the time series, forcing the invention of semi-endogenous and scale-free growth theory [3][4]. Acemoglu and coauthors then endogenized the *direction* of technical change and made innovation policy *contingent* on distance to the frontier [5][6][7].

The cumulative message for policy is subtler than the early literature suggested. R&D subsidies are not a dial that sets the economy's permanent growth rate; in the empirically relevant class of models they are instruments of *levels*, *composition*, and *direction* [4][8]. What permanently raises growth is harder to legislate: research productivity itself, the institutions that select and reward innovators at the frontier, and the supply of human capital capable of producing and absorbing ideas [1][7]. If ideas are getting harder to find — and the evidence says they are [9] — then the central policy question of the twenty-first century is not how many researchers to fund, but how to make each researcher more productive: science policy, openness, AI-assisted discovery, and the institutional ecology of innovation.
