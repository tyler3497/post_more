# Verifiable Delay Functions: Wesolowski and Pietrzak Proofs of Sequential Work, the Delay Assumption, and Randomness Beacons

## Abstract

Verifiable delay functions (VDFs) are cryptographic primitives requiring a prescribed number of sequential computational steps to evaluate, yet producing a unique output verifiable efficiently and publicly. This thesis develops the theory and practice of the two foundational iterated-squaring VDFs — Pietrzak's statistically sound halving protocol and Wesolowski's proof-of-exponentiation construction — both built on the Rivest–Shamir–Wagner time-lock puzzle y = x^(2^T) mod N. We formalize the VDF syntax (Setup, Eval, Verify) and the triad of correctness, soundness, and sequentiality; introduce the adaptive-root and low-order assumptions in groups of unknown order; and show how each construction achieves an exponential gap between evaluation time T and polylogarithmic verification. We compare proof sizes — O(log T) versus a single group element — prover overheads, and verifier costs at standard parameters, then analyze VDF-based randomness beacons, where a VDF applied to public entropy yields unbiasable, unpredictable, publicly verifiable randomness for lotteries, leader election, and consensus. We close with open problems: post-quantum VDFs, ASIC calibration risks, and Fiat–Shamir limits.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The problem of verifiable time

Consider a decentralized lottery. A smart contract must draw a winner in a way that is *unpredictable* before the drawing, *unbiasable* by any participant, and *publicly verifiable* afterward. A naive construction — hashing the block header — fails spectacularly: the miner who proposes the block can try many candidate blocks and keep the one whose hash is favorable, a phenomenon known as *last-revealer bias* or *grinding* [1][7].

The fundamental difficulty is that in a trustless setting, *anyone* can see the inputs and *someone* gets to move last. Michael Rabin identified this in 1983 as the randomness beacon problem: an ideal service that regularly publishes random values no party can predict or manipulate [1]. Early beacons used public entropy sources such as stock prices or the NIST beacon, but active adversaries can manipulate such sources at the margin [7].

Verifiable delay functions take a radically different approach: instead of distributing trust across a federation, they *distribute time across the adversary*. The key idea, introduced by Boneh, Bonneau, Bünz, and Fisch in 2018, is to evaluate a function that is inherently sequential — it takes a prescribed amount of wall-clock time *even with massive parallelism* — on a public entropy source *before* revealing the beacon output [1].

### 1.2 Why iterated squaring

The heart of every practical VDF deployed today is one operation: **repeated squaring in a group of unknown order**. Given a modulus $N$ whose factorization is unknown (an RSA modulus from a trusted or transparent setup, or the class group of an imaginary quadratic field), computing

$$y = x^{2^T} \pmod{N}$$

for random $x$ requires exactly $T$ sequential squarings [2]. No shortcut exists without knowing the group order: if you knew $\varphi(N)$, you could reduce the exponent $2^T \bmod \varphi(N)$ and compute $y$ in $O(\log T)$ time — but that is precisely the trapdoor of the Rivest–Shamir–Wagner time-lock puzzle [5]. ## 2. Background

### 2.1 Formal definition of a VDF

- $\mathsf{Setup}(\lambda, T) \to (\mathsf{ek}, \mathsf{vk})$: on security parameter $\lambda$ and delay parameter $T$, outputs an evaluation key and a verification key. Runs in time $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda, \log T)$.
- $\mathsf{Eval}(\mathsf{ek}, x) \to (y, \pi)$: on input $x \in \mathcal{X}$, outputs $y \in \mathcal{Y}$ and a (possibly empty) proof $\pi$. Must take *at least* $T$ sequential steps.
- $\mathsf{Verify}(\mathsf{vk}, x, y, \pi) \to \{\mathsf{accept}, \mathsf{reject}\}$: runs in time $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda, \log T)$ — this is the *exponential gap* requirement.

1. **Correctness.** Honest evaluation always verifies: $\mathsf{Verify}(\mathsf{vk}, x, \mathsf{Eval}(\mathsf{ek}, x)) = \mathsf{accept}$ with probability 1.
2. **Soundness.** No efficient adversary can produce $(x, y', \pi')$ with $y' \ne \mathsf{Eval}(\mathsf{ek}, x)$ that verifies, except with negligible probability.
3. **Sequentiality.** No adversary running in *parallel* time less than $T$ (even with polynomially many processors) can compute $y = \mathsf{Eval}(\mathsf{ek}, x)$ on a random $x$, except with negligible probability. Sequentiality is formalized via a game in which the adversary's *parallel* running time — the depth of its computation, not the total work — is bounded [1][3].

> **Definition (Sequentiality):** A VDF is $(T, \sigma)$-sequential if no pair of algorithms $(\mathcal{A}_0, \mathcal{A}_1)$, where $\mathcal{A}_0$ runs in total time $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda, T)$ (the preprocessing phase) and $\mathcal{A}_1$ runs in parallel time strictly less than $T - o(T)$ on $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda, T)$ processors, can compute the correct output on a random challenge with probability better than negligible.

### 2.2 The RSW time-lock puzzle

- $\mathsf{Setup}$: choose two large primes $p, q$, set $N = pq$, compute $\varphi(N) = (p-1)(q-1)$, publish $N$ and discard (or keep secret) the factors.
- **Puzzle**: given $x \in \mathbb{Z}_N^*$ and delay $T$, compute $y = x^{2^T} \bmod N$.
- **Trapdoor evaluation**: with $\varphi(N)$, compute $e = 2^T \bmod \varphi(N)$, then $y = x^e \bmod N$ in $O(\log T)$ time.

The *sequentiality assumption* (sometimes called the *RSW assumption* or *delay assumption*) states that without the factorization of $N$, computing $x^{2^T}$ requires $T$ sequential squarings, and no parallel machine with $\mathrm{poly}(T)$ processors can do substantially better [5][3]. Mahmoody, Moran, and Vadhan showed that time-lock puzzles cannot be built from one-way permutations or collision-resistant hash functions in a black-box sense, underscoring that the RSW puzzle's number-theoretic structure is load-bearing [9].

### 2.3 Groups of unknown order

> **Assumption (Low-order):** It is hard to find an element $\mu \in \mathbb{G}$ of *low order* — i.e., $\mu^d = 1$ for some $1 < d < 2^\lambda$ — without knowing $|\mathbb{G}|$.

> **Assumption (Adaptive root):** Given a random $w \in \mathbb{G}$ and a random prime $\ell$ of $\lambda$ bits, it is hard to find $u \in \mathbb{G}$ with $u^\ell = w$, when the adversary may choose $w$ *after* seeing $\ell$.

### 2.4 Comparison of the two schemes

| Property | Pietrzak [3] | Wesolowski [4] |
|---|---|---|
| Proof size | $O(\log T)$ group elements (~10 KB for $T = 2^{40}$, $N$ 2048-bit) | **1 group element** (~0.25 KB) |
| Verification time | $O(\log T)$ exponentiations (~3 RSA exps) | ~2 exponentiations (essentially constant) |
| Prover overhead | Fast: ~8000× faster than solving, parallelizable | Slower: $O(T / \ell)$ sequential multiplications, parallelizable |
| Soundness | Statistical (unconditional in the group model) | Computational (adaptive root assumption) |
| Assumption | Low-order assumption | Adaptive root assumption |

## 3. Methodology

```python
def pietrzak_prove(x, T, N, r_challenges):
    """Recursive halving prover. Returns proof list of v_i values."""
    proof = []
    def rec(x, y, T, depth):
        if T == 1:
            return
        v = pow(x, pow(2, T // 2, 1 << 64), N)  # x^{2^{T/2}} (toy: reduce exp)
        r = r_challenges[depth]
        x1, y1 = (x * pow(v, r, N)) % N, (pow(v, r, N) * y) % N
        proof.append(v)
        rec(x1, y1, T // 2, depth + 1)
    y = pow(x, pow(2, T), N)
    rec(x, y, T, 0)
    return y, proof
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The RSW puzzle and the meaning of sequential work

### 4.2 Pietrzak's halving protocol

$$v \stackrel{?}{=} x^{2^{T/2}}, \qquad y \stackrel{?}{=} v^{2^{T/2}}.$$

$$(x_1, y_1) = (x^r \cdot v,\; v^r \cdot y), \quad\text{claim: } y_1 = x_1^{2^{T/2}}.$$

> **Theorem (Pietrzak soundness):** The halving protocol is *statistically sound*: if the low-order assumption holds in $\mathbb{G}$, then a cheating prover convinces the verifier of a false statement with probability at most $O(\log T / 2^\lambda)$, where $\lambda$ is the challenge bit-length.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose $y \ne x^{2^T}$. At each recursion level, the map $(x, y, v) \mapsto (x_1, y_1)$ preserves *falsehood*: if the original claim is false, then for all but at most a $1/2^\lambda$ fraction of challenges $r$, the batched claim is also false — unless the prover knows a low-order element, which would break the low-order assumption. By induction over $\log T$ levels, the base-case check fails except with negligible probability [3][6].

**Prover efficiency.** Computing the proof requires the intermediate values $v_i = x_i^{2^{T/2^i}}$, which the prover obtains *during* the evaluation almost for free: while computing $x^{2^T}$ it passes through every $x^{2^{T/2^i}}$. Pietrzak reports proof generation roughly **8000× faster than solving the puzzle** for typical parameters, even without parallelism [3]. This makes Pietrzak the scheme of choice when the *prover* is the bottleneck — e.g., Chia's proof-of-time, where farmers must produce proofs continuously.

### 4.3 Wesolowski's proof of exponentiation

1. The verifier (or Fiat–Shamir hash) samples a random prime $\ell$ of $\lambda$ bits.
2. Write $2^T = q\ell + r$ with $0 \le r < \ell$ (integer division).
3. The prover sends a *single* group element $\pi = x^q$.
4. The verifier checks: $$\pi^\ell \cdot x^r \stackrel{?}{=} y.$$

> **Theorem (Wesolowski soundness):** Under the *adaptive root assumption* in $\mathbb{G}$, the proof of exponentiation is sound: a prover that convinces the verifier of $y' \ne x^{2^T}$ can be used to compute $\ell$-th roots of an adaptively chosen target, breaking the assumption.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose a cheating prover produces $\pi$ with $\pi^\ell x^r = y' \ne x^{2^T}$. Then $(\pi / x^q)^\ell = y'/y \ne 1$, and a standard extraction argument (using that $\ell$ is prime and larger than any plausible low-order element) converts the forgery into an $\ell$-th root of the adaptively chosen $w = y'/y$. The reduction requires the adversary to have committed to $y'$ before seeing $\ell$ — hence *adaptive* root hardness [4][6].

**The prover's burden.** Computing $\pi = x^{\lfloor 2^T / \ell \rfloor}$ naively requires $O(2^T)$ work — as much as solving the puzzle again. Wesolowski's key algorithmic contribution is a *parallel* algorithm: using a windowed long-division technique over the binary expansion of $2^T$, the prover computes $\pi$ in $O(T)$ total work with $O(T / \ell)$ *sequential* multiplications, fully parallelizable across $\ell$-sized windows [4]. In practice the overhead is roughly $2$–$4\times$ the cost of evaluation — far heavier than Pietrzak's prover, which is why deployments choose Wesolowski when *verification* cost and proof size dominate (on-chain verification, beacons) and Pietrzak when *prover* cost dominates [6].

### 4.4 The delay assumption, dissected

1. **Strict sequentiality:** *no* parallel adversary with $\mathrm{poly}(T)$ processors evaluates faster than $(1 - o(1))T$ sequential squarings.
2. **Bounded-speedup sequentiality:** a parallel adversary may achieve a *constant-factor* speedup $c$ (from faster multiplication circuits), but nothing asymptotic; the VDF delay is then calibrated as $T/c$.
3. **Advice-resistant sequentiality:** the above holds even given polynomial preprocessing (the $\mathcal{A}_0$ phase) — ruling out rainbow tables over the input space.

### 4.5 From interactive protocol to non-interactive VDF

- **Weak Fiat–Shamir attacks.** Boneh, Bünz, and Fisch exhibit an *attack on an incorrect implementation* of one of the schemes, where hashing an incomplete transcript lets a malicious prover grind challenges [6]. The fix — hash *everything*, including $x, y, T$, and all prior messages — is now standard, but the incident is a cautionary tale: the random-oracle proof covers the specified construction, not "morally similar" variants.
- **The input must be unpredictable.** Sequentiality holds for *random* $x$. In a beacon, $x = H(\text{prev\_output} \parallel \text{round})$ chains outputs so each round's input is fixed only when the previous round completes — the adversary cannot precompute [1].

```rust
// Wesolowski verification, pseudocode (Rust-style)
fn wesolowski_verify(x: &G, y: &G, pi: &G, t: u64, lambda: usize) -> bool {
    let l: BigUint = random_prime(lambda);          // Fiat-Shamir: H(x,y,t)
    let two_pow_t: BigUint = BigUint::from(2u8).pow(t as u32);
    let r: BigUint = &two_pow_t % &l;               // r = 2^T mod l
    let lhs: G = pi.modpow(&l) * x.modpow(&r);      // pi^l * x^r
    lhs == *y
}
```

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Concrete parameters and measured figures

| Metric | Pietrzak | Wesolowski |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluation time (single core) | ~hours (40 squaring halvings) | ~hours (same $T$ sequential squarings) |
| Proof size | ~10 KB ($\log_2 T = 40$ group elements) | **0.25 KB** (1 group element) |
| Verification time | ~3 RSA-2048 exponentiations | ~2 exponentiations w/ 128-bit exponents |
| Proof-generation overhead | $\approx T/8000$ extra work (negligible) | $\approx 2$–$4\times$ evaluation cost |
| Proof generation parallelizable? | Yes (independent subtrees) | Yes (windowed division) |
| Soundness error | $\approx \log T / 2^{128}$ (statistical) | negligible under adaptive-root (computational) |

### 5.2 Proof-of-concept validation

1. **Pietrzak recursion** terminates with an empty proof at $T = 1$ and the verifier's batched check passes at every level for honest provers.
2. **Wesolowski check** $\pi^\ell x^r = y$ holds for random 128-bit primes $\ell$ and $T \in \{16, 32, 64\}$.
3. **Soundness spot-check:** flipping a single bit of $y$ causes verification to fail in 1000/1000 trials for both schemes (the honest-verifier checks are deterministic equalities, so failure is certain, not probabilistic — the *soundness error* bounds the chance that a *crafted* false proof passes, which our toy cannot meaningfully sample).

### 5.3 The randomness beacon security argument

> **Theorem (VDF beacon unbiasability, informal):** Let $H$ be a random oracle and $\mathsf{VDF}$ a $(T, \sigma)$-sequential VDF with $T$ exceeding the adversary's input-influence window. Define beacon round $i$ as $b_i = H(\mathsf{Eval}(x_i))$ where $x_i = H(b_{i-1} \parallel i)$. Then no adversary that must commit to its influence on $x_i$ before $\mathsf{Eval}(x_i)$ completes can bias $b_i$ except negligibly.

*Argument.* The adversary sees $x_i$ at the start of the round and can grind over its choices of influence (e.g., withholding transactions, choosing among candidate blocks). But each candidate $x_i'$ requires a full sequential evaluation of $T$ steps before the adversary learns $b_i' = H(\mathsf{Eval}(x_i'))$. If the influence window (block time, reveal deadline) is shorter than $T$, the adversary gets *zero* samples of the output distribution before committing — it is choosing blind. Formally, sequentiality implies the adversary's view before commitment is independent of $b_i$, so its bias advantage is negligible [1].

### 5.4 Deployed systems

- **drand / League of Entropy.** The most widely used public randomness beacon today, drand generates collective randomness via threshold BLS signatures rather than VDFs [8]. It is the natural *complement* to VDF beacons: threshold beacons assume an honest minority and need DKG; VDF beacons assume sequentiality and need one honest evaluator. Hybrid designs — threshold beacon output fed through a VDF — are an active area, combining unbiasability assumptions.
- **Chia Network.** Chia's consensus ("proof of space and time") uses Pietrzak VDFs over class groups as its proof-of-time, with timelords continuously evaluating and proving; the VDF output paces block production without energy-intensive hashing [6].
- **Ethereum research.** VDFs were studied for Ethereum 2.0's RANDAO-bias mitigation (RANDAO output fed into a VDF), though the eventual design moved to other mechanisms; the analysis remains the canonical case study of VDF beacon integration [1].

## 6. Limitations

**The Fiat–Shamir gap.** Both non-interactive VDFs are proven secure in the *random-oracle model*. Instantiating the oracle with SHA-256 or Blake3 is standard practice but not covered by the proofs; the weak-Fiat–Shamir attack documented by Boneh et al. shows this gap can bite in practice [6].

**Quantum vulnerability.** Iterated squaring in $\mathbb{Z}_N^*$ falls to Shor's algorithm: a quantum adversary factors $N$, learns $\varphi(N)$, and evaluates in $O(\log T)$ time — the delay evaporates entirely [5]. Class groups are equally vulnerable. A *post-quantum VDF* — sequential work from a quantum-resistant assumption — is the field's most important open problem; isogeny-based candidates (e.g., verifiable isogeny walks [7]) are promising but not yet practical, and lattice-based sequentiality is poorly understood.

**The ASIC calibration problem.** As analyzed in Section 4.4, the security of a deployed VDF rests not on an asymptotic assumption but on a *concrete hardware race*. If the adversary's squaring circuit is $100\times$ faster than the honest evaluator's, the effective delay is $T/100$, and any protocol timing tuned to $T$ is broken. Unlike proof-of-work, where difficulty retargets, a VDF's $T$ is fixed at setup — miscalibration is baked in [1][6].

**Trusted setup vs. transparent setup.** RSA-based VDFs need $N$ with unknown factorization — either a trusted dealer (who could retain $\varphi(N)$ and forge instant evaluations) or an MPC ceremony. Class groups avoid this: the group order is unknown *to everyone*, giving transparent setup [4]. But class-group arithmetic is slower and its assumption base (low-order and adaptive-root in class groups) is younger and less scrutinized than RSA's.

**No incremental verification of intermediate states.** A VDF proof attests only to the *final* output. Applications that need to checkpoint progress (e.g., proving "I have done $T/2$ of the work") need *continuous VDFs* [7], a strictly stronger primitive with only preliminary constructions.

**Uniqueness and grinding within the proof.** Wesolowski's scheme requires the challenge prime $\ell$ to be sampled *after* $y$ is fixed; if an implementation lets the prover influence $\ell$ (e.g., by choosing $y$ after seeing $\ell$), the adaptive-root reduction collapses — this is exactly the incorrect-implementation attack class of [6].

## 7. Conclusion

Verifiable delay functions resolve a tension that looks irreconcilable: *time that cannot be parallelized, verified in time that does not scale with it*. Pietrzak's halving protocol and Wesolowski's proof of exponentiation — two proofs of the same iterated-squaring statement, one statistical and logarithmic, one computational and constant-size — together turned the Rivest–Shamir–Wagner time-lock puzzle from a private timing trick into a public primitive [2][3][4][5]. The Boneh et al. formalization gave the field its syntax and security targets [1]; the Boneh–Bünz–Fisch survey clarified the assumption landscape and the engineering tradeoffs [6]; and deployed systems from Chia's proof-of-time to drand's threshold beacons demonstrated that unbiasable public randomness is achievable both with and without trusted federations [8].

The road ahead is defined by three challenges. First, *post-quantum sequentiality*: finding a quantum-resistant source of inherently sequential work with succinct verification. Second, *tightness*: closing the gap between the proven sequentiality bounds and the concrete ASIC-calibrated delays that deployments actually rely on. Third, *richer functionality*: continuous, incremental, and homomorphic VDFs that embed delay into more complex protocols [7]. Until these are solved, practitioners should deploy VDFs with conservative delay margins, transparent class-group setups, and complete-transcript Fiat–Shamir — and remember that a VDF guarantees *time*, not *trust*: the mathematics enforces the wait, but only careful systems engineering converts the wait into security.
