# Rational Curves on Fano Varieties: Bend-and-Break, the Graber–Harris–Starr Theorem, and Free Rational Curves

## Abstract

Rational curves are the fundamental probes of the geometry of Fano varieties: they detect the positivity of the anticanonical divisor, they span the Mori cone of curves, and — when they are *free* — they enjoy a flexibility that underwrites the deepest theorems of higher-dimensional geometry. We develop the three pillars of the modern theory. First, Mori's bend-and-break lemma, the deformation-plus-degeneration machine that manufactures rational curves on any variety carrying a curve of positive anticanonical degree, together with the characteristic-$p$ Frobenius argument of Miyaoka and Mori that forces every Fano variety to be covered by rational curves. Second, the theory of free and very free rational curves: the Grothendieck splitting criterion, the smoothing of combs, and the maximally rationally connected fibration. Third, the Graber–Harris–Starr theorem, which guarantees that every family of rationally connected varieties over a curve in characteristic zero admits a section, proved by smoothing combs of very free curves. We close with the cone theorem, Mori's characterization of projective space, and the obstructions that persist in positive characteristic. [1][5][7]

##1 Intro

Let $X$ be a smooth projective variety over an algebraically closed field. A *Fano variety* is one whose anticanonical divisor $-K_X$ is ample — the algebro-geometric incarnation of positive Ricci curvature. Fano varieties sit at the positive end of the trichotomy organizing the minimal model program: general type (negative), Calabi–Yau (flat), Fano (positive). Their geometry is governed, to a remarkable extent, by the rational curves they contain.

Mori's foundational insight is that positivity of $-K_X$ *forces* rational curves, produced by a universal mechanism — *bend-and-break* — turning a sufficiently positive-dimensional family of maps from a higher-genus curve into a reducible limit with a rational component [5]. This yields the cone theorem, extremal contractions, boundedness of Fano manifolds, and the characterization of projective space by ampleness of its tangent bundle [7].

A deformation-theoretic refinement asks for *free* rational curves: maps $f$ from the projective line with $f^*T_X$ globally generated. Free curves deform in the expected dimension fixing a point; very free curves fix two points. Their existence on Fano varieties — and *smoothing combs* of very free curves — drives the Graber–Harris–Starr theorem that every family of rationally connected varieties over a curve admits a section [1].

This article tours this circle of ideas — the bend-and-break machine, the Miyaoka–Mori criterion, free curves and comb smoothing, Graber–Harris–Starr, and the cone-theoretic consequences — working over $\mathbf{C}$ and flagging where characteristic zero is essential.

##2 Background

### Varieties with many rational curves

For $X$ smooth projective of dimension $n$ over an algebraically closed field, rational curves sweep out $X$ in the following hierarchy:

| Notion | Definition | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Uniruled** | Through a general point passes a rational curve | Weakest |
| **Rationally chain connected** | Two general points joined by a connected chain of rational curves | Intermediate |
| **Rationally connected** | Two general points joined by a single rational curve | Strong |
| **Rational** | Birational to projective space | Strongest |

Every Fano variety is rationally connected in characteristic zero (Campana; Kollár–Miyaoka–Mori [7]) and rationally chain connected in arbitrary characteristic. The implications rational $\Rightarrow$ rationally connected $\Rightarrow$ rationally chain connected $\Rightarrow$ uniruled are strict: a smooth cubic threefold is rationally connected but not rational, and some uniruled varieties are not rationally chain connected.

*Uniruledness* is numerical: Miyaoka–Mori proved $X$ is uniruled iff $K_X$ is not nef, i.e., some irreducible curve $C$ has $K_X \cdot C < 0$ [7]. For Fano $X$ this holds for *every* curve, so every Fano variety is covered by rational curves of anticanonical degree at most $n+1$.

### Deformation theory of maps

Morphisms are parametrized by the Hom scheme $\mathrm{Hom}(B, X)$. For $f: B \to X$ with $B$ smooth of genus $g$, the Zariski tangent space at $[f]$ is $H^0(B, f^*T_X)$ and obstructions lie in $H^1(B, f^*T_X)$; the *expected dimension* is the Euler characteristic [6]:

> **Formula (Expected dimension).** $\dim_{[f]} \mathrm{Hom}(B, X) \ge \chi(B, f^*T_X) = -K_X \cdot f_*[B] + n(1-g).$

Fixing $f(p)$ cuts the expected dimension by $n$, replacing the tangent space by $H^0(B, f^*T_X \otimes \mathcal{I}_p)$; when $H^1$ vanishes the Hom scheme is smooth of the expected dimension and $f$ deforms freely. In characteristic $p$, precomposing with Frobenius powers $F^e$ multiplies the degree — hence the expected dimension — by $p^e$, manufacturing positive-dimensional deformation spaces out of thin air.

### The cone of curves

For $X$ projective, let $N_1(X)$ be the real vector space of 1-cycles modulo numerical equivalence, and let $\overline{NE}(X) \subset N_1(X)$ be the closed cone generated by classes of effective curves — the *Mori cone*. For Fano $X$, Mori proved $\overline{NE}(X)$ is a *rational polyhedral* cone, spanned by classes of rational curves $C_i$ with $0 < -K_X \cdot C_i \le n+1$ [7]. Its extremal rays index the extremal contractions of the minimal model program, and their lengths control the Picard number [4].

##3 Methodology

The method is classical: **deformation plus degeneration**. Study a positive-dimensional family of morphisms, compactify the parameter space, and analyze the limit — which, by rigidity, acquires a rational component. Three movements:

**Bend.** Given $C \subset X$ with $-K_X \cdot C > 0$, produce positive-dimensional deformations of a multiple of $C$ fixing one or two points. Over $\mathbf{C}$ this is obstructed in general; Mori's masterstroke was to work in characteristic $p$, where precomposing with Frobenius raises the anticanonical degree — hence the expected dimension of the deformation space — arbitrarily [5].

**Break.** Compactify the one-parameter family to a map from a surface $S$ fibered over a curve $T$, with a section contracted to the fixed point. The evaluation $S \dashrightarrow X$ cannot be everywhere defined — otherwise the rigidity lemma forces factorization through the projection, contradicting nontriviality. Resolving the indeterminacy yields a reducible fiber, and a dimension count shows one component is rational and passes through the fixed point [5][6].

**Lift and iterate.** The curves produced in characteristic $p$ have $(-K_X)$-degree at most $n+1$; bounded-degree curves lift to characteristic zero by properness of the Hom schemes. Applied to a *minimal* dominating family, breaking strictly lowers the degree until an unsplittable, hence bounded, family remains [7].

For the Graber–Harris–Starr theorem the method is refined: instead of breaking, one *smooths*. A *comb* — a handle curve with very free rational teeth attached in fibers — deforms to an irreducible curve when the teeth are sufficiently positive; attaching teeth to a multisection and smoothing lowers the degree over the base until a section emerges [1].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Bend-and-Break Lemma, Precisely

We state the lemma in the form most used in practice, following Kollár's exposition [5][7]:

> **Theorem (Bend-and-break I).** Let $X$ be smooth projective, $C$ a smooth projective curve of genus $g \ge 1$, and $f: C \to X$ a morphism. Fix $p \in C$. If the locus of deformations of $f$ keeping $f(p)$ fixed contains a curve $T \subset \mathrm{Mor}(C, X; f|_p)$ through the point corresponding to $f$, then there exists a rational curve on $X$ passing through $f(p)$.

> **Theorem (Bend-and-break II).** Let $f: \mathbf{P}^1 \to X$ be a morphism and $c_1, c_2 \in \mathbf{P}^1$ distinct points. If deformations of $f$ fixing $f(c_1)$ and $f(c_2)$ are positive-dimensional, then there is a rational curve through $f(c_1)$ or $f(c_2)$ of strictly smaller $(-K_X)$-degree than $f_*\mathbf{P}^1$.

*Sketch of proof of (I).* Normalize $T$ and form the universal family $U = C \times T$ with evaluation $\mathrm{ev}: U \to X$ contracting the section $\{p\} \times T$. Compactify $T$ to $\bar{T}$. If $\mathrm{ev}$ extended to $C \times \bar{T}$, the rigidity lemma — a contracted section forces factorization through the projection — would make all fibers give the same map, contradicting the choice of $T$. Hence $\mathrm{ev}$ has indeterminacy; resolving it blows up $C \times \bar{T}$, and the exceptional fiber is a tree of rational curves. One component maps non-constantly to $X$ through $f(p)$: the *broken* rational curve. For (II), with two fixed points the broken limit has a component through one of them of smaller degree, since total $(-K_X)$-degree is conserved across the degeneration [6].

The hypothesis "deformations are positive-dimensional" is where characteristic $p$ enters. For $f_e = f \circ F^e$, the expected dimension of deformations fixing a point is $-p^e K_X \cdot C - n(g-1) - n$, tending to infinity with $e$ once $-K_X \cdot C > 0$. For large $e$ the Hom scheme has positive dimension at $[f_e]$, so (I) applies and produces rational curves through $f(p)$ of $(-K_X)$-degree bounded independently of $e$ by (II): Mori's original argument [5][7].

### 4.2 Free Rational Curves and the Splitting Criterion

Let $f: \mathbf{P}^1 \to X$ be a morphism. By Grothendieck's theorem, $f^*T_X \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^{n} \mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}^1}(a_i)$ for integers $a_1 \ge \cdots \ge a_n$.

> **Definition.** The map $f$ is *free* if all $a_i \ge 0$, and *very free* if all $a_i \ge 1$.

Equivalently, $f$ is free iff $H^1(\mathbf{P}^1, f^*T_X \otimes \mathcal{I}_p) = 0$ for all $p$ — $f$ deforms fixing any point — and very free iff $H^1(\mathbf{P}^1, f^*T_X \otimes \mathcal{I}_{p,q}) = 0$ for all $p, q$. A free curve has unobstructed deformations of the expected dimension $-K_X \cdot f_*\mathbf{P}^1 + n$; a very free curve passes through any two prescribed general points — the germ of rational connectedness [7].

A fundamental theorem of Kollár–Miyaoka–Mori asserts that a dominating family of rational curves contains a free member; choosing one of minimal $(-K_X)$-degree yields a *minimal dominating family*, which is *locally unsplit* — through a general point its curves do not degenerate. Bend-and-break bounds the degree: a locally unsplit family satisfies $-K_X \cdot C \le n+1$ [7]. Hence through a general point of a Fano variety passes a free rational curve of anticanonical degree at most $n+1$, and smoothing chains produces a *very* free one. *Non-free* rational curves are constrained instead by extremal contractions [3], and moduli of rational curves on Fano threefolds, where these bounds are sharp, rest on this deformation theory [8].

The workhorse for producing very free curves is the **smoothing of combs**. A *comb* is a nodal curve $D \cup T_1 \cup \cdots \cup T_m$ with $D$ the handle and the $T_i$ smooth rational *teeth* meeting $D$ transversally in single nodes:

> **Theorem (Smoothing of combs).** Let $D \cup T_1 \cup \cdots \cup T_m \subset X$ be a comb with each tooth $T_i$ very free and $H^1(D, N_{D/X}) = 0$. Then the comb smooths: there exists an irreducible curve in $X$ specializing to it, and if the teeth are chosen in fibers of a morphism, the smoothing can be controlled.

Positivity of $N_{T_i/X}$ at the nodes kills the obstruction to smoothing each node independently [1][7].

### 4.3 The Graber–Harris–Starr Theorem

We arrive at the centerpiece. Let $k$ be algebraically closed of characteristic zero.

> **Theorem (Graber–Harris–Starr [1]).** Let $\pi: X \to B$ be a proper morphism with $B$ a smooth curve, and suppose the geometric generic fiber is rationally connected. Then $\pi$ admits a section.

In particular every Fano fibration over a curve has a section — over $K = k(B)$, a rationally connected $K$-variety has a $K$-rational point.

*Proof sketch.* Reduce to $B = \mathbf{P}^1$ and choose a *multisection*: an irreducible $C \subset X$ of degree $d \ge 1$ over $B$; if $d = 1$ we are done. Otherwise attach to $C$ many very free rational curves in fibers of $\pi$ — the teeth of a comb with handle $C$ — chosen very free and disjoint from the singularities of the total space. Comb smoothing deforms this to an irreducible curve $C'$. An intersection computation shows that once enough teeth are attached, smoothing *strictly decreases* the degree over $B$: teeth contribute positively against a relatively ample divisor while smoothing trades fiber components for sections. Iterating drops the degree to $d = 1$, producing a section. Characteristic zero guarantees separability and very free curves in the fibers [1].

Graber, Harris, Mazur, and Starr proved the converse: for proper $\pi: X \to B$ of relative dimension at most $d$, the pullback to a very general curve $C \to B$ in a bounded family admits a section *if and only if* $\pi$ admits a pseudosection — a subvariety dominating $B$ with rationally connected general fiber [2]. The theorem also underlies weak approximation over function fields and the "rationally simply connected" program.

### 4.4 The Cone Theorem and the Characterization of Projective Space

Bend-and-break is the engine of the cone theorem: an extremal face carrying curve classes of arbitrarily large $(-K_X)$-degree would, after breaking, carry smaller-degree rational curves on the same face, contradicting extremality. The outcome:

> **Theorem (Cone theorem for Fano varieties).** $\overline{NE}(X)$ is a rational polyhedral cone. Every extremal ray is spanned by the class of a rational curve $C$ with $0 < -K_X \cdot C \le n+1$.

Each extremal ray $R$ admits a contraction $\mathrm{cont}_R: X \to Y$ contracting exactly the curves of class in $R$ [7]; the lengths of extremal rays bound the Picard number of Fano manifolds [4].

The most striking application is Mori's solution of Hartshorne's conjecture:

> **Theorem (Mori [5][7]).** Let $X$ be smooth projective with $T_X$ ample. Then $X \cong \mathbf{P}^n$.

*Proof sketch.* $T_X$ ample gives $-K_X = \det T_X$ ample, so $X$ is Fano. Choose a rational curve $C$ minimizing $-K_X \cdot C$. For the normalization $\nu: \mathbf{P}^1 \to C$, Grothendieck splitting gives $\nu^*T_X = \bigoplus \mathcal{O}(a_i)$ with all $a_i \ge 1$ and some $a_i \ge 2$ (since $T_{\mathbf{P}^1} = \mathcal{O}(2)$ injects). Hence $-K_X \cdot C = \sum a_i \ge n+1$, with equality forcing $\nu^*T_X \cong \mathcal{O}(2) \oplus \mathcal{O}(1)^{\oplus(n-1)}$. Bend-and-break shows the minimum is exactly $n+1$, and deformations of such a minimal curve through a fixed point sweep out $X$, giving $X \cong \mathbf{P}^n$ [5][6].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

We collect the key statements as a proof atlas with computational illustrations of the driving numerical invariants.

> **Theorem (Miyaoka–Mori numerical criterion [7]).** A projective manifold $X$ is uniruled iff $K_X$ is not nef. Moreover, if $K_X \cdot C < 0$ for some curve $C$, then through every point of $X$ there passes a rational curve $C'$ with $0 < -K_X \cdot C' \le n+1$.

> **Theorem (Kollár–Miyaoka–Mori [7]).** Every Fano manifold (over $\mathbf{C}$) is rationally connected. The same holds with "rationally chain connected" in arbitrary characteristic.

The expected-dimension formula is the quantitative backbone. The following Python routine computes, for a putative splitting type, whether a rational curve is free or very free and the expected dimension of its deformation space:

```python
def splitting_invariants(a, anticanonical_degree):
    """a: splitting degrees of f^*T_X. Returns freeness and expected dim of Hom."""
    n = len(a)
    assert sum(a) == anticanonical_degree
    free = all(ai >= 0 for ai in a)
    very_free = all(ai >= 1 for ai in a)
    h0 = sum(ai + 1 for ai in a if ai >= 0)   # h^0(P^1, f^*T_X)
    h1 = sum(-ai - 1 for ai in a if ai < 0)   # h^1(P^1, f^*T_X)
    expected_dim = anticanonical_degree + n    # chi = -K_X.C + n
    assert h0 - h1 == expected_dim
    return {"free": free, "very_free": very_free,
            "h0": h0, "h1": h1, "expected_dim": expected_dim}

```

The same check in Haskell, emphasizing the type-level reading of the splitting:

```haskell
-- | Grothendieck splitting type of f^*T_X for f : P^1 -> X.
type Splitting = [Int]

classify :: Splitting -> (Bool, Bool, Int)
classify a = (free, veryFree, chi)
  where
    free     = all (>= 0) a
    veryFree = all (>= 1) a
    chi      = sum a + length a   -- -K_X.C + dim X

```

A Rust fragment computing the bend-and-break degree bound for a minimal dominating family — the quantity that forces termination of the breaking process:

```rust
/// Mori's bound: a locally unsplit family on an n-fold has -K_X.C <= n+1.
fn moris_bound(dim: u32) -> u32 {
    dim + 1
}

fn is_unsplittable(degree: u32, dim: u32) -> bool {
    // A family whose general member has degree <= n+1 cannot break further.
    degree <= moris_bound(dim)
}

fn main() {
    let n = 4u32;
    assert!(is_unsplittable(5, n));
    assert!(!is_unsplittable(12, n));
}
```

Finally, a TLA+ sketch of the comb-smoothing iteration at the heart of the Graber–Harris–Starr proof — the degree over the base strictly decreases until a section (degree one) is reached:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE CombSmoothing ------------------------------
EXTENDS Integers, Naturals
VARIABLES degreeOverBase, teethAttached

Init == degreeOverBase \in Nat /\ degreeOverBase > 0 /\ teethAttached = 0

(* Attaching enough very free teeth, then smoothing, lowers the *)
(* degree of the multisection over the base.                       *)
AttachAndSmooth ==
    /\ teethAttached' = teethAttached + 1
    /\ degreeOverBase' \in 1..degreeOverBase
    /\ (teethAttached >= degreeOverBase) => (degreeOverBase' < degreeOverBase)

Next == AttachAndSmooth
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<degreeOverBase, teethAttached>>
IsSection == <>(degreeOverBase = 1)   (* a section is eventually produced *)
=============================================================================
```

##6 Limitations

The theory as presented leans on characteristic zero in several essential places, and the positive-characteristic picture is strictly weaker:

1. **Separability and very free curves.** The Graber–Harris–Starr theorem needs characteristic zero: in characteristic $p$ the maps in comb smoothing need not be separable, very free curves in fibers are not guaranteed, and the section theorem fails in general. Paradoxically, bend-and-break *uses* characteristic $p$ (Frobenius) as a tool but *proves* characteristic-zero theorems by lifting bounded-degree curves [1].

2. **Rational connectedness versus chain connectedness.** In characteristic $p$, Fano varieties are rationally *chain* connected, but rational connectedness can fail — some are not separably rationally connected. The gap is exactly whether the connecting rational curve can be chosen *very free* [3].

3. **Singular Fano varieties.** Bend-and-break as stated needs smoothness to control the normal bundle and the Hom scheme. Variants produce low-degree rational curves on many singular Fano varieties, but the full cone and contraction theorems need the minimal model program for singular pairs [7].

4. **Effectivity of the section.** The Graber–Harris–Starr proof is strongly non-constructive: the number of teeth to attach, hence the complexity of the section, is not effectively bounded by the input data. Explicit sections and height bounds over function fields remain active research [2].

5. **Higher-dimensional bases.** The theorem is sharp for curves: over a surface base, a family of rationally connected varieties need not admit even a rational section.

---

##7 Conclusion

From a single insight — a moving curve with a fixed point must break, and the broken pieces contain a rational curve — Mori built higher-dimensional birational geometry. Bend-and-break yields rational curves wherever $-K_X$ is positive; the Miyaoka–Mori criterion gives uniruledness of Fano varieties and polyhedrality of their Mori cones; free curves and comb smoothing refine existence into flexibility; and Graber–Harris–Starr converts flexibility into sections of fibrations, hence rational points over function fields of curves.

The open edges — positive characteristic, singular varieties, effectivity, higher-dimensional bases — are the current frontier, each marking where the bend-and-break machine meets a genuine obstruction. Which positivity hypotheses survive the passage to characteristic $p$ remains a central project of modern algebraic geometry [1][2][7].
