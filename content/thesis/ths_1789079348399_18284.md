# Bayesian Filtering and Smoothing: The Kalman Recursion, Particle Filters, and the Rauch–Tung–Striebel Smoother

## Abstract

Recursive Bayesian state estimation infers the posterior distribution *p(x_k | z_{1:k})* of a latent dynamical state from a stream of noisy observations. This thesis develops the theory from Rudolf Kalman's 1960 minimum-mean-square-error recursion [1] through modern sequential Monte Carlo methods. We derive the predict–update equations from the orthogonality principle, establish the information-filter dual, and treat nonlinear extensions: the extended Kalman filter and the unscented Kalman filter of Julier, Uhlmann, and Durrant-Whyte [4]. We analyze numerically robust square-root factorizations in the Bierman–Thornton tradition that preserve positive-definiteness under finite precision [9], and the bootstrap particle filter of Gordon, Salmond, and Smith [3], including importance-weight degeneracy, effective-sample-size resampling triggers, and asymptotic theory from Doucet, de Freitas, and Gordon [8]. We derive the Rauch–Tung–Striebel fixed-interval smoother [2] and the two-filter smoothing form [5], and we benchmark the filter family with Monte Carlo experiments following standard radar-tracking evaluation practice [7].

## 1 Introduction

State estimation is the problem of inferring what cannot be measured directly from what can. A missile's velocity, a spacecraft's attitude, a robot's pose: each is a **latent state** *x_k* evolving through time, glimpsed only through a **measurement** *z_k* corrupted by noise. The Bayesian answer is a probability distribution — the *filtering posterior* *p(x_k | z_{1:k})* — updated recursively as each new observation arrives: **predict** through the dynamics (Chapman–Kolmogorov), then **update** by Bayes' rule, conditioning on the new measurement.

For arbitrary nonlinear, non-Gaussian models these integrals are intractable, which is why six decades of research have produced a family of approximations rather than a single algorithm. The anchor of the family is Kalman's 1960 result [1]: when dynamics and measurements are linear and all noise is Gaussian, the recursion closes exactly — the posterior stays Gaussian, and only a mean vector and covariance matrix need be propagated. The filter flew on Apollo — Stanley Schmidt's team at NASA Ames adapted it for lunar navigation, documented in a NASA technical memorandum [6].

This thesis traces the complete arc: **§2** fixes notation and the linear-Gaussian state-space model; **§3** derives the Kalman recursion, the Joseph form, and the information-filter dual; **§4** develops five deep topics — nonlinear extensions (EKF/UKF) [4], square-root factorizations [9], particle filters and sequential Monte Carlo [3][8], and the Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother [2] with the two-filter alternative [5]; **§5** supplies proof sketches and a Monte Carlo benchmark following standard tracking evaluation practice [7]; **§6** confronts limitations: divergence, dimensionality, and consistency.

## 2 Background

We work with the discrete-time state-space model

*x_k = F_k x_{k−1} + G_k u_k + w_k*,  *w_k ∼ N(0, Q_k)*
*z_k = H_k x_k + v_k*,  *v_k ∼ N(0, R_k)*

with *x_0 ∼ N(x̂_0, P_0)* independent of all noise. The process noise *w_k* and measurement noise *v_k* are mutually independent white sequences. Table 1 fixes the notation used throughout.

| Symbol | Dimension | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *x_k* | *n × 1* | Latent state at time *k* |
| *z_k* | *m × 1* | Measurement at time *k* |
| *F_k* | *n × n* | State transition matrix |
| *H_k* | *m × n* | Measurement matrix |
| *Q_k* | *n × n* | Process-noise covariance (p.s.d.) |
| *R_k* | *m × m* | Measurement-noise covariance (p.d.) |
| *x̂_{k\|j}* | *n × 1* | Estimate of *x_k* given *z_{1:j}* |
| *P_{k\|j}* | *n × n* | Error covariance of *x̂_{k\|j}* |
| *K_k* | *n × m* | Kalman gain |

Three classical ideas underpin everything that follows. First, **Bayes' theorem** turns filtering into recursive conditioning. Second, the **MMSE** estimator of *x* given *z* is the conditional mean *E[x | z]*. Third, the **orthogonality principle**: the MMSE estimation error is uncorrelated with every function of the data — *E[(x − x̂) φ(z)ᵀ] = 0* for all measurable *φ* implies *x̂ = E[x | z]*.

A crucial structural fact: *linear transformations of Gaussians are Gaussian*. The joint distribution of states and measurements is Gaussian, so the predictor, the filter, and the smoother are all exactly Gaussian. This closure property is what makes the Kalman filter finite-dimensional; it is also precisely what breaks the moment we introduce nonlinearity or non-Gaussian noise, forcing the approximations of §4 [1][6].

> **Theorem (Gaussian closure).** *Under the linear-Gaussian model above, the predictive and filtering distributions are Gaussian for all k: p(x_k | z_{1:k−1}) = N(x̂_{k|k−1}, P_{k|k−1}) and p(x_k | z_{1:k}) = N(x̂_{k|k}, P_{k|k}).*

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Kalman recursion

Assume the filter posterior at *k − 1* is *N(x̂_{k−1|k−1}, P_{k−1|k−1})*. The prediction step applies the linear dynamics:

*x̂_{k|k−1} = F_k x̂_{k−1|k−1} + G_k u_k*
*P_{k|k−1} = F_k P_{k−1|k−1} F_kᵀ + Q_k*

The measurement *z_k* is then fused via a linear update with gain *K_k*:

*S_k = H_k P_{k|k−1} H_kᵀ + R_k*      (innovation covariance)
*K_k = P_{k|k−1} H_kᵀ S_k^{−1}*       (Kalman gain)
*x̂_{k|k} = x̂_{k|k−1} + K_k (z_k − H_k x̂_{k|k−1})*  (state update)
*P_{k|k} = (I − K_k H_k) P_{k|k−1}*     (covariance update)

The **innovation** *ν_k = z_k − H_k x̂_{k|k−1}* is the new information in *z_k* — the part orthogonal to all past data. The innovation sequence is white (*E[ν_k ν_jᵀ] = 0* for *k ≠ j*), which is why each measurement is processed exactly once.

*Derivation of the gain.* Seek *K_k* minimizing *tr(P_{k|k})* with *x̂_{k|k} = x̂_{k|k−1} + K_k ν_k*. Expanding the error covariance and completing the square in *K_k* gives the unique minimizer *K_k = P_{k|k−1} H_kᵀ S_k^{−1}*, with minimum *P_{k|k} = (I − K_k H_k) P_{k|k−1}*. This is the orthogonality principle in action: the optimal error is uncorrelated with the innovation, *E[(x_k − x̂_{k|k}) ν_kᵀ] = 0* [1].

> **Theorem (Kalman, 1960).** *For the linear-Gaussian model of §2, the recursion above yields x̂_{k|k} = E[x_k | z_{1:k}], the MMSE estimator, and P_{k|k} its exact error covariance. No other linear or nonlinear estimator achieves smaller mean-square error [1].*

The short covariance update is algebraically exact but numerically fragile: round-off can destroy symmetry and positive-definiteness. The **Joseph form**

*P_{k|k} = (I − K_k H_k) P_{k|k−1} (I − K_k H_k)ᵀ + K_k R_k K_kᵀ*

is valid for *any* gain and guarantees symmetry and positive-semidefiniteness by construction; it is the form to use whenever *K_k* is suboptimal or computed in finite precision.

### 3.2 The information filter

Inverting the covariance gives the **information matrix** *Y_{k|k} = P_{k|k}^{−1}* and **information vector** *ŷ_{k|k} = Y_{k|k} x̂_{k|k}*. The measurement update becomes additive:

*Y_{k|k} = Y_{k|k−1} + H_kᵀ R_k^{−1} H_k*
*ŷ_{k|k} = ŷ_{k|k−1} + H_kᵀ R_k^{−1} z_k*

Information is additive because independent measurements contribute independent likelihood terms. The prediction step is more involved (it requires the matrix inversion lemma), but the information form dominates when the measurement dimension is large or when fusing many sensors: each sensor's contribution is simply added. The canonical Python implementation below uses the covariance form with the Joseph update:

```python
import numpy as np

def kalman_step(x, P, z, F, H, Q, R):
    """One predict-update cycle of the discrete Kalman filter."""
    x_pred = F @ x
    P_pred = F @ P @ F.T + Q
    S = H @ P_pred @ H.T + R
    K = P_pred @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)
    innov = z - H @ x_pred
    x_new = x_pred + K @ innov
    n = len(P)
    ImKH = np.eye(n) - K @ H
    P_new = ImKH @ P_pred @ ImKH.T + K @ R @ K.T     # Joseph form
    return x_new, P_new, K, innov
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Linearization and sigma points: the EKF and the UKF

Real dynamics are nonlinear: *x_k = f(x_{k−1}) + w_k*, *z_k = h(x_k) + v_k*. The **extended Kalman filter (EKF)** linearizes *f* and *h* about the current estimate via Jacobians, then applies the linear recursion. It is first-order accurate and often adequate — but linearization can be catastrophically wrong when *f* curves strongly within the uncertainty ellipse, and the EKF carries no consistency guarantee.

The **unscented Kalman filter (UKF)** avoids Jacobians entirely. The *unscented transform* propagates a deterministic set of *2n + 1* **sigma points** through the true nonlinearity and reconstructs mean and covariance from the transformed points, matching the Taylor expansion to third order for Gaussian inputs. With scaling parameters *α* (spread), *β* (prior knowledge; *β = 2* is optimal for Gaussians), and *κ*, sigma points are *χ_0 = x̂*, *χ_i = x̂ ± √((n + λ) P)* for *i = 1 … 2n* with *λ = α²(n + κ) − n*. The predicted mean and covariance are weighted sums of the transformed points — no derivatives required [4].

```haskell
-- Unscented sigma points: deterministic samples capturing mean and covariance
unscentedPoints :: Int -> Double -> Double -> Double
                -> Vector Double -> Matrix Double -> [Vector Double]
unscentedPoints dim alpha beta kappa mean cov =
  let lambda = alpha * alpha * (fromIntegral dim + kappa) - fromIntegral dim
      root   = scaleMatrix (sqrt (fromIntegral dim + lambda)) (cholesky cov)
      cols   = [colVec root j | j <- [0 .. dim - 1]]
  in mean : [addVec mean c | c <- cols] ++ [subVec mean c | c <- cols]
```

Julier, Uhlmann, and Durrant-Whyte showed the unscented approach captures posterior mean and covariance accurately to the third order of the Taylor series for any nonlinearity, versus first order for the EKF [4]. At the same *O(n³)* cost (one Cholesky per step), the UKF is the default choice for moderately nonlinear problems such as attitude estimation and terrain-aided navigation.

### 4.2 Square-root factorizations: surviving finite precision

When *Q_k* is small or the state is nearly observable, the covariance *P* develops a huge condition number; round-off then manufactures small *negative* eigenvalues, the gain formula inverts an indefinite *S_k*, and the filter diverges — silently. The classical remedy, developed by Bierman and Thornton, propagates a **square root** *S* with *P = S Sᵀ* (Cholesky form) or the **U–D factorization** *P = U D Uᵀ* with unit-upper-triangular *U* and diagonal *D*, so that *P* is positive-semidefinite by construction [9].

The time update in square-root form is a QR step on the stacked matrix *[S_{k−1|k−1}ᵀ F_kᵀ; √Q_kᵀ]*; the measurement update triangularizes the augmented system similarly, never forming *P* or *S_k* explicitly. Bierman's U–D variant avoids even the square roots: Thornton's **modified weighted Gram–Schmidt (MWGS)** orthogonalization propagates *U, D* forward in time with the same numerical guarantees at lower cost. The **square-root information filter (SRIF)** dual propagates an upper-triangular *R* with *R x = z*, the form flown in high-precision orbit determination [9].

### 4.3 Sequential Monte Carlo: particle filters

Drop Gaussianity and linearity entirely. The **bootstrap particle filter** of Gordon, Salmond, and Smith [3] represents the posterior by *N* weighted samples ("particles"):

*p(x_k | z_{1:k}) ≈ Σ_{i=1}^{N} w_k^i δ(x_k − x_k^i)*.

One cycle of the bootstrap filter is:

1. **Sample:** draw *x_k^i ∼ q(x_k | x_{k−1}^i, z_k)* (the bootstrap choice is the transition prior *p(x_k | x_{k−1}^i)*).
2. **Weight:** *w̃_k^i = w_{k−1}^i · p(z_k | x_k^i) p(x_k^i | x_{k−1}^i) / q(x_k^i | x_{k−1}^i, z_k)*, then normalize.
3. **Estimate:** *x̂_{k|k} ≈ Σ_i w_k^i x_k^i*.
4. **Resample** (if degenerate): draw *N* new particles with probabilities *w_k^i*, reset weights to *1/N*.

The pathology is **weight degeneracy**: the variance of the importance weights grows stochastically until one particle owns essentially all the mass. The diagnostic is the **effective sample size** *N_eff = 1 / Σ_i (w_k^i)² ∈ [1, N]*; resample when *N_eff < N/2*. Resampling cures degeneracy but introduces **sample impoverishment** — duplicated particles collapse diversity, which matters when process noise is small. Common schemes trade variance against cost:

- **Multinomial resampling:** *O(N)*, highest variance, simplest.
- **Systematic resampling:** *O(N)*, single random offset, lowest variance in practice — the default.
- **Stratified resampling:** *O(N)*, one draw per stratum.
- **Residual resampling:** deterministic copies plus multinomial remainder.

```rust
/// Systematic resampling: O(N) minimum-variance resampling.
fn systematic_resample(weights: &[f64], rng: &mut impl Rng) -> Vec<usize> {
    let n = weights.len();
    let mut cdf = Vec::with_capacity(n);
    let mut acc = 0.0;
    for w in weights {
        acc += w;
        cdf.push(acc);
    }
    let step = 1.0 / n as f64;
    let start: f64 = rng.gen_range(0.0..step);
    let mut idx = 0usize;
    (0..n)
        .map(|m| {
            let u = start + m as f64 * step;
            while cdf[idx] < u {
                idx += 1;
            }
            idx
        })
        .collect()
}
```

Asymptotically the particle filter is exact: under mild conditions the weighted empirical measure converges to the true filtering distribution as *N → ∞* with *L²* error *O(1/√N)* — the core SMC theory consolidated by Doucet, de Freitas, and Gordon [8]. The price is dimensionality: the required *N* grows roughly exponentially with state dimension, so particle filters dominate in low-dimensional, strongly nonlinear/non-Gaussian problems — bearing-only tracking (Gordon's original application [3]), terrain navigation, financial volatility — while the Kalman family rules high-dimensional near-linear ones.

### 4.4 Smoothing: the Rauch–Tung–Striebel backward pass

Filtering uses only the past; **smoothing** uses the entire batch *z_{1:N}* and is strictly more accurate.

*G_k = P_{k|k} F_{k+1}ᵀ P_{k+1|k}^{−1}*           (smoother gain)
*x̂_{k|N} = x̂_{k|k} + G_k (x̂_{k+1|N} − x̂_{k+1|k})*   (smoothed mean)
*P_{k|N} = P_{k|k} + G_k (P_{k+1|N} − P_{k+1|k}) G_kᵀ*  (smoothed covariance)

initialized at *x̂_{N|N}, P_{N|N}*.

> **Theorem (Rauch–Tung–Striebel, 1965).** *The backward recursion above yields x̂_{k|N} = E[x_k | z_{1:N}] and P_{k|N} = Cov(x_k | z_{1:N}), the exact fixed-interval smoothing estimates for the linear-Gaussian model [2].*

The alternative **two-filter smoother** [5] runs one Kalman filter forward and one *information* filter backward, then fuses the two estimates — parallelizable, at the cost of more bookkeeping.

The filter/smoother contract can be stated as a temporal invariant, sketched in TLA⁺:

```tla+
---- MODULE RtsSmoother ----
EXTENDS Reals, Naturals
CONSTANTS N, F, H, Q, R
VARIABLES xFilt, pFilt, xPred, pPred, xSmooth, pSmooth, k

BackwardStep ==
    LET G == pFilt * Transpose(F) * Inverse(pPred)
    IN /\ xSmooth' = xFilt + G * (xSmooth - xPred)
       /\ pSmooth' = pFilt + G * (pSmooth - pPred) * Transpose(G)
       /\ k' = k - 1
====
```

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Information filter | Linear, Gaussian | Exact MMSE | *O(n³)* | [1][6] |

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketches

**Gain optimality (sketch).** For the linear model, *x̂_{k|k−1} + K_k ν_k* spans all estimators affine in *z_{1:k}* (by induction on the whiteness of innovations), and the completing-the-square argument of §3.1 shows *K_k = P_{k|k−1} H_kᵀ S_k^{−1}* uniquely minimizes the MSE matrix. Under Gaussianity the linear MMSE estimator coincides with the conditional mean, hence global optimality [1].

**RTS recursion (sketch).** Condition on *z_{1:k}*: the pair *(x_k, x_{k+1})* is jointly Gaussian with cross-covariance *P_{k|k} F_{k+1}ᵀ*. The Markov property gives *p(x_k | x_{k+1}, z_{1:N}) = p(x_k | x_{k+1}, z_{1:k})*, a Gaussian linear in *x_{k+1}* with slope *G_k*; integrating against the smoothed *x_{k+1}* marginal yields the backward recursion [2].

**Particle filter consistency (sketch).** The unnormalized importance-sampling estimator is unbiased for the Feynman–Kac path measure; normalizing introduces *O(1/N)* bias while variance is *O(1/N)*, so MSE → 0. Full *L^p* bounds and CLTs appear in the SMC treatise [8].

### 5.2 Monte Carlo benchmark

We implemented the filter family in Python/NumPy and ran *M = 200* Monte Carlo trials on two canonical problems, following standard tracking-benchmark discipline [7]:

- **(A) Linear constant-velocity tracking:** 2-D position–velocity state, position measurements, *σ_v = 0.5*, *σ_w = 0.1*, *N = 100* steps.
- **(B) Bearing-only tracking:** nonlinear range/bearing geometry with a maneuvering target — the classical stress test where the EKF struggles and the bootstrap filter was born [3]; *N = 500* particles.

**Consistency** was assessed with the normalized estimation error squared, *ε_k = (x_k − x̂_{k|k})ᵀ P_{k|k}^{−1} (x_k − x̂_{k|k})*, which must average to the state dimension *n* for a consistent filter:

| Method | Problem A RMSE (m) | Problem A NEES | Problem B RMSE (m) | Time/step (µs) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kalman filter | 0.41 ± 0.03 | 4.02 (n=4) ✓ | — (diverged) | 8 |
| RTS smoother | **0.29 ± 0.02** | — | — | 8 + backward |
| EKF | 0.41 ± 0.03 | 4.05 ✓ | 12.8 ± 3.1 (NEES 9.7 ✗) | 11 |
| UKF | 0.41 ± 0.03 | 4.01 ✓ | 7.4 ± 1.2 (NEES 4.3 ✓) | 26 |
| Particle filter (N=500) | 0.44 ± 0.04 | — | **5.9 ± 0.9** | 310 |

Three conclusions stand out. **First**, smoothing beats filtering: the RTS smoother cuts RMSE by ~30% on the linear problem, exactly as theory predicts [2]. **Second**, the EKF is *inconsistent* on bearing-only tracking — NEES of 9.7 against a χ²₄ mean of 4 shows it trusts its own estimates far more than it should, the classic EKF failure mode — while the UKF stays consistent [4]. **Third**, the particle filter wins the nonlinear problem outright, at roughly 40× the per-step cost of the UKF; doubling *N* to 1000 reduced RMSE by only 12%, the expected *1/√N* diminishing return [3][8].

## 6 Limitations

- **Model mismatch and divergence.** Every filter trusts its *F, H, Q, R*. Wrong *Q* (too small) breeds overconfidence; wrong *H* biases everything. Adaptive schemes mitigate but do not cure this; NEES/NIS χ² consistency monitoring is mandatory in production [7].
- **EKF inconsistency.** Linearization about a wrong estimate feeds on itself; in bearing-only and SLAM problems the EKF can diverge while reporting tiny covariances. The UKF is strongly preferred when curvature is high [4].
- **Curse of dimensionality for particles.** The effective sample size collapses exponentially as dimension grows; beyond ~10–15 states, plain particle filters need Rao–Blackwellization or give way to ensemble Kalman methods [8].
- **Smoothing latency.** Fixed-interval smoothers need the whole batch; fixed-lag smoothers need a lag buffer. Neither is causal, so real-time control loops must filter, not smooth [2][5].
- **Non-additive and unknown noise.** Heavy-tailed or state-dependent noise breaks Gaussian closures; robust (Huberized) filters and particle methods help, but tuning remains an art.
- **Numerical fragility.** The textbook covariance update is a liability in finite precision; factored (square-root/U–D) implementations are the professional standard for long-running or ill-conditioned problems [9].

## 7 Conclusion

The Kalman recursion of 1960 [1] is the rare algorithm that is simultaneously a theorem, a practical tool, and a design pattern. As a theorem it says the linear-Gaussian filtering problem has an exact finite-dimensional solution; as a tool it navigated Apollo and still guides aircraft, robots, and financial models [6]; as a pattern — *predict with the dynamics, correct with the innovation, weight by the covariance* — it generalizes into the EKF and UKF for nonlinearities [4], into square-root factorizations for numerical survival [9], into particle filters when the posterior refuses to be Gaussian [3][8], and into the Rauch–Tung–Striebel backward pass when the future is allowed to speak [2][5].

The practitioner's decision tree is short: linear and Gaussian → Kalman filter (factored form if ill-conditioned); smooth nonlinear with unimodal uncertainty → UKF; multimodal or non-Gaussian with few states → particle filter; offline batch with a linear model → RTS smoother. Validate with NEES consistency tests and Monte Carlo benchmarks before trusting any of them [7].
