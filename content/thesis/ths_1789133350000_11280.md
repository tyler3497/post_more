# Block-Structured Adaptive Mesh Refinement for Time-Dependent Conservation Laws: Patch Hierarchies, Conservative Refluxing, and Space-Filling-Curve Load Balancing

## Abstract

Uniform-grid discretization of time-dependent conservation laws becomes intractable when the solution develops localized structure — shock fronts, contact discontinuities, or thin reaction zones — demanding a mesh far finer than the ambient flow requires. Block-structured adaptive mesh refinement (SAMR) covers the domain with a dynamic hierarchy of nested, logically rectangular patches, subcycled in time so every level independently satisfies its Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy bound. This article develops the SAMR formalism from first principles: the Berger–Oliger recursive procedure, the Berger–Colella aligned-patch simplification, Richardson-based truncation error estimation, and Berger–Rigoutsos clustering. We analyze the two operations that make a hierarchy conservative — *refluxing* of coarse–fine interface fluxes and *synchronization* of covered states — and prove they restore exact global conservation and the single-grid accuracy order. We then treat parallel implementation: Morton-order space-filling-curve partitioning and the scaling demonstrated by p4est on over two hundred thousand cores. Validation spans the Sod shock tube, the double Mach reflection, and AMReX multilevel benchmarks. We close with limitations — elliptic coupling, anisotropic refinement, deep-hierarchy communication.

## 1. Introduction

Consider the compressible Euler equations of gas dynamics, written in the canonical conservative form

$$\frac{\partial U}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot F(U) = 0, \qquad U = \begin{pmatrix} \rho \\ \rho\mathbf{v} \\ \rho E \end{pmatrix},$$

posed on a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ with appropriate boundary conditions. A straightforward finite-volume discretization places one computational cell at every point of a uniform Cartesian lattice with spacing $h$. The cost of such a scheme scales as $(L/h)^d$ in memory and, via the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition, as $(L/h)^{d+1}$ in floating-point operations per unit of simulated time — because refining the mesh by a factor of $r$ in $d$ dimensions multiplies the number of cells by $r^d$ and, for an explicit hyperbolic integrator, forces $r$ times as many time steps on every one of them [1][2].

Yet most cells in a typical flow rarely need that resolution. A shock wave is, to within numerical smearing, a discontinuity occupying an $O(h)$-thick layer; the smooth expansion fan on either side is well captured by a far coarser mesh. This is the entire economic argument for adaptivity: *concentrate degrees of freedom where the truncation error demands them, and nowhere else*. Two broad families of data structure have competed to realize it. *Unstructured* adaptation refines individual cells wherever an indicator fires, producing conforming meshes at the price of indirect addressing, complex refinement-coarsening logic, and relatively poor arithmetic intensity. *Block-structured* adaptation, the subject of this thesis, retains the cache-friendly, vectorizable kernels of uniform-grid codes by restricting refinement to unions of logically rectangular patches — grids within grids — while the adaptivity lives entirely in the metadata that manages the hierarchy [2][6].

The intellectual lineage of SAMR begins with Berger and Oliger [1], who introduced the recursive subcycled procedure, and continues through Berger and Colella [2], who restricted patches to be axis-aligned so that conservative interpolation and flux correction become tractable.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Finite-volume discretizations of conservation laws

Let $\mathcal{T}_h$ be a Cartesian mesh with uniform spacing $h$ in each coordinate direction, and let $U_i^n$ denote the cell average of the conserved state over cell $i$ at time $t^n$. An explicit conservative finite-volume update takes the form

$$U_i^{n+1} = U_i^n - \frac{\Delta t}{h} \sum_{d=1}^{D} \left( F_{i+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}_d} - F_{i-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}_d} \right),$$

where $F_{i+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}_d}$ is a numerical flux through the face between cells $i$ and $i+\mathbf{e}_d$, computed by (approximately) solving the Riemann problem there. Under the CFL restriction

$$\Delta t \le C \frac{h}{\max_j |\lambda_j|},$$

with $\lambda_j$ the characteristic speeds and $C \le 1$ a method-dependent constant, these schemes are stable and converge at their design order for smooth flow. The crucial property for everything that follows is *telescoping*: summed over all cells, the flux terms cancel pairwise, so that the total mass $\sum_i U_i$ is exactly conserved up to boundary contributions.

### 2.2 The nested hierarchy

A SAMR hierarchy consists of levels $\ell = 0, 1, \ldots, \ell_{\max}$, where level $0$ is the coarsest base grid covering the entire domain and each subsequent level is strictly nested inside its parent. If $\rho_s$ denotes the spatial refinement ratio (conventionally $2$ or $4$), then $h_{\ell+1} = h_\ell / \rho_s$. Each level $\ell$ is itself a disjoint union of rectangular patches $G_{\ell,k}$, each carrying:

- a **valid region** — cells not covered by any finer patch;
- a **ghost (halo) region** — typically two to four cells wide, filled by interpolation from the parent level or from same-level neighbors before each advance;
- a pointer to its **parent** patch on level $\ell-1$ and its **children** on level $\ell+1$.

Two regularity conditions govern admissible hierarchies. *Proper nesting* requires that every fine patch be buffered at least one coarse cell inside its parent region, so that ghost-cell interpolation stencils never reach outside known data. The *2:1 balance* constraint of tree-based implementations requires neighboring cells to differ by at most one level, bounding interface stencil complexity [3].

### 2.3 SAMR versus tree-based refinement

Two architectural philosophies compete. Classical SAMR (Berger–Colella, in AMReX and Chombo) permits arbitrary rectangular patches — efficient coverage of diagonal shocks at the cost of bookkeeping. Tree-based AMR (octrees/quadtrees, in p4est) restricts refinement to tree cells, wasting cells on staircase coverings but making metadata trivial: Morton codes order the leaves, and partitioning reduces to cutting a one-dimensional array [3]. Subcycling is natural in SAMR but awkward in tree-based schemes, which typically advance the whole tree at the finest global step [7].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Berger–Oliger recursive integration procedure

The heart of SAMR is a recursive procedure that advances the hierarchy while preserving the CFL condition level by level. In the simplified aligned-patch variant of Berger and Colella [2], the algorithm is:

```python
def advance_level(level: int, t: float, dt: float) -> None:
    """Advance hierarchy from `level` to `lmax`, subcycling finer levels."""
    # 1. Fill ghost cells of every patch on this level
    fill_ghost_cells(level, t)
    # 2. Advance every patch on this level by one step of size dt
    for patch in patches[level]:
        single_grid_update(patch, dt)
    # 3. Recurse: finer level takes r temporal substeps of dt/r
    if level < lmax:
        for _ in range(refinement_ratio):
            advance_level(level + 1, t, dt / refinement_ratio)
            t += dt / refinement_ratio
        # 4. Synchronize: project fine data onto coarse, reflux interface fluxes
        average_down(level + 1, level)
        reflux_fluxes(level + 1, level)
```

Here `refinement_ratio` is the temporal refinement ratio $\rho_t$, usually equal to $\rho_s$ for hyperbolic problems so that $\Delta t_\ell / h_\ell$ — and hence the Courant number — is identical on every level. Temporal interpolation of ghost cells during the fine substeps uses the coarse solution at $t^n$ and $t^{n+1}$, which is why the coarse level is advanced *before* the recursion: the fine boundary data must already exist at both endpoints of the coarse step.

### 3.2 Error estimation: flagging cells for refinement

A hierarchy is only as good as its refinement criterion. Two approaches dominate:

1. **Feature-based indicators.** Compute an inexpensive local measure of nonsmoothness — undivided differences, density or pressure gradients, vorticity magnitude, or the second-difference sensor
   $$\eta_i = \frac{|U_{i+1} - 2U_i + U_{i-1}|}{|U_{i+1}| + 2|U_i| + |U_{i-1}| + \varepsilon},$$
   and flag cells where $\eta_i$ exceeds a threshold. This is cheap and robust for shocks but blind to smooth regions where the *error* is large even though the *gradient* is small.

2. **Richardson extrapolation.** Advance the solution on the current grid and, independently, advance an averaged-down coarse copy for one step; the difference estimates the local truncation error. This is the theoretically principled choice for smooth flow [1][5]: it measures error rather than features, at the negligible cost of an extra coarse advance.

In practice, production codes blend the two: Richardson estimation where the flow is smooth, gradient sensors where discontinuities dominate [2][4].

### 3.3 Clustering: the Berger–Rigoutsos algorithm

Flagged cells are scattered points; a SAMR level needs rectangles. The Berger–Rigoutsos algorithm converts a binary flag field into a set of efficient patches [2]:

1. Compute the *signature* of the flag field along each coordinate axis — the number of flagged cells in each row, column, or slab.
2. If the bounding box is sufficiently *efficient* (the ratio of flagged cells to box volume exceeds a threshold, typically $0.7$–$0.9$), accept it as a patch.
3. Otherwise, find the zero crossing or inflection point of a signature and split the box perpendicular to that axis, recursing on both halves.

Because splits follow signature structure, a diagonal shock decomposes into a staircase of rectangles rather than one mostly empty box. The efficiency parameter trades patch count (metadata overhead, ghost-cell duplication) against wasted cells; values near $0.8$ are standard [6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The anatomy of a patch: valid, ghost, and covered cells

Consider a single patch $G_{\ell,k}$. Its cells partition into three disjoint categories. *Valid* cells are those not overlain by any level-$\ell+1$ patch; they hold the definitive solution on that region. *Covered* cells lie beneath finer patches; their values are stale between synchronizations and must never be used as the representative solution. *Ghost* cells form a halo around the valid region, filled by interpolation before each advance — from sibling patches where they abut, or from the parent level via conservative interpolation at interior patch boundaries. Ghost width must span the update plus interpolation stencils [2].

> **Definition (Proper nesting).** A hierarchy is *properly nested* if every level-$\ell+1$ patch is contained in the union of level-$\ell$ patches with a buffer of at least $n_{\text{buf}}$ coarse cells, and if $\partial\Omega$ is treated by the base level alone.

### 4.2 Refluxing: restoring conservation at coarse–fine interfaces

Independent integration of the two sides of a coarse–fine interface destroys the telescoping property of Section 2.1: the coarse cell abutting the interface updates its state with a single coarse flux $F^c$, while the $\rho_s^{d-1}$ fine faces on the other side use fine fluxes $F^f_j$. In general $F^c \neq \frac{1}{\rho_s^{d-1}} \sum_j F^f_j$, so total mass leaks at a rate proportional to the interface area — catastrophic for shock positions and contact discontinuities.

The *refluxing* (flux correction) operation repairs this. After the fine level has been advanced to the coarse time and its data averaged down, the coarse cells adjacent to the interface receive the correction [2]:

$$\delta U_i = -\frac{\Delta t_\ell}{h_\ell} \left( F^c - \frac{1}{\rho_s^{d-1}} \sum_{j=1}^{\rho_s^{d-1}} F^f_j \right),$$

where the sum runs over the fine faces covering the coarse face, and $\Delta t_\ell$ is the coarse step. The quantity in parentheses is the *flux mismatch*; it is $O(h^p)$ for a $p$-th order scheme, so refluxing restores both conservation and the formal order of accuracy. Implementation requires a flux register recording every fine flux that crosses a coarse–fine interface during the substeps — one of the subtler pieces of SAMR infrastructure.

> **Theorem (Global conservation).** Let each level be advanced by a conservative finite-volume scheme and let flux registers record all coarse–fine interface fluxes during subcycling. After `average_down` and `reflux_fluxes` are applied at every level interface, the total conserved quantity over the valid regions satisfies $\sum_{\text{valid } i} |C_i| U_i = \text{const}$ up to physical boundary fluxes, exactly.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Telescoping holds levelwise. The only non-telescoping terms in the uncorrected scheme are the mismatches $F^c - \langle F^f \rangle$ at coarse–fine interfaces. Refluxing adds precisely the negative of each mismatch to the adjacent coarse cell, so the sum over all valid cells telescopes globally. Averaging down does not alter the total because the fine values it copies already participated in the telescoping sum. ∎

### 4.3 Subcycling in time: stability without global penalty

Subcycling is what makes deep hierarchies affordable. With temporal refinement ratio $\rho_t = \rho_s$, the fine level takes $\rho_s$ steps of size $\Delta t / \rho_s$ for each coarse step, and the recursive procedure of Section 3.1 extends to arbitrary depth: level $\ell$ advances with $\Delta t_\ell = \Delta t_0 / \rho_s^\ell$, taking $\rho_s^{\ell_{\max}}$ micro-steps at the finest level per base step. Because the Courant number $\nu_\ell = \max|\lambda| \Delta t_\ell / h_\ell$ is level-independent, the *same* stability bound applies everywhere, and the total work scales with the number of cells at each level rather than with the finest step applied globally [1][2].

The price is temporal accuracy at ghost cells. During fine substeps, ghost values at intermediate times are interpolated between the coarse states at $t^n$ and $t^{n+1}$. Analysis shows this does not degrade global order for second-order schemes, provided the ghost region is wide enough to keep boundary errors from reaching the valid region within one coarse step [5].

| Quantity | Level 0 (base) | Level 1 | Level 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mesh spacing $h$ | $h_0$ | $h_0/2$ | $h_0/4$ |
| Time step $\Delta t$ | $\Delta t_0$ | $\Delta t_0/2$ | $\Delta t_0/4$ |
| Steps per base step | 1 | 2 | 4 |
| Courant number $\nu$ | $\nu_0$ | $\nu_0$ | $\nu_0$ |
| Typical cell count | $N$ | $\sim 0.1N$–$0.3N$ | $\sim 0.05N$ |

*Table 1: Subcycling schedule for a three-level hierarchy with refinement ratio 2. Fine levels take exponentially more steps on exponentially fewer cells; the uniform-grid alternative would pay $4^{d+1}$ times the base cost.*

### 4.4 Parallel load balancing via space-filling curves

Regridding changes the workload, so the hierarchy must be repartitioned dynamically. For patch-based SAMR, each patch carries a computational weight (proportional to its cell count times the number of substeps it performs), and patches are assigned to ranks by a knapsack or greedy bin-packing heuristic. For tree-based AMR, partitioning is far more elegant: the leaves of the forest are totally ordered by their position along a Morton (Z-order) space-filling curve, and the one-dimensional array of octants is cut into contiguous segments of equal weight [3]. Because the Morton order interleaves the bits of the spatial coordinates, contiguous segments correspond to spatially compact subdomains with small surface-to-volume ratio, which minimizes ghost-cell communication.

The p4est library demonstrates the scalability of this approach: forests of up to $5.13 \times 10^{11}$ octants have been generated and adapted on $220{,}320$ CPU cores, with the 2:1 balance algorithm executing in under $10$ seconds per million octants per process [3]. Partitioning costs $O(N/P)$ per rank plus a single `MPI_Allgather` of one integer per rank for global offsets — negligible beside the PDE work.

### 4.5 Coupling to elliptic and parabolic physics

Purely hyperbolic SAMR is only half the story: incompressible flow needs a pressure projection, diffusion needs implicit solves, and self-gravity needs a Poisson solve. The standard approach is a *multilevel multigrid* solver relaxing on the composite hierarchy, using the coarse–fine interface to supply fine-level boundary conditions and correcting the coarse residual with the fine solution [4]. A subtlety arises for time-dependent coupling: elliptic solves are instantaneous, yet hyperbolic levels subcycle. Pretorius [5] introduced an "extrapolation and delayed solution" technique for this situation in numerical relativity: the elliptic field is extrapolated forward during fine substeps and the constraint equations are re-solved at synchronization points, controlling the accumulation of constraint-violating modes. Modern frameworks such as AMReX provide native multilevel linear solvers (geometric multigrid with aggregation-based algebraic coarsening) that make this coupling a library call rather than a research project [4].

## 5. Empirical Results and Validation

### 5.1 Canonical shock-capturing benchmarks

The Sod shock tube — a one-dimensional Riemann problem with left state $(\rho, u, p) = (1, 0, 1)$ and right state $(0.125, 0, 0.1)$ — exercises every component of the machinery: a right-moving shock, a contact discontinuity, and a left-moving rarefaction. With a base grid of $100$ cells and two additional levels ($\rho_s = 2$, refinement triggered by the density-gradient sensor), the SAMR solution reproduces the exact Riemann solution with $L_1$ errors indistinguishable from a uniform $400$-cell computation, at roughly one-third the cell-updates [2]. Crucially, omitting the refluxing step shifts the shock position by several fine cells after a few hundred steps — a vivid demonstration that conservation, not merely resolution, is what the flux correction buys.

The Woodward–Colella double Mach reflection, a Mach $10$ shock impinging on a $30^\circ$ wedge, remains the standard two-dimensional stress test. Berger and Colella's original computation [2] resolved the triple-point structure and the jet formation with an effective resolution unattainable on the uniform grids of the era, discovering flow features — including a second Mach stem configuration at low adiabatic index — that coarser studies had missed entirely.

### 5.2 Scaling evidence from production frameworks

Two independent lines of evidence establish that SAMR scales to the largest machines available. On the tree-based side, the p4est benchmarks cited in Section 4.4 adapt and balance half a trillion octants on $220{,}320$ cores with parallel efficiency dominated by the $O(N/P)$ local work [3]. On the patch-based side, the AMReX framework [4] underpins exascale applications — the PeleC compressible combustion code, the AMR-Wind atmospheric solver, and the WarpX plasma accelerator code. The common architectural lesson is that *metadata must be distributed*: no rank may hold the global patch list, and all hierarchy operations must complete in $O(N/P)$ time with $O(1)$ collective communication per rank.

> **Theorem (Subcycled accuracy).** Consider the Berger–Oliger procedure with temporal refinement ratio $\rho_t = \rho_s$, a $p$-th order single-grid scheme, and ghost-cell interpolation of temporal order at least one. Then the composite solution at synchronization times is $O(h_0^p)$ accurate in the discrete $L_1$ norm, uniformly in the number of levels.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Decompose the error into interior truncation error, interface interpolation error, and refluxing mismatch. Interior terms are $O(h_\ell^p)$ levelwise. Ghost interpolation contributes $O(\Delta t_\ell) = O(h_\ell)$ per substep over $O(1/\Delta t_\ell)$ substeps, but the error is confined to the $O(h_\ell)$-thick boundary layer of each patch by the finite domain of dependence; the $L_1$ contribution is therefore $O(h_\ell^2)$, subdominant. The refluxing mismatch is $O(h_\ell^p)$ by the consistency of the numerical flux. Summation over the $O(\ell_{\max})$ levels yields the claim, with a constant growing only linearly in the number of levels. ∎

## 6. Limitations

SAMR is not a universal solvent, and an honest account must name its failure modes.

1. **Elliptic bottlenecks.** Multigrid on a composite hierarchy converges well for the Poisson equation with smooth coefficients, but strongly varying coefficients, embedded boundaries with small cut cells, and saddle-point systems (as in incompressible flow) can degrade or stall convergence. The projection step, not the hyperbolic update, usually dominates the runtime of low-Mach SAMR codes [4].
2. **Anisotropic features.** Block-structured refinement is isotropic by construction: a thin diagonal flame front forces refinement of the full bounding rectangle, and the Berger–Rigoutsos clustering can only mitigate, not eliminate, this waste. Anisotropic or $hp$-adaptive methods handle such features more economically.
3. **Deep-hierarchy communication.** Each level adds a ghost exchange and a synchronization pass; for $\ell_{\max} \gtrsim 6$ with small patches, communication rather than floating-point work becomes the bottleneck — the classic strong-scaling wall of adaptive codes.
4. **Temporal interpolation error.** Subcycling couples levels through time-interpolated ghost data; for stiff source terms (chemical kinetics, radiation transport) the operator-splitting error at coarse–fine boundaries can dominate unless the stiff physics is integrated as carefully as the fluid update [4].
5. **Load-balance transients.** Repartitioning is periodic, not continuous; between repartitions a moving shock can leave ranks severely imbalanced. Frequent repartitioning restores balance but pays the redistribution cost — an unavoidable tuning parameter.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Block-structured adaptive mesh refinement remains, four decades after Berger and Oliger [1], the workhorse discretization for multiscale hyperbolic problems. Its durability rests on three ideas: *recursive subcycled integration* keeping every level at its own CFL limit; *refluxing and averaging* making a hierarchy of independent grids behave as one conservative scheme; and the *separation of metadata from data* that lets patch lists and Morton-ordered octant arrays scale to hundreds of thousands of cores [3][4]. The modern frontier — GPU-resident hierarchies, embedded-boundary geometry, coupled particle and kinetic descriptions — is engineering built atop this foundation rather than a replacement of it.
