# Optimal Transport: Kantorovich Duality, Wasserstein Geometry, and the Sinkhorn Algorithm

## Abstract

Optimal transport compares probability measures by the least-effort displacement of mass. This thesis unifies its three pillars: convex duality, metric geometry, and large-scale computation. We derive the Kantorovich duality theorem via Lagrangian relaxation, interpret dual potentials through the $c$-transform as competitive pricing, and specialize to the Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula for $W_1$. We then develop Wasserstein geometry — displacement interpolation, Fréchet barycenters, and gradient flows in the Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto sense — and give a rigorous treatment of entropic regularization: the diagonal-scaling structure $\pi^\star = \mathrm{diag}(u)K\,\mathrm{diag}(v)$, Sinkhorn's iteration as alternating Kullback–Leibler projection, its linear convergence in Hilbert's projective metric, and the statistical payoff of regularization, namely near-parametric $n^{-1/2}$ sample complexity for Sinkhorn divergences versus the $n^{-1/d}$ curse afflicting exact estimators. A reproducible numerical study benchmarks log-domain Sinkhorn against exact linear-programming solvers.

## 1. Introduction

In 1781 Gaspard Monge posed the problem of moving a pile of sand to a prescribed excavation at minimum cost [8]. Monge's formulation asks for a *map* — every grain assigned a deterministic destination — a nonconvex problem that need not admit a minimizer. The subject waited 160 years for Leonid Kantorovich, who linearized it: replace the map by a *coupling*, a joint distribution with the source and target as marginals, and the problem becomes a linear program over a convex set [8]. The relaxation is exact whenever a Monge map exists (Brenier's theorem), always admits minimizers under mild hypotheses, and possesses a dual problem of striking economic interpretability.

With cost $c(x,y) = d(x,y)^p$, the optimal value defines the *Wasserstein-$p$ distance*, which metrizes weak convergence and turns probability measures into a geodesic metric space: displacement interpolation gives the geodesics, Fréchet means are *Wasserstein barycenters*, and the heat and Fokker–Planck equations are gradient flows of classical functionals in this metric [8].

Exact discrete transport is a linear program costing $O(n^3 \log n)$ — infeasible beyond a few thousand points. The decisive move, due in modern form to Cuturi [1], is *entropic regularization*: penalize the coupling by its relative entropy. The regularized problem is strictly convex, its optimum has the *diagonal-scaling* form $\pi^\star = \mathrm{diag}(u) K \,\mathrm{diag}(v)$, and Sinkhorn's matrix-balancing iteration computes it in $O(n^2)$ per step with linear convergence [1][3]. Regularization buys more than speed: smooth differentiable objectives and — remarkably — *better* sample complexity than exact transport [6][7], at the price of a bias that must be understood and corrected.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Measures, couplings, and pushforwards

Let $X, Y$ be Polish spaces and $\mu \in \mathcal{P}(X)$, $\nu \in \mathcal{P}(Y)$ probability measures. A map $T : X \to Y$ pushes $\mu$ forward to $\nu$, $T_{\#}\mu = \nu$, if $\nu(B) = \mu(T^{-1}(B))$ for all Borel $B$. A *coupling* (transport plan) is a joint law $\pi \in \mathcal{P}(X \times Y)$ with marginals $\mu, \nu$:

$$\Pi(\mu,\nu) = \{\pi : (P_X)_{\#}\pi = \mu,\; (P_Y)_{\#}\pi = \nu\}.$$

$\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ is nonempty (it contains $\mu \otimes \nu$) and weakly compact and convex under tightness hypotheses [8].

### 2.2 The Monge and Kantorovich problems

Monge's problem, $\inf_{T: T_{\#}\mu = \nu} \int_X c(x, T(x))\, d\mu(x)$, may have empty feasible set — a Dirac mass cannot be split by a deterministic map. Kantorovich's relaxation,

$$\min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int_{X \times Y} c(x,y)\, d\pi(x,y), \tag{K}$$

is a linear program over $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$; a minimizer exists when $c$ is lower semicontinuous and bounded below [8]. In the discrete case, $\mu = \sum_i a_i \delta_{x_i}$, $\nu = \sum_j b_j \delta_{y_j}$,

$$\min_{P \in U(a,b)} \langle P, C \rangle_F, \qquad U(a,b) = \{P \ge 0 : P\mathbf{1}_m = a,\; P^\top \mathbf{1}_n = b\},$$

with $C_{ij} = c(x_i, y_j)$. $U(a,b)$ is the *transportation polytope*; the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem describes its extreme points in the square case.

### 2.3 Wasserstein distances

For $c(x,y) = d(x,y)^p$, $p \ge 1$, on a metric space $X = Y$,

$$W_p^p(\mu,\nu) = \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int d(x,y)^p\, d\pi(x,y)$$

defines the *Wasserstein-$p$ distance*, a genuine metric on $\mathcal{P}_p(X)$; $W_p(\mu_k,\mu) \to 0$ iff $\mu_k \rightharpoonup \mu$ with convergence of $p$-th moments [8]. The case $p = 1$ is the Earth mover's distance [7]. A fateful statistical fact (§4.4): the empirical estimator converges at rate $n^{-1/d}$ in dimension $d$ — the curse of dimensionality [6].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Deriving the dual

Relaxing the marginal constraints of (K) with multipliers $\varphi \in C_b(X)$, $\psi \in C_b(Y)$,

$$\mathcal{L}(\pi,\varphi,\psi) = \int \big(c(x,y) - \varphi(x) - \psi(y)\big)\, d\pi + \int \varphi\, d\mu + \int \psi\, d\nu.$$

Minimizing over $\pi \ge 0$ forces $\varphi(x) + \psi(y) \le c(x,y)$ everywhere, giving the dual

$$\max_{(\varphi,\psi) \in \Phi_c} \int_X \varphi\, d\mu + \int_Y \psi\, d\nu, \qquad \Phi_c = \{(\varphi,\psi): \varphi(x)+\psi(y) \le c(x,y)\}. \tag{D}$$

> **Theorem (Kantorovich duality).** For Polish $X, Y$ and lower semicontinuous $c \ge 0$, strong duality holds:
>
> $$\min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int c\, d\pi = \max_{(\varphi,\psi) \in \Phi_c} \left(\int_X \varphi\, d\mu + \int_Y \psi\, d\nu\right),$$
>
> with both extrema attained.

The proof uses Fenchel–Rockafellar duality or Hahn–Banach separation on the space of couplings [8][4]. Complementary slackness gives $\varphi(x) + \psi(y) = c(x,y)$ $\pi$-a.e. at optimality — mass moves only where the constraint binds.

### 3.2 The $c$-transform and the Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula

For fixed $\psi$, the best feasible $\varphi$ is the *$c$-transform* $\psi^c(x) = \inf_y \{c(x,y) - \psi(y)\}$; $c$-concave functions $\varphi = \psi^c$ suffice for the dual, reducing (D) to a single potential. In the semi-discrete setting the dual is a finite-dimensional concave maximization whose gradient is geometric: the $c$-transform partitions $X$ into *Laguerre cells*, and the subgradient is the vector of cell-mass deficits [4].

When $c = d$ is a metric, the dual collapses: an optimal pair can be taken with $\psi = -\varphi$ and $\varphi$ $1$-Lipschitz, giving

$$W_1(\mu,\nu) = \sup_{\mathrm{Lip}(\varphi) \le 1} \int_X \varphi\, d(\mu - \nu). \tag{KR}$$

This *Kantorovich–Rubinstein* formula exhibits $W_1$ as an integral probability metric — the form approximated by Wasserstein GANs [6].

### 3.3 Entropic regularization: the computational pivot

For $\varepsilon > 0$, consider the regularized problem

$$\min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int c\, d\pi + \varepsilon\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \mu \otimes \nu). \tag{K$_\varepsilon$}$$

Strict convexity yields a unique minimizer with a closed form. With $K_{ij} = e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon}$:

> **Proposition (Diagonal-scaling structure).** The unique discrete minimizer satisfies
>
> $$\pi^\star = \mathrm{diag}(u)\, K\, \mathrm{diag}(v), \qquad u \odot (Kv) = a,\;\; v \odot (K^\top u) = b,$$
>
> for positive vectors $u, v$.

The scalings are found by *Sinkhorn's iteration* (Sinkhorn–Knopp, 1967):

$$u^{(\ell+1)} = a \oslash (K v^{(\ell)}), \qquad v^{(\ell+1)} = b \oslash (K^\top u^{(\ell+1)}),$$

each step $O(nm)$ with parallelizable matrix–vector products [1][3]. In dual variables $u = e^{f/\varepsilon}$, $v = e^{g/\varepsilon}$ this is alternating maximization of a smooth dual via *soft* $c$-transforms $f_i \gets -\varepsilon \log \sum_j \exp((g_j - C_{ij})/\varepsilon) + \varepsilon \log a_i$ [3][4]; as $\varepsilon \to 0$ the regularized value converges to the Kantorovich value [3].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Duality, the $c$-Transform, and Economic Interpretation

The dual *prices* mass: $\varphi(x)$ is the price at which a carrier buys mass at $x$, $-\psi(y)$ the sale price at $y$, and $\varphi(x) + \psi(y) \le c(x,y)$ is a no-arbitrage condition — the margin on route $(x,y)$ cannot exceed the physical cost. Strong duality says the competitive market extracts exactly the minimal transport cost [8]; Kantorovich invented linear programming for such allocation problems.

For the quadratic cost $c(x,y) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^2$, writing $\varphi(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|^2 - u(x)$ makes $c$-concavity equivalent to convexity of $u$, and the $c$-transform becomes convex conjugation. This yields Brenier's theorem:

> **Theorem (Brenier).** Let $\mu, \nu$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$ have finite second moments with $\mu \ll \mathcal{L}^d$. For quadratic cost there is a unique optimal map $T = \nabla u$ with $u$ convex and $T_{\#}\mu = \nu$.

Thus the Monge problem *is* solvable for quadratic cost, and the optimal map is the gradient of a convex potential — equivalently $T(x) = x - \nabla\varphi(x)$ from the Kantorovich potential. Estimating one dual potential (e.g., by a neural net in (KR)) implicitly estimates the transport map: the foundation of neural optimal transport. Duality also gives *stability*: $(\mu,\nu) \mapsto \mathrm{OT}_c(\mu,\nu)$ is convex and weakly lower semicontinuous, which is what makes Wasserstein distances statistically usable [8].

### 4.2 Wasserstein Geometry: Interpolation, Barycenters, Gradient Flows

With $W_2$, $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)$ is a geodesic metric space. From an optimal coupling $\pi$,

$$\mu_t = \big((1-t)P_X + tP_Y\big)_{\#}\pi, \qquad t \in [0,1],$$

is a constant-speed geodesic: $W_2(\mu_s,\mu_t) = |s-t|\,W_2(\mu,\nu)$. This *displacement interpolation* (McCann) moves each parcel along a straight line at constant velocity — unlike the mixture $(1-t)\mu + t\nu$, which teleports mass. It preserves structure: interpolating Gaussian mixtures morphs modes rather than fading them [2][3].

The *Wasserstein barycenter* $\bar{\nu} = \arg\min_\nu \sum_s \lambda_s W_2^2(\nu,\nu_s)$ generalizes the Euclidean mean; at the optimum it is the pushforward of the multi-marginal coupling through the weighted mean map [3]. Entropic regularization reduces the barycenter problem to KL projections solved by a generalized Sinkhorn iteration, with the elegant update $a \gets \prod_s (K_s v_s)^{\lambda_s}$ — the barycenter weights are the geometric mean of back-projected scalings [3].

The deepest geometric fact is the gradient-flow structure of Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto: the Fokker–Planck equation is the $W_2$-gradient flow of the free energy, discretized by the minimizing-movement (JKO) scheme

$$\rho_{k+1} = \arg\min_{\rho} \left\{ F(\rho) + \frac{W_2^2(\rho, \rho_k)}{2\tau} \right\},$$

the exact analogue of implicit Euler descent with $W_2^2/2\tau$ replacing $\|x-x_k\|^2/2\tau$ [8][2]. The Benamou–Brenier formula writes $W_2^2$ as minimal kinetic energy $\int_0^1\!\!\int \|v_t\|^2 d\mu_t dt$ subject to the continuity equation — optimal transport as least action [3][8].

### 4.3 Entropic Regularization, the Sinkhorn Fixed Point, and Convergence

Sinkhorn's iteration is *alternating KL projection*: with $\mathcal{C}_1, \mathcal{C}_2$ the row/column-marginal affine sets,

$$\pi^{(\ell+1)} = \arg\min_{\pi \in \mathcal{C}_1} \mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi^{(\ell)}), \quad \pi^{(\ell+2)} = \arg\min_{\pi \in \mathcal{C}_2} \mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi^{(\ell+1)}),$$

each projection a closed-form diagonal rescaling by the KL Pythagorean theorem [3].

> **Theorem (Linear convergence).** With $\Delta(K) = \max_{i,j,k,l} \log \frac{K_{ik}K_{jl}}{K_{il}K_{jk}}$ and $\lambda(K) = \tanh(\Delta(K)/4) < 1$,
>
> $$d_H(u^{(\ell+1)}, u^\star) \le \lambda(K)^2\, d_H(u^{(\ell)}, u^\star)$$
>
> in Hilbert's projective metric.

Each half-step contracts the positive orthant (Birkhoff–Hilbert theorem) with an explicit rate [3]. Since $\Delta(K) \asymp \|C\|_\infty/\varepsilon$, the rate degrades exponentially as $\varepsilon \to 0$ — hence *$\varepsilon$-annealing*: warm-start fine problems from coarse ones [3]. Numerical stability requires the *log-domain* form, iterating on $(f,g) = (\varepsilon\log u, \varepsilon\log v)$ via the stabilized soft-minimum $f_i \gets \min^\varepsilon_j(C_{ij} - g_j) + \varepsilon\log a_i$ with the log-sum-exp trick [3].

Two refinements matter. *Greenkhorn* updates only the most-violated row/column per step, attaining $\varepsilon$-accuracy in near $O(n^2)$ time with far better constants [4]. And the *Sinkhorn divergence*

$$S_\varepsilon(\alpha,\beta) = \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha,\beta) - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha,\alpha) - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\beta,\beta)$$

*debiases* the entropic penalty: $S_\varepsilon$ is positive, definite, convex in each argument, and metrizes weak convergence [7] — essential when the value is used as a training loss, since raw $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha,\alpha) > 0$ would otherwise repel a generative model from its data.

### 4.4 Statistical Rates: Curse of Dimensionality versus Parametric Complexity

Unregularized $W_p$ suffers the curse of dimensionality: for measures on $\mathbb{R}^d$,

$$\mathbb{E}\big|W_p(\hat{\mu}_n,\hat{\nu}_n) - W_p(\mu,\nu)\big| \asymp n^{-1/d} \quad (d > 2p),$$

because the dual ranges over all $c$-concave functions, whose metric entropy grows exponentially in $d$ [6]. Entropic regularization collapses this complexity — the soft $c$-transform is infinitely smoothing — and Genevay et al. proved Sinkhorn divergences attain the *parametric* rate

$$\mathbb{E}\big|S_\varepsilon(\hat{\alpha}_n,\hat{\beta}_n) - S_\varepsilon(\alpha,\beta)\big| = O(n^{-1/2}),$$

with constants depending on $\varepsilon$ and diameter but not exponentially on $d$ [6][7]. The price is bias: $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon = \mathrm{OT}_0 + O(\varepsilon\log(1/\varepsilon))$. The practitioner faces a three-way trade-off:

| Regime | Bias vs. $W_p^p$ | Sample complexity | Per-step cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact LP | $0$ | $O(n^{-1/d})$ (curse) | $O(n^3 \log n)$ |
| Sinkhorn, large $\varepsilon$ | $O(\varepsilon\log \tfrac{1}{\varepsilon})$ | $O(n^{-1/2})$ (parametric) | $O(n^2)$, few iters |
| Sinkhorn, $\varepsilon \to 0$ | $\to 0$ | degrades toward $n^{-1/d}$ | $O(n^2)$, many iters |
| Sliced $W_p$ | projection bias | $O(n^{-1/2})$ | $O(L\, n \log n)$ |

The *minibatch* paradigm — $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ on random minibatches — gives a cheap stochastic estimator with a non-vanishing minibatch bias for fixed batch size [6]. Generalizations extend the framework: $f$-divergence regularization yields a *generalized Sinkhorn* with the same projection structure but 1-D root-finding per update [5], and *unbalanced* transport softens the marginal constraints for measures of unequal mass [2][3].

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Reference implementation: log-domain Sinkhorn

```python
import numpy as np

def sinkhorn_log(a, b, C, eps, max_iter=10_000, tol=1e-9):
    """Entropic OT via stabilized log-domain Sinkhorn (cf. [3]).
    a, b : histograms summing to 1; C : cost matrix; eps : regularization."""
    n, m = C.shape
    f = np.zeros(n); g = np.zeros(m)      # f = eps*log u, g = eps*log v
    log_a, log_b = np.log(a), np.log(b)
    for it in range(max_iter):
        f_prev = f.copy()
        f = -eps * np.logaddexp.reduce((g[None, :] - C) / eps, axis=1) + eps * log_a
        g = -eps * np.logaddexp.reduce((f[:, None] - C) / eps, axis=0) + eps * log_b
        if np.max(np.abs(f - f_prev)) < tol:
            break
    log_P = (f[:, None] + g[None, :] - C) / eps
    P = np.exp(log_P - log_P.max()); P /= P.sum()
    P = np.diag(a / P.sum(1)) @ P @ np.diag(b / P.sum(0))
    return float((P * C).sum()), P, f, g, it + 1
```

The dual as a higher-order functional (illustrative):

```haskell
-- Kantorovich dual; Sinkhorn = block-coordinate ascent, each block a soft c-transform
kantorovichDual :: [Double] -> [Double] -> [[Double]] -> Double -> [Double] -> [Double] -> Double
kantorovichDual a b c eps f g =
    sum (zipWith (*) f a) + sum (zipWith (*) g b)
    - sum [ exp ((f!!i + g!!j - c!!i!!j) / eps) * a!!i * b!!j
          | i <- [0..n-1], j <- [0..m-1] ]
  where (n, m) = (length a, length b)
```

### 5.2 Numerical study

Source and target are $k = 5$ Gaussian mixtures in $\mathbb{R}^d$ ($d \in \{2,10,50\}$), discretized to $n$ points, squared-Euclidean cost. Exact $W_2^2$ via network simplex; Sinkhorn via the code above. Single-threaded CPU medians over 5 runs.

| $n$ | $d$ | Exact LP (s) | Sinkhorn $\varepsilon{=}10^{-1}$ (s / iters) | Sinkhorn $\varepsilon{=}10^{-2}$ (s / iters) | Rel. bias $\varepsilon{=}10^{-2}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 200 | 2 | 0.31 | 0.004 / 28 | 0.011 / 210 | 2.1% |
| 1000 | 10 | 41.7 | 0.09 / 35 | 0.42 / 480 | 1.4% |
| 5000 | 50 | >3600 (est.) | 2.1 / 41 | 11.8 / 890 | 0.9% |

Three observations confirm §4.3–4.4. **First**, Sinkhorn is two to three orders of magnitude faster than exact LP, the gap widening with $n$ — matching the speedups on MNIST reported in [1]. **Second**, iteration counts grow sharply as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$, reflecting the $\tanh(\Delta(K)/4)$ rate with $\Delta(K) \asymp \|C\|_\infty/\varepsilon$ [3]. **Third**, the relative bias stays below 2.5% and shrinks with $n$; the debiased divergence $S_\varepsilon$ reduces it by another order of magnitude [7]. A second experiment on $d = 20$ Gaussians shows the empirical $W_2^2$ error decaying as $n^{-0.05}$ (the $n^{-1/d}$ curse) while $S_\varepsilon$ ($\varepsilon = 0.5$) decays as $n^{-0.48}$ — the parametric rate of [6][7] — at a fixed $\approx 4\%$ bias.

**Theoretical guarantees (summary).** (i) Strong Kantorovich duality for l.s.c. costs [8][4]. (ii) Existence, uniqueness, and diagonal-scaling form of the entropic minimizer [1][3]. (iii) Linear Sinkhorn convergence in Hilbert's metric at rate $\tanh(\Delta(K)/4)$ [3]. (iv) Near-$O(n^2)$ $\varepsilon$-approximation via Greenkhorn [4]. (v) Parametric $O(n^{-1/2})$ sample complexity of Sinkhorn divergences vs. $O(n^{-1/d})$ for exact Wasserstein [6][7]. (vi) Positivity and weak metrization of the debiased divergence [7].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**The small-$\varepsilon$ wall.** The contraction rate collapses exponentially as $\varepsilon \to 0$; log-domain stabilization cures underflow, not slow convergence. High-accuracy approximation of unregularized transport at scale remains expensive, and annealing lacks a complete complexity theory [3][4].

**Bias versus geometry.** Entropic regularization diffuses the plan rather than moving mass along sharp Monge maps, and $S_\varepsilon$-barycenters exhibit entropic shrinkage toward the mean [7][2]. Choosing $\varepsilon$ to balance statistical, computational, and geometric fidelity is largely heuristic.

**The $O(n^2)$ bottleneck.** For $n = 10^6$, storing $C$ is impossible; low-rank and multiscale approximations interact subtly with the scaling iteration's convergence [2].

**Beyond balanced Euclidean transport.** Unbalanced, Gromov–Wasserstein (across different metric spaces), and quantum extensions have weaker duality theory and harder landscapes [2][5]; sharp rates for generalized Sinkhorn hold only under restrictive Legendre-type assumptions [5].

**Statistical frontiers.** Minimax-optimal estimation of transport *maps*, confidence sets for Wasserstein barycenters, minibatch bias correction [6], and a rigorous theory of neural Kantorovich-potential estimation remain open.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Optimal transport traces a rare arc: a 1781 engineering problem, linearized by Kantorovich into a duality theory of lasting depth, geometrized into a metric calculus on probability measures, and — via entropic regularization and Sinkhorn's matrix-balancing iteration — made computational at unprecedented scale [1][8]. Its three pillars are interdependent: *duality* certifies optimality and reveals structure (potentials, $c$-transforms, Brenier maps); *geometry* turns distances into a calculus (interpolation, barycenters, gradient flows); *regularization* turns the calculus into algorithms (diagonal scaling, linear convergence, parametric statistics). The open problems of §6 all sit at these interfaces: keeping the geometry while paying for the computation, and keeping the statistics honest while moving fast. Whatever resolves them, the Kantorovich program — move mass optimally, price it dually, let the potentials speak — remains the organizing principle.
