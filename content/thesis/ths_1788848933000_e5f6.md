# DAG-Based Byzantine Fault Tolerant Consensus: Narwhal Mempool Separation, Tusk Asynchronous Ordering, and Bullshark's Partially Synchronous Fast Path

## Abstract

Byzantine fault tolerant (BFT) consensus has long coupled data dissemination with agreement, forcing leader-centric protocols such as PBFT and HotStuff to bottleneck on a single proposer's bandwidth. This thesis presents a systematic treatment of the DAG-based BFT family that decouples these concerns: Narwhal, a round-structured mempool producing a certified directed acyclic graph of availability certificates; Tusk, a fully asynchronous zero-message consensus protocol layered atop it; and Bullshark, a partially synchronous refinement that adds a two-round steady-state fast path with an asynchronous fallback. We formalize the certified DAG abstraction, prove the safety of retroactive leader election via a global perfect coin, analyze the throughput-latency Pareto frontier, and compare against HotStuff and PBFT. Evaluated figures show Bullshark sustaining over 100,000 transactions per second with 33% lower latency than Tusk, doubling HotStuff's throughput, and degrading gracefully under faults where leader-based protocols collapse.

---

## 1 Introduction

State machine replication (SMR) under Byzantine faults is the beating heart of modern permissioned blockchains. For two decades the field was dominated by *leader-based* designs: a designated proposer collects transactions, broadcasts them, and shepherds votes through prepare and commit phases. PBFT [4] demonstrated that practical Byzantine agreement was possible with quadratic communication in the common case; HotStuff [5] refined the paradigm to linear communication and optimistic responsiveness, becoming the de facto consensus of systems such as Diem, AptosBFT, and Flow.

Yet leader-based architectures carry a structural defect that no amount of protocol engineering fully removes: the leader's egress bandwidth is the system's throughput ceiling. Every transaction must pass through the proposer before it can be ordered, and the remaining $n-1$ validators sit comparatively idle. Increasing the committee size therefore *degrades* per-validator efficiency rather than adding capacity. Worse, the leader is a single point of targeted attack — distributed denial of service against the current proposer can stall progress entirely before the global stabilization time (GST) in partially synchronous models.

The DAG-based BFT program, inaugurated by DAG-Rider [3] and industrialized by Narwhal and Tusk [1], proposes a radical architectural inversion: *separate data dissemination from ordering*. A high-throughput mempool layer — Narwhal — continuously builds a round-structured directed acyclic graph of certified vertices, each carrying transaction batches and $2f+1$ references to the prior round. A thin consensus layer — Tusk, or its partially synchronous successor Bullshark [2] — then orders the vertices of the already-constructed DAG, adding *zero* protocol messages of its own in the asynchronous case. Because every validator proposes in every round, aggregate throughput scales with the committee: adding validators adds bandwidth rather than consuming it.

This thesis makes four contributions:

1. A formal exposition of the **certified DAG abstraction**, including quorum certificates of availability, the round structure, and the worker/primary engineering decomposition that lets Narwhal saturate network links.
2. A complete treatment of **Tusk's zero-message asynchronous consensus**, centered on retroactive leader election with a global perfect coin and the $f+1$-support commit rule over three-round waves.
3. A comparative analysis of **Bullshark's partially synchronous design**, its two-round steady-state fast path and fallback voting type, and the garbage collection and fairness tradeoffs it resolves.
4. An empirical and analytical comparison of the **throughput-latency Pareto frontier** of DAG-BFT versus HotStuff and PBFT, grounded in published WAN measurements, production deployments in Sui and Aptos, and the successor protocol Mysticeti.

We assume the standard model: $n = 3f+1$ parties, up to $f$ Byzantine, computationally bounded adversary controlling the network, with asynchronous eventually-reliable links for the mempool and Tusk, and partial synchrony (unknown GST) for Bullshark.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Classical BFT: PBFT and HotStuff

PBFT [4] operates in views with a primary that multicasts pre-prepare messages, followed by all-to-all prepare and commit phases. Its normal-case communication is $O(n^2)$ and its throughput is bounded by the primary's ability to disseminate full transaction payloads. View changes are expensive and fragile.

HotStuff [5] recasts BFT as a chained three-phase (later two-phase) pipelined protocol with threshold signatures, achieving $O(n)$ communication per view and *optimistic responsiveness*: progress at the speed of the actual network delay $\delta$ rather than a known bound $\Delta$. These properties made HotStuff the consensus engine of choice for a generation of blockchains. Yet HotStuff remains leader-centric: all data flows through the leader, and before GST an adaptive adversary can delay each successive leader's proposal and drive throughput to zero [2].

### 2.2 Asynchronous BFT and the FLP Barrier

The Fischer–Lynch–Paterson impossibility [7] proves that deterministic consensus is impossible in fully asynchronous systems with even one crash fault. Asynchronous BFT protocols therefore rely on *randomization* — typically a common coin — to terminate with probability 1. Classical asynchronous protocols (Ben-Or, Bracha, HoneyBadgerBFT) paid steep costs in communication rounds and complexity, relegating them to niche use.

DAG-Rider [3] reframed the problem: instead of running consensus *messages* over an asynchronous network, build a reliable-broadcast DAG first and then run a *local, deterministic* ordering function over each validator's local view of the DAG, using a global perfect coin only to elect leaders retroactively. This yields optimal amortized communication complexity and post-quantum safety, but DAG-Rider remained a theoretical construct with unbounded memory and high synchronous-period latency.

### 2.3 The Separation Thesis

Narwhal and Tusk [1] turned DAG-Rider's insight into an engineered system with a crisp architectural claim: **mempool and consensus are separable concerns**. The mempool's job is data availability — ensuring every honest party can eventually retrieve every committed transaction. Consensus's job is ordering — selecting a linear extension of the DAG's causal history. Once availability is certified by quorum, consensus needs no additional messages at all.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is threefold. *First*, we reconstruct the protocols formally from the primary sources [1][2][3], defining the certified DAG, the wave structure, and the commit rules with exact quorum parameters. *Second*, we analyze safety and liveness arguments, identifying precisely where randomization enters and why the commit rules guarantee that all honest validators order the same vertices. *Third*, we compare quantitatively using published WAN benchmarks [1][2], production measurements from the Sui deployment [6], and analytical models of the throughput-latency tradeoff, treating HotStuff and PBFT as baselines. All performance claims are drawn from peer-reviewed or artifact-evaluated publications; we reproduce no experiments of our own and flag where methodologies differ.

> **Theorem: (Certified DAG Availability).** If a vertex $v$ carries a certificate of availability — $2f+1$ distinct signatures on its digest — then at least $f+1$ honest validators store the full payload of $v$, and any honest validator can retrieve it.

*Proof sketch.* A certificate contains $2f+1$ signatures; at most $f$ are Byzantine, so at least $f+1$ honest validators signed only after validating and storing $v$'s payload and its causal history. Retrieval follows from eventual reliability of honest-to-honest links. ∎

This theorem is the load-bearing wall of the entire architecture: it lets the consensus layer reason about *digests* while trusting *data* to be retrievable.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Narwhal: The Certified DAG Mempool

Narwhal structures communication into numbered *rounds*. In each round $r$, every validator reliably broadcasts a single *vertex* containing (i) a batch of transactions (or, in the worker/primary decomposition, digests of worker-created batches), and (ii) references to at least $2f+1$ vertices of round $r-1$. A vertex is *certified* once its author collects $2f+1$ signatures on its digest, forming a *certificate of availability*. Validators advance to round $r+1$ upon observing $2f+1$ certified vertices of round $r$.

Three properties make this design powerful:

- **No equivocation.** Reliable broadcast guarantees that if two honest validators deliver a vertex from the same author and round, they deliver the identical vertex. Byzantine authors cannot fork their own slot.
- **Causal completeness.** Because each vertex references $2f+1$ predecessors, any certified vertex's entire causal history is retrievable — the DAG is self-certifying.
- **Parallel proposal.** Unlike leader-based mempools, *every* validator proposes in *every* round, so dissemination throughput aggregates across the committee rather than bottlenecking on one node.

The engineering decomposition into *workers* and a *primary* is what converts this into line-rate throughput. Workers continuously create signed batches and disseminate them; the primary's vertices carry only 32-byte digests of those batches. A 1,000-byte vertex can thus commit to roughly 32,000 transactions [1]. Narwhal further pipelines signature verification and storage across cores and uses garbage collection keyed to committed rounds, bounding memory — the practical fix for DAG-Rider's unbounded state.

| Layer | Responsibility | Messages | Bottleneck |
|---|---|---|---|
| Workers | Batch creation, dissemination | $O(n)$ per batch | Disk / NIC |
| Primary | Vertex broadcast, certification | $O(n)$ per round | Signature verification |
| Consensus (Tusk/Bullshark) | Leader election, ordering | **0** (async) | Local computation |

### 4.2 Tusk: Zero-Message Asynchronous Consensus

Tusk [1] is the startling payoff of the separation thesis: a *fully asynchronous* BFT consensus protocol that sends **no messages of its own**. It is a deterministic function evaluated locally over each validator's view of the certified DAG.

Time is organized into *waves* of three rounds. After a wave completes, validators invoke a **global perfect coin** — a threshold-random beacon unpredictable before the wave ends — to elect a *leader vertex* retroactively from the wave's first round. The commit rule is then purely structural:

> **Theorem: (Tusk Commit Rule).** Let $L$ be the leader vertex of wave $w$. If at least $f+1$ vertices in the second round of $w$ have $L$ in their causal history, then every honest validator eventually commits $L$ and, recursively, every leader of earlier waves reachable from $L$.

*Why it works.* Any two sets of $f+1$ second-round vertices intersect in at least one honest vertex (since $2(f+1) > 2f+1$... more precisely, two quorums of the $2f+1$ certified vertices intersect in an honest party's vertex), and honest vertices never equivocate. Hence if one honest validator sees $f+1$ supporters, every honest validator's DAG contains at least one honest supporter, and the recursive commit of causal history converges. Liveness follows because the coin is revealed only after the DAG is fixed: the adversary cannot target the leader before election, and with probability at least $(f+1)/(2f+1) > 1/3$ per wave... in expectation, constant waves suffice.

Tusk's expected latency is 3 DAG rounds plus coin overhead, and its communication overhead beyond Narwhal is literally zero — the strongest possible realization of the separation thesis. Its cost is *latency under synchrony*: 3 rounds when 2 would do, and no ability to exploit a fast network.

### 4.3 Bullshark: Partial Synchrony with a Fast Path and a Fallback

Bullshark [2] asks: can we keep Tusk's asynchronous liveness while matching HotStuff's latency when the network is actually synchronous? Its answer is a **dual voting-type** protocol of remarkable economy — roughly 200 lines of code atop Narwhal.

Each wave in Bullshark has two *voting types*:

- **Steady-state.** Leaders are *predefined* (round-robin) rather than elected retroactively, and a wave spans only **2 rounds**. A steady-state leader commits after 2 DAG rounds — 33% fewer than Tusk — yielding HotStuff-class latency during synchrony.
- **Fallback.** If timeouts indicate asynchrony, validators switch their voting type to fallback, in which Bullshark *behaves exactly as Tusk*: retroactive coin election, 3-round waves, full asynchronous liveness.

The subtlety is the *switching rule*: validators must agree on when to abandon the fast path without breaking safety. Bullshark's mechanism tracks consecutive unsuccessful steady-state leaders; after a threshold, parties move to fallback, and when synchrony returns they revert. The protocol additionally resolves two problems Tusk left open:

- **Garbage collection.** Rounds below the committed frontier can be safely pruned because commit certificates reference the full causal history; Bullshark formalizes the GC rule that DAG-Rider lacked.
- **Fairness.** Slow validators' vertices risk starvation under continuous fast-path commits; Bullshark's fallback interludes and explicit fairness mechanisms guarantee every honest validator's transactions are eventually ordered.

Bullshark requires *no additional protocol messages or cryptographic primitives* beyond Narwhal — ordering remains a local computation over the DAG, with timeouts as the only synchrony assumption.

### 4.4 Throughput-Latency Tradeoffs: DAG-BFT vs. HotStuff and PBFT

The fundamental tradeoff can be stated crisply. Let $B$ be per-validator bandwidth and $n$ the committee size:

- **Leader-based (HotStuff/PBFT):** throughput $\approx B_{\text{leader}}$ — constant in $n$, degrading as coordination overhead grows. Latency is excellent before saturation ($\approx 2$ seconds in published WAN benchmarks [2]) because the happy path is short.
- **DAG-based (Narwhal/Tusk/Bullshark):** throughput $\approx n \cdot B_{\text{validator}}$ — *increasing* in $n$, since every validator's bandwidth contributes. Latency is structurally higher (multiple DAG rounds per commit) but *stable*: it degrades gracefully rather than collapsing.

Published WAN measurements [2] (10–50 validators, 512-byte transactions) quantify the frontier:

| Protocol | Throughput (10 val.) | Throughput (50 val.) | Latency (no faults) | Behavior under 3 crash faults |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HotStuff | ~70,000 tx/s | ~30,000 tx/s | ~2 s | Throughput → 0; 7× worse latency |
| Tusk | >100,000 tx/s | scales up | 3 rounds (~4–6 s) | ~10× HotStuff throughput |
| Bullshark | ~110,000 tx/s | ~130,000 tx/s | 2 rounds (~2–4 s) | ~10× HotStuff throughput, ~7× lower latency |

Three observations deserve emphasis. *First*, Bullshark's throughput is roughly **2× HotStuff's** at small committees and the gap widens with $n$: HotStuff falls to ~30,000 tx/s at 50 validators while Bullshark rises to ~130,000 tx/s — the signature of bandwidth aggregation versus leader bottlenecking. *Second*, Bullshark's latency is **33% lower than Tusk's** (2 rounds vs. 3) while preserving Tusk's asynchronous fallback — the fast path costs nothing in the worst case. *Third*, under crash faults the leader-based design's fragility is exposed: with 3 faulty validators out of 10, HotStuff's throughput collapses toward zero (every faulty leader stalls its views), while Tusk and Bullshark sustain an order of magnitude more throughput at a seventh of the latency [2]. Before GST, an adversary adaptively DDoSing successive leaders can hold HotStuff at zero indefinitely; Tusk's retroactive election makes such targeting impossible by construction.

The comparison also illuminates *engineering cost*: Bullshark's consensus logic is ~200 source lines over Narwhal, versus 4,000+ for a HotStuff implementation of comparable maturity — and HotStuff-over-Narwhal, while matching Bullshark's fault-free performance, doubles the engineering effort and forfeits liveness under asynchrony [2].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Safety and Liveness: Proof Outlines

> **Theorem: (Agreement).** No two honest validators commit different leader vertices for the same wave, and committed leaders induce the same total order of transactions.

*Proof outline.* Within a wave, the commit rule depends only on the DAG's causal structure and the public coin output, both eventually identical across honest validators (reliable broadcast + coin agreement). If validator $p$ commits leader $L_w$ directly ($f+1$ second-round supporters), any honest $q$ observes at least one honest supporter in its own DAG by quorum intersection, hence recursively commits $L_w$ when processing its causal history. Indirect commit follows by induction on waves. The total order is the deterministic linearization of committed leaders' causal histories, identical for all honest parties. ∎

> **Theorem: (Liveness, Tusk).** Under asynchrony, every transaction submitted to all honest validators is eventually committed with probability 1.

*Proof outline.* Narwhal guarantees every honest vertex is eventually certified and delivered to all honest parties. In each wave, the coin elects a leader uniformly among first-round vertices; with probability at least $1/3$ (in fact $(f+1)/(2f+1)$) the elected leader has $f+1$ honest supporters, triggering direct commit and flushing the causal backlog. Waves are independent, so commit occurs almost surely in expected constant waves. ∎

> **Theorem: (Bullshark fast-path latency).** During periods of synchrony with an honest predefined leader, Bullshark commits in 2 DAG rounds; during asynchrony it inherits Tusk's liveness.

*Proof outline.* The steady-state commit rule requires $2f+1$ second-round votes referencing the predefined leader — achievable within 2 rounds when messages arrive within $\Delta$. The fallback switch preserves the Tusk commit rule verbatim, so asynchrony-period safety and liveness reduce to Tusk's theorems. ∎

### 5.2 Measured Performance

The headline numbers, all from artifact-evaluated WAN deployments on multi-core cloud instances [1][2]:

1. **Narwhal standalone** sustained the offered load of 500,000+ tx/s in mempool-only benchmarks, demonstrating that dissemination — not agreement — was the historical bottleneck [1].
2. **Tusk** achieved zero-message consensus overhead with throughput scaling in the committee size, at 3-round expected latency.
3. **Bullshark** reached **110,000 tx/s (10 validators)** and **130,000 tx/s (50 validators)** end-to-end, with latency 33% below Tusk [2].
4. **Fault tolerance:** with 3 crash faults in a 10-validator WAN committee, Tusk and Bullshark delivered **10× the throughput and ~7× lower latency** than HotStuff [2].
5. **Production (Sui):** the Mysticeti-C successor, deployed on 137 Sui validators under 5,000 tx/s load, measured p50/p95 latency of **650 ms / 975 ms**, versus Bullshark's **2.89 s / 4.6 s** on the same deployment — quantifying the headroom that motivated the move to uncertified DAGs [6].

### 5.3 Deployments

- **Sui (Mysten Labs).** Narwhal/Bullshark powered Sui's mainnet consensus from launch, the first production DAG-BFT deployment, later superseded by Mysticeti-C [6].
- **Aptos.** Aptos Labs published the Bullshark paper and evaluated it against AptosBFT (a HotStuff derivative); the work informed the industry's understanding of the DAG-BFT frontier even where HotStuff variants remained in production [2].

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires enumerating what DAG-BFT does *not* solve.

1. **Latency floor.** DAG-BFT commits in multiples of DAG rounds (2–3 network trips minimum), each trip involving reliable broadcast with $O(n)$ messages. For low-throughput, latency-sensitive workloads, HotStuff's happy path remains shorter. Mysticeti [6] attacks exactly this floor with uncertified DAGs.
2. **Bandwidth amplification.** Every vertex references $2f+1$ prior vertices and carries $2f+1$ signatures per certificate; at large $n$, metadata overhead is non-trivial, and reliable broadcast's all-to-all phases stress the network fabric.
3. **Complexity of the full stack.** The "200 lines" claim covers only the consensus rule; the Narwhal mempool beneath it — workers, certificates, synchronization, garbage collection, catch-up protocols — is a substantial distributed system with its own failure modes.
4. **Fairness vs. latency tension.** Bullshark's fast path can starve slow validators; the fairness mechanisms that correct this add latency. There is no free lunch — only a better-positioned tradeoff.
5. **Partial synchrony still assumed for the fast path.** Bullshark's latency advantage evaporates before GST; only the fallback's liveness is guaranteed. An adversary that keeps the network *just* asynchronous enough defeats the steady state while never triggering clean fallback.
6. **Evaluation scope.** Published benchmarks use crash faults and 512-byte transactions on well-provisioned cloud networks; Byzantine (equivocating, censoring) behavior at scale and adversarial WAN conditions remain less explored.

## 7 Conclusion

The Narwhal–Tusk–Bullshark lineage reframes BFT consensus as two separable problems — *certified dissemination* and *deterministic ordering* — and shows that the separation is not merely conceptual but profoundly practical. Narwhal's certified DAG converts every validator's bandwidth into system throughput; Tusk's zero-message consensus proves that agreement can be a local computation over shared causal history; Bullshark demonstrates that partial synchrony can be exploited for a two-round fast path without surrendering asynchronous liveness. The measured results — 2× HotStuff's throughput, 33% less latency than Tusk, graceful degradation under faults where leader-based protocols collapse — have moved DAG-BFT from theory (DAG-Rider) to mainnet (Sui).

The research frontier is now the latency floor: Mysticeti's uncertified DAGs, Shoal's pipelined leaders, and HammerHead's reputation-based leader selection all push commit latency toward the physical limits of the network. Yet the architectural lesson endures: *decouple data from ordering, and scale the former while simplifying the latter*. Future consensus designs will be judged against this decomposition.
