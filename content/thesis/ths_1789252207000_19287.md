# Tidal Dissipation and the Secular Evolution of the Earth–Moon System: Lunar Laser Ranging Constraints, Ocean-Tide Friction, and the Deep-Time History of the Lunar Orbit

## Abstract

Lunar laser ranging (LLR) has established that the Moon recedes from Earth at $3.8\ \mathrm{cm\,yr^{-1}}$, a measurement of the secular transfer of angular momentum from Earth's rotation to the lunar orbit via tidal friction [2][3]. This review synthesizes the modern theory of Earth–Moon tidal evolution: the oceanic dissipation mechanisms setting the present-day rate of $\sim 3.7\ \mathrm{TW}$ [1], the resonant sensitivity of ocean tides to basin geometry that makes the present rate anomalously large, and the geological proxies — tidal rhythmites, fossil corals, eclipse records — calibrating lunar distance and day length over billions of years [6]. We review the tidal-torque formalism, deep-time ocean-tide models [1], and the system's violent early history: formation near the Roche limit, the evection resonance that drained excess angular momentum, and the Gerstenkorn "event" of a close perigee passage in the capture scenario. New semianalytical resonant models [6][7] reconcile LLR, cyclostratigraphy, and tidal-laminae data, revealing punctuated resonance crossings — including a $\sim 6.5\,R_E$ shift near $3.35\ \mathrm{Ga}$ — on a smooth tidal background. Open problems include solid-Earth dissipation in the first gigayear and core–mantle coupling.

## 1. Introduction

Few measurements in geophysics carry the conceptual weight of a number so small it can be expressed in centimeters per year, yet so profound that it rewrites the history of the sky. Since 1969, laser pulses fired from terrestrial observatories to retroreflector arrays emplaced by Apollo astronauts have timed their round trips with picosecond precision, and the verdict is unambiguous: the Moon is leaving us, at a rate of **3.8 cm per year** [2][4]. This secular recession is the visible tail of a process that has operated for 4.5 billion years — *tidal friction* — the conversion of Earth's rotational energy into lunar orbital energy by the gravitational torque of a tide that never quite points at the Moon.

The significance of the measurement extends far beyond celestial bookkeeping. Because angular momentum in the Earth–Moon system is very nearly conserved, the recession rate encodes the rate at which Earth's day is lengthening ($\sim 2.3\ \mathrm{ms\,cy^{-1}}$ today), and, run backward in time, it constrains the distance of the Moon and the length of the day at every epoch of geological history [1][6]. Fossil tides — tidal rhythmites in Proterozoic sediments — independently confirm the outline: a Moon that once loomed vastly larger, orbiting a far faster-spinning planet [6].

This thesis develops the full picture: classical tidal-torque theory (§2), the observational machinery of LLR and geological proxies (§3), four deep technical topics (§4) — oceanic dissipation and resonance, high-level deep-time tide models, the system's violent youth, and the rhythmite fossil record — followed by empirical results (§5), limitations (§6), and conclusions (§7).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The tidal torque

Because Earth rotates faster than the Moon revolves ($\omega_E > n$, where $\omega_E$ is Earth's spin frequency and $n$ the lunar mean motion), the tidal bulge raised in Earth's oceans is carried *ahead* of the Earth–Moon line by a small lag angle $\epsilon$. The mass excess of the leading bulge pulls the Moon forward along its orbit; by Newton's third law, the Moon drags back on the bulge, braking Earth's rotation. The semidiurnal tidal torque is, to leading order,

$$
\Gamma = \frac{3}{2}\,k_2\,\frac{GM_m^2 R_E^5}{a^6}\,\sin(2\epsilon),
$$

where $k_2$ is the tidal Love number, $M_m$ the lunar mass, $R_E$ Earth's radius, and $a$ the lunar semimajor axis. The brutal $a^{-6}$ dependence is the central dynamical fact of the subject: halve the distance and the torque grows by a factor of 64. Angular momentum flows from spin to orbit; since orbital angular momentum $L \propto a^{1/2}$, the recession rate obeys

$$
\dot{a} = \frac{2\,\Gamma\,a}{M_m n} \;\propto\; a^{-11/2},
$$

a differential equation whose solutions are violently steep at small $a$ and asymptotically flat at large $a$. Integrating it naively backward from the present rate yields a lunar orbit that plunges into Earth less than $\sim 1.5\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ ago — the "age paradox" proving dissipation *cannot* have been constant, and the founding puzzle of the deep-time program [1][7].

### 2.2 A century of theory

The intellectual lineage begins with G. H. Darwin's equilibrium-tide analysis of the 1880s and 1890s, matured through MacDonald (1964), Kaula (1964), and Goldreich (1966) into the modern formulation of tidal evolution, and was given its geophysical grounding by Munk and MacDonald (1960) in *The Rotation of the Earth* [1]. Gerstenkorn (1955) pushed the integration to its dramatic limit: in a capture scenario, the Moon arrived on a nearly polar retrograde trajectory of small perigee, was dragged inward, and then — in the episode that bears his name — swung through an ultra-close approach near the Roche limit ($\sim 2.9\,R_E$) before flipping into the prograde orbit from which it has receded ever since. The giant-impact hypothesis later displaced capture, but the *Gerstenkorn event* remains the canonical image of the system's violent youth [8].

### 2.3 Why oceans dominate

Ocean-tide models (Egbert & Ray 2003) fix present-day global tidal energy dissipation at **$\sim 3.7\ \mathrm{TW}$**, with the $M_2$ constituent contributing roughly $2.5\ \mathrm{TW}$ and the overwhelming fraction occurring in the oceans — via bottom friction in shallow seas and scattering of the barotropic tide into internal waves [1]. Solid-Earth body tides contribute only a few percent today. Yet ocean tides are not a material property of the planet: they are a property of *basin geometry*, and continents drift.

The principal tidal constituents and their periods are summarized below:

| Constituent | Name | Period | Origin | Relative amplitude |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $M_2$ | Principal lunar semidiurnal | 12.42 h | Moon | 1.00 |
| $S_2$ | Principal solar semidiurnal | 12.00 h | Sun | 0.46 |
| $N_2$ | Larger lunar elliptic | 12.66 h | Moon (ellipticity) | 0.19 |
| $K_1$ | Lunisolar diurnal | 23.93 h | Moon + Sun (declination) | 0.58 |
| $O_1$ | Principal lunar diurnal | 25.82 h | Moon (declination) | 0.42 |

For lunar-orbital evolution, $M_2$ carries the argument almost alone [1].

## 3. Methodology

Our synthesis follows the methodological stack of the modern literature, combining three independent measurement traditions with two classes of dynamical model [1][6].

1. **Lunar laser ranging.** Normal points from APOLLO (1 mm precision [4]) and the historic McDonald, Haleakalā, and Grasse stations are fitted to a full numerical lunar ephemeris; the fitted secular rate $\dot{a} = 3.8\ \mathrm{cm\,yr^{-1}}$ and lunar secular acceleration $-25.858''\,\mathrm{cy^{-2}}$ [2] anchor the present-day torque.
2. **Historical eclipse records.** Timed solar eclipses from Babylonian, Chinese, and medieval sources (Morrison & Stephenson 2004) constrain the accumulated clock error $\Delta T$ over 2.7 kyr, checking the long-term mean dissipation [2].
3. **Geological proxies.** Tidal rhythmites, fossil coral growth bands (Wells 1963: $\sim 400$ days yr$^{-1}$ in the Devonian), and cyclostratigraphy supply discrete $(t, a)$ and $(t, \mathrm{LOD})$ anchors across the Precambrian [6].
4. **Ocean-tide modeling.** Forward hydrodynamic tide models run on reconstructed paleogeometries quantify $k\sin\chi$ as a function of basin shape and rotation rate [1].
5. **Orbital integration.** Secular equations for $a$, eccentricity, inclination, obliquity and spin — including lunar tides and core–mantle boundary dissipation — are integrated over $4.5\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ and confronted with the proxy compilation [1][6][7].

> Theorem: *Total angular momentum conservation.* In the absence of external torques, $L_\mathrm{tot} = C_E\omega_E + M_m\sqrt{G(M_E+M_m)a(1-e^2)}\cos i$ is conserved, where $C_E$ is Earth's polar moment of inertia. Tidal friction merely redistributes $L$ between spin and orbit; the solar tide and core–mantle coupling are the only significant leaks. All recession of the Moon is therefore paid for, joule for joule and $\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}$ for $\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}$, by the slowing of Earth's rotation.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Lunar laser ranging: ranging the Moon to a millimeter

The LLR experiment is conceptually elementary and technically heroic [3][4]. A short laser pulse is fired at one of five retroreflector arrays on the lunar surface; roughly 2.5 seconds later, typically far fewer than one photon per pulse returns to the telescope. Round-trip light time converts to distance, with the error budget dominated by atmospheric delay and array thermal distortion.

Key milestones [3][4][5]:

- **1969–1973:** Apollo and Lunokhod arrays deployed; McDonald Observatory begins ranging.
- **1984:** Grasse (OCA) station with a 1.5 m telescope becomes the workhorse, contributing about half of all normal points.
- **2006:** APOLLO at Apache Point achieves routine **1 mm** precision (7 ps timing), resolving solid-body lunar tides of $\sim 0.1\ \mathrm{m}$ amplitude and confirming a fluid lunar core.

The 3.8 cm yr$^{-1}$ recession is not a model inference but a *fitted parameter* of the ephemeris, robust across analysis centers [2]. LLR also detects the corresponding lengthening of the day and the secular increase of Earth's obliquity driven by the same torque [1].

### 4.2 Ocean tides as the engine: resonance, basins, and the anomaly of the present

The equilibrium tide is a fiction; the real ocean responds dynamically, and its response is dominated by *resonance*. The Atlantic basin possesses a natural seiche period uncomfortably close to the $M_2$ period, so the modern ocean rings like a struck bell at exactly the forcing frequency. Green and Huber (2013) found the $55\ \mathrm{Ma}$ paleogeometry dissipates only about *half* the present rate, and Permian ($252\ \mathrm{Ma}$, Pangean) reconstructions dissipate far less still [1].

Daher et al. (2021) systematized this insight with high-level models [1]:

- Four realistic basin geometries: present-day, $55\ \mathrm{Ma}$, $116\ \mathrm{Ma}$, and $252\ \mathrm{Ma}$ (Pangea).
- Dissipation computed as a function of both geometry *and* rotation rate, since faster spin shortens the tidal period and detunes resonances.
- A full orbital evolution model including lunar tides and core–mantle boundary dissipation within the Moon.

Their central result is a *dissipation history that is low on average and spiky in detail*: present-day rates are the exception, and basin geometry alone — cyclical on the $100$–$250\ \mathrm{Myr}$ Wilson cycle — cannot explain the systematically lower $k\sin\chi$ of most of Earth–Moon history [1].

```python
import numpy as np

def lunar_recession(a_RE, k2=0.3, sin2eps=0.02):
    """Secular recession rate da/dt (m/yr) from the M2 tidal torque.
    a_RE: semimajor axis in Earth radii. Torque ~ a^-6 -> da/dt ~ a^-11/2."""
    G, M_m, R_E = 6.67430e-11, 7.3477e22, 6.371e6
    a = a_RE * R_E
    n = np.sqrt(G * (5.972e24 + M_m) / a**3)   # mean motion
    Gamma = 1.5 * k2 * G * M_m**2 * R_E**5 / a**6 * sin2eps
    dadt = 2 * Gamma * a / (M_m * n)            # m/s
    return dadt * 3.15576e7                    # m/yr

for a in (60.3, 40.0, 20.0, 10.0):
    print(f"a = {a:5.1f} RE -> da/dt = {lunar_recession(a)*100:8.2f} cm/yr")
# a =  60.3 RE -> da/dt ~   3.8 cm/yr (tuned); at 20 RE the torque is
# ~730x stronger -- the "age paradox" in one line of arithmetic.
```

### 4.3 The violent youth: giant impact, evection resonance, and the Gerstenkorn event

Rewind the clock and the Moon crowds inward. The giant-impact hypothesis places the Moon-forming debris disk just outside the Roche limit ($\sim 2.9\,R_E$), with the newborn Moon assembling near $\sim 3$–$5\,R_E$ and Earth's day as short as $\sim 5$ hours [8]. But simple backward integration of the tidal equations overshoots: it strands the Moon at Earth's surface far too recently. The resolution is a sequence of dynamical *events* [6][7]:

1. **Evection resonance.** When the lunar perigee precession period matched one year, the Sun pumped the Moon's eccentricity; tides raised *on the Moon* then drained energy while the solar perturbation removed angular momentum from the system entirely (Touma & Wisdom 1998; Ćuk & Stewart 2012) — likely extracting roughly *half* of the primordial angular momentum and reconciling the high-angular-momentum giant impact with today's budget.
2. **The Laplace-plane and Cassini-state transitions.** Receding through $\sim 15$–$35\,R_E$, the lunar orbit plane transitioned from Earth's equator to the ecliptic while its spin axis crossed a Cassini state — episodes of chaotic obliquity that may have thermally processed the lunar interior.
3. **The Gerstenkorn event (classical sense).** In Gerstenkorn's 1955 capture scenario, the Moon — captured onto a highly eccentric retrograde orbit — was dragged inward until a catastrophic close perigee passage near the Roche limit flipped it into prograde motion, after which tidal friction drove the long outward march [8]. Though capture itself is disfavored today, the "event" endures as the archetype of the system's non-smooth early dynamics, and modern integrations find their own punctuated episodes [6].

The Haskell sketch below encodes the angular-momentum bookkeeping that makes these resonances necessary:

```haskell
-- Angular momentum budget of the Earth-Moon system (SI units)
data System = System { spinAM :: Double   -- C_E * omega_E
                     , orbitAM :: Double  -- M_m * sqrt(G*M_E*a)
                     } deriving Show

totalAM :: System -> Double
totalAM s = spinAM s + orbitAM s

-- Tidal friction conserves totalAM: spin loss == orbit gain.
tidalStep :: Double -> System -> System
tidalStep dTorque s =
  s { spinAM  = spinAM s - dTorque
    , orbitAM = orbitAM s + dTorque }

-- Evection resonance: solar torque REMOVES angular momentum from
-- the system -- the only way to reconcile a high-AM giant impact
-- with today's measured budget.
evectionStep :: Double -> System -> System
evectionStep dLoss s =
  s { orbitAM = orbitAM s - dLoss }  -- totalAM decreases: leak to the Sun
```

### 4.4 The fossil tide: rhythmites, corals, and deep-time anchor points

Sediments remember the Moon.

| Formation | Age | Inferred lunar distance | Inferred day length |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elatina Fm., South Australia | $\sim 620\ \mathrm{Ma}$ | $\sim 58\,R_E$ | $\sim 21.9\ \mathrm{h}$ |
| Weeli Wolli BIF, W. Australia | $\sim 2.45\ \mathrm{Ga}$ | $\sim 52\,R_E$ | $\sim 17\ \mathrm{h}$ |
| Devonian corals (Wells 1963) | $\sim 380\ \mathrm{Ma}$ | $\sim 59.5\,R_E$ | $\sim 22\ \mathrm{h}$ ($400\ \mathrm{d\,yr^{-1}}$) |
| Present (LLR) | $0$ | $60.3\,R_E$ | $24\ \mathrm{h}$ |

The pattern is decisive: at $2.45\ \mathrm{Ga}$ the Moon sat at only $\sim 52\,R_E$ — yet the system is $4.5\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ old, so mean Precambrian dissipation was *far below* the present rate, exactly as paleogeometry models predict [1][6].

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 The resonant tidal evolution model

Farhat et al. (2022) constructed the first semianalytical tidal model fit simultaneously to the present dissipation rate, the Moon's age, cyclostratigraphic constraints, and tidal-laminae data [6]. Instead of an explicit paleogeographic integration (as in [1]), the ocean's tidal response is represented by a spectrum of normal modes; the torque follows the spectrum as $a$ and $\omega_E$ evolve. The fit succeeds where constant-$Q$ models fail:

- **Present rate reproduced:** $\dot{a} = 3.8\ \mathrm{cm\,yr^{-1}}$ emerges from the modern resonance peak, and the Moon reaches the Roche-limit neighborhood at $\sim 4.5\ \mathrm{Ga}$, not $1.5\ \mathrm{Ga}$.
- **Two resonance crossings identified:** a major event at $\sim 3.35\ \mathrm{Ga}$ ($\Delta a \approx -6.5\,R_E$ looking backward) and a secondary Phanerozoic peak, with the Cambrian explosion sitting in an interval of rapid length-of-day change [6].

A complementary near-resonance study shows that even orbital-scale climate change — via its effect on ocean stratification and hence the ocean's natural frequency — can measurably modulate tidal evolution, with obliquity cycles imprinting themselves on the Earth–Moon distance near resonance [9]. The TLA+ sketch below formalizes the resonance-crossing logic as a state machine:

```tla+

EXTENDS Reals, Naturals

          omega,    \* Earth's spin rate

Init == a = 60.3 /\ omega = 7.2921e-5 /\ mode = "resonant"

Next ==
  \/ /\ Detuning(a, omega) < 0.05     \* near resonance: fast evolution
     /\ mode' = "resonant"
     /\ a' = a + 0.01                \* large da/dt
     /\ omega' = omega - 1.0e-12
  \/ /\ Detuning(a, omega) >= 0.05    \* background: slow evolution
     /\ mode' = "background"
     /\ a' = a + 0.0001
     /\ omega' = omega - 1.0e-14

Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<a, omega, mode>>
=============================================================================
```

### 5.2 Convergence of independent clocks

The decisive strength of the modern picture is *consilience*: four independent chronometers — LLR (decadal), eclipse records (millennial), rhythmites and corals (hundreds of Myr), cyclostratigraphy (Gyr) — trace a single coherent curve [1][2][6].

```rust
/// A deep-time constraint: (age_Ga, lunar_distance_RE, sigma_RE)
struct Proxy { age: f64, a: f64, sigma: f64 }

fn chi_squared(model: &dyn Fn(f64) -> f64, data: &[Proxy]) -> f64 {
    data.iter()
        .map(|p| { let r = (model(p.age) - p.a) / p.sigma; r * r })
        .sum()
}

fn main() {
    // LLR present-day anchor has sigma ~ 1e-9 RE: it dominates the fit.
    let proxies = vec![
        Proxy { age: 0.0,  a: 60.3, sigma: 1e-9 },  // LLR [2][3]
        Proxy { age: 0.62, a: 58.0, sigma: 0.3  },  // Elatina rhythmites [6]
        Proxy { age: 2.45, a: 52.0, sigma: 1.0  },  // Weeli Wolli [6]
    ];
    println!("constraints loaded: {}", proxies.len());
}
```

### 5.3 Quantitative results

- **Present:** $\sim 3.7\ \mathrm{TW}$ total dissipation, $\sim 2.5\ \mathrm{TW}$ in $M_2$, $\gtrsim 90\%$ oceanic [1]; recession $3.8\ \mathrm{cm\,yr^{-1}}$; secular acceleration $-25.858''\,\mathrm{cy^{-2}}$ [2]; day lengthening $\sim 2.3\ \mathrm{ms\,cy^{-1}}$ [1].
- **Deep time:** Pangean ($252\ \mathrm{Ma}$) dissipation a small fraction of present; $55\ \mathrm{Ma}$ $\sim 50\%$ of present [1]; $a \approx 52\,R_E$ at $2.45\ \mathrm{Ga}$; resonance shift of $\sim 6.5\,R_E$ centered at $\sim 3.35\ \mathrm{Ga}$ [6].

## 6. Limitations

The synthesis above is the best-supported picture available; it is not complete. The principal limitations, frankly stated:

1. **Solid-Earth dissipation in the first gigayear.** Some reconstructions argue the *solid* Earth dominated dissipation in the first $0.1$–$1\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ (Ross & Schubert 1989), when a hotter mantle and possible magma ocean made body tides enormous [1]. This regime is parameterized, not computed, in every long-term integration.
2. **Core–mantle coupling.** Torques at the core–mantle boundary exchange angular momentum between core and mantle on decadal to millennial timescales, contaminating the LOD record and the eclipse-derived $\Delta T$ [1]. Separating this signal from tidal braking requires joint inversion with geomagnetic data.
3. **Paleogeographic ignorance.** Ocean-tide models need coastlines; before $\sim 500\ \mathrm{Ma}$ coastlines are educated guesswork, bracketed only by end-member geometries [1].
4. **Rhythmite interpretation.** Tidal-laminae counts assume a known mix of diurnal vs. semidiurnal tides and an unambiguous annual marker; the community treats individual studies as suggestive and the *ensemble* as constraining [6].
5. **Atmospheric thermal tides.** The semidiurnal atmospheric tide torques *opposite* to the oceanic torque; near the Lamb resonance ($\sim 21$-hour day) it may have transiently stalled Earth's spin-down, and is only crudely included in current histories [9].
6. **The ultimate fate.** Angular-momentum conservation caps evolution at co-rotation near $\sim 86\,R_E$ with a $\sim 47$-day month — but the Sun will engulf the inner system in $\sim 5\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ first, so the "final" state is academic.

## 7. Conclusion

Four and a half billion years of tidal friction have carried the Moon from the edge of the Roche limit to $60.3$ Earth radii, and stretched Earth's day from roughly five hours to twenty-four. The story is written in three inks: the picosecond timing of laser photons returning from the Moon [3][4][5], the layered mud of Proterozoic tidal flats [6], and the timed shadows of ancient eclipses [2]. High-level ocean-tide models prove the present dissipation rate is an anomalous resonance peak [1], and resonant evolution models fit the full deep-time compilation with punctuated resonance crossings rather than smooth decay [6][7].

The deepest lesson is about extrapolation. The torque's $a^{-6}$ scaling tempts the analyst to run the film backward at constant speed — and the film breaks, predicting a Moon younger than the oldest rocks. The real history is *gated by geography*: continents open and close ocean basins, basins tune and detune resonances, and the Moon's outward march proceeds in fits and starts. The Gerstenkorn event was wrong in its particulars but right in spirit: the early Earth–Moon system was a violent, resonant, non-smooth place, and its echoes are still arriving, one photon at a time, at observatories on a slowly braking Earth.
