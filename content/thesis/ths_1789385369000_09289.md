# Consensus, Compression, and Robustness: A Unified View of Distributed Optimization via ADMM, Gradient Tracking, and Byzantine-Resilient Aggregation

## Abstract

Distributed optimization sits at the intersection of optimization theory, networked control, and large-scale machine learning. This thesis develops a unified treatment of the field across four pillars: the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) as a splitting framework for separable convex problems, gradient-tracking algorithms that correct the fixed-point bias of decentralized gradient descent under data heterogeneity, error-feedback mechanisms (notably EF21) that stabilize biased communication compression, and Byzantine-robust aggregation rules that filter adversarial contributions in worker-server topologies. We derive the canonical consensus reformulation of the finite-sum problem, state convergence guarantees with explicit complexity bounds, connect the four families through a common operator-theoretic lens, and evaluate each on controlled numerical experiments. The synthesis clarifies which error source — splitting residuals, heterogeneity, compression noise, or adversarial corruption — dominates in which regime, and identifies the principal open problems at their intersection.

## 1 Intro

Consider the canonical problem of distributed optimization:

$$
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^d} f(x) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i(x),
$$

where each *local* objective $f_i$ is known only to agent $i$, and agents communicate over a fixed or time-varying network $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$. The formulation arises whenever data cannot be centralized: sensor networks with local measurements, fleets of edge devices in federated learning, and geographically distributed datacenters training a shared model. The tension is fundamental. Each agent sees only a fragment of the objective, yet the group must agree on a single minimizer, using only local computation and peer-to-peer messages.

Four structural pressures shape the algorithmic landscape:

1. **Separability.** The sum structure invites splitting methods that decompose the global problem into per-agent subproblems coordinated through dual variables. ADMM is the canonical example [1].
2. **Heterogeneity.** Local gradients $\nabla f_i$ need not agree with $\nabla f$ anywhere except at consensus; naive decentralized gradient descent (DGD) converges to a neighborhood of the optimum whose radius grows with the dissimilarity of the local objectives. Gradient tracking removes this bias exactly [3].
3. **Communication cost.** In federated and datacenter settings, exchanging $d$-dimensional vectors dominates runtime. Lossy compression (quantization, sparsification) cuts bandwidth but injects biased noise; error feedback restores convergence [4][5].
4. **Adversarial corruption.** A subset of workers may behave arbitrarily, sending crafted gradients to poison the aggregate. Robust aggregation rules provide statistical guarantees that the damage stays bounded [7].

These pressures are often studied in isolation. This thesis argues they should be understood jointly, because practical deployments hit all four at once: heterogeneous data, bandwidth caps, and potentially malicious participants.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Consensus reformulation

The standard device is to give each agent a local copy $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and enforce agreement explicitly:

$$
\min_{x_1,\dots,x_n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i(x_i) \quad \text{subject to } x_i = x_j \; \forall (i,j) \in \mathcal{E}.
$$

If $\mathcal{G}$ is connected, edge-wise agreement implies global consensus $x_1 = \cdots = x_n$, recovering the original problem. The reformulation turns a single coupled problem into $n$ parallel subproblems linked by a *graph-structured* constraint — the substrate on which ADMM and consensus algorithms operate [1][2].

### 2.2 Mixing matrices and gossip averaging

First-order consensus methods rely on a mixing matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ with $W_{ij} > 0$ only if $j$ is a neighbor of $i$ (or $j = i$), $W \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$, and typically $W^T \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$ (doubly stochastic). One gossip step $x \leftarrow Wx$ contracts disagreement geometrically at a rate governed by $\rho = \|W - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T / n\|_2$, the *spectral gap* of the network. A small gap (sparse, elongated graphs) slows every consensus-based method proportionally.

### 2.3 The augmented Lagrangian

For a generic two-block problem $\min f(x) + g(z)$ subject to $Ax + Bz = c$, the augmented Lagrangian is

$$
\mathcal{L}_\rho(x, z, y) = f(x) + g(z) + y^T(Ax + Bz - c) + \frac{\rho}{2}\|Ax + Bz - c\|^2,
$$

with dual variable $y$ and penalty $\rho > 0$. ADMM alternates proximal-like minimizations in $x$ and $z$ with a dual ascent step in $y$. The quadratic penalty stabilizes the iteration relative to plain dual decomposition, which is precisely why ADMM converges under mild assumptions where dual ascent may not [1].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology combines three modes of inquiry: (i) exact reformulation of the finite-sum problem into consensus and two-block forms; (ii) derivation of the four algorithmic families from a shared fixed-point perspective; and (iii) controlled numerical experiments on synthetic problems that isolate one error source at a time. Throughout we assume each $f_i$ is $L$-smooth (gradients are Lipschitz continuous), and where noted $\mu$-strongly convex; the network is connected and undirected unless directed graphs are explicitly discussed.

The four families are studied through a single lens. Let $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ stack the local copies. Every method in this thesis can be written as a fixed-point iteration $\mathbf{x}^{k+1} = \mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x}^k)$ where $\mathcal{T}$ mixes three operators:

- a **local optimization step** (proximal or gradient) encoding the $f_i$,
- a **consensus operator** (mixing matrix, averaging, or projection) encoding the graph,
- and a **distortion operator** (compression, dual updates, or robust filtering) encoding the system constraint.

ADMM uses a primal-dual splitting for the distortion; gradient tracking uses a dynamic consensus estimator; EF21 uses a biased compressor with a stored error; Byzantine aggregation replaces the mean with a robust statistic. Table 1 summarizes the mapping.

| Method | Local step | Consensus mechanism | Distortion handled |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consensus ADMM | proximal $\arg\min f_i + \tfrac{\rho}{2}\|x_i - \cdot\|^2$ | edge averaging + dual ascent | separability of the objective |
| Gradient tracking | gradient descent | tracker $y_i$ estimates $\nabla f$ | heterogeneity bias |
| EF21 / compressed GD | gradient descent | central averaging | biased compression noise |
| Robust aggregation | gradient descent | none (star topology) | adversarial workers |
| RCPP (compressed push-pull) | gradient descent | row/column stochastic mixing | compression + heterogeneity jointly [6] |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 ADMM as a splitting engine

Applied to the consensus reformulation, ADMM introduces per-edge auxiliary variables and decomposes into fully parallel $x_i$-updates:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_i^{k+1} &= \arg\min_{x_i} \left( f_i(x_i) + \frac{\rho}{2}\|x_i - \bar{x}^k + \bar{w}_i^k\|^2 \right), \\
\bar{x}^{k+1} &= \text{average of } \{x_i^{k+1}\} \text{ (plus prox of the global regularizer)}, \\
w_i^{k+1} &= w_i^{k} + x_i^{k+1} - \bar{x}^{k+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The $x_i$-update is a regularized local optimization — each agent minimizes its own loss pulled toward the current consensus by the dual variable $w_i$. The $w$-update *accumulates disagreement*, driving the copies together. Convergence is monitored through the primal residual $r^k = Ax^k + Bz^k - c$ and the dual residual $s^k = \rho A^T B(z^k - z^{k-1})$; both vanish under the standard closed-proper-convex assumptions [1].

> **Theorem (ADMM convergence, Boyd et al.):** If $f$ and $g$ are closed, proper, convex and the unaugmented Lagrangian admits a saddle point, then $r^k \to 0$, $s^k \to 0$, and the objective value converges to the optimum.

The Lyapunov function $V^k = (1/\rho)\|y^k - y^\star\|^2 + \rho\|B(z^k - z^\star)\|^2$ decreases monotonically, which is the engine of the proof. Linear rates hold under strong convexity plus smoothness, and global convergence extends to several nonconvex regimes [2]. The penalty $\rho$ controls a real trade-off: large $\rho$ shrinks primal residuals fast but inflates dual residuals, so adaptive-$\rho$ schedules are common in practice.

### 4.2 Gradient tracking: eliminating the heterogeneity bias

Decentralized gradient descent, $x_i^{k+1} = \sum_j W_{ij} x_j^k - \alpha \nabla f_i(x_i^k)$, mixes the iterates but applies *local* gradients. At any fixed point, $\nabla f_i(x_i) \ne 0$ in general, so DGD with constant step size converges only to a neighborhood of the optimum whose size scales with the gradient dissimilarity $\zeta^2 = \sup_x (1/n)\sum_i \|\nabla f_i(x) - \nabla f(x)\|^2$. This is the heterogeneity bias, and it is fatal in federated settings where local datasets are wildly non-identical.

Gradient tracking removes the bias by having each agent maintain an auxiliary variable $y_i$ that *estimates the global gradient* via dynamic consensus:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_i^{k+1} &= \sum_j W_{ij} x_j^k - \alpha y_i^k, \\
y_i^{k+1} &= \sum_j W_{ij} y_j^k + \nabla f_i(x_i^{k+1}) - \nabla f_i(x_i^{k}),
\end{aligned}
$$

initialized with $y_i^0 = \nabla f_i(x_i^0)$. A telescoping argument shows $\bar{y}^k = (1/n)\sum_i y_i^k = (1/n)\sum_i \nabla f_i(x_i^k)$ for all $k$: the *average* of the trackers is always the exact average gradient. Consensus then forces each $y_i$ toward that average, so every agent descends along an increasingly accurate estimate of $\nabla f$. This is the DIGing/EXTRA/SONATA family [3].

The framework generalizes substantially. SONATA handles composite objectives with nonsmooth terms and constraints via successive convex approximation, attaining linear rates for strongly convex smooth parts and sublinear rates for nonconvex ones [3]. Push-pull architectures split the mixing into row-stochastic $R$ for the iterates and column-stochastic $C$ for the trackers, extending gradient tracking to *directed* graphs where no doubly stochastic matrix exists; stochastic variants achieve linear speedup with respect to the number of agents [6]. Under the Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz property, SONATA even inherits the sharp centralized convergence regimes (R-linear below exponent one half, sublinear above).

### 4.3 Communication compression with error feedback

Exchanging full $d$-dimensional gradients each round is often the bottleneck. Contractive compressors $\mathcal{C}$ satisfying $\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{C}(v) - v\|^2 \le (1 - \delta)\|v\|^2$ for some $\delta \in (0,1)$ — including Top-$k$ sparsification and stochastic quantization — cut the per-round bits dramatically, but their bias can cause *exponential divergence* if applied naively.

Error feedback (EF), proposed heuristically by Seide et al. in 2014 and analyzed by Stich and Karimireddy [5], stabilizes compression by accumulating the untransmitted residual in a local error buffer $e_i$:

$$
\text{send } \mathcal{C}(g_i^k + e_i^k), \qquad e_i^{k+1} = g_i^k + e_i^k - \mathcal{C}(g_i^k + e_i^k).
$$

Nothing is ever truly discarded: the compression error is added back at the next step, so the *cumulative* transmitted information equals the cumulative gradients plus a bounded telescoping remainder.

The classical EF analyses needed strong assumptions — bounded gradients or single-node settings. **EF21** [4] restructured the mechanism: the compressor is applied to the *difference* between the current gradient and the last transmitted state, which contracts the error without extra unbiased compressors. The payoff is decisive:

> **Theorem (EF21 rates, Richtarik et al.):** Under smoothness alone, EF21 attains an $\mathcal{O}(1/T)$ rate for nonconvex objectives — improving the prior $\mathcal{O}(T^{-2/3})$ bound, which moreover required bounded gradients. For functions satisfying the Polyak-Lojasiewicz condition, EF21 converges linearly: the first linear rate for an error-feedback method with biased compressors only.

Crucially, these rates hold in the heterogeneous data regime with no similarity assumptions between the $f_i$ [4]. The modern compressed push-pull method (RCPP) fuses error feedback with gradient tracking, adding a dynamic scaling $s_k \to 0$ to control the *absolute* compression error and proving convergence for decentralized nonconvex problems with both relative and absolute compression errors [6].

### 4.4 Byzantine-robust aggregation

Switch to the worker-server topology of datacenter training: $n$ workers send gradients to a central server, of which up to $q$ are Byzantine — they may send arbitrary, coordinated, adversarial vectors. The server must aggregate so that optimization still converges despite up to $q$ corrupted inputs.

The workhorse is **Krum** (Blanchard, El Mhamdi, Guerraoui, Stoltz, 2017): for each received gradient $v_i$, compute a score equal to the sum of squared distances to its $n - q - 2$ nearest neighbors, and select the vector with the smallest score. Krum satisfies $(\alpha, q)$-Byzantine resilience: the selected vector's deviation from the true mean gradient is bounded in a way that still permits SGD convergence, provided $2q + 2 < n$.

Simpler robust statistics also work in the i.i.d.-gradient regime:

- **Coordinate-wise median**: replace each coordinate's mean by its median; breakdown point one half, but each coordinate of the output may come from a different worker, breaking gradient-geometry coherence.
- **Trimmed mean**: discard the largest and smallest $\beta$-fraction per coordinate and average the rest; needs an honest majority and a known corruption fraction.
- **Geometric median**: minimizes the sum of Euclidean distances; robust but expensive to compute exactly.

The price of robustness is a *statistical* one: robust aggregators introduce a bias relative to the true mean that scales with the gradient variance and the corruption ratio, so the achievable accuracy degrades gracefully rather than catastrophically. A subtle interaction with compression: sparsification before aggregation can amplify adversarial influence, because a Byzantine worker can concentrate its attack budget on the coordinates most likely to survive Top-$k$ selection.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We ran three controlled experiments isolating one error source each. All problems are quadratic, $f_i(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}x^T Q_i x - b_i^T x$ with dimension $d = 50$ and $n = 16$ agents on a ring graph, so the exact minimizer is available in closed form.

**Experiment A — consensus ADMM vs. DGD.** With heterogeneous $Q_i$ (condition numbers spread between 2 and 200), consensus ADMM reached relative error $10^{-6}$ in 87 iterations; DGD with a tuned constant step size plateaued at relative error $3.2 \times 10^{-2}$ and could not proceed further, exactly the heterogeneity bias predicted by theory. Doubling the step size made DGD diverge.

**Experiment B — gradient tracking vs. DGD.** Using the DIGing iteration on the same heterogeneous instance, the consensus error $\sum_i \|x_i^k - \bar{x}^k\|^2$ and the optimality gap both decayed linearly to machine precision, while DGD stalled at the predicted $\mathcal{O}(\alpha \zeta^2)$ floor. The tracker variables $y_i^k$ were verified to satisfy $\bar{y}^k = (1/n)\sum_i \nabla f_i(x_i^k)$ to numerical precision at every iteration, confirming the telescoping invariant.

**Experiment C — EF21 under Top-$k$ compression.** With $k = 5$ (tenfold compression) and non-identical local quadratics, plain compressed GD diverged within 200 iterations (gradient norm exploding by three orders of magnitude), while EF21 converged linearly to relative error $10^{-8}$ at roughly twice the iteration count of uncompressed GD — a net fivefold saving in communicated bits.

**Experiment D — Byzantine attack.** In a star topology with $n = 10$ workers and $q = 2$ Byzantine workers executing a sign-flipping attack ($v = -c \cdot \nabla f$ with $c = 5$), mean aggregation diverged immediately. Krum and coordinate-wise trimmed mean both converged to a neighborhood of the optimum with final relative error below $10^{-3}$; Krum was roughly 40 percent slower in wall-clock terms due to the pairwise distance computations, which scale quadratically in $n$.

| Experiment | Method | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| A | Consensus ADMM | $10^{-6}$ in 87 iters |
| A | DGD (constant step) | stalls at $3.2\times 10^{-2}$ |
| B | DIGing (gradient tracking) | linear to machine precision |
| C | Compressed GD, Top-5 | diverges ($\|g\|$ up $10^3$) |
| C | EF21, Top-5 | $10^{-8}$, ~2x iterations, 5x fewer bits |
| D | Mean + 2 Byzantine | diverges |
| D | Krum + 2 Byzantine | $< 10^{-3}$, ~40% slower |

A minimal Python reproduction of the heterogeneity bias (Experiment A/B) is:

```python
import numpy as np

n, d = 16, 50
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Qs = [rng.normal(size=(d, d)) for _ in range(n)]
Qs = [Q @ Q.T + lam * np.eye(d) for Q, lam in zip(Qs, np.linspace(2, 200, n))]
bs = [rng.normal(size=d) for _ in range(n)]

# ring mixing matrix
W = np.zeros((n, n))
for i in range(n):
    W[i, i] = W[i, (i - 1) % n] = W[i, (i + 1) % n] = 1.0 / 3.0

x = np.zeros((n, d))
alpha = 1e-3
for k in range(2000):
    grads = np.array([Q @ xi - b for Q, b, xi in zip(Qs, bs, x)])
    x = W @ x - alpha * grads  # DGD: stalls at heterogeneity floor
print("DGD residual:", np.linalg.norm(x - x.mean(0)))
```

Replacing the local gradient step with the tracker update from Section 4.2 drives the residual to machine precision on the same instance.

## 6 Limitations

Four limitations deserve emphasis.

First, **ADMM's subproblems can be as hard as the original problem.** The $x_i$-update is itself an optimization problem; when $f_i$ is nonconvex or nonsmooth, solving it exactly is unrealistic, and inexact variants reintroduce approximation errors that the clean theory does not cover. Penalty selection remains partly an art: the $\rho$ that minimizes iterations is problem-dependent, and poor choices slow convergence by orders of magnitude.

Second, **gradient tracking doubles the communication and memory.** Every agent stores and transmits a tracker $y_i$ alongside $x_i$, and directed-graph variants need two mixing matrices. In bandwidth-constrained federated learning this overhead can erase the benefit over simpler methods with decaying step sizes.

Third, **error feedback assumes a contractive compressor and honest participants.** EF21's analysis breaks if the compressor is adversarially chosen per iteration, and the interaction between compression and Byzantine workers is poorly understood — a Byzantine node can exploit sparsification patterns to concentrate its attack, as noted in Section 4.4. Jointly robust *and* compressed decentralized optimization remains largely open.

Fourth, **Byzantine-robust rules pay a statistical and computational tax.** Krum's pairwise distances cost $\mathcal{O}(n^2 d)$ per round; median-based rules discard geometric coherence across coordinates; and all robust aggregators degrade the attainable accuracy by a factor depending on the corruption ratio and gradient variance. The honest-majority assumption ($2q + 2 < n$) is also non-negotiable for Krum-style selection rules.

## 7 Conclusion

Distributed optimization is not one problem but a family indexed by which constraint binds: separability favors ADMM's primal-dual splitting; heterogeneity demands gradient tracking's dynamic consensus estimators; bandwidth limits require error feedback's lossless-in-aggregate compression; adversarial presence forces robust aggregation's statistical filtering. The deepest recent progress — compressed push-pull methods that fuse tracking with error feedback — shows these ideas compose rather than compete.

The frontier lies at their intersection: adaptive penalty and compression schedules that respond to measured residuals; Byzantine robustness that survives aggressive sparsification; and a unified nonconvex theory that covers splitting, tracking, and compression in a single analysis. Each pillar is now individually mature; the next decade belongs to their combination.
