# Bosonic Quantum Error Correction via GKP Lattices and Dissipative Cat Qubits: Bias-Preserving Gates for Fault-Tolerant Superconducting Circuits

## Abstract

Superconducting microwave cavities offer millisecond coherence and a well-characterized noise model dominated by single-photon loss and dephasing, making them natural hosts for hardware-efficient quantum error correction. This thesis studies the two bosonic encodings that have advanced furthest toward fault tolerance: Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill lattice codes, which digitize small phase-space displacements into correctable errors and admit Gaussian-implemented Clifford gates, and Schrödinger-cat qubits, whose two-photon stabilized manifold converts photon loss into a strongly biased channel with bit-flips suppressed exponentially in the mean photon number. We develop the stabilizer and Lindblad theory of both families, present bias-preserving gate constructions whose noise never converts the dominant error into its suppressed conjugate, and analyze concatenation with outer repetition and surface codes, including quantitative results such as fault-tolerant squeezing near 13 dB and logical error below one part per million per cycle at repetition distance seven. We survey superconducting implementations from Kerr-cat devices to cat-transmon hybrids and identify the remaining barriers: ancilla-limited GKP memory, phase-flip growth with cat size, and non-adiabatic gate error.

##1 Intro

A single quantum harmonic oscillator has an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, and bosonic quantum error correction exploits that redundancy to protect a logical qubit inside *one* physical mode rather than across dozens of two-level systems [1]. In superconducting circuits this is not merely an aesthetic preference: three-dimensional microwave cavities now reach single-photon lifetimes in the millisecond range, and their dominant noise processes — single-photon loss at rate kappa_1 and pure dephasing — map onto small, tractable displacements in phase space. Two encoding philosophies have emerged as the leading candidates for fault-tolerant computation on this platform.

The first, the Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill code, discretizes the continuum. Ideal code states are Dirac combs in position and momentum, stabilized by phase-space translations, so that any sufficiently small displacement is a correctable error and large displacements are the logical operators [1]. The second, the Schrödinger-cat qubit, embraces asymmetry. A two-photon driven-dissipative process pins the oscillator to a manifold spanned by coherent states of opposite phase; single-photon loss then acts almost entirely as phase flips, while bit flips require tunneling between macroscopically distinct phase-space regions and are suppressed as exp(-2 nbar) in the mean photon number nbar [2]. The residual noise is *biased*, and biased noise is cheaper to correct: a repetition code, which would be a terrible general-purpose code, becomes a powerful one when only one error type survives.

The central claim of this thesis is that these two philosophies are complementary rather than competing. GKP codes supply symmetric, high-rate protection with Gaussian Clifford gates; cat qubits supply exponential bias that collapses the overhead of the outer code. In both cases the decisive engineering advance of the last decade has been the same: *gates that preserve the structure of the noise they operate on*. A bias-preserving gate never converts the dominant error into the suppressed one, and a fault-tolerant GKP Clifford never converts a small displacement into a large one. We make the following contributions:

1. A unified stabilizer and Lindblad treatment of finite-energy GKP codes and dissipatively confined cat qubits, with the scaling laws that govern their physical error rates.
2. A taxonomy of bias-preserving gate constructions — topological-phase CNOTs, Zeno gates, vacuum-conditional beam-splitter gates, and Gaussian GKP Cliffords — with their error budgets and scaling.
3. A quantitative comparison of concatenation strategies, from repetition-cat memories to GKP-surface hybrids, anchored in published threshold and logical-error estimates.
4. An assessment of superconducting-circuit realizations, identifying which barriers are fundamental and which are engineering.

##2 Background

### Oscillators, displacements, and the bosonic error model

The quadrature operators q and p of a single mode satisfy the canonical commutation relation, and physical noise in a cavity is well approximated by random small displacements D(beta) = exp(beta a† - beta* a) together with photon loss and dephasing [9]. This error model is the bosonic analogue of the Pauli model: it is local, Markovian to good approximation, and — crucially — *structured*. Loss and dephasing do not scramble the oscillator arbitrarily; they nudge it. Bosonic codes are designed around that structure.

### The GKP construction

Gottesman, Kitaev, and Preskill encode a qubit in the simultaneous eigenspace of two commuting displacement stabilizers, S_q = exp(i 2 sqrt(pi) q) and S_p = exp(-i 2 sqrt(pi) p) for the square lattice [1].

### The cat-qubit construction

The two-component cat code takes as its logical basis the even and odd Schrödinger-cat states, proportional to |alpha> + |-alpha> and |alpha> - |-alpha>.

Two confinement mechanisms realize this physics in hardware.

##3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on three levels. *Analytically*, we use the stabilizer and lattice formalism for GKP codes — tracking how Gaussian unitaries conjugate displacement errors — and the Lindblad spectral theory for cat confinement, where the dissipative gap sets the rate of autonomous correction and the strong Z_2 symmetry of the two-photon process protects the logical subspace. *Numerically*, we simulate master equations truncated in the Fock basis and Monte Carlo trajectories of biased Pauli channels to estimate logical error rates under circuit-level noise, following the methodology of the threshold studies we cite [3][6][8]. *Architecturally*, we compare concatenation schemes by their scaling of logical error with code distance and by their circuit volume per logical operation.

To make the bias scaling concrete, the following snippet computes the ideal noise bias of a dissipatively stabilized cat as a function of mean photon number, using the standard asymptotic rates:

```python
import numpy as np

def cat_error_rates(nbar, kappa1=1.0):
    """Asymptotic physical rates for a two-photon stabilized cat qubit."""
    p_bit = np.exp(-2.0 * nbar)      # tunneling: exponentially suppressed
    p_phase = nbar * kappa1          # single-photon loss: linear in size
    return p_bit, p_phase

print(f"{'nbar':>6} {'p_X':>12} {'p_Z':>12} {'bias eta':>12}")
for n in (4, 6, 8, 10, 12):
    px, pz = cat_error_rates(n)
    print(f"{n:>6} {px:>12.2e} {pz:>12.2e} {pz/px:>12.2e}")
```

The output exhibits the defining trade-off of the cat encoding: every added photon multiplies the bias by roughly e^2 while linearly increasing the absolute phase-flip rate.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 GKP codes: lattices, finite squeezing, and Gaussian Cliffords

The square-lattice GKP code is the simplest member of a large family: any symplectic lattice defines a code, with hexagonal lattices offering slightly better packing against isotropic displacement noise [9]. What matters for fault tolerance is not the ideal code but the approximate one. Finite squeezing Delta introduces an intrinsic logical error even without external noise, because neighboring comb teeth overlap; the effective error channel of a finitely squeezed GKP state is well modeled as a Gaussian displacement channel whose variance shrinks with squeezing. Fault-tolerant thresholds are therefore stated in decibels: depending on noise assumptions, the required squeezing lies between about 11.2 dB and 18.6 dB, while experiments have reached roughly 0.62 dB to 12.91 dB across platforms [9]. A complete measurement-based architecture with GKP states on a three-dimensional cluster finds a threshold of 13.2 dB including gate noise from finite squeezing [6].

Two developments have narrowed the gap between theory and device.

### 4.2 Cat qubits: dissipative, Hamiltonian, and combined confinement

Confinement is the cat-qubit analogue of syndrome measurement: it is the mechanism that keeps the oscillator inside the codespace.

Hamiltonian confinement inverts the trade-off.

![Kerr-cat double-well potential and error channels](ths_1789580758887_13281-1.webp)

> Theorem (Confinement dichotomy): For a cat qubit with mean photon number nbar, dissipative confinement corrects arbitrary leakage at a rate set by the dissipative gap kappa_2 while limiting gate speed to the adiabatic regime; Hamiltonian confinement permits gates fast compared with kappa_2^-1 but converts leakage into uncorrectable errors unless an outer code intervenes. The combined scheme achieves gate times below the adiabatic limit with residual leakage suppressed by the dissipative gap.

### 4.3 Bias-preserving gates: never converting the dominant error

A gate is *bias-preserving* if its error channel, like the idle channel, is dominated by phase flips with bit flips exponentially suppressed. This is a nontrivial constraint: in strictly two-level systems a bias-preserving CNOT is impossible, because any entangling operation can convert Z errors into X errors on the target [3]. The oscillator's larger Hilbert space evades the no-go result. Puri and collaborators showed that a CNOT between stabilized cat qubits can be implemented by a conditional rotation of the target cat in phase space, exploiting the topological (geometric) phase accumulated as the cat is carried around the origin; the control cat's state merely selects whether the rotation occurs. Throughout the gate both cats remain in their stabilized manifolds, so the exponential bit-flip suppression survives the operation [3].

![Bias-preserving CNOT between dissipative cat qubits](ths_1789580758887_13281-2.webp)

Subsequent work has attacked the dominant residual error of the adiabatic Zeno-style gates: non-adiabatic transitions during the conditional rotation. A recent proposal implements a bias-preserving cat-cat CNOT entirely through coherent dynamics — a vacuum-conditional beam-splitter mediated by cross-Kerr coupling — with no engineered dissipation active during the gate [8]. The control cat is transiently displaced to vacuum, where it is an eigenstate of the loss operator and hence inert; the target undergoes the conditional flip. Numerical simulation of the resulting repetition-cat memory finds logical error below 10^-6 per syndrome cycle at distance seven (thirteen cats) with T_1 of one millisecond and nbar of ten, reaching roughly 10^-9 at distance eleven [8]. Equally important, the gate shields the data cat from ancilla errors during syndrome extraction, since the data cat's conditional operation requires only a coarse distinction of the ancilla state [8].

### 4.4 Concatenation: bosonic inner codes feeding outer qubit codes

Neither GKP nor cat codes are complete fault-tolerant schemes alone at realistic parameters: finite squeezing leaves residual GKP logical errors, and finite bias leaves residual cat bit flips. The standard remedy is concatenation with the bosonic code as the inner layer. For GKP, the natural outer code is the surface code; the inner code's analog syndrome information (the measured displacement modulo the lattice) can be passed as soft information to the outer decoder, substantially reducing the qubit overhead relative to a bare surface code at the same target logical error rate [9]. The single-mode GKP memory record notwithstanding, current analyses agree that practical quantum memory requires this concatenation, because auxiliary-qubit errors during GKP stabilizer measurement limit the unconcatenated lifetime [9].

![Concatenated bosonic-surface architecture](ths_1789580758887_13281-3.webp)

For cat qubits the outer code is chosen to match the bias. The repetition code corrects the dominant phase flips while the inner stabilization handles bit flips; rectangular surface codes with small X distance and large Z distance exploit the same asymmetry with better threshold scaling [10]. A particularly hardware-efficient direction is the cat-transmon hybrid architecture, in which transmon ancillae perform syndrome extraction on cat data qubits: the different physical implementations for data and ancilla allow stabilizer circuits that avoid Hadamard-like operations on the cats, which would convert Z errors into X errors and destroy the bias [10]. Kerr-cat qubits have additionally been proposed as biased-noise ancillae for error detection on *other* logical qubits, leveraging their noise structure as a resource rather than merely protecting it [4].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

We now state the quantitative backbone of the thesis: what has been proved, what has been simulated at circuit level, and what has been measured.

> Theorem (Bias-tailored repetition memory): Consider n cat qubits with physical phase-flip probability p and bit-flip probability p/eta, eta >> 1, measured with bias-preserving CNOTs whose gate errors respect the same bias. Then there exist constants c_1, c_2 such that the logical error per syndrome cycle of the distance-d repetition code satisfies p_L <= c_1 (c_2 p)^((d+1)/2) + d * p / eta. In particular the bit-flip floor d * p / eta is exponentially small in nbar and the threshold in p is set by the phase-flip channel alone.

*Proof sketch.* (1) Bias-preserving gates guarantee that no operation maps a Z error to an X error, so X and Z error processes decouple. (2) The Z process is a standard repetition code with effective error rate p per location, giving the usual exponential suppression below threshold by a Chernoff/union bound over minimum-weight uncorrectable sets. (3) The X process is uncorrected but each location contributes at most p/eta, and a logical X requires a single physical X on the logical operator's support up to stabilizer equivalence; the union bound over d locations gives the linear floor. (4) Leakage events are either autonomously corrected by the dissipative confinement or, in Hamiltonian confinement, flagged to the outer decoder. The quantitative threshold values quoted below instantiate this structure at circuit level [3][8].

> Lemma (GKP Clifford fault tolerance): A Gaussian unitary implementing a logical GKP Clifford maps any displacement error of magnitude less than half the code distance to a displacement error of bounded magnitude. Hence physical gate imperfections modeled as small displacements cannot cause logical errors below the squeezing threshold.

Decoding the outer repetition code is a classical majority vote over syndrome rounds, which in a functional formulation is:

```haskell
-- Syndrome decoding for a distance-d repetition-cat memory
type Round = [Bool]          -- ancilla outcomes in one syndrome cycle
type History = [Round]

majority :: [Bool] -> Bool
majority xs = length (filter id xs) * 2 > length xs

decode :: History -> [Bool]  -- inferred phase-flip locations
decode hist = map majority (transpose hist)
  where transpose = foldr (zipWith (:)) (repeat [])
```

The simplicity of this decoder is the payoff of the bias: with only one error type to correct, minimum-weight matching on a general graph collapses to a per-site majority vote, and the soft information from the inner bosonic measurements can be incorporated as weighted votes [9][10].

##6 Limitations

The results above should not be read as a claim that fault tolerance is around the corner. The limitations are specific and, in several cases, fundamental:

- **The squeezing gap.** The most optimistic fault-tolerant squeezing thresholds (near 13 dB) sit above the best reported experimental values (up to about 12.91 dB), and the gap widens under realistic ancilla and measurement noise [9]. Every decibel must be earned against photon loss in the state-preparation circuit itself.
- **Ancilla-limited GKP memory.** In superconducting implementations the GKP stabilizers are measured with the help of a transmon ancilla, and ancilla errors feed directly into the bosonic mode. The record quantum-error-correction gain with GKP notwithstanding, the unconcatenated memory lifetime remains limited by the auxiliary qubit rather than by the code [9].
- **Phase-flip growth with cat size.** The exponential bias is not free: the absolute phase-flip rate grows linearly with nbar, so exploiting larger cats demands proportionally better cavity T_1. The distance-eleven, 10^-9 logical-error projections assume millisecond T_1, which is at the edge of current 3D cavity performance [8].
- **Non-adiabatic gate errors.** Dissipative-cat gates pay for their robustness with speed; pushing gate times down reintroduces leakage that the confinement cannot catch. The vacuum-conditional beam-splitter gate removes the Zeno non-adiabatic channel but replaces it with sensitivity to cross-Kerr calibration and thermal population of the mediating mode [8].
- **Leakage and thermal photons.** Residual thermal population, photon gain processes, and Kerr-induced distortion of the cat manifold all produce errors outside the idealized biased-Pauli model. The Kerr-cat experiment identified photon gain as a contributor to bit-flip events beyond the simple tunneling picture [4].
- **Frequency crowding and crosstalk.** Multi-cavity processors must stabilize many cats at distinct frequencies with shared or neighboring parametric pumps; the critical dissipative cat's wide detuning tolerance helps, but a full multiplexed architecture has not been demonstrated [12].
- **Toffoli and magic-state overhead.** The bias-preserving universal set includes a Toffoli construction, but its circuit volume at large bias has not been demonstrated at the fidelity needed for algorithms; GKP magic-state distillation inherits the full cost of the underlying squeezing [3][9].

##7 Conclusion

Bosonic codes have moved, in two decades, from a mathematical curiosity — a qubit hidden in the phase space of an oscillator [1] — to the leading hardware-efficient path toward fault-tolerant superconducting quantum computation. The GKP code contributes a complete, Gaussian-implemented Clifford group whose fault tolerance is native to the displacement error model, with thresholds now quantified near 13 dB of squeezing and gate constructions adapted to real superconducting hardware [6][7]. The cat qubit contributes something rarer: a physical error channel with exponential bias, stabilized autonomously by engineered dissipation or Hamiltonian gaps, and manipulated by gates that provably preserve the bias [2][3][4][5]. Concatenation turns each inner code's residual weakness into the outer code's problem — soft-information surface decoding for GKP, repetition and rectangular-surface decoding for cats — and the quantitative projections, from sub-10^-6 logical error per cycle at distance seven to percent-level circuit thresholds at realistic bias, are the strongest fault-tolerance numbers reported on any superconducting platform at comparable hardware cost [8][3][10].

The road ahead is defined less by new codes than by closing three engineering gaps: pushing squeezing and state-preparation fidelity past the fault-tolerant threshold in the presence of lossy ancillae, extending cavity coherence so that larger cats can be exploited without drowning in phase flips, and demonstrating bias-preserving two-qubit gates at the speed and fidelity their simulations promise. None of these is forbidden by physics. That is precisely why the bosonic program, more than any of its competitors, deserves to be taken as the baseline against which the cost of fault tolerance is measured.
