# Liquid Neural Networks and Neural Ordinary Differential Equations: Continuous-Time Learning Dynamics

## Abstract

Classical deep networks fix their computation into a discrete stack of layers, yet most phenomena they model — neural spikes, climate, traffic, market microstructure — evolve in continuous time. This thesis develops the theory of **neural differential equations**: models that parameterize the *derivative* of a hidden state by a neural network and obtain predictions by numerical integration. We begin with Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (NODEs), which reinterpret residual blocks as Euler steps of an ODE and train through any black-box solver at constant memory cost via the adjoint sensitivity method [1]. We then examine Liquid Time-Constant Networks (LTCs), whose neuronal time constants vary with the input, yielding provably bounded, stable dynamics and superior expressivity per neuron [2][3]. A further refinement, Closed-form Continuous-time networks (CfCs), approximates the liquid integral in closed form, eliminating the solver bottleneck and accelerating training by up to five orders of magnitude [4]. We establish universal approximation guarantees, trajectory-length expressivity measures, and explicit state bounds, and close with the remaining hard problems: stiff dynamics, adjoint instability, topological constraints on flow maps, and the cost of adaptive solvers.

---

## 1. Introduction

Deep learning's dominant architectural idiom is discrete: a fixed integer depth, an integer width, a sequence of matrix multiplications interleaved with nonlinearities. Biological neurons integrate continuously; sensor streams arrive at irregular intervals; control laws must be evaluated at arbitrary temporal resolution. A network that hard-codes its temporal discretization into its architecture is forced to re-learn whenever the sampling rate changes.

The continuous-time program replaces the discrete layer stack with a *vector field*. Instead of declaring $\mathbf{h}_{t+1} = \mathbf{h}_t + f(\mathbf{h}_t, \theta_t)$, we declare the infinitesimal law $d\mathbf{h}(t)/dt = f(\mathbf{h}(t), t, \theta)$ and compute the output by integrating with a numerical ODE solver [1]. Depth becomes a continuous variable controlled by solver tolerance; backpropagation memory becomes constant; and irregularly sampled data — the norm in medicine, robotics, and climate science — is handled natively rather than by imputation.

This thesis synthesizes three strands. The first is the **Neural ODE** of Chen et al., which established the adjoint method as the scalable training route and introduced continuous normalizing flows [1]. The second is the **Liquid Time-Constant Network** of Hasani, Lechner, Amini, Rus, and Grosu, which enriches the ODE with input-dependent time constants and proves stability and expressivity bounds within the NODE family [2][3]. The third is the **Closed-form Continuous-time network**, which derives a tightly bounded closed-form approximation to the liquid integral, cutting the solver out of the loop [4].

Our contribution is expository but rigorous: we unify these developments in a single mathematical framework, state the core guarantees precisely, and evaluate the empirical record with attention to where continuous-time models genuinely outperform discrete baselines and where they do not.

> **Thesis claim:** Continuous-time neural models are not merely a reparameterization of depth. The varying time constants of liquid networks and the solver-free closed form constitute a genuinely more expressive and efficient function class for sequential decision-making, albeit one whose training dynamics and topological constraints demand new theory.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Residual Networks to Continuous Depth

He et al.'s residual networks parameterize the *residual* $f(\mathbf{h}_t, \theta_t)$ rather than the full map — an explicit Euler discretization of $d\mathbf{h}/dt = f(\mathbf{h}, t, \theta)$ with unit step size (Lu et al.; Haber–Ruthotto). Sharing parameters across steps and letting step size go to zero yields a model in which "depth" is the integration horizon, a real number.

This continuous-depth view has three immediate consequences [1]:

1. **Adaptive computation.** An adaptive solver (e.g., Dormand–Prince 5(4)) allocates function evaluations where the trajectory varies rapidly and coasts where it is smooth — per input, at test time.
2. **Parameter efficiency.** A single vector field $f(\cdot, \cdot, \theta)$ replaces $T$ layer-specific parameter sets.
3. **New model classes.** Latent-variable time-series models and generative flows become natural: any ODE defines a diffeomorphism.

### 2.2 Continuous-Time Recurrent Networks and Universal Approximation

The ODE perspective on recurrence predates modern deep learning. A continuous-time recurrent neural network (CTRNN) is typically written

$$\tau_i \frac{dx_i}{dt} = -x_i + \sigma\!\left(\sum_j w_{ij} x_j + \sum_k v_{ik} I_k + b_i\right),$$

or equivalently $dx_i/dt = -x_i/\tau_i + f_i(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{I}, \theta)$ [2]. Funahashi and Nakamura (1993) proved the foundational universal approximation result: for any $C^1$ vector field $F$ on a compact set and any finite time horizon, a CTRNN exists whose trajectories approximate those of $d\mathbf{x}/dt = F(\mathbf{x})$ uniformly to arbitrary precision. Constructive procedures based on this theorem train a feedforward network on the vector field and recast it as a CTRNN, with explicit trajectory-error bounds in terms of the feedforward training error [7].

### 2.3 The Adjoint Sensitivity Method

Training a neural ODE by unrolling the solver's internal steps stores one activation per function evaluation — prohibitive for adaptive solvers. The remedy comes from optimal control: the **adjoint sensitivity method** (Pontryagin's maximum principle) computes gradients by solving a *second* ODE backward in time [1].

Define the adjoint state $\mathbf{a}(t) = \partial L / \partial \mathbf{h}(t)$ for a scalar loss $L$ depending on $\mathbf{h}(t_1)$. Then

$$\frac{d\mathbf{a}(t)}{dt} = -\mathbf{a}(t)^\top \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{h}(t), t, \theta), \qquad \frac{dL}{d\theta} = -\int_{t_1}^{t_0} \mathbf{a}(t)^\top \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}\,dt.$$

> **Theorem:** *The gradient of a loss over an ODE solution can be obtained by a single backward integration of an augmented ODE of dimension $D + P + 1$ (state, adjoint, parameter gradient), with memory cost independent of the number of forward solver steps* [1].

The forward trajectory $\mathbf{h}(t)$ is reconstructed backward from $\mathbf{h}(t_1)$, so no activations are stored. This constant-memory property made large neural ODEs trainable and was later ported to neural SDEs, controlled differential equations, and universal differential equations that couple learned correction terms to known physics [8].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is threefold: (i) formalize the liquid time-constant ODE and derive its closed-form approximation; (ii) implement reference models in PyTorch/`torchdiffeq` with strict numerical hygiene; (iii) evaluate on irregularly sampled sequence benchmarks against LSTM, GRU, and vanilla NODE baselines under matched parameter budgets.

Two properties follow immediately and are proved in [2]:

- **Boundedness.** For bounded inputs and standard activation choices, the hidden states remain within a fixed compact set for all time; the dynamics cannot blow up.
- **Stability.** The autonomous part is contractive: perturbations decay rather than amplify, suppressing the exploding-gradient pathology of discrete RNNs on long sequences.

### 3.2 Trajectory-Length Expressivity

Hasani et al. quantify expressivity through the *trajectory length* of the hidden dynamics: how long a path the latent state traces in response to input variation. LTCs achieve strictly greater trajectory length per parameter than vanilla NODEs, because the liquid time constant lets different neurons operate at different effective rates *simultaneously* — a temporal multi-resolution that fixed-$\tau$ networks can only approximate with many more units [2][3].

### 3.3 Closed-Form Approximation

The practical bottleneck of LTCs is the numerical solver: every forward pass integrates a nonlinear ODE, and training cost scales with solver steps. Hasani et al. [4] show that the solution of the liquid ODE can be approximated in closed form. For a piecewise-constant input over $[0, t]$, the exact solution of the linear-in-$x$ structure is

$$x(t) = x(0)\, e^{-t\left(\frac{1}{\tau} + f\right)} + A\left(1 - e^{-t\left(\frac{1}{\tau} + f\right)}\right),$$

an exponential relaxation toward $A$ with rate $1/\tau + f$. The key contribution is a **tightly bounded approximation of the integral** appearing when $f$ varies with $x$, which had no known closed-form solution. Because time appears *explicitly*, no solver is needed: the resulting **Closed-form Continuous-time (CfC)** network is a feedforward computation with recurrent structure "baked in," running one to five orders of magnitude faster than ODE-based counterparts while retaining the liquid inductive bias [4].

Embedded reimplementations (LTC-SE) have since unified the LTC cell with spiking neurons and CT-GRU variants in a single TensorFlow/Keras library [5].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Liquid Neurons as Adaptive Filters

A single liquid neuron with scalar input $I(t)$ is a leaky integrator whose leak is *gated*:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = -\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{\tau} + \sigma(w x + v I + b)\right)}_{\text{liquid rate } \lambda(x, I)} x + \sigma(w x + v I + b)\, A.$$

When $\sigma(\cdot) \approx 0$, $\lambda \approx 1/\tau$ and the neuron integrates slowly — it *remembers*. When $\sigma(\cdot)$ is large, the neuron rapidly relaxes toward $A$ — it *forgets and tracks*. The network thus implements a continuum of effective memory horizons, allocated dynamically: LSTM-like gating with one nonlinearity evaluation per unit per step instead of four [2].

A minimal PyTorch sketch of the LTC cell:

```python
class LTCCell(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, input_size, hidden_size, tau=1.0):
        super().__init__()
        self.f = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(input_size + hidden_size, hidden_size),
                               nn.Tanh())
        self.tau = tau
        self.A = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(hidden_size))

    def forward(self, x, I):
        # liquid rate: 1/tau + f(x, I)
        gate = self.f(torch.cat([x, I], dim=-1))
        dxdt = -(1.0 / self.tau + gate) * x + gate * self.A
        return dxdt
```

### 4.2 Adjoint Training: Mechanics and Pitfalls

The adjoint method's constant memory comes at a price: the backward pass re-integrates the forward trajectory in reverse, so forward numerical error contaminates $\mathbf{a}(t)$. In practice:

- **Tolerance coupling.** The adjoint ODE is often stiffer than the forward ODE; practitioners set backward tolerances an order of magnitude tighter than forward ones.
- **Checkpointing hybrids.** Interpolated checkpointing (store a coarse forward trajectory, refine locally) trades a small memory increase for substantially more reliable gradients.

A Haskell-flavored specification of the adjoint loop clarifies the data flow:

```haskell
-- augmented state: (h, a, dL/dθ)
adjointStep :: VectorField -> Time -> AugState -> AugState
adjointStep f t (h, a, g) =
  let dhdt = f h t theta
      dadt = negate (a `dot` jacobian f h)   -- da/dt = -aᵀ ∂f/∂h
      dgdt = negate (a `dot` jacobianTheta f h)
  in (dhdt, dadt, dgdt)
-- integrate backward from t1 to t0, then read off dL/dθ
```

### 4.3 Expressivity: What Continuous Time Buys

Two results delimit what liquid and NODE models can and cannot represent:

1. **Universal approximation of dynamics.** LTC networks approximate any finite trajectory of an $n$-dimensional continuous dynamical system with hidden units plus $n$ output units [3], sharpening Funahashi–Nakamura by making the time constant itself part of the approximation machinery.
2. **Trajectory-length dominance.** Within the NODE family, LTCs maximize the latent trajectory length per parameter [2].

| Property | Vanilla NODE [1] | LTC [2][3] | CfC [4] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solver required | Yes (adaptive) | Yes (adaptive) | **No** (closed form) |
| Time constant | N/A (depth = time) | Input-dependent (liquid) | Input-dependent (liquid) |
| Memory (backward) | $O(1)$ via adjoint | $O(1)$ via adjoint | $O(1)$ feedforward |
| Speed vs. ODE baseline | 1× | ~1× | 10×–10⁵× |
| Trajectory-length expressivity | Baseline | Strictly greater | Matches LTC |
| Topological constraints | Homeomorphism | Homeomorphism | Homeomorphism |

### 4.4 Continuous Normalizing Flows and FFJORD

The vector field also defines a **continuous normalizing flow**: pushing a base density through the ODE yields a model density whose log-likelihood evolves by the *instantaneous change of variables* [1]:

$$\frac{d \log p(\mathbf{z}(t))}{dt} = -\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{z}}\right).$$

The trace costs $O(D^2)$ naively. FFJORD replaces it with Hutchinson's unbiased estimator, $\operatorname{tr}(A) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}}[\mathbf{v}^\top A \mathbf{v}]$, evaluated by a single vector–Jacobian product — $O(D)$ per step — making continuous flows competitive with coupling-based flows (Real NVP, Glow) while permitting completely unrestricted architectures [6].

A Rust-flavored sketch of the Hutchinson estimator core:

```rust
/// Unbiased stochastic trace estimate: tr(J) ≈ vᵀ (J v)
fn hutchinson_trace(jvp: impl Fn(&[f64]) -> Vec<f64>, dim: usize) -> f64 {
    let v: Vec<f64> = (0..dim).map(|_| rademacher()).collect();
    let jv = jvp(&v);
    v.iter().zip(jv.iter()).map(|(a, b)| a * b).sum::<f64>()
}
```

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Irregular time-series classification.** Latent ODE models on human-activity data handle arbitrary observation grids without imputation, matching RNN baselines while adjoint training keeps memory flat as sequence length grows [1].

**Sequential benchmarks with tiny networks.** LTCs with as few as 19 liquid neurons solve lane-keeping tasks requiring hundreds of LSTM/GRU units; liquid time constants visibly specialize across temporal scales [2].

**Solver-free scaling.** CfCs run one to five orders of magnitude faster than ODE-based counterparts and *improve* on long-horizon prediction by eliminating solver error accumulation, with best or tied-best accuracy against recurrent baselines at fewer parameters [4][5].

**Density estimation.** FFJORD attains state-of-the-art likelihood among exact-likelihood generative models at publication, sampling in a single ODE solve rather than hundreds of autoregressive steps [6].

**Proof sketch — boundedness of LTC states.** With $0 \le \sigma(\cdot) \le 1$, the dynamics $dx_i/dt = -\lambda_i x_i + f_i A_i$ with $\lambda_i \ge 1/\tau_i > 0$ imply $|x_i(t)| \le \max(|x_i(0)|, \tau_i \max |f_i A_i|)$ by comparison: whenever $|x_i|$ exceeds the equilibrium magnitude, the derivative points inward. Trajectories enter and remain in a compact set — no finite-time blowup, and gradients through long horizons stay controlled [2].

**Proof sketch — trajectory-length advantage.** The liquid rate $\lambda(x, I) = 1/\tau + f(x, I)$ spans a continuum of timescales within a single neuron. The latent path length $\int \|\dot{\mathbf{x}}\|\,dt$ is maximized when neurons desynchronize across scales; fixed-$\tau$ networks must allocate distinct neurons per scale and need proportionally more parameters for the same length [3].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Stiffness and solver cost.** Learned vector fields routinely become stiff; adaptive explicit solvers then take thousands of micro-steps per forward pass, erasing the theoretical efficiency. CfCs sidestep this [4], but vanilla NODEs and LTCs remain vulnerable.
2. **Adjoint instability.** Reverse-time integration amplifies forward numerical error, and the adjoint ODE itself can be unstable for chaotic learned dynamics. Checkpointed adjoints help but sacrifice the pure constant-memory guarantee [1].
3. **Topological constraints.** Flows are homeomorphisms; unaugmented NODEs and liquid networks cannot learn maps that change topology [1].
4. **Hyperparameter sensitivity.** Solver tolerances, time horizons, and initialization of $\tau$ interact strongly; small changes can flip a model from smooth integration to solver failure. Closed-form models are markedly more robust [4].
5. **Theory gaps.** Universal approximation theorems are existence results with no sample-complexity bounds; trajectory length is a proxy, not a generalization guarantee; and the interaction between liquid time constants and *stochastic* dynamics (neural SDEs, diffusion models) remains poorly understood.
6. **Evaluation skew.** Reported speedups often compare optimized closed-form code against unoptimized solver baselines; hardware-matched wall-clock comparisons remain rare.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Neural differential equations reframe learning as the identification of a vector field rather than the fitting of a layer stack. The arc from NODEs [1] through liquid time-constant networks [2][3] to closed-form models [4] is an arc of *removing bottlenecks*: the memory bottleneck via the adjoint method, the expressivity-per-parameter bottleneck via liquid time constants, the solver bottleneck via the closed-form integral. Each step rests on approximation theory — Funahashi–Nakamura universality, trajectory-length expressivity, tight integral bounds — and is validated on irregular time series, generative modeling [6], and embedded control [5].

The research frontier is clear: training algorithms provably stable for stiff learned dynamics; a generalization theory for continuous-time models beyond trajectory length; hybrids of liquid ODEs with attention and state-space models at scale; and universal differential equations [8] that fuse the liquid inductive bias with hard physical constraints, so the learned vector field is not merely predictive but scientifically legible. The differential equation, after three centuries as the language of physics, is becoming the language of learning too.
