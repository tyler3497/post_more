# Verified Microkernels and Memory-Safe OS Kernels: The seL4 Functional Correctness Proof and the Rust-for-Linux Programme

## Abstract

Two radically different strategies now contend for the future of trustworthy operating system kernels. The first, exemplified by **seL4**, pursues *proof*: a machine-checked functional-correctness theorem in Isabelle/HOL establishing that the C implementation of a complete general-purpose microkernel refines a high-level abstract specification, with roughly 500,000 lines of proof covering some 8,700 lines of C [1][2]. The second, exemplified by **Rust-for-Linux**, pursues *language-based safety*: incrementally replacing memory-unsafe C with Rust, whose ownership discipline eliminates entire classes of temporal and spatial memory errors by construction, with initial Rust support merged into Linux 6.1 and the first Rust drivers shipping in 6.8 [5][7]. This thesis dissects both approaches — the refinement calculus, capability model, and proof architecture of seL4; the safe-abstraction methodology, bindgen-generated bindings, and invariant discipline of Rust-for-Linux — and evaluates them on a common axis: assurance strength, trusted computing base, development cost, and deployability. We argue the approaches are complementary rather than rival: proofs buy deep assurance of a small core; languages buy shallow assurance at monolithic scale.

## 1 Introduction

The operating system kernel is the most privileged and least forgiving software artifact in a computing system. A single defect — a buffer overflow, a use-after-free, a confused-deputy capability bug — can collapse the isolation boundary on which every higher-level security property depends. Empirical studies of large C kernels have consistently shown that memory-safety violations dominate exploitable kernel vulnerabilities; Google's Android programme, for example, reported that memory-safety bugs fell from 76% to 35% of total vulnerabilities between 2019 and 2022 as new memory-safe code displaced C [6]. Two engineering philosophies have emerged in response.

The *verification* philosophy holds that assurance must be *demonstrated*, not hoped for. Its flagship is seL4, a third-generation L4 microkernel whose C implementation was proven functionally correct against an abstract specification in 2009 — the first such result for a general-purpose OS kernel, later inducted into the ACM SIGOPS Hall of Fame [1][4]. The proof is a classical refinement: every behaviour of the C code is already a behaviour of the abstract model, established through an intermediate executable specification in Haskell and formalised in Isabelle/HOL with forward-simulation arguments [2][3].

The *language-safety* philosophy holds that assurance must be *affordable at scale*. Rewriting or re-verifying a 30-million-line monolithic kernel is infeasible, so instead one changes the economics of defect introduction: make whole bug classes unrepresentable. Rust's affine type system, borrow checker, and fearless-concurrency discipline deliver exactly this, and the Rust-for-Linux project — launched in 2020 and merged into mainline with Linux 6.1 in December 2022 — is the vehicle bringing it to the world's most deployed kernel [5][6]. Its central doctrine is that Rust modules *never touch C APIs directly*; all interaction passes through carefully reviewed **safe abstractions** whose `unsafe` interiors are small, documented, and audited [7].

This thesis compares the two programmes rigorously: their threat models, their mechanisms, their costs, and their limits.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The kernel assurance problem

A kernel mediates *all* interactions between mutually distrusting principals — processes, drivers, devices — on shared hardware. Its correctness requirements are unusually strong: not merely absence of crashes, but preservation of *confidentiality* (no illicit information flow), *integrity* (no unauthorised state mutation), and *availability* (no denial of service to legitimate resource use) across adversarial inputs [4]. Traditional quality assurance — testing, fuzzing, code review — samples behaviour; for kernels, the residual risk of unsampled behaviour is precisely the attack surface.

### 2.2 Two answers

*Formal verification* answers with mathematical proof: model the system, state the property, and machine-check every logical step. Its unit of currency is the *line of proof* (lop), and its price is legendary — the seL4 proof required roughly 50 lines of proof per line of C [4].

*Language-based safety* answers with type discipline: make the compiler reject the programme before it can misbehave. Rust eliminates data races, use-after-free, double-free, and null dereference *by construction* for safe code; residual risk concentrates in explicitly marked `unsafe` blocks, which are small and reviewable [5][7].

| Dimension | seL4 (proof) | Rust-for-Linux (language) |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of assurance | Machine-checked theorem | Compiler-checked type discipline |
| Scope | ~8.7 kLOC microkernel core | Incremental, whole-kernel ambition |
| Cost metric | ~50 lop per line of C | Near-parity dev cost after abstractions |
| TCB | Kernel + compiler + HW + boot | Kernel + compiler + unsafe interiors |
| Deployability | Niche, high-assurance domains | Mainline Linux, billions of devices |

## 3 Methodology

Our comparison proceeds along four axes, each grounded in primary sources — the SOSP 2009 seL4 paper and its companion lecture material [1][2][3], and the Rust-for-Linux RFC, project history, and mainline merge record [5][7][8]:

1. **Mechanism**: what exactly is proven, or ruled out, and how.
2. **Architecture**: how each system structures authority (capabilities) and unsafety (abstraction boundaries).
3. **Evidence**: quantitative results — proof sizes, bug counts, vulnerability trends.
4. **Limits**: what each approach explicitly does *not* cover, stated by its own authors.

We treat both programmes on their own terms: seL4's claims are evaluated against the refinement statement and its documented assumptions [3]; Rust-for-Linux is evaluated against the safe-abstraction doctrine articulated in its RFC [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The seL4 refinement tower

The seL4 proof is organised as a **three-level refinement**, connected by forward-simulation theorems [2]:

```
Abstract Specification      (Isabelle/HOL, ~4.9 kLOC, 117,000 lop)
        ⇕  refines  (forward simulation)
Executable Specification    (Haskell-style model, ~13 kLOC Isabelle, 50,000 lop)
        ⇕  refines  (via C parser → Simpl → AutoCorres)
C Implementation            (~8.7 kLOC C, ARM)
```

Refinement here has a precise meaning: *all possible behaviours of the lower level are contained in the behaviours of the upper level* [3]. The abstract specification is a total function over an abstract state — deliberately nondeterministic where the implementation is free to choose (e.g., scheduling decisions). The executable specification is a deterministic functional prototype, originally written in Haskell and transliterated into Isabelle/HOL, that mirrors the C code's control flow closely enough that the C→Isabelle translation (via the C parser, Simpl, and AutoCorres) can be related to it statement by statement [2].

> **Theorem:** (Functional correctness) Every execution of the seL4 C implementation corresponds to an execution of the abstract specification producing identical observable outputs; the kernel never crashes, never performs undefined operations, and never violates an assertion, under the assumptions of compiler, linker, assembly, hardware, and boot-code correctness [1][3].

The power of this formulation is *property transfer*: any property proved of the abstract model and preserved by refinement holds of the C code. Integrity, authority confinement, and (later) information-flow noninterference were all proved at the abstract level and inherited by the implementation [4].

### 4.2 Capabilities, untyped memory, and the derivation tree

seL4 is a **capability system**: every kernel object — thread control blocks, page tables, endpoints, notification objects — can only be named and manipulated through an unforgeable capability held in a capability space (CNode) [1][4]. Authority in the system is exactly the set of capabilities held; there is no ambient authority, which makes the access-control policy *decidable* and, crucially, provable [2].

Memory management is explicit and proof-friendly. All free memory begins as **Untyped** capabilities; a thread with an Untyped capability may *retype* it into concrete kernel objects, and the kernel tracks every derivation in the **capability derivation tree (CDT)**. Retype is only permitted when it cannot violate type safety (e.g., an Untyped region cannot be retyped while live references to objects carved from it exist), and the CDT invariants — no dangling capabilities, no overlapping live objects — are proved preserved by every kernel operation [1][2].

```c
/* seL4 retype, simplified: authority flows only through capabilities */
seL4_Error seL4_Untyped_Retype(
    seL4_Untyped service,      /* capability to untyped memory */
    seL4_Word   type,          /* object type to create */
    seL4_Word   size_bits,     /* object size */
    seL4_CNode  root,          /* destination CNode */
    seL4_Word   node_index,    /* slot in destination */
    seL4_Word   node_depth,
    seL4_Word   node_offset,
    seL4_Word   num_objects);
```

The proof found **460 bugs** during verification — 160 in the C implementation, 150 in the design, and 150 in the specification — against only 16 found by prior shallow testing, a striking demonstration that proof effort discovers defects testing misses [2]. At roughly **$400 per line of code**, the verified kernel was reportedly cheaper than comparable high-assurance (but unverified) kernels at $1,000/LoC [4].

### 4.3 Rust-for-Linux: safe abstractions over unsafe bindings

Rust-for-Linux inverts the seL4 strategy: rather than proving a small kernel correct, it makes a huge kernel *safer to extend*. The project's load-bearing architectural rule, stated in the original RFC, is [7]:

> *Modules written in Rust should never use the C kernel APIs directly. The whole point of using Rust in the kernel is that we develop safe abstractions so that modules are easier to reason about and, therefore, to review, refactor, etc.*

Concretely, the stack has three strata. At the bottom, `bindgen` generates **raw bindings** to C kernel APIs on the fly — these are `unsafe` by construction and never used directly [7]. In the middle sit the **safe abstractions**: hand-written Rust types that encapsulate kernel concepts (e.g., `Device`, `PlatformDriver`, `File`, `Mutex`) behind interfaces whose type signatures *enforce* the kernel's undocumented invariants — reference-counting discipline, locking rules, lifetime constraints — using Rust's ownership and borrow checking. At the top, **driver authors** write ordinary safe Rust against these abstractions.

```rust
// RFL idiom: ownership of a device resource, enforced by the type system
struct MyDriver {
    regs: IoMem<Registers>,   // MMIO region: owned, exclusively borrowed
    irq: IrqRequest,           // interrupt line: moved, not copied
}

impl platform::Driver for MyDriver {
    type IdInfo = ();
    const OF_ID_TABLE: Option<of::IdTable<Self::IdInfo>> = Some(&OF_TABLE);

    fn probe(dev: &device::Device<device::Bound>, _info: Option<&Self::IdInfo>)
        -> Result<Pin<KBox<Self>>>
    {
        // `?` propagates errors; no unchecked NULLs, no manual refcounts
        let regs = dev.iomap_resource(0)?;
        let irq = dev.irq_request(0, c_str!("mydrv"), IrqFlags::SHARED)?;
        Ok(KBox::new(Self { regs, irq }, GFP_KERNEL)?.into())
    }
}
```

Ownership here is doing the work that seL4's CDT does by proof: the `IoMem` handle *cannot* be aliased or leaked without the compiler objecting, and the `IrqRequest` is freed exactly once when dropped — the temporal-safety bugs that dominate C driver CVEs become compile errors. Google's Android data supplies the empirical warrant: as new memory-safe code displaced C, memory-safety vulnerabilities fell from 76% (2019) to 35% (2022) of the total, the first year they were not the majority [6].

The rollout has been deliberately incremental: project launch 2020–2021 under Miguel Ojeda, initial infrastructure merged in **Linux 6.1 (December 2022)**, continuous abstraction growth through 6.2–6.7, the first Rust drivers accepted in **December 2023 and shipped in 6.8**, and a December 2025 announcement that Rust is **no longer experimental** but an official part of kernel development [5][8].

### 4.4 Kernel UB and the residual safety gap

Both programmes must confront *undefined behaviour* (UB) — and neither eliminates it entirely. seL4's theorem is conditional on the C compiler's correctness and on hardware behaving as modelled; the proof explicitly excludes kernel initialisation (at the time of the original proof), models the MMU and caches abstractly, does not cover multicore execution, and does not preclude covert timing channels [2]. Translation validation of the compiler itself arrived later (PLDI 2013), and worst-case execution time analysis separately (RTSS 2011, RTAS 2016) [2].

Rust-for-Linux's gap is the mirror image: safe Rust is sound, but every abstraction bottoms out in `unsafe` code that must manually uphold the invariants the safe interface promises — *safety preconditions, `unsafe` blocks, and type invariants must all be documented* as a matter of project policy [7]. A soundness bug in one widely used abstraction (say, the `File` or `Mutex` wrapper) would reintroduce memory unsafety into every driver built on it. The project manages this by keeping the unsafe interiors small, heavily reviewed, and — critically — *shared*, so audit effort amortises across all drivers.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

| Result | seL4 | Rust-for-Linux |
|---|---|---|
| Assurance artifact | Machine-checked refinement theorem (Isabelle/HOL) | Compiler-checked safe code + audited `unsafe` interiors |
| Proof/audit size | ~500 kLoP total (117k + 50k + descendants) [2][4] | Abstractions reviewed line-by-line; unsafe surface minimised [7] |
| Defects found by method | 460 (160 C, 150 design, 150 spec) [2] | N/A (defects *prevented* at compile time) |
| Memory-safety CVEs | 0 by construction of theorem (under assumptions) | 76% → 35% of Android vulns, 2019–2022 [6] |
| Cost | ~$400/LoC; ~50 lop per line of C [4] | Near C-parity once abstractions exist |
| Scale deployed | Niche high-assurance systems | Mainline Linux; billions of devices [5] |

The deepest lesson is about *where* each method pays off. Verification found bugs testing could not — including 150 specification bugs, i.e., cases where the *intended* behaviour was wrong, which no type system can catch [2]. Language safety, conversely, scales: it improves every new driver written, without a per-line proof tax, and its benefits compound as the safe-abstraction library grows [8].

## 6 Limitations

**seL4.** The proof's assumptions are its boundary: compiler, linker, assembly, hardware, and boot code are trusted; the MMU is modelled abstractly; multicore and timing channels were out of scope of the original proof [2][3]. More fundamentally, the ~50:1 proof-to-code ratio makes evolution expensive — changing the kernel means changing the proof, which "drastically increases the cost of development and slows development velocity" [4]. Functional correctness is also not the whole of security: it took *additional* proofs (integrity, confidentiality, noninterference) to cash out the security claims [4].

**Rust-for-Linux.** Safe abstractions are only as sound as their `unsafe` interiors and their documentation of safety preconditions [7]. The kernel's C core — scheduler, VM, VFS — remains C for the foreseeable future ("Rust will not be replacing C in the kernel anytime this decade" [6]), so language safety covers the *margins* (drivers, new subsystems) rather than the core. Concurrency bugs that are *logically* wrong but *memory*-safe (deadlocks, lost wakeups) are outside Rust's guarantees, as are specification bugs of the kind seL4's proof caught.

## 7 Conclusion

seL4 and Rust-for-Linux represent the two poles of credible kernel assurance: **prove a little, deeply** versus **harden a lot, cheaply**. The seL4 programme demonstrates that full functional correctness of a real kernel is achievable — with a machine-checked theorem, a capability architecture that makes authority decidable, and a bug yield that vindicates the effort [1][2][4]. The Rust-for-Linux programme demonstrates that memory safety can be retrofitted onto the world's largest kernel incrementally, through a disciplined architecture of safe abstractions over generated bindings, with measurable reductions in vulnerability rates [5][6][7].

They are complements, not competitors. The verified microkernel is the right answer where the TCB must be minimal and the assurance case absolute — separation kernels, cryptographic modules, safety-critical controllers. Language-based safety is the right answer where the codebase is irreplaceably large and the threat is the steady drumbeat of memory-corruption CVEs. The most promising frontier is their intersection: Rust-style ownership disciplines informing the design of verified systems, and verification techniques applied to the small, shared `unsafe` interiors on which Rust-for-Linux's safety case rests. Assurance, in the end, is not one property but a portfolio — and a mature kernel ecosystem will hold both assets.
