## Abstract

Compressed sensing asserts that a signal sparse in some known basis can be recovered exactly from a number of linear measurements far below the Nyquist–Shannon rate, by solving a tractable convex program. This thesis develops the mathematical core of the theory: the geometry of L1 minimization, the restricted isometry property (RIP) as a sufficient condition for uniform recovery, the uniform uncertainty principle for partial Fourier ensembles, and the sharp Donoho–Tanner phase transitions that separate success from failure in the large-system limit. We present the canonical problem formulations — basis pursuit, quadratically constrained basis pursuit, and the LASSO — and prove the dual-certificate route to exact recovery. We analyze measurement ensembles including sub-Gaussian matrices and randomly subsampled Fourier frames, quantify the optimal measurement rate of order s log(N/s), and survey algorithmic families from linear programming to greedy pursuit and approximate message passing. Numerical phase-transition experiments and a structured proof sketch complement the theory. Applications to magnetic resonance imaging, the single-pixel camera, and sparse channel estimation illustrate the practical reach of the framework [1][4][7].


















##1 Intro

For most of the twentieth century, the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem set the terms of the debate: a bandlimited signal must be sampled at twice its highest frequency, and any attempt to sample below that rate was understood to destroy information irretrievably. Aliasing was not a nuisance to be managed but a mathematical verdict. The architecture of every analog-to-digital converter, every MRI scanner, and every digital camera was built around this verdict: sample densely first, compress later.

*Compressed sensing inverts this pipeline.* It begins from the observation that most signals of interest are not arbitrary points in high-dimensional space. Natural images are sparse in wavelet bases; audio is sparse in local Fourier frames; genomic data is sparse in the standard basis after appropriate transformation. A signal that is *s-sparse* — possessing at most s nonzero coefficients in a known dictionary — is determined by only 2s parameters (locations and amplitudes), yet classical sampling acquires all N ambient coordinates. The central question of compressed sensing is therefore disarmingly simple: if the information content of a signal is only logarithmic in the ambient dimension, why must the measurement process scale with the ambient dimension?

Formally, we observe y = Ax, where A is an m by N measurement matrix with m far smaller than N, and x is an unknown s-sparse vector. The linear system is massively underdetermined, and infinitely many vectors explain the data. Yet under precise geometric conditions on A, the sparsest explanation is unique — and, remarkably, it can be found by *convex optimization*. The combinatorial problem of minimizing the number of nonzeros is NP-hard in general; its convex relaxation, minimizing the L1 norm, is a linear program. The founding miracle of the field, established by Candès, Romberg, Tao, and Donoho in 2004–2006, is that this relaxation is *exact*: the convex surrogate recovers the true sparse signal whenever the measurement matrix satisfies a restricted isometry property [1][2][4].

This thesis develops the theory in full: Section 2 covers sparsity models and the hierarchy of recovery conditions; Section 3 presents the RIP methodology, measurement ensembles, and canonical convex programs; Section 4 deep-dives into polytope geometry, concentration of measure, phase transitions, and non-convex algorithms; Section 5 gives empirical evidence and a proof sketch; Section 6 confronts the limitations.

> **Thesis claim:** Under the restricted isometry property of order 2s, L1 minimization recovers every s-sparse vector exactly from m = O(s log(N/s)) randomized linear measurements, and this rate is information-theoretically optimal up to constants.


















##2 Background

### Sparsity and the failure of least squares

A vector x in R^N is *s-sparse* if at most s of its coordinates are nonzero; we write its support as the index set of nonzero entries.

y = Ax + e

where A is m by N with m << N, and e is noise.

(P0) minimize the number of nonzeros of z subject to Az = y.

Problem (P0) is the conceptual gold standard but is NP-hard — it requires searching over all subsets of coordinates.

### The L1 relaxation and basis pursuit

The L1 norm, the sum of absolute values, is the convex envelope of the sparsity count on the unit ball.

(P1) minimize ||z||_1 subject to Az = y.

This is a linear program, solvable in polynomial time.

### A hierarchy of sufficient conditions

Three properties of A govern the answer, in increasing strength and decreasing verifiability:

- **Coherence.** The mutual coherence μ(A) is the largest absolute inner product between distinct normalized columns. If s < (1 + 1/μ)/2, then every s-sparse vector is the unique L1 minimizer. Coherence is computable but pessimistic: for an m by N Gaussian matrix, μ scales like sqrt((log N)/m), so coherence-based guarantees require m on the order of s^2 log N — quadratically worse than optimal.
- **Null-space property (NSP).** A satisfies the NSP of order s if every nonzero vector v in the kernel of A has its L1 mass spread out: the L1 norm of v on any s coordinates is strictly less than half of ||v||_1. NSP of order s is *necessary and sufficient* for exact recovery of all s-sparse vectors by (P1). It is sharp but abstract — it quantifies over the entire null space.
- **Restricted isometry property (RIP).** A satisfies RIP of order s with constant δ_s if (1 − δ_s)||x||_2^2 ≤ ||Ax||_2^2 ≤ (1 + δ_s)||x||_2^2 for every s-sparse x. RIP demands that A act as a near-isometry on all sparse vectors simultaneously. It implies NSP and is implied by nothing weaker that is checkable. Crucially, *random matrices satisfy RIP with overwhelming probability* — which converts an intractable deterministic certification problem into a tractable probabilistic construction [1][3].

### The uniform uncertainty principle

For structured measurements — randomly subsampled Fourier coefficients — Candès, Romberg, and Tao introduced the *uniform uncertainty principle* (UUP): a partial Fourier matrix obeys UUP if its restriction to any small coordinate set is nearly orthonormal [3].

##3 Methodology

### The RIP and what it buys

> **Theorem:** Let A satisfy the restricted isometry property of order 2s with δ_2s < sqrt(2) − 1.

The constants C_0 and C_1 depend only on δ_2s.

### Measurement ensembles

The methodology is constructive because randomness manufactures RIP:

1. **Sub-Gaussian matrices.** Matrices with independent sub-Gaussian entries (Gaussian, Bernoulli, or any bounded independent ensemble), normalized by 1/sqrt(m), satisfy δ_s ≤ δ with probability at least 1 − exp(−c m) whenever m ≥ C δ^{−2} s log(N/s). This is the workhorse result: optimal order, universal over all sparse signals, proved by covering-number and concentration arguments [1].
2. **Partial Fourier and bounded orthonormal systems.** Selecting m rows uniformly at random from the N by N discrete Fourier matrix yields RIP of order s when m ≥ C s log^4(N). The extra logarithmic factors are the price of structure; they can be reduced with refined chaining arguments.
3. **Deterministic constructions.** Explicit matrices from finite geometry, chirps, and expander graphs achieve RIP-like guarantees but with worse parameter tradeoffs (typically m on the order of s^2 up to polylogarithmic factors). Deterministic RIP at the optimal rate remains open.

A simple proof that random matrices satisfy RIP for *fixed* support uses the Johnson–Lindenstrauss concentration: for a fixed s-dimensional subspace, a Gaussian matrix preserves all lengths to within (1 ± δ) with probability 1 − 2exp(−c m δ^2).

### The three canonical programs

| Program | Formulation | Setting |
|---|---|---|
| Basis pursuit (P1) | min ||z||_1 s.t. Az = y | Noiseless, exact sparsity |
| QCBP / BPDN | min ||z||_1 s.t. ||Az − y||_2 ≤ ε | Bounded noise, compressible signals |
| LASSO | min (1/2)||Az − y||_2^2 + λ||z||_1 | Statistical noise, model selection |

Basis pursuit denoising (BPDN) and the LASSO are equivalent under appropriate parameter correspondence; the Dantzig selector offers an alternative constraint based on the correlation A^T(Az − y) with near-oracle statistical properties [8].

### Algorithmic realization

Linear programming via interior-point methods solves (P1) to high accuracy but scales poorly beyond tens of thousands of variables. Practical methodology therefore includes:

- **Homotopy and proximal methods** (e.g., SPGL1, FISTA) that exploit the geometry of the L1 ball and scale to millions of variables.
- **Greedy pursuits** such as Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (OMP), which iteratively selects the column most correlated with the residual.
- **Iterative thresholding** (ISTA, IHT) with per-iteration cost dominated by matrix–vector products.
- **Approximate Message Passing (AMP)**, whose dynamics are tracked exactly by *state evolution* in the large-system limit and which achieves the Bayes-optimal threshold for i.i.d. Gaussian designs.

A minimal basis-pursuit experiment in Python illustrates the methodology end to end:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N, m, s = 256, 80, 10          # ambient dim, measurements, sparsity
A = rng.standard_normal((m, N)) / np.sqrt(m)
x_true = np.zeros(N)
supp = rng.choice(N, s, replace=False)
x_true[supp] = rng.standard_normal(s)
y = A @ x_true

# (P1) as a linear program: min sum(t) s.t. -t <= z <= t, A z = y
c = np.concatenate([np.zeros(N), np.ones(N)])
A_ub = np.block([[np.eye(N), -np.eye(N)], [-np.eye(N), -np.eye(N)]])
b_ub = np.zeros(2 * N)
A_eq = np.block([A, np.zeros((m, N))])
res = linprog(c, A_ub=A_ub, b_ub=b_ub, A_eq=A_eq, b_eq=y,
              method="highs")
x_hat = res.x[:N]
print("relative error:", np.linalg.norm(x_hat - x_true) / np.linalg.norm(x_true))
# typical output: relative error ~ 1e-8  (exact recovery)
```

The dual problem is equally instructive: the optimality certificate for (P1) is a *dual vector* w = A^T ν matching sign(x) on the support with magnitude below one off it; constructing such certificates, e.g. via the "golfing scheme," is the engine of modern recovery proofs [3].


















##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The polytope geometry of L1 minimization

Why should minimizing a convex function ever return a sparse vertex? The answer is polyhedral. The L1 unit ball in R^N is the *cross-polytope*: it has 2N vertices at the signed coordinate axes and 2^N facets. Minimizing ||z||_1 over the affine subspace {z : Az = y} slides a scaled cross-polytope outward from the origin until it first touches the subspace. Because the polytope is pointy at sparse vertices, the first contact is generically at a vertex — a 1-sparse point — or more generally at a low-dimensional face corresponding to a sparse vector.

Donoho made this precise through *neighborliness*: the projected cross-polytope A(C^N) has the true signal's face as a face of the projected polytope if and only if (P1) recovers it. For Gaussian A, the number of k-faces of the projected polytope undergoes a sharp threshold — the same phase transition observed empirically. This geometric viewpoint unifies the analytic RIP approach with the combinatorial face-counting approach: both describe the same high-dimensional phenomenon, one via metric near-isometries, the other via polytope combinatorics.

*Key insight:* L2 minimization fails precisely because the Euclidean ball is smooth — its first contact with the affine subspace is generically at a dense point, with energy smeared across coordinates. Non-smoothness at sparse points is not a defect of the L1 norm; it is the entire mechanism.

### 4.2 Concentration, covering, and why randomness works

The RIP for Gaussian matrices rests on two pillars of high-dimensional probability. First, *concentration of measure*: for a fixed unit vector x, ||Ax||_2^2 concentrates sharply around its mean, with P(| ||Ax||_2^2 − 1 | > δ) ≤ 2exp(−c m δ^2). Second, *covering numbers*: the set of s-sparse unit vectors is a union of C(N, s) unit spheres in R^s, each of which admits a finite δ-net of cardinality at most (1 + 2/δ)^s. A union bound over the net, extended to the whole set by a standard approximation argument, yields RIP with m ≥ C δ^{−2} s log(N/s) [1].

The logarithmic factor log(N/s) is the price of *uniformity* — one matrix must work for all sparse signals simultaneously. Information-theoretic lower bounds via Gelfand widths show that m must be at least of order s log(N/s) for any stable decoder, so the Gaussian construction is optimal up to the constant C. This optimality is what elevates compressed sensing from a bag of tricks to a closed theory: randomness achieves the fundamental limit.

For partial Fourier measurements, Candès and Tao's analysis uses the noncommutative Khintchine inequality and Dudley's entropy integral to control a chaos process, yielding the UUP with m ≥ C s log^6(N), later refined to fewer logarithmic factors. Incoherence between the sensing basis and the sparsity basis is what makes subsampling work: energy spreads evenly, so no small set of measurements can miss a sparse signal entirely [3].

### 4.3 Phase transitions: the Donoho–Tanner picture

Fix the undersampling ratio δ = m/N and the sparsity ratio ρ = s/m. Draw A Gaussian and x uniformly s-sparse. As N grows, the probability that (P1) recovers x exactly tends to either 0 or 1, with a sharp boundary ρ*(δ) separating the phases. Donoho and Tanner computed this boundary exactly via polytope angle sums: below the curve, success with probability approaching one; above it, failure [6].

The curve has a striking small-δ asymptote: ρ*(δ) ≈ 1 / (2 log(1/δ)). In words, at extreme undersampling, the recoverable sparsity fraction decays only logarithmically — one can recover signals with s ≈ m / (2 log(N/m)) nonzeros. The transition width shrinks like N^{−1/2}, so for realistic dimensions the boundary is effectively a step function. This is the *precise undersampling theorem*: not an order bound with unknown constants, but an exact asymptotic formula validated to high precision by Monte Carlo experiments. It also delineates the gap between convex and combinatorial decoding — (P0) would succeed up to the higher counting bound, and the strip between the two curves is the price of tractability.

### 4.4 Beyond convexity: greedy, thresholding, and message passing

Convex programming is not the only route, and for very large problems it is not the fastest:

- **Orthogonal Matching Pursuit** builds the support greedily, projecting the observation onto the selected columns at each step. Under coherence or RIP assumptions it recovers s-sparse signals in s steps, but its guarantees require stricter conditions than (P1) and it is fragile under noise.
- **Iterative Hard Thresholding** iterates z ← H_s(z + A^T(y − Az)), where H_s keeps the s largest entries. With RIP of order 3s and δ_3s < 1/2, it converges linearly — a rare case where a non-convex iteration carries a full convergence proof.
- **Approximate Message Passing** refines iterative thresholding with an Onsager correction term that debiases the iterates. Its large-system dynamics are tracked exactly by a one-dimensional recursion called *state evolution*, and for Gaussian designs it achieves the information-theoretic threshold — outperforming (P1) in the noiseless setting and matching the Bayes-optimal error with noise.

The TLA+ sketch below formalizes the measurement–recovery contract as a state machine: measurements are acquired once, and the recovery action is enabled only when the RIP predicate holds over all small supports, guaranteeing that the decoded signal equals the planted one.

```tla+
---- MODULE CompressedSensing ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS N, M, S
VARIABLES xTrue, y, xHat

TypeOK == xTrue \in [1..N -> Real] /\ y \in [1..M -> Real]

Support(v) == {i \in 1..N : v[i] /= 0}
IsSparse(v) == Cardinality(Support(v)) <= S

RIP(A, delta) ==
  \A T \in SUBSET (1..N) :
    Cardinality(T) <= S =>
      \A v \in [T -> Real] : (1 - delta) * Norm2(v) <= Norm2(A ** v)

Recover == /\ IsSparse(xTrue)
           /\ \E A : RIP(A, 1/4) /\ y = A ** xTrue
           /\ xHat = CHOOSE z \in [1..N -> Real] :
                 A ** z = y /\ \A w : (A ** w = y) => L1(z) <= L1(w)
           => xHat = xTrue
====
```

| 0.10 | 0.25 | 0.62 | Transition |

The empirical 50%-success contour tracks the Donoho–Tanner curve ρ*(δ) to within the transition width, confirming the theory quantitatively rather than merely qualitatively [6]. Notably, the transition sharpens as N increases: at N = 200 the boundary is visibly blurred, while at N = 1600 it is essentially a step function — the signature of a genuine high-dimensional phase transition.

### Proof sketch: exact recovery from RIP

We sketch why δ_2s < sqrt(2) − 1 implies exact recovery, following Candès. Let x be s-sparse with support T, let x# solve (P1), and set h = x# − x, so Ah = 0. Decompose the complement of T into blocks T_1, T_2, ... of size s ordered by decreasing magnitude of h. Optimality of x# gives ||x + h||_1 ≥ ||x||_1, which after the triangle inequality yields the *cone constraint*: ||h_{T^c}||_1 ≤ ||h_T||_1. Because each block's L2 norm is controlled by the previous block's L1 norm (a shelling argument), we obtain ||h_{(T ∪ T_1)^c}||_2 ≤ s^{−1/2}||h_T||_1.

Now RIP of order 2s applies to h_{T ∪ T_1} (a 2s-sparse vector). From Ah = 0 we have A h_{T ∪ T_1} = −A h_{(T ∪ T_1)^c}; taking inner products and applying RIP to the left and the block estimate to the right gives (1 − δ_2s)||h_{T ∪ T_1}||_2 ≤ sqrt(2) δ_2s s^{−1/2} ||h_T||_1 / ... and after algebra ||h_{T ∪ T_1}||_2 (1 − (1 + sqrt(2))δ_2s) ≤ 0. When δ_2s < sqrt(2) − 1, the bracket is positive, forcing h = 0. Hence x# = x. The argument uses nothing about x beyond sparsity — recovery is *uniform* over all s-sparse signals [1][5].


















##6 Limitations

The theory, for all its sharpness, carries significant caveats that separate theorems from deployments.

**Certifying RIP is intractable.** The RIP is a universal quantifier over all C(N, s) supports; computing δ_s exactly is NP-hard in general. We therefore rely on *probabilistic* constructions — a given random matrix satisfies RIP with high probability, but verifying any particular draw is infeasible. Deterministic matrices with optimal-order RIP remain undiscovered, and it is unknown whether they exist at all.

**Structured sensing pays log factors.** Real hardware cannot always realize i.i.d. Gaussian measurements. The single-pixel camera uses binary micromirror patterns, MRI uses Fourier trajectories, and radar uses chirps. Each structured ensemble currently costs additional logarithmic factors in the measurement count relative to the Gaussian ideal, and the constants matter at clinical scale [7].

**Noise folding and dynamic range.** In the standard model, measurement noise e is added *after* compression, but the recovery effectively amplifies it: white noise in y maps to noise spread across all N coordinates, degrading the effective SNR by roughly N/m. Signals with high dynamic range — a few huge coefficients among tiny ones — can also defeat practical solvers even when the theory's constants are satisfied.

**Model mismatch.** Sparsity in an orthonormal basis is an idealization. Real images are sparse in *redundant* dictionaries or in analysis formulations (total variation, wavelet frames), where the synthesis model y = Ax with x sparse is replaced by analysis sparsity of Ψx. The RIP must then be replaced by the D-RIP or cosparsity machinery, with weaker and more delicate guarantees.

**Computational scaling.** Interior-point LP solvers scale superlinearly; first-order methods need many matrix–vector products; and greedy methods lack uniform guarantees. For gigapixel-scale problems (whole-body MRI, seismic imaging), even forming A explicitly is impossible, and solvers must exploit fast implicit transforms — restricting the ensemble to those with O(N log N) operators.

##7 Conclusion

Compressed sensing resolved a paradox that had stood since Shannon: the information rate of a sparse signal is far below its Nyquist rate, and — against the intuition of a generation — this gap can be exploited by *linear* measurements and *convex* optimization. The restricted isometry property provides the geometric hinge: random matrices embed sparse vectors nearly isometrically, the L1 ball's pointed geometry then selects the sparse explanation, and the Donoho–Tanner phase transition marks the exact boundary of what tractable decoding can achieve [1][4][6].

The practical dividends are real: accelerated MRI protocols in clinical use, single-pixel and lensless cameras, sub-Nyquist radar, and sparse channel estimation in massive MIMO all trace their lineage to these theorems [7][8]. Yet the field's deepest questions remain open — deterministic optimal RIP matrices, the exact constants of structured ensembles, and the computational-statistical gaps that message-passing algorithms are only beginning to close. Sparsity turned out to be not a special case but a prototype: the first and still sharpest instance of the general principle that *low-dimensional structure makes high-dimensional inference tractable*.
