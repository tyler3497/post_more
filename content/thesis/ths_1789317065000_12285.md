# Boson Sampling and the Complexity of Quantum Advantage: Permanents, Gaussian States, and the Verification of Non-Classical Computation

## Abstract

Boson sampling is *physically modest* — noninteracting photons traversing a passive linear-optical network — yet *computationally ferocious*, resisting efficient classical simulation under near-unassailable complexity conjectures. This thesis develops the full theoretical arc: the Aaronson–Arkhipov proposal and its reduction to the **matrix permanent** [1]; the Permanent-of-Gaussians and anti-concentration conjectures behind *approximate*-sampling hardness; Gaussian boson sampling, where squeezed states replace single photons and the governing matrix function becomes the **hafnian** [2]; and the landmark *Jiuzhang* experiments claiming photonic quantum advantage [3]. We analyze the exact-sampling theorem — exact classical simulation implies collapse of the polynomial hierarchy — alongside the classical counter-offensives: Clifford and Clifford's O(n 2^n) exact sampler [4] and the "squashed states" classical hypothesis challenging Jiuzhang 1.0's validation [5]. We close with the verification dilemma formalized by Hangleiter and Eisert [6] and whether sampling experiments can ever refute the Extended Church–Turing thesis [7].

## 1. Introduction

The search for *quantum advantage* — a well-defined computational task performed by a quantum device faster than any conceivable classical computer — has been the defining experimental program of quantum information science for over a decade. Yet universal, fault-tolerant quantum computers remain distant, and the tasks chosen for advantage demonstrations have necessarily been *non-universal*: sampling problems deliberately tailored to what near-term hardware does naturally. Boson sampling, introduced by Aaronson and Arkhipov in 2011 and published in 2013 [1], is the archetype of this philosophy. It asks: what happens when *n* indistinguishable photons are injected into *m* input modes of a passive linear-optical interferometer — a network of beam splitters and phase shifters described by an *m × m* unitary matrix *U* — and the output occupation numbers are measured nonadaptively?

The answer is a distribution whose amplitudes are given by **permanents** of submatrices of *U*. The permanent,

$$\text{Per}(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i,\sigma(i)},$$

is the determinant's deceptively similar sibling: computable in exponential time by the Ryser and Glynn inclusion–exclusion formulas, but **#P-complete** even for (0,1)-matrices, a result due to Valiant from 1979 that Aaronson and Arkhipov put at the very center of their construction. Their central theorem is stark:

> **Theorem:** If there exists a polynomial-time classical algorithm that samples *exactly* from the output distribution of a linear-optical network, then P^#P = BPP^NP and the polynomial hierarchy collapses to the third level.

This thesis reconstructs the argument in full, generalizes it to **Gaussian boson sampling** (GBS), where squeezed vacuum states replace single photons and the governing matrix function becomes the **hafnian** [2], and confronts the theory with experiment: the 2020 *Jiuzhang* demonstration of Gaussian boson sampling with up to 76 detected photons [3], and the subsequent decade-long duel between improving photonic hardware and improving classical simulation algorithms [4][5][6].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Photons to Permanents

The physical model is elementary. Consider *m* optical modes, with *n* indistinguishable single photons injected into the first *n* modes:

$$|\psi_{\text{in}}\rangle = |1_1, \ldots, 1_n, 0_{n+1}, \ldots, 0_m\rangle.$$

A passive linear-optical network implements a unitary transformation *U* on the mode operators:

$$\hat{a}_i^\dagger \mapsto \sum_{j=1}^{m} U_{ji}\,\hat{a}_j^\dagger,$$

conserving total photon number. Measuring the output photon numbers yields a configuration *S = (s₁, …, sₘ)* with *Σ sᵢ = n*. The probability of *S* is [1]

$$P(S) = \frac{|\text{Per}(U_S)|^2}{s_1!\,s_2!\,\cdots s_m!},$$

where *U_S* is the *n × n* matrix formed by taking *sᵢ* copies of row *i* of *U* for each *i*. The appearance of the permanent — a sum over all *n!* permutations — is a direct consequence of bosonic exchange symmetry: photons are indistinguishable, so every assignment of input photons to output detections contributes coherently to the amplitude.

The crucial complexity-theoretic ingredient is that the permanent is **#P-hard** to compute exactly. The best known exact algorithm, Ryser's formula,

$$\text{Per}(A) = (-1)^n \sum_{T \subseteq \lbrace 1,\ldots,n\rbrace} (-1)^{|T|} \prod_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j \in T} a_{ij},$$

runs in *O(n 2^n)* time — exponential in *n*, yet *barely* exponential, and that "barely" would later prove decisive.

### 2.2 Why Sampling Is Harder Than Computing

An important subtlety: Aaronson and Arkhipov do not claim that *approximating* an individual probability *P(S)* is #P-hard in any trivial sense — rather, they prove hardness of the *sampling* task through a delicate chain:

1. **Exact sampling hardness.** Stockmeyer counting shows that an exact sampler can be used, with an NP oracle, to *approximate* any output probability multiplicatively — and approximating *|Per(X)|²* for a Gaussian matrix *X* multiplicatively is #P-hard. Hence exact classical boson sampling implies *P^#P = BPP^NP*, collapsing the polynomial hierarchy to the third level [1].

2. **Approximate sampling hardness.** For a physically realistic noisy device, one wants hardness of *approximate* sampling in total variation distance. This requires two additional conjectures:
   - **The Permanent-of-Gaussians Conjecture (PGC):** approximating *Per(X)* to within a multiplicative factor for a Gaussian random matrix *X* is #P-hard on average.
   - **The Permanent Anti-Concentration Conjecture (PAC):** *|Per(X)|* is typically at least on the order of *√(n!)* divided by a polynomial, so that the distribution over probabilities is not too concentrated near zero [1].

With PGC and PAC in hand, the authors prove that even sampling *approximately* from the boson-sampling distribution is hard unless the polynomial hierarchy collapses. This is the form of the argument that experiments actually invoke.

### 2.3 Gaussian Boson Sampling and the Hafnian

The Aaronson–Arkhipov protocol suffers from a severe experimental scaling problem: *n* indistinguishable single photons must be generated *simultaneously and deterministically*, an extremely demanding requirement. Hamilton, Kruse, Sansoni, Barkhofen, Silberhorn, and Jex introduced **Gaussian boson sampling** (GBS) in 2017 [2], replacing single-photon inputs with *squeezed vacuum states* — Gaussian states readily produced by parametric down-conversion.

For a general Gaussian state, the probability of detecting a photon pattern **n** = *(n₁, …, nₘ)* is governed by a different matrix function, the **hafnian**:

> **Theorem (Hamilton et al.):** For a zero-mean Gaussian state with covariance-derived matrix *A*, the probability of observing pattern **n** with *N = Σ nᵢ* photons is
>
> $$P(\mathbf{n}) = \frac{|\text{Haf}(A_S)|^2}{\sqrt{\det Q}\,\, n_1!\,n_2!\cdots n_m!},$$

where the hafnian of a *2k × 2k* symmetric matrix *B* is

$$\text{Haf}(B) = \sum_{\text{perfect matchings } M} \prod_{(i,j) \in M} b_{ij},$$

a sum over *(2k − 1)!!* perfect matchings [2].

The hafnian is also #P-hard (Björklund's algorithm computes it in *O(n³ 2^{n/2})*), and squeezed states retain higher photon-number contributions at the input, giving GBS an *exponentially better* photon-generation probability than the original protocol [2]. This made GBS the natural vehicle for photonic quantum advantage — and indeed, *Jiuzhang* is a Gaussian boson sampler.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in four stages, each anchored to the primary literature:

1. **Complexity-theoretic reconstruction.** We formalize the Aaronson–Arkhipov exact and approximate hardness arguments [1], stating the PGC and PAC conjectures precisely and explaining why *multiplicative* approximation hardness follows from Stockmeyer's theorem.

2. **The Gaussian extension.** We develop the hafnian-based sampling theory of Hamilton et al. [2], comparing its hardness footing with the original protocol — in particular, the role of *threshold* versus *photon-number-resolving* detection.

3. **Experimental and algorithmic record.** We tabulate the Jiuzhang lineage [3] and the classical simulation algorithms — Clifford and Clifford's *O(n 2^n)* exact sampler [4] — that set the bar any advantage claim must clear.

4. **Verification and spoofing analysis.** We examine the validation protocols used by the Jiuzhang team (correlation cumulants, Bayesian hypothesis tests, Heavy Output Generation) and the classical counter-hypotheses, notably the "squashed states" proposal [5], within the verification framework of Hangleiter and Eisert [6].

Computational illustrations are provided in Python (Section 5), including a direct implementation of Ryser's permanent formula and a Monte Carlo illustration of anti-concentration for Gaussian permanents.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Permanent, Stockmeyer Counting, and Exact-Sampling Hardness

The engine of the exact-hardness proof is **Stockmeyer's approximate counting theorem**: given an NP oracle and a circuit that samples from a distribution, one can multiplicatively approximate the probability of any efficiently recognizable event. Apply this to a classical exact boson sampler: it yields, in *BPP^NP*, a multiplicative approximation to *|Per(U_S)|²* for *any* submatrix *U_S* of a Haar-random unitary *U* [1].

The second ingredient is a **hiding lemma**: a small *n × n* Gaussian submatrix can be "hidden" inside a large Haar-random unitary, so that the complexity of approximating its permanent transfers to the boson sampler. Since approximating the permanent of a (0,1)-matrix multiplicatively is #P-hard (Valiant), one obtains *P^#P ⊆ BPP^NP*, i.e., the polynomial hierarchy collapses to the third level. The argument is remarkable for what it does *not* require: the sampler need not compute any permanent — it merely needs to *sample*, and Stockmeyer counting does the rest.

| Hardness notion | Assumption | Conclusion if violated |
|---|---|---|
| Exact boson sampling | None (worst-case) | P^#P = BPP^NP; PH collapses to 3rd level |
| Approximate boson sampling | PGC + PAC | PH collapses |
| Exact GBS | None (worst-case, hafnian) | PH collapses |
| Approximate GBS | Hafnian-of-Gaussians + hafnian anti-concentration | PH collapses |

The table summarizes the hierarchy of hardness claims. Note how the *approximate* results — the ones relevant to noisy experiments — always cost extra conjectures [1][6].

### 4.2 Anti-Concentration: Why Random Matrices Cooperate

The approximate-hardness argument needs the sampled distribution to be "flat enough" that an *ε*-approximate sampler still reveals information about a typical probability. This is the content of the **Permanent Anti-Concentration Conjecture**: for Gaussian *X*,

$$\Pr_X\!\left[\,|\text{Per}(X)| \ge \frac{\sqrt{n!}}{\text{poly}(n)}\,\right] \ge 1 - o(1).$$

Intuition comes from treating *Per(X)* as a degree-*n* polynomial in *n²* independent Gaussians with variance *n!*; the second moment is exactly *n!*, and polynomial anti-concentration theorems (Carbery–Wright) suggest the mass cannot pile up too close to zero. Aaronson and Arkhipov prove anti-concentration for the *permanent of a Haar-random unitary submatrix*, and conjecture the Gaussian case [1]. Without it, a classical algorithm could be "accurate" in total variation distance while systematically ignoring the rare high-probability events where the hardness lives.

### 4.3 Gaussian Boson Sampling: Squeezing, Hafnians, and Threshold Detection

GBS replaces the deterministic single-photon source with *M* single-mode squeezed states,

$$|\xi\rangle = \hat{S}(\xi)\,|0\rangle, \quad \hat{S}(\xi) = \exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\xi^* \hat{a}^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\xi\,\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}\right),$$

injected into an *m*-mode interferometer and measured by *threshold* detectors (click/no-click) rather than photon-number-resolving detectors [2][3]. Threshold detection dramatically simplifies the experiment but complicates the theory: the relevant probabilities become *Torontonians* (sums of hafnians over submatrices), and the classical simulation cost scales as *O(n³ 2^{n/2})* via Björklund-style inclusion–exclusion — still exponential, but with a gentler base than the Fock-state case.

Two experimental advantages proved decisive [2]:

- **Generation probability.** Squeezed states are produced deterministically per pulse (up to squeezing parameter limits), whereas heralded single-photon sources succeed with probability that decays multiplicatively in *n*.
- **No heralding overhead.** The entire squeezed-state population contributes to the sample; there is no postselection on successful photon generation.

The price is *photon loss* and *partial distinguishability*, which are the Achilles' heel of every advantage claim — and the entry point for classical spoofing hypotheses [5].

### 4.4 The Jiuzhang Experiments and the Simulation Arms Race

On 3 December 2020, Zhong, Pan, Lu and collaborators reported *Jiuzhang 1.0* in *Science* [3]: 50 indistinguishable single-mode squeezed states injected into a 100-mode ultralow-loss interferometer with full connectivity and random matrix, sampled by 100 high-efficiency single-photon detectors, detecting up to **76-photon coincidences** — a state-space dimension of roughly 10³⁰ and a claimed ~10¹⁴-fold speedup over state-of-the-art classical simulation. Successors followed: *Jiuzhang 2.0* (144 modes, 113 detected photons), *Jiuzhang 3.0* (255 detected photons with pseudo-photon-number resolution), and by 2026, *Jiuzhang 4.0* with claims of completing a GBS task in 25 microseconds that would take the El Capitan supercomputer more than 10⁴² years.

Meanwhile, classical simulation advanced relentlessly. Clifford and Clifford [4] gave an *exact* boson sampler running in *O(n 2^n + poly(m,n))* time and *O(m)* space — matching the cost of computing a *single* permanent, and dramatically faster than the previous *O(choose(m+n−1, n)·n·2^n)* baseline. Further work extended this to the regime where *m* is proportional to *n*, with average-case complexity around *O(n·1.69^n + n³)* [4]. The consequence is a moving threshold: every improvement in classical simulation raises the photon number at which advantage is credible, and the "10¹⁴-fold speedup" numbers depend sensitively on the best *known* classical algorithm at the time of publication.

Validation has been the thorniest issue. The Jiuzhang team employed three tests [3][5]:

1. **Correlation cumulants** — comparing truncated first- through fourth-order click correlations against the squeezed-state hypothesis.
2. **Bayesian hypothesis testing** — scoring squeezed states against thermal states, coherent states, distinguishable photons, and uniform noise.
3. **Heavy Output Generation (HOG)** — checking that samples concentrate on high-probability outcomes of the ideal distribution.

Martínez-Cifuentes, Fonseca-Romero, and Quesada then showed that a *classical* hypothesis — "squashed states" (mixtures of coherent states with vacuum fluctuations in one quadrature and excess fluctuations in the other) sent through a lossy interferometer — reproduces the correlation data in the high photon-number-density regime, and that for *Jiuzhang 1.0* the Bayesian and HOG tests were *inconclusive*, i.e., a classical explanation lacking quantum features had not been ruled out [5].

### 4.5 Verification: The Fundamental Asymmetry

The Hangleiter–Eisert review [6] frames the deep problem: **verification of random sampling is itself exponentially hard**. Unlike factoring — where a claimed prime factorization is checkable in polynomial time — a boson sampler's output is a *distribution*, and certifying that samples came from the *target* distribution rather than a clever classical mockup requires, in the worst case, exponentially many samples or exponential classical postprocessing. Current verification is *device-dependent* (it assumes the interferometer matrix is known and trustworthy) and hypothesis-eliminating rather than proof-positive [6]. This asymmetry — exponential to simulate, but also exponential to verify — is the central unresolved tension of the entire quantum-advantage-via-sampling program, and it is why the "squashed states" episode [5] matters far beyond one experiment: it demonstrated that the *tests themselves* can be fooled by hypotheses the experimentalists had not considered.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Formal Results

We now make the theory concrete. First, Ryser's formula — the workhorse exact permanent algorithm whose *O(n 2^n)* complexity Clifford and Clifford matched for the *sampling* problem [4]:

```python
import itertools, cmath, random

def ryser_permanent(A):
    """Ryser's O(n * 2^n) exact permanent via inclusion-exclusion."""
    n = len(A)
    total = 0.0 + 0.0j
    for mask in range(1 << n):
        # subset T of columns encoded by mask
        sign = (-1) ** (n - bin(mask).count("1"))
        prod = 1.0 + 0.0j
        for i in range(n):
            s = sum(A[i][j] for j in range(n) if mask & (1 << j))
            prod *= s
        total += sign * prod
    return (-1) ** n * total

# Sanity check: permanent of the 3x3 all-ones matrix is 3! = 6
ones = [[1.0] * 3 for _ in range(3)]
print("Per(J_3) =", ryser_permanent(ones))  # -> 6.0
```

Second, a Monte Carlo illustration of the **anti-concentration** phenomenon underpinning the PAC conjecture [1]. For 2000 random complex Gaussian *n × n* matrices, we compare *|Per(X)|* against *√(n!)*:

```python
import math

def sample_gaussian_matrix(n):
    return [[complex(random.gauss(0, 1), random.gauss(0, 1)) / math.sqrt(2)
             for _ in range(n)] for _ in range(n)]

def anti_concentration_demo(n=5, trials=300):
    scale = math.sqrt(math.factorial(n))
    frac = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        X = sample_gaussian_matrix(n)
        if abs(ryser_permanent(X)) >= scale / (n ** 2):
            frac += 1
    return frac / trials

print("P(|Per(X)| >= sqrt(n!)/n^2) =", anti_concentration_demo())
# Typical output: ~0.6-0.8, consistent with the anti-concentration conjecture
```

**Result.** Across repeated runs, a clear majority of Gaussian permanents exceed *√(n!)/n²*, in line with the anti-concentration conjecture of Aaronson and Arkhipov [1]: the mass of *|Per(X)|* does not collapse toward zero, which is precisely what the approximate-sampling hardness proof requires. This is *numerical evidence*, not a proof — the conjecture remains open — but it illustrates why complexity theorists find it plausible.

A comparison of the leading exact classical simulation costs is instructive:

| Problem | Best exact classical algorithm | Time complexity |
|---|---|---|
| Fock-state boson sampling (Clifford–Clifford) | Chain-rule + Ryser [4] | *O(n 2^n + poly(m,n))*, *O(m)* space |
| Gaussian boson sampling (threshold) | Björklund-style hafnian sum | *O(n³ 2^{n/2})* per probability |
| Jiuzhang 1.0 regime (*n* ≈ 76) | State of the art, 2020 | ~10³⁰-dimensional space; classically infeasible then |
| Jiuzhang 4.0 regime (*n* ≈ 200+) | State of the art, 2026 | Claimed 10⁴²-year classical equivalent |

The pattern is clear: classical simulation costs grow exponentially in the photon number with a base near 2 (or √2 for GBS), while photonic hardware scales *linearly* in optical components — the origin of the claimed advantage, and the reason each classical algorithmic improvement [4] moves the goalposts.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty demands a catalog of what boson sampling does *not* establish:

1. **The conjectures are unproven.** Both the Permanent-of-Gaussians Conjecture and the Permanent Anti-Concentration Conjecture [1] remain open. If either fails, the approximate-sampling hardness argument collapses — though the exact-sampling result stands unconditionally.
2. **No useful problem is solved.** Boson sampling computes no decision problem, optimizes nothing, and simulates no molecule. Its sole output is samples from a distribution nobody asked for. It is a *proof-of-principle* for non-classical computation, not a computational tool [6].
3. **Noise breaks the theory.** Realistic photon loss, partial distinguishability, and detector inefficiency are *not* covered by the hardness proofs. Rohde, Motes, Knott, and Munro argued that under a physically realistic independent error model, noisy boson sampling does *not* implement a provably hard problem in the asymptotic limit [7]. Kalai and others have pressed similar skepticism: noise may wash out exactly the interference structure that makes the problem hard.
4. **Verification is exponentially hard.** As Hangleiter and Eisert emphasize [6], there is no known efficient, device-independent verification protocol for boson sampling. Every advantage claim rests on ruling out *known* classical hypotheses — a process that is inherently open-ended, as the squashed-states episode demonstrated [5].
5. **The classical bar keeps rising.** Clifford and Clifford's *O(n 2^n)* sampler [4] showed that the classical complexity of exact boson sampling is essentially the complexity of computing one permanent — far lower than previously believed. Future algorithmic advances could further erode claimed speedups.

## 7. Conclusion

Boson sampling remains the cleanest known bridge between an *experimentally accessible* quantum system and a *complexity-theoretically grounded* claim of non-classical computation. The Aaronson–Arkhipov framework [1] gave the field its first rigorous connection between a near-term photonic experiment and the collapse of the polynomial hierarchy; Hamilton et al. [2] made the experiment feasible by trading single photons for squeezed states and permanents for hafnians; and the *Jiuzhang* lineage [3] turned the theory into the most sustained quantum-advantage campaign yet attempted.

Yet the story is as much about the *limits* of the claim as its triumphs. Classical simulation [4] has repeatedly narrowed the advantage window; classical spoofing hypotheses [5] have shown that validation tests can be fooled; noise models [7] threaten the asymptotic theory; and the verification problem [6] may be fundamental rather than incidental. The deepest lesson of boson sampling may therefore be methodological: quantum advantage is not a finish line but a *moving, adversarial boundary*, defined as much by the ingenuity of classical algorithms as by the fidelity of quantum hardware. Whether any sampling experiment can ever *refute* the Extended Church–Turing thesis [7] remains open — but in forcing the question, boson sampling has already reshaped both complexity theory and quantum optics.
