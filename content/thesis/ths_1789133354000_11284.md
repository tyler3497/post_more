# Progressive Lowering in MLIR: Dialect Hierarchies, Type Conversion, and the Semantics of Multi-Level Compilation

## Abstract

Multi-Level Intermediate Representation (MLIR) reframes compilation as a sequence of small, verifiable refinements across a hierarchy of dialects rather than a monolithic translation from source to machine code. This thesis develops a rigorous account of progressive lowering: the discipline by which a program descends from high-level, domain-specific abstractions (e.g., `linalg.generic`, `affine.for`) through successively more concrete dialects (`scf`, `memref`, `arith`) to the LLVM dialect. We formalize the machinery that makes this descent sound: the dialect conversion framework, comprising the `ConversionTarget` legality specification, the `TypeConverter` that mediates type-domain crossings, and the `ConversionPatternRewriter` with its transactional, rollback-on-failure semantics. Drawing on the CGO 2021 foundation, the composable code-generation flow of Vasilache et al., and recent formal treatments of rewriting in MLIR, we evaluate the framework's real guarantees (termination, completeness, but not confluence) and expose the principled limits of legality-based lowering.

## 1. Introduction

Classical compiler architecture rests on a seductive fiction: that a program can be faithfully expressed in a *single* intermediate representation — LLVM IR, say, or the JVM bytecode format — and that all optimization and lowering decisions can be staged along one axis of increasing concreteness. This fiction was productive for decades, but it imposes a tax. LLVM IR is, roughly, *"C with vectors"*: a fixed abstraction level that sits far below the concepts compilers increasingly need to manipulate — tensor contractions, loop nests with dependence structure, hardware datapaths, quantum gates [2]. Frontends that care about these concepts have historically compensated by building *their own* private IRs (XLA's HLO, Swift's SIL, Julia's typed IR), each re-implementing SSA construction, parsing, verification, and pass management from scratch. The resulting software fragmentation is both an engineering duplication and a semantic hazard: every private IR is another surface on which correctness arguments must be rebuilt.

MLIR's answer, presented by Lattner et al. at CGO 2021, is to standardize the *infrastructure of IRs* rather than the IR itself [1]. The core insight is that compilers need not share one abstraction level; they can share a common *substrate* — SSA values, operations with regions and blocks, attributes, types, declarative definitions, location tracking, multithreaded pass management — on which many coexisting abstractions, called **dialects**, are defined. Compilation then becomes **progressive lowering**: the same function, in the same module, may simultaneously contain high-level tensor operations, mid-level loop structures, and low-level address arithmetic, with each abstraction refined downward only when its optimization opportunities have been exhausted. As the foundational paper states, MLIR's design is guided by parsimony (minimal builtin semantics), progressivity (avoid premature lowering), and traceability (retain information declaratively) [1].

This thesis examines the lowering half of that contract. Lowering is where semantic risk concentrates: every refinement step reinterprets the program under a weaker, more machine-like theory, and a single unsound step invalidates everything above it. Our central questions are:

1. What is the *formal mechanism* by which MLIR guarantees that a conversion pass either fully succeeds or leaves the IR untouched?
2. How do `TypeConverter`s coordinate the crossing of type domains (e.g., `tensor` to `memref`) that accompanies op lowering?
3. What guarantees — termination, completeness, confluence — does the dialect conversion framework actually provide, and where does the responsibility fall back on the dialect designer?
4. What are the principled limits of progressive lowering as a compilation strategy?

We answer these with reference to the framework's design [1][3], its use in production code-generation flows [3], recent formal treatments of rewriting semantics in MLIR [4], and program-raising research that runs the pipeline in reverse [5].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The dialect as a compilation unit

An MLIR **dialect** groups operations, types, and attributes that belong together — conceptually, the IR analog of a dynamic library [3]. A dialect is defined declaratively in TableGen (ODS): each operation specifies its operands, results, attributes, regions, and assembly format; each type and attribute specifies its parameters and constraints. At load time these definitions are registered with the `MLIRContext`, and the framework derives parsing, printing, documentation, and verification logic. Crucially, dialects are *namespaced* (`linalg.matmul`, `affine.for`, `arith.addi`), so hundreds of independently developed dialects can coexist — the repository ships dozens, from `tosa` and `vector` to `firrtl` and hardware dialects [1][5].

### 2.2 SSA, regions, and the operation as the universal atom

Every dialect shares one structural theory: the *operation*. An operation has a name, operands (SSA values), results, attributes (compile-time constants), successors, and *regions* — nested graphs of blocks containing further operations. This single recursive structure is expressive enough to model instruction-level dataflow (flat blocks), structured control flow (`scf.if` carries its branches as regions), and even whole modules. Because regions are lexically scoped and SSA dominance is defined over the region tree, verification — type consistency, dominance of definitions, region isolation — is uniform across dialects. Progressivity is built on this uniformity: a rewrite can replace a high-level op with a region of lower-level ops without leaving the structural theory.

### 2.3 Lowering before MLIR: all-or-nothing strata

Traditional multi-level compilers (e.g., the lowering from HLO to LLVM via a sequence of bespoke translators) suffer the *all-or-nothing* problem [3]: each level is a complete, self-contained IR, and translation is a wholesale, uncheckable step. Debugging requires trusting the translator; optimizing across the boundary requires duplicating analysis on both sides. MLIR's contribution is to make the boundary *permeable*: because all dialects share one IR substrate, an intermediate state can legally mix `linalg` ops with `memref` ops and even raw `llvm` instructions [3]. Lowering becomes a gradual, auditable process rather than a leap.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on three fronts.

**First, architectural reconstruction.** We reconstruct the dialect conversion framework from its design documentation and the CGO 2021 paper [1], tracing the roles of `ConversionTarget`, `ConversionPattern`, `TypeConverter`, and the `ConversionPatternRewriter`. We pay particular attention to the *transactional* character of conversion — the guarantee that a failed conversion rolls back — and to the insertion of `unrealized_conversion_cast` placeholders where the type-conversion path has gaps.

**Second, formal-semantic analysis.** We adopt the term-rewriting perspective developed in recent work that reads MLIR compilation through formal theories [4], classifying the framework's guarantees: termination (the source dialect is exhausted by construction), completeness (every illegal op must be matched or the pass fails), and the conspicuous absence of a confluence guarantee, which remains the dialect designer's responsibility.

**Third, case studies of production pipelines.** We examine the composable code-generation flow that lowered Google production linear-algebra workloads through MLIR [3], the FIRRTL-to-Verilog progressive pipeline in hardware compilation [1], and the `mlirSynth` program-raising system [5] as a mirror image that tests the invertibility of lowering steps. Where quantitative claims are made, they are attributed to their sources; where the literature is silent, we say so explicitly.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The ConversionTarget: legality as a declarative contract

The heart of dialect conversion is the `ConversionTarget`, a declarative specification of what the IR must look like *after* the pass. The designer declares:

- **Legal dialects** — e.g., `target.addLegalDialect<arith::ArithDialect>()`; every op in these dialects is accepted as-is.
- **Illegal dialects** — ops in these dialects *must* be rewritten; any survivor is a conversion failure.
- **Dynamically legal ops** — ops legal only under a predicate, typically on types: `target.addDynamicallyLegalOp<func::ReturnOp>([](func::ReturnOp op) { return typeConverter.isLegal(op.getOperandTypes()); })`. This is the standard idiom for *type-driven* legality: an op is legal only if its types have already been converted to the target domain [4].

Legality is thus not a property of the IR but a *contract* the rewrite must satisfy. The conversion driver first collects every illegal op in dominance order, then attempts pattern matching; when all patterns are exhausted it re-verifies legality, and if any illegal op remains, the pass fails. This two-phase structure — match, then audit — is what makes conversion legible to audit: the legality contract is checked by the framework, not trusted from the patterns.

> **Theorem (Legality audit):** If `applyFullConversion` returns success, then every operation in the resulting IR satisfies the `ConversionTarget`'s legality predicate. *Sketch:* the driver re-scans the entire IR after rewriting and fails if any op violates the predicate; success therefore implies universal satisfaction. ∎

### 4.2 TypeConverter: crossing the type domain

Operation lowering is rarely type-preserving. The canonical example is **bufferization**: `tensor<128x768xf32>` (an immutable, value-semantic type) must become `memref<128x768xf32>` (a mutable, reference-semantic type) as the program descends toward explicit memory management. The `TypeConverter` mediates this crossing [4]:

1. **Type mappings** — `typeConverter.addConversion([](TensorType t) { return MemRefType::get(t.getShape(), t.getElementType()); })` registers a 1→1 (or 1→N) mapping.
2. **Materializations** — when a value's type changes but a consumer still expects the old type (or vice versa), the converter inserts *materialization* ops, typically `unrealized_conversion_cast`, as explicit evidence of the type boundary.
3. **Signature conversions** — function and block signatures are rewritten so that entry-block arguments and call sites agree on converted types.

The `unrealized_conversion_cast` is philosophically important: rather than silently coercing, the framework *reifies the gap* as an op that later conversions must eliminate. A lowering pipeline is complete only when no such casts survive — a check the final export (e.g., LLVM IR translation) enforces [1]. In practice, production pipelines use a mix of conversion modes [4]:

| Mode | Semantics | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| `applyFullConversion` | All illegal ops must be rewritten; failure rolls back everything | Bufferization: every tensor must become a memref |
| `applyPartialConversion` | Only matched ops are rewritten; unmatched illegal ops survive | Incremental legalization passes, staged pipelines |

### 4.3 The ConversionPatternRewriter: transactional rewriting

The `ConversionPatternRewriter` extends the greedy pattern rewriter with transactional semantics [4]. Its distinguishing features:

- **Ordered, benefit-driven matching.** Patterns carry a *benefit* (priority); for each illegal op, patterns are tried in benefit order, first match wins. This is deterministic but *not* confluent in general — the choice among applicable patterns is a design decision with semantic weight.
- **N→M rewrites.** Unlike the canonicalizer's 1→1 patterns, conversion patterns may replace one op with many (e.g., `linalg.matmul` → nested `scf.for` loops with `arith` arithmetic), and the rewriter tracks all created ops for recursive legalization.
- **Rollback on failure.** All IR mutations are recorded in an undo log. If conversion fails — an illegal op with no matching pattern, a type conversion with no mapping — the log is replayed in reverse and the IR is restored to its pre-pass state. The pass therefore has *all-or-nothing* semantics at the transaction level, even as it permits *partial* semantic progress across passes.

> **Theorem (Transactional conversion):** If `applyFullConversion` returns failure, the IR is bit-identical to its state before the call. *Sketch:* every mutation (op creation, erasure, operand update) is journaled; failure triggers reverse replay of the journal, restoring the exact prior state. ∎

This guarantee is what makes speculative lowering pipelines practical: a pass can attempt an aggressive lowering and, on failure, the pipeline can fall back to a more conservative sequence — without bespoke cleanup logic.

### 4.4 Termination, completeness, and the missing confluence

Reading the framework through term-rewriting theory [4] yields a precise characterization:

- **Termination** is *structural*: legality rules forbid rewriting a legal op back into an illegal one, so the source dialect is exhausted by construction. No rewrite cycle can persist within one conversion pass, because every successful rewrite strictly decreases the count of illegal ops.
- **Completeness** is *enforced by failure*: under full conversion, every illegal op must have at least one applicable pattern, or the pass fails loudly rather than silently producing a half-lowered IR.
- **Confluence is not guaranteed.** When two patterns both match an illegal op, the higher-benefit pattern wins; nothing in the framework proves the alternatives equivalent. For 1→1 op mappings (e.g., `arith` → `llvm`) confluence is trivial, but for genuinely alternative lowerings — say, two tilings of a `linalg.generic` — the dialect designer owns the equivalence argument [4].

This is a deliberate division of labor: the framework guarantees *mechanical* properties (termination, atomicity, auditability) and leaves *semantic* properties (confluence, meaning preservation) to verified pattern authors — a division that mirrors the parsimony principle of the original design [1].

### 4.5 Mixing dialects: progressivity in practice

The payoff of the machinery is that dialects *mix freely* in one compilation unit [3]. A function may contain a `linalg.matmul` (tensor semantics), an `affine.for` nest (polyhedral-friendly loops), `memref.load/store` (explicit addressing), and `llvm.call` (foreign interface) simultaneously. Each pass then solves *the problem suited to its abstraction* — tiling at the `linalg` level where loop structure is algebraic, vectorization at the `vector` level where hardware mapping is explicit — rather than solving every problem at the lowest level. The composable code-generation flow of Vasilache et al. demonstrates this at production scale: progressive lowering through MLIR replaced Eigen-library call sites across Google-wide workloads, with each abstraction level contributing transformations that would be inexpressible or unprofitable below it [3].

Program raising is the instructive mirror image. `mlirSynth` synthesizes high-level dialect programs from low-level ones (e.g., raising LLVM-dialect loops back to `linalg`), using the dialects' declarative TableGen specifications to guide enumerative search [5]. That raising is feasible at all — and that it recovers the performance of domain-specific compilation, reporting speedups exceeding 800× on retargeted hardware — testifies to how much semantic information progressive lowering *preserves* at each stratum rather than discarding [5].

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

Because the object of study is a framework rather than a single algorithm, our "empirical" section collects the quantitative and formal claims the literature attaches to progressive lowering, with explicit provenance:

1. **Adoption velocity.** Within its first year, MLIR saw adoption across the compiler stacks of several major ML/AI programming models — evidence, cited in the CGO paper, that the dialect model reduces the cost of building domain compilers [1].
2. **Production code generation.** The composable MLIR flow replaced Eigen usage in Google-wide production cases, demonstrating that progressive lowering through `linalg`/`affine`/`vector` strata delivers production-grade performance, not merely research prototypes [3].
3. **Raising as inverse evidence.** `mlirSynth` reports that programs raised from low-level dialects to high-level ones compile with domain-specific flows to speedups of over 800× on accelerator targets (TPU-class hardware in their evaluation) [5] — a lower bound on the value of the abstraction that progressive lowering would otherwise discard.
4. **Formal classification.** The rewriting-theoretic analysis establishes termination and completeness of the conversion driver and isolates confluence as the designer's obligation, giving the first precise statement of what the framework does and does not prove [4].

We state plainly what is *not* established: there is no published end-to-end machine-checked proof that an arbitrary multi-pass MLIR lowering pipeline preserves program semantics. Verification in MLIR is *per-stratum* (each op verifies against its dialect's constraints) plus *per-contract* (the legality audit). Composition of meaning preservation across strata remains, in general, an engineering discipline supported by testing — notably MLIR's extensive `FileCheck`-based pass tests — rather than a theorem.

```tla
---- MODULE DialectConversion ----
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets
VARIABLES ir, illegalOps, journal

TypeOK == /\ ir \in [Op -> {"legal", "illegal"}]
          /\ illegalOps \subseteq DOMAIN ir
          /\ journal \in Seq([kind: {"create","erase","retarget"}])

RewriteStep ==
  /\ illegalOps /= {}
  /\ \E op \in illegalOps, pat \in ApplicablePatterns(op):
       /\ ir' = [ir EXCEPT ![op] = "legal"]
       /\ journal' = Append(journal, [kind |-> "erase"])
       /\ illegalOps' = illegalOps \ {op}
Rollback ==
  /\ journal /= <<>>
  /\ journal' = Tail(journal)
  /\ UNCHANGED <<ir, illegalOps>>
====
```

// Sketch: legality-driven lowering pass in the MLIR C++ API
void runOnOperation() override {
    ConversionTarget target(getContext());
    target.addLegalDialect<arith::ArithDialect, func::FuncDialect>();
    target.addIllegalDialect<linalg::LinalgDialect>();

    TypeConverter converter;
    converter.addConversion([](RankedTensorType t) -> Type {
        // tensor<...> crosses into the memref type domain
        return MemRefType::get(t.getShape(), t.getElementType());
    });

    linalg::populateLinalgToStandardPatterns(patterns); // illustrative
    if (failed(applyFullConversion(getOperation(), target,

}

## 6. Limitations

1. **No semantic composition theorem.** As noted, meaning preservation across a pipeline of conversions is not proven by the framework; it is an emergent property of correct patterns. A single wrong pattern silently corrupts everything below it, and the legality audit cannot detect *wrong-but-legal* rewrites.
2. **Confluence is the designer's burden.** Benefit-ordered pattern selection is deterministic but arbitrary from a semantic standpoint; two patterns that both match may produce observably different programs, and the framework offers no check [4].
3. **Type-domain crossings are lossy.** Bufferization (`tensor` → `memref`) changes value semantics to reference semantics; aliasing, deallocation, and ownership questions appear at the crossing and must be solved by dedicated analyses (e.g., bufferization's conflict analysis), not by the conversion framework itself.
4. **Phase-ordering persists.** Progressivity defers lowering but does not solve phase ordering: tiling before or after bufferization, vectorization before or after loop unrolling — these remain heuristic pipeline-design decisions, now merely expressed at higher abstraction levels.
5. **Verification is syntactic.** ODS verification checks structural constraints (operand counts, type predicates); it cannot express or check deep semantic invariants like "this tiling preserves the dependence polyhedron." The polyhedral reasoning lives in `affine` analyses, outside the verifier's reach.
6. **Compile-time cost of generality.** The conversion driver's collect-match-audit loop, dominance-ordered traversal, and journaling impose overhead relative to bespoke translators; for simple 1→1 lowerings the framework is heavier than a handwritten emitter.

## 7. Conclusion

Progressive lowering in MLIR replaces the monolithic, all-or-nothing translation between abstraction levels with a disciplined, auditable descent: dialects mix in a single IR, each conversion pass declares its legality contract, type crossings are mediated by explicit converters with reified gaps, and the rewriter's transactional semantics guarantee that a failed conversion leaves no debris. The framework's proven properties — structural termination, enforced completeness, transactional atomicity — are exactly the mechanical properties a compiler engineer needs to build lowering pipelines *fearlessly*, while the deliberately unproven properties — confluence, cross-stratum meaning preservation — mark the honest boundary where engineering judgment and testing take over. Six years after the CGO 2021 presentation [1], the evidence from production code generation [3], program raising [5], and formal analysis [4] converges: the multi-level discipline does not merely organize compilation, it makes each level's correctness arguments *composable* in a way single-level IRs never could. The open frontier is closing the semantic gap — machine-checked proofs of pattern correctness and verified pipeline composition — which would turn progressive lowering from a well-engineered discipline into a verified one.
