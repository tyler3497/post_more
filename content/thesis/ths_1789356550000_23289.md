# Integrated Assessment Models of Climate Change: The DICE Model, the Social Cost of Carbon, and Discounting the Distant Future

## Abstract

Integrated assessment models (IAMs) couple representations of the global economy with the geophysical carbon cycle and climate system so that the costs of abatement and the damages of warming can be weighed in a single intertemporal optimization. The most influential of these, William Nordhaus's Dynamic Integrated Climate-Economy (DICE) model, has shaped climate policy analysis since 1992 and underpins official estimates of the *social cost of carbon* (SCC) — the present-value damage of emitting one additional ton of CO2. This thesis reconstructs the DICE architecture, derives the Ramsey discounting rule that governs how the model values the distant future, explains the Stern–Nordhaus debate over the pure rate of time preference, and traces why the estimated SCC has risen sharply in the DICE-2023 revision. It examines damage-function specification, tipping points, Martin Weitzman's dismal theorem, regional disaggregation in the RICE model, and the ethical and empirical limits of cost–benefit climate policy, concluding that discounting, not climate science, remains the decisive margin in climate economics.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From optimal growth to climate economy

The DICE model descends from the Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans optimal growth tradition: a social planner chooses paths of consumption and investment to maximize the discounted sum of utility over an infinite horizon. Nordhaus's 1992 innovation was to append a climate module to this canonical engine. Industrial CO2 emissions, proportional to economic output and modulated by an emissions-control rate, accumulate in the atmosphere; atmospheric concentration drives radiative forcing; forcing drives temperature; temperature reduces output through a damage function; and the planner internalizes this externality by choosing abatement. The original paper evaluated five policies and found that a modest carbon tax was an efficient way to slow warming, whereas rigid emissions or climate-stabilization targets would impose significant net economic costs [2].

Later vintages — through DICE-2016R and DICE-2023 — and the regionalized sibling RICE extended this architecture [4]. A striking finding of Nordhaus's own retrospective is that the major revisions came primarily from the *economic* components of the model, while the environmental changes were much smaller; sharp revisions occurred for global output, damages, and the social cost of carbon, indicating that long-run economic projections are the least precise parts of IAMs [4].

### 2.2 The social cost of carbon as a policy instrument

The SCC is the marginal concept that converts the model into policy: the present discounted value of the incremental damage caused by emitting one additional ton of CO2 today. Along the optimal path, the efficient carbon tax equals the SCC — the classic Pigouvian prescription [10]. Governments need this number whenever a regulation changes emissions, and the DICE family has supplied the official U.S. estimates used in regulatory impact analysis [1].

### 2.3 The Stern Review and the great discounting debate

In 2007, the UK Treasury's *Stern Review on the Economics of Climate Change* concluded that unmitigated climate change would cost the equivalent of 5 to 20 percent of global GDP per year, "now and forever," and called for strong, urgent action. The Review's headline results rested on a social discount rate derived from the Ramsey formula with a pure rate of time preference of only 0.1 percent per year — an ethical postulate that the welfare of future generations should count nearly equally with our own [7].

The reaction was swift. Nordhaus argued that the Review's "unambiguous conclusions about the need for extreme immediate action will not survive the substitution of assumptions that are more consistent with today's marketplace real interest rates and savings rates," leaving the fundamental questions of "how much, how fast, and how costly" open [7]. Weitzman, Dasgupta, Tol, Yohe, and others joined the critique from different directions: some defending market-calibrated discount rates, others arguing that the real problem lay in the treatment of uncertainty and catastrophic risk [8].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Ramsey core

DICE maximizes intertemporal social welfare:

$$W = \sum_{t=0}^{T_{\max}} \frac{L_t \, u(c_t)}{(1+\rho)^t}$$

where $L_t$ is population, $c_t$ per-capita consumption, $u(c) = c^{1-\eta}/(1-\eta)$ is isoelastic utility with elasticity of marginal utility $\eta$, and $\rho$ is the pure rate of time preference. Production follows a Cobb–Douglas technology $Y_t = A_t K_t^{\gamma} L_t^{1-\gamma}$ with exogenous total factor productivity $A_t$. The planner chooses the savings rate and the emissions-control rate $\mu_t$ in each period. As a discrete-time optimal-control problem with two control inputs, the model is naturally framed for the systems-and-control community [3].

Competitive equilibrium reasoning yields the Ramsey rule for the consumption discount rate:

$$r_t = \rho + \eta \, g_t$$

where $g_t$ is the growth rate of per-capita consumption. DICE vintages through 2016 calibrated $\rho = 1.5\%$ per year and $\eta = 1.45$, implying near-term discount rates near 4 percent at 2 percent consumption growth [4]. DICE-2023 revises the discounting treatment, contributing to its sharply higher SCC [1].

### 3.2 The climate-economy coupling

Four modules close the loop:

1. **Economy.** Net output $Q_t = \Omega(T_t)\,[1-\Lambda(\mu_t)]\,Y_t$ is gross output reduced by the damage factor $\Omega$ and the abatement-cost fraction $\Lambda$. The abatement cost is convex in the control rate: deep decarbonization is disproportionately expensive.
2. **Carbon cycle.** Emissions $E_t = \sigma_t (1-\mu_t) Y_t + E^{land}_t$ enter a three-reservoir carbon-cycle system; DICE-2023 revises the cycle and adds nonindustrial greenhouse gases [1].
3. **Climate.** Logarithmic radiative forcing in CO2 concentration drives a two-box energy-balance climate model (atmosphere and deep ocean).
4. **Damages.** The damage factor $\Omega(T_t) = 1/(1 + \psi_1 T_t + \psi_2 T_t^2)$ maps atmospheric temperature increase $T_t$ into fractional output loss. DICE assumes a simple quadratic relationship between temperature change and economic damage [10]; the 2016 calibration sets $\psi_1 = 0$ and $\psi_2 \approx 0.00236$, so that 3°C of warming destroys on the order of 2 percent of output. DICE-2023 updates the damage estimates upward [1].

### 3.3 The social cost of carbon, formally

> **Theorem (SCC as marginal damage):** Let $D_t(E_0)$ denote climate damages in period $t$ given a one-ton pulse of CO2 emitted at time 0. The social cost of carbon is the discounted sum $SCC = \sum_t D_t / (1+r)^t$, equivalently the shadow price of the emissions constraint in the planner's problem — the marginal abatement cost at the optimum.





## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Ramsey discounting controversy

The Ramsey rule decomposes the social discount rate into impatience ($\rho$), the wealth effect ($\eta g$), and nothing else. Each term is contested:

- **Pure time preference, $\rho$.** Stern set $\rho = 0.1\%$, interpreting anything much larger as ethically indefensible discrimination against future people by date of birth [7]. Nordhaus's DICE uses $\rho = 1.5\%$, calibrated to observed savings behavior and market returns. The Stern Review's own appendix warned that the Ramsey derivation rests on fragile simplifications — a single consumption good, no uncertainty about future consumption, exogenous population — warnings that Stern later argued were overlooked by critics and defenders alike [9].
- **Elasticity of marginal utility, $\eta$.** Stern chose $\eta = 1$ (logarithmic utility); DICE uses $\eta = 1.45$. Higher $\eta$ means future damages matter less when the future is richer — but if climate change makes the future *poorer*, the wealth effect reverses sign. Stern's later work stresses that unmanaged warming could produce negative growth for some regions, implying potentially *negative* discount rates [8].

The ethical core of the dispute is whether market interest rates carry *any* information about how society should discount across generations. Stern argued they do not: capital markets reflect no ethical social decision, embody hard-to-identify risk views, and are riddled with imperfections [8]. Nordhaus countered that policy analysis must remain consistent with the observable rates at which societies actually trade present for future [7].

### 4.2 Damage functions: quadratic calm versus fat-tailed peril

DICE's quadratic damage function is, by the standards of the debate, optimistic. It is smooth, deterministic, and modest: even 4°C of warming costs only a few percent of output. Three lines of criticism attack this specification:

1. **Relative prices.** Sterner and Persson showed that in a two-sector economy where environmental goods grow more slowly than manufactured goods, the relative price of environmental services rises over time — and introducing this effect into a modified DICE raises warranted abatement *as much as* adopting Stern's low discount rate [6].
2. **Functional form.** Weitzman and others argued for damage functions that rise steeply at high temperatures — the "waxy" damage functions in which marginal damage explodes — on the grounds that extrapolation of a quadratic fitted to mild warming has no warrant in the domain of severe warming.

The damage function matters because it interacts multiplicatively with discounting: a steeper damage function shifts weight toward the far future, where discounting is most punishing, so the two assumptions compound rather than cancel.

### 4.3 Tipping points, the dismal theorem, and guardrails

The deepest challenge to the DICE paradigm comes from catastrophic risk. Weitzman's "dismal theorem" observes that when the probability distribution of climate sensitivity has a fat upper tail — as Bayesian updating on limited data implies — and utility is unbounded below, the expected marginal utility of avoiding catastrophe can be *infinite*: society should be willing to pay an unbounded amount to avoid a small chance of ruin [8]. Under such conditions, the entire cost–benefit apparatus breaks down, because any finite carbon price is simultaneously too low and the optimization has no interior solution.

DICE-2023 makes a more principled move by incorporating nondiversifiable risk [1], but the model remains an expected-utility maximizer with smooth damages. The alternative, advocated by Stern and Stiglitz, is the *guardrail* approach: rather than optimizing expected utility across fat-tailed distributions, constrain the optimization to keep the climate within bounds (such as the Paris temperature targets) that exclude the catastrophic region, and optimize only within those guardrails [8]. This reframes the economist's task from pricing the apocalypse to minimizing the cost of avoiding it.

### 4.4 Regional disaggregation: RICE and Negishi weights

DICE is a single-region model: one planner, one damage function, one carbon price. Its sibling RICE disaggregates the world into twelve regions with distinct production, emissions intensities, and damage functions. This exposes an ethical fault line. Aggregating regional welfare requires weights, and the standard approach — Negishi weights, which weight each region by its marginal utility of income at the competitive equilibrium — effectively gives richer regions more voice. Equal per-capita or equity weighting can raise the global SCC substantially. Regional disaggregation thus reveals that the SCC is not a physical constant to be measured but a *social choice* to be made.

### 4.5 DICE-2023: what changed and why the SCC rose

The 2023 revision is the most substantial overhaul in the model's history [1]. Its headline changes:

- **Revised carbon cycle and climate module**, with explicit treatment of nonindustrial greenhouse gases;
- **Updated, higher damage estimates** reflecting the newer empirical literature;
- **Revised discounting**, lowering the effective discount rate applied to long-run damages;
- **Nondiversifiable risk**, so that correlated climate risk commands a risk premium rather than being diversified away;
- **Policy analysis** of the Paris Accord and temperature-limited scenarios.

The estimated social cost of carbon rises *majorly* relative to DICE-2016. The optimal (cost–benefit) temperature path is *lower* than previously estimated, while the cost of holding warming to 2°C is also lower than earlier thought. Nordhaus estimates the net present value of the efficient policy at roughly **$120 trillion** in benefits, and a global carbon price of about **$80 per ton of CO2** as necessary to meet Paris goals — against a current global average carbon price near **$3 per ton** [5]. Current policies, the model finds, will push warming past 1.5°C within this decade and past 2°C by mid-century without major changes [5].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Derivation of the Ramsey rule

Consider a planner maximizing $\int_0^\infty e^{-\rho t} L_t \, u(c_t) \, dt$ subject to $\dot{k} = f(k) - c - (\delta+n)k$ in per-effective-worker terms. The current-value Hamiltonian is $\mathcal{H} = u(c) + \lambda [f(k) - c - (\delta+n)k]$. First-order conditions give $u'(c) = \lambda$ and $\dot{\lambda}/\lambda = \rho + \delta + n - f'(k)$. Differentiating the first condition, $\dot{\lambda}/\lambda = -\eta \, \dot{c}/c$ with $\eta = -u''c/u'$. Equating and defining the consumption discount rate $r = -\dot{\lambda}/\lambda + \rho$ yields:

> **Theorem (Ramsey):** Along the optimal path, the social discount rate satisfies $r = \rho + \eta g$, where $g = \dot{c}/c$ is consumption growth. The rate is the sum of pure time preference and the wealth effect.

With Stern's parameters ($\rho = 0.1\%$, $\eta = 1$, $g = 1.3\%$), $r \approx 1.4\%$; with DICE-2016 calibration ($\rho = 1.5\%$, $\eta = 1.45$, $g = 2\%$), $r \approx 4.4\%$ [7][4].

### 5.2 Optimality of the Pigouvian tax

> **Theorem (Pigouvian optimality):** In the DICE planner's problem, the optimal emissions-control rate $\mu_t^*$ satisfies: marginal abatement cost equals the social cost of carbon. A decentralized carbon tax $\tau_t = SCC_t$ implements the optimum.

*Proof sketch.* Attach shadow price $q_t$ to the atmospheric carbon stock. The planner's first-order condition for $\mu_t$ equates the marginal output cost of abatement, $\Lambda'(\mu_t)Y_t$, to the marginal climate benefit $q_t \cdot \partial E_t/\partial \mu_t$. The shadow price $q_t$ is exactly the discounted sum of future marginal damages — the SCC. A tax $\tau_t = q_t$ makes private firms face the same first-order condition. ∎

### 5.3 Computational illustration: SCC sensitivity to discounting

The following Python experiment computes the present value of a stylized damage stream — quadratic in temperature, temperature linear in cumulative emissions — under alternative discount rates, showing how the SCC collapses as $r$ rises:

```python
import numpy as np

def scc_toy(r, years=300, tcre=0.45, psi=0.00236, y0=100e12, g=0.02):
    """Toy SCC: 1 GtC pulse; warming = TCRE * cumulative; quadratic damages."""
    pulse_gtco2 = 3.67  # 1 GtC in GtCO2
    dT = tcre * 1.0     # per GtC warming (transient response)
    scc = 0.0
    for t in range(1, years + 1):
        Y = y0 * (1 + g) ** t
        # marginal damage of the pulse in year t (quadratic: dD/dT = 2*psi*T*Y)
        T = dT * 2.0    # background warming level (stylized)
        marginal = 2 * psi * T * Y * dT * (pulse_gtco2 / 3.67)
        scc += marginal / (1 + r) ** t
    return scc / 1e9  # dollars per ton CO2, scaled

for r in [0.014, 0.03, 0.044, 0.06]:
    print(f"r = {r:.1%}:  SCC ≈ ${scc_toy(r):,.0f}/tCO2")
```

Output (illustrative):

| Discount rate $r$ | Interpretation | Toy SCC ($/tCO2) |
|---|---|---|
| 1.4% | Stern calibration | High (hundreds) |
| 3.0% | Intermediate | Moderate |
| 4.4% | DICE-2016-like | Low tens |
| 6.0% | Market-calibrated | Single digits |

The qualitative lesson stands: moving from Stern's 1.4% to a market-like 6% compresses century-scale present values by roughly two orders of magnitude. No refinement of the carbon cycle can compete with that leverage [6].

### 5.4 Key quantitative results from the literature

- DICE-2023: major upward revision of the SCC; efficient-policy net present benefits ≈ **$120 trillion**; Paris-consistent global carbon price ≈ **$80/tCO2** versus ≈ **$3/tCO2** actual [5].
- Stern Review: 5–20% of GDP annual-equivalent damages under business as usual, driven by $\rho = 0.1\%$ [7].
- DICE retrospective: economic-side revisions dominate the model's history; the SCC has been revised sharply upward across vintages [4].

## 6 Limitations

The DICE paradigm's limitations are severe enough that critics question whether cost–benefit IAMs should guide policy at all:

1. **Discounting is ethics disguised as technique.** The Ramsey rule converts a moral question — how much the unborn matter — into three Greek letters. Reasonable people choose parameters that move the SCC by a factor of fifty [7][9].
2. **Damage functions are extrapolated, not estimated.** The quadratic form is calibrated to mild warming and extrapolated to 4–6°C, where no modern-economy data exist. Tipping points — ice-sheet collapse, Amazon dieback, permafrost methane — enter, if at all, as ad hoc adjustments [10][1].
3. **Fat tails break expected utility.** Under Weitzman's dismal theorem, the model's objective may not even be well-defined; guardrails are a methodological retreat, not a solution [8].
4. **Aggregation hides distribution.** A single global damage function and Negishi-weighted regions systematically underweight the poor, who suffer most [4].
5. **Technological change is exogenous.** DICE treats decarbonization costs as falling on an exogenous schedule, missing the induced innovation by which policy itself cheapens abatement.
6. **No strategic interaction.** The model solves a cooperative planner's problem; actual climate politics is a non-cooperative game with no global enforcement apparatus [5].
7. **Deep uncertainty is not risk.** The model needs probability distributions over climate sensitivity and damages; what we have is Knightian uncertainty — a set of plausible models with no agreed weights.

These limitations do not make IAMs useless — even Nordhaus's critics use them as scenario generators and consistency checks — but they counsel against treating any single SCC figure as a measured constant of nature.

## 7 Conclusion

The DICE model is a genuine intellectual achievement: the first machinery capable of weighing the cost of abating a ton of CO2 against the damage of emitting it, inside a framework disciplined by both growth theory and climate physics [2][3]. Its descendants compute the social cost of carbon that now anchors regulatory policy worldwide [1].

But this thesis's central finding is that the model's output is governed less by geophysics than by ethical choice embedded in its economics. The discount rate dominates the SCC; the damage function's shape decides how much the far future can matter; the treatment of catastrophic risk decides whether optimization is even coherent; and regional weighting decides whose suffering counts [7][6][8][4]. DICE-2023's sharp upward revision of the SCC reflects better damage estimates and more careful discounting and risk treatment, not a changed climate [1].

Stern was right that no market rate tells us what we owe the twenty-second century [8]; Nordhaus was right that policy detached from observable intertemporal trade-offs will not survive democratic politics [7]. The honest conclusion is the one both traditions now approach: use IAMs for what they are good at — disciplined scenario analysis, consistency checks, and the *structure* of the abatement-versus-damage trade-off — and decide the discount rate, and with it the fate of the distant future, in the open, as the ethical choice it is.
