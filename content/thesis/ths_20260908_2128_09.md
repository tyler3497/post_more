# Deterministic Databases: Serializability Theory, Deterministic Scheduling, and Replication Without Consensus

## Abstract

Deterministic databases reorder the foundations of distributed transaction processing: by fixing a total order of transactions *before* execution begins, they eliminate distributed commit protocols, deadlocks, and the coordination overhead that has long bounded the scalability of ACID systems. This thesis develops a unified account of the deterministic database literature, from the serializability theory of conflict graphs [5] through Calvin's sequencer-driven deterministic locking [1], Aria's deterministic optimistic concurrency control [2], and multiversion determinism in BOHM [3]. We analyze how deterministic scheduling reduces replication to the replication of transaction *inputs*, how deterministic aborts remove agreement from failure handling, and how in-network multisequencing [4] pushes ordering guarantees into the datacenter fabric itself. Reconstructing the published correctness arguments and evaluation results, we show that determinism trades batching latency and advance knowledge of read/write sets for coordination-free scalability, and we characterize performance under contention, where deterministic locking and deterministic optimistic schemes [2][6][7] diverge sharply. The thesis closes with the open problems separating deterministic prototypes from general-purpose engines.

## 1. Introduction

For four decades, the received wisdom held that strong consistency is purchased with coordination. Atomic commitment demanded two-phase commit; replica agreement demanded Paxos or Raft; isolation demanded two-phase locking with its deadlocks, or optimistic validation with its aborts. Each protocol added round trips, and round trips dominated both latency and throughput. The deterministic database program, launched by Abadi and Thomson [8] and realized in Calvin [1], rejects the premise: *if every replica agrees on the order of transactions before executing any of them, execution becomes deterministic, and almost all coordination evaporates*.

Traditional concurrency control derives an *implicit* order from the interleaving of operations — locks acquired as transactions run, timestamps assigned as they arrive. Deterministic systems invert this: they *establish* the order first, through a sequencing layer, then execute against it. Once the order is fixed, locking becomes deterministic (acquire locks in transaction order and deadlocks are impossible), aborts become deterministic (identical inputs abort identically on every replica), and replication reduces to shipping ordered transaction inputs rather than negotiating their effects.

This thesis makes three contributions. **First**, we ground deterministic systems in classical serializability theory, showing how conflict graphs and the serialization-graph theorem [5] explain both why determinism is correct and why it is restrictive. **Second**, we analyze the principal designs — Calvin's deterministic locking [1], Aria's deterministic optimistic concurrency [2], DOCC's optimistic processing atop a Calvin substrate [6], BOHM's deterministic multiversion protocol [3], and Eris's in-network multisequencing [4] — in a common vocabulary. **Third**, we synthesize the published evidence on performance under contention and identify the limitations keeping these systems from displacing general-purpose engines.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Serializability and conflict graphs

The correctness criterion for concurrency control is *serializability*: a concurrent execution is correct if its effect is equivalent to some serial execution of the same transactions. The classical formalization [5] models an execution as a *schedule* — a total order of the read and write operations of a set of transactions, preserving each transaction's internal order — and defines *conflict equivalence* between schedules.

Two operations **conflict** if they belong to different transactions, access the same data item, and at least one is a write: **write–read** (*wr*), **read–write** (*rw*), and **write–write** (*ww*). From a schedule $S$ we build the **serialization graph** $SG(S)$: nodes are committed transactions, with a directed edge $T_i \to T_j$ whenever an operation of $T_i$ precedes and conflicts with an operation of $T_j$. The central result:

> **Theorem (Serialization Graph [5]):** A schedule $S$ is *conflict-serializable* if and only if $SG(S)$ is acyclic. Any topological order of $SG(S)$ is an equivalent serial schedule.

This theorem is the hinge of the deterministic program. Conventional systems *discover* the serialization order dynamically and must detect or prevent cycles — via deadlock detection in two-phase locking or validation in optimistic concurrency control. Deterministic systems *impose* the order in advance: if every transaction holds a position in a total order before execution, and conflicting operations respect that order, then $SG(S)$ is acyclic *by construction*. No cycle detection is ever required. The price is that the order must be chosen before the conflicts are known.

### 2.2 The nondeterministic status quo

Traditional architectures layer three coordination mechanisms. **Concurrency control** (two-phase locking, timestamp ordering, optimistic validation [5]) establishes isolation. **Atomic commitment** (two-phase commit) ensures all-or-nothing execution across partitions. **Replication** (Paxos/Raft or log shipping) keeps replicas consistent. Each reacts to a *nondeterministic* execution: because replicas may interleave operations differently, they must negotiate lock grants, commit decisions, and log positions. Aborts are especially costly — a transaction aborted on one replica may commit on another, so abort handling itself requires agreement, and cascading aborts propagate through read dependencies. Multiversion concurrency control softened some costs, but snapshot isolation sacrificed serializability itself: the *write-skew* anomaly admits executions no serial order can explain, a gap BOHM [3] takes as its starting point.

### 2.3 The deterministic inversion

Abadi and Thomson [8] observe that ACID's guarantees are not too strong for scalability but *too weak*: promising only that *some* serializable order exists forces every replica to discover and agree on one. Promise a *specific, predetermined* order instead, and the coordination collapses:

1. **Deadlock becomes impossible.** Locks acquired in global transaction order yield an acyclic wait-for graph by construction.
2. **Distributed commit becomes unnecessary.** With the order fixed up front, each partition knows which transactions touch its data and in which order; multi-partition transactions execute without two-phase commit [1].
3. **Replication becomes input replication.** Replicas receiving the same ordered inputs and executing deterministically reach identical states — active state-machine replication at transaction granularity.
4. **Aborts become deterministic.** An abort that is a pure function of ordered inputs and pre-batch state occurs identically on every replica; no agreement protocol handles failure [1].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is an architectural and theoretical synthesis. **First**, we reconstruct the correctness arguments of Calvin [1], Aria [2], DOCC [6], BOHM [3], and Eris [4] in the serialization-graph vocabulary of [5], making their design choices directly comparable. **Second**, we taxonomize them along five axes: *ordering mechanism*, *read/write-set knowledge* (declared vs. discovered), *execution model* (locking, optimistic, multiversion), *abort semantics*, and *replication strategy* (input vs. effect replication). **Third**, we synthesize the published TPC-C and YCSB evaluations under low and high contention to locate where deterministic designs win and lose [1][2][4][6][7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Calvin: sequencing, deterministic locking, and the three-layer architecture

Calvin [1] is the canonical deterministic database, separating the three problems determinism decouples:

1. **Sequencing layer.** Client transactions are collected into *epochs* and appended to a sharded, totally ordered log — the only component that must achieve agreement, once per batch rather than once per transaction.
2. **Scheduling layer.** Given the ordered batch and each transaction's declared read/write set, the scheduler grants locks *deterministically*, in global order. A transaction never waits on one ordered after it, so deadlock is impossible; distributed commit is likewise eliminated, since each partition knows exactly which transactions touch its data and in which order [1].
3. **Storage layer.** A conventional engine executes the scheduled transactions against local data.

Two consequences matter. **First**, Calvin requires read/write sets *in advance*; *dependent transactions* whose access sets depend on reads use *optimistic lock location prediction* (OLLP) — a cheap reconnaissance query discovering the read/write set before sequencing [1]. **Second**, Calvin replicates *inputs, not effects*, so it can offer multiple consistency levels — including Paxos-based strong consistency across datacenters — at no cost to transactional throughput, since wide-area agreement concerns only the input log [1].

```rust
// Deterministic sequencer: order fixed here, before any execution.
struct Sequencer { epoch: u64, batch: Vec<Transaction> }
impl Sequencer {
    fn ingest(&mut self, txn: Transaction) {
        self.batch.push(txn);
        if self.batch_full() { self.seal_epoch(); }
    }
    fn seal_epoch(&mut self) {  // the ONLY agreement: append one batch
        self.replicated_log.append(LogEntry { epoch: self.epoch,
                                              txns: take(&mut self.batch) });
        self.epoch += 1;
    }
}
```

### 4.2 Deterministic optimistic concurrency: Aria, DOCC, and the hybrid turn

Calvin's advance-knowledge requirement is its deepest limitation, and the deterministic optimistic line removes it. **Aria** [2] divides time into epochs; each epoch's transactions form a batch executed in two phases. In the *execution phase*, every replica runs the batch against the *same* database snapshot, holding writes in thread-local copies. In the *commit phase*, each transaction is independently and deterministically classified as commit or abort: the system computes a deterministic serializable subset of the batch's dependency graph, committing transactions in dependency order and aborting those that would create cycles. Every replica applies the same rule to the same snapshot, so decisions agree without communication. Aria adds *deterministic reordering* to choose a commit order minimizing conflicts, and aborted transactions re-execute at the head of the next batch [2] — no declared read/write sets, no locks, and a commit check parallelized per transaction.

> **Theorem (Aria commit correctness [2], paraphrased):** For a batch $B$ executed against snapshot $S$, let $C \subseteq B$ be the deterministically chosen commit set with deterministic order $\prec$. The execution is conflict-equivalent to serial execution of $C$ in $\prec$-order followed by re-execution of $B \setminus C$.

**DOCC** [6] implements deterministic optimistic concurrency *atop a Calvin-style substrate*, adding snapshot reads and prefetching so execution overlaps scheduling. Its evaluation compares an optimized Calvin baseline against DOCC on TPC-C under low and high contention, locating where optimistic execution wins (low contention, read-heavy mixes) and where deterministic locking retains its edge. DOCC further exploits *multiversioning*: committing transactions create new versions rather than overwriting in place, so read-only transactions never block execution [6].

The **hybrid** line [7] argues the dichotomy is false: deterministic and nondeterministic concurrency control can be integrated, routing each transaction to the mechanism suiting it. Its measurements are sobering — as the fraction of *undeclared* transactions grows, pure scheduling-based determinism degrades, while optimistic deterministic schemes pay a "2PC-like" communication cost in their commit phases [7].

```python
# Deterministic commit check, Aria-style: per-transaction, no coordination.
def commit_phase(batch, snapshot):
    deps = build_dependency_graph(batch, snapshot)  # identical on all replicas
    committed, applied, aborted = [], set(), []
    for txn in deterministic_topological_order(deps):
        if all(d in applied for d in deps.predecessors(txn)):
            committed.append(txn); applied.add(txn)
        else:
            aborted.append(txn)  # re-executed in the next batch
    return committed, aborted
```

### 4.3 Replication without consensus and deterministic aborts

In a nondeterministic replicated database, every commit is a small consensus problem: replicas that interleaved operations differently must agree on the outcome. Deterministic execution dissolves this into state-machine replication at transaction granularity: agree once on the ordered inputs, and deterministic execution guarantees identical states. Calvin exploits this to replicate transaction inputs across datacenters, layering Paxos-based strong consistency over the input log without touching the transactional fast path [1].

**Eris** [4] pushes ordering into the network fabric. Its *multisequencing* primitive assigns each transaction a *multistamp* ordering it consistently across all shards it touches, implemented in an in-network sequencer. *Independent* transactions commit in a single client-to-storage round trip with no inter-shard or inter-replica coordination; general transactions decompose into a preliminary transaction gathering read dependencies and a conclusory independent transaction committing them. The authors report throughput 3.6×–35× higher and latency 72–80% lower than a conventional layered design [4].

Deterministic *aborts* complete the picture. In Calvin, an abort — e.g., from an integrity-constraint violation — is a pure function of the ordered batch and pre-batch state, hence identical on every replica: no two-phase protocol makes the abort durable [1]. Aria makes the abort set itself the deterministic output of the commit phase [2]. Contrast optimistic nondeterministic systems, where a validation failure on one replica need not occur on another, forcing abort agreement back into the protocol.

> **Theorem (Deterministic abort agreement, folk):** If the abort predicate $A(B, S)$ is a pure function of the ordered batch $B$ and pre-batch state $S$, then all correct replicas compute identical commit/abort decisions, and no agreement protocol is required for abort handling.

The corollary is that deterministic systems *fail without aborting transactions* in the coordination sense: a crashed participant's fragment can be re-executed deterministically elsewhere, because the order — the only shared state — survives the crash [8].

### 4.4 Serializability theory revisited: conflict graphs under a fixed order

In a deterministic system the serialization order is not discovered — it *is* the input order (or a deterministic function of it, as in Aria's reordering [2]). Every conflict edge points forward along a known total order, so $SG(S)$ is acyclic by construction: no cycle prevention, no deadlock detection.

**BOHM** [3] extends this idea under multiversioning. It separates *concurrency control* — assigning timestamp-ordered versions to writes in batch order — from *execution* — running transaction logic against the appropriate versions. Reads always find the version their timestamp dictates, so *reads never block writes*, yet execution remains fully serializable, unlike snapshot isolation with its write-skew anomalies [3]. The batch order is the serialization order of the classical theorem made constructive.

```haskell
-- Conflict-serializability as a pure function: the deterministic ideal.
type TxnId = Int
data Op = R TxnId String | W TxnId String deriving (Eq, Show)
conflicts :: Op -> Op -> Bool
conflicts (R t1 x) (W t2 y) = t1 /= t2 && x == y
conflicts (W t1 x) (R t2 y) = t1 /= t2 && x == y
conflicts (W t1 x) (W t2 y) = t1 /= t2 && x == y
conflicts _ _               = False
txnOf (R t _) = t; txnOf (W t _) = t
-- Under a predetermined order every edge respects the order: no cycles.
sgEdges :: [Op] -> [(TxnId, TxnId)]
sgEdges ops = [ (txnOf a, txnOf b) | (i, a) <- zip [0..] ops,
                (j, b) <- zip [0..] ops, i < j,
                conflicts a b, txnOf a /= txnOf b ]
```

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Calvin [1] | Sequencer epochs, sharded log | Declared in advance (OLLP fallback) | Deterministic locking | Deterministic (pure function of batch) | Input replication; multi-level consistency |
| Aria [2] | Epoch batches, deterministic reorder | Not required | Optimistic + deterministic commit | Deterministic commit-phase rule | Input replication, no coordination |
| DOCC [6] | Calvin-style batches | Not required | Optimistic + snapshot/prefetch, MV | Deterministic | Input replication |

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness arguments

The proofs across the family share a skeleton. **Lemma (replica convergence):** replicas beginning an epoch in identical states and applying the same ordered batch through the same deterministic transition function end the epoch in identical states — by induction on batches. **Theorem (Calvin serializability [1]):** conflicting locks are granted in global transaction order, so conflicting operations occur in global order; every serialization-graph edge points forward, and the schedule is conflict-serializable by [5]. **Theorem (Aria serializability [2]):** the commit phase outputs a set $C$ and order $\prec$ with each committed transaction's dependencies in $C$ and preceding it in $\prec$; the schedule is conflict-equivalent to serial execution in $\prec$-order. **Theorem (BOHM serializability [3]):** timestamp-ordered version creation in batch order makes each transaction observe exactly the versions its serialization position dictates.

```tla+
---- MODULE DeterministicBatch ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Sequences
CONSTANTS Txns
VARIABLES log, state, executed
Init == log = << >> /\ state = [t \in Txns |-> "init"] /\ executed = {}
SealBatch(b) == log' = Append(log, b)   \* the single agreement step
ExecuteBatch == LET b == Head(log) IN
    state' = [t \in Txns |-> ApplyDeterministic(state[t], b)]
    /\ executed' = executed \cup {b} /\ log' = Tail(log)
Next == (\/ \E b \in Seq(Txns): SealBatch(b)) \/ ExecuteBatch
====
```

### 5.2 Performance under contention

**Calvin** [1] scales near-linearly on a commodity cluster on TPC-C, including multi-partition transactions that cripple lock-based distributed designs — but hot records serialize through the deterministic lock order: head-of-line blocking is the price of deadlock-freedom. **Aria** [2] reports large margins over nondeterministic baselines and prior deterministic systems — up to a factor of two on an eight-node cluster — because optimistic execution parallelizes non-conflicting transactions within a batch, paying only when the commit phase aborts. **DOCC**'s head-to-head [6] sharpens the crossover: under *low* contention, optimistic deterministic execution dominates via intra-batch parallelism; under *high* contention, abort rates climb and Calvin-style deterministic locking — which never aborts for concurrency reasons — regains ground, while multiversioning [3][6] softens read-write conflicts for both. The **hybrid** study [7] quantifies the degradation curve: as the undeclared-transaction rate rises, pure scheduling-based determinism loses its advantage and the 2PC-like commit-phase communication of optimistic deterministic schemes becomes the binding cost. **Eris** [4] shows that when ordering is free (in-network multisequencing), even residual commit-path coordination approaches zero for independent transactions.

The unifying law: *determinism converts coordination cost into ordering cost plus abort/serialization cost*. Ordering cost is the batching latency of epochs and the sequencer's agreement step — amortized over the batch, favorable at high throughput, punishing under strict latency SLOs. Abort/serialization cost is workload-dependent: read/write-set knowledge favors Calvin [1]; conflict sparsity favors Aria [2]; versionability favors BOHM [3]; network support favors Eris [4].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Read/write-set knowledge remains the fundamental tax.** Calvin's elegance is conditional on declared access sets; dependent transactions need reconnaissance queries whose cost and staleness handling complicate the programming model [1]. Aria and DOCC remove the requirement but reintroduce aborts — deterministic, yet aborts — and their commit phases carry communication reminiscent of the two-phase commit they sought to escape [7].

**Contention is relocated, not solved.** Deterministic locking serializes hot records; deterministic optimism aborts on hot writes, and re-executing an aborted transaction at the next batch's head risks *deterministic livelock* if the conflict pattern repeats. Multiversioning [3][6] mitigates read-write contention but not write-write hotspots, the hard core of the problem.

**The programming model is restrictive.** Transactions must be single-shot stored procedures submitted in one request; interactive multi-round-trip transactions cannot be sequenced before their reads are known. This alone bars deterministic engines from general-purpose adoption.

**Latency and batching are in tension.** Epoch batching amortizes the sequencer's agreement cost but adds an epoch of latency per transaction; shrinking epochs recovers latency at the cost of agreement overhead. Eris's in-network sequencing [4] attacks this trade-off but needs datacenter network support.

**Open problems.**
- *Adaptive determinism*: hybrid controllers [7] routing each transaction to the optimal mechanism online, with provable regret bounds.
- *Deterministic HTAP*: batch determinism for mixed transactional/analytical workloads.
- *Weaker isolation, deterministically*: efficient deterministic protocols for snapshot isolation and read-committed.
- *A general theory*: current correctness arguments are per-system; a unified formalism characterizing which schedules deterministic schedulers can realize — and at what abort cost — is missing.
- *Recovery with NVM*: integrating byte-addressable persistent memory with epoch-based recovery is largely unexplored.

## 7. Conclusion

Deterministic databases are the most serious challenge to the coordination-centric orthodoxy of distributed transaction processing. Fixing the transaction order before execution let Calvin [1] eliminate distributed commit and deadlock; Aria [2] and DOCC [6] showed optimism and determinism compose; BOHM [3] proved multiversion serializability needs neither blocking reads nor bookkeeping writes; Eris [4] moved ordering into the network; and the serializability theory of [5] explains *why* it all works — a predetermined order makes the serialization graph acyclic by construction. The costs are real: batching latency, read/write-set knowledge, hot-spot serialization, and a restrictive programming model. But each generation moves the agreement boundary outward — from per-transaction to per-batch to per-network — and the residual coordination shrinks each time. Whether deterministic engines remain specialized OLTP accelerators or grow into general-purpose systems will be decided not by their correctness, which is settled, but by whether the open problems of Section 6 yield to the same ruthless simplification that created the field.
