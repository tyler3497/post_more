# Verkle Trees and Vector Commitments: Polynomial Commitments, Multiproofs, and the Road to Stateless Ethereum Validation

## Abstract

Verkle trees — a portmanteau of "vector" and "Merkle" — replace the hash-based parent-child relation of classical Merkle trees with a vector commitment at each internal node, enabling dramatically smaller witnesses for stateless validation. Introduced by John Kuszmaul and adapted to Ethereum's state by Dankrad Feist, Vitalik Buterin, and collaborators, the Verkle construction adopted for the Ethereum roadmap uses 256-ary internal nodes whose children are committed via the Kate–Zaverucha–Goldberg (KZG) polynomial commitment scheme. Because KZG opening proofs are constant-sized and additively homomorphic, the multiproof aggregation technique of Dankrad Feist and others compresses every parent–child link along every accessed path into a single fixed-size proof, shrinking typical block execution witnesses from tens of megabytes under the hexary Merkle–Patricia trie to roughly a megabyte. This thesis develops the cryptographic foundations of vector and polynomial commitments, reconstructs the Verkle tree construction and its multiproof scheme, analyzes asymptotic and measured witness sizes, and evaluates the stateless Ethereum roadmap — EIP-6800, the overlay transition of EIP-7612, and the eventual binary-tree reconsideration — concluding with the open security, engineering, and quantum-resistance problems that remain.

## 1 Introduction

The problem of stateless validation in Ethereum is, at its core, a problem of bandwidth. Every Ethereum block modifies a small fraction of a state that currently spans hundreds of millions of accounts and storage slots, and a validator that does not store the full state — a *stateless client* — must receive, alongside the block, a *witness*: the set of state elements it needs plus cryptographic proofs that they belong to the committed state. Under the existing hexary Merkle–Patricia trie (MPT), a single leaf proof requires roughly 16 siblings per level times about 6 levels, and a block touching several thousand keys produces a witness measured in tens of megabytes — far too large to propagate every 12 seconds [5]. Shrinking the witness is therefore the central prerequisite for statelessness, and it is the problem Verkle trees were invented to solve.

The crucial observation is that a hash function is a remarkably poor *vector commitment*. In a *d*-ary Merkle tree, proving one child requires revealing all *d* minus 1 siblings, so a single proof costs on the order of *(d − 1) log_d n* hashes — strictly worse than the binary case, which is why high-arity Merkle trees are never used [1]. Vector commitments break this trade-off: a commitment to a vector of length *d* can be opened at any position with a proof whose size is independent of *d*. With KZG polynomial commitments, the opening proof is a single group element [7]. Kuszmaul's Verkle trees exploit this by making every internal node a vector commitment to its children, achieving membership proofs of size proportional to *log_k n* with *k* up to the hundreds — the bandwidth reduction depends only on the branching factor, not on the tree depth [3]. Ethereum's chosen instantiation fixes the arity at 256, organizes the 32-byte key space into a 31-byte stem plus 1-byte suffix, and replaces keccak with elliptic-curve-based commitments throughout the state tree [4].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 surveys the background: vector commitments, KZG polynomial commitments, the MPT, and Kuszmaul's original construction. Section 3 describes the methodology — a theoretical reconstruction of the Verkle construction, the multiproof aggregation scheme, and the EIP-6800 state layout, cross-checked against the published specifications and benchmark literature. Section 4 contains the deep dive: the KZG commitment algebra, the Verkle tree node structure, the multiproof quotient construction, and the witness composition and verification procedure. Section 5 evaluates empirical results: asymptotic witness sizes, measured benchmarks from recent work [5], and the security proofs underlying the construction. Section 6 discusses limitations — trusted setup, quantum vulnerability, witness generation cost, and the evolving roadmap — and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Vector commitments and polynomial commitments

A vector commitment scheme allows a committer to commit to an ordered vector of messages with a single short commitment, and later to open the commitment at any individual position with a proof whose size is sublinear — ideally constant — in the vector length. Polynomial commitments are the most important class of vector commitments: to commit to the vector *(v_0, …, v_{d−1})*, the committer interpolates the unique degree-bounded polynomial *φ* with *φ(i) = v_i* and commits to *φ*; opening at position *i* then reduces to an evaluation proof for *φ(i) = v_i*. The celebrated KZG scheme of Kate, Zaverucha, and Goldberg [7] yields commitments and opening proofs each consisting of a single group element, with verification via a constant number of pairings. Its power for Verkle trees comes from two further properties: openings at multiple points can be aggregated into a single constant-size multiproof [8], and commitments are additively homomorphic, allowing provers to combine commitments algebraically.

### 2.2 Merkle trees, Patricia tries, and the witness problem

In a binary Merkle tree, an inclusion proof for a leaf among *n* leaves requires *log_2 n* sibling hashes. Ethereum's state trie, the hexary MPT, extends this with hex-nibble branching and extension nodes that compress paths, but the witness accounting is essentially the same: for each of roughly 6 levels, up to 15 siblings of 32 bytes must be supplied. With a few thousand transactions per block and several thousand state accesses, block witnesses under the MPT are estimated in the tens of megabytes [5] — a hard barrier to stateless validation. Naive high-arity Merkle trees do not help, because the *(d − 1)* sibling factor grows faster than the depth shrinks [1].

### 2.3 Kuszmaul's Verkle trees

Kuszmaul's 2018 construction [3] is deceptively simple: build a Merkle-like tree, but let each parent be a vector commitment to its children rather than a hash of them. A Verkle tree with branching factor *k* achieves *O(k n)* construction time and *O(log_k n)* membership proof size, and experiments showed that *k = 1024* gives a factor-of-10 bandwidth reduction at a construction cost of about 110 ms per leaf for a tree with 2^14 leaves [3]. The construction was originally analyzed with generic vector commitments; the Ethereum adaptation substitutes KZG commitments and adds multiproof aggregation, which pushes the witness size from logarithmic to effectively constant per additional proof.

### 2.4 The Ethereum statelessness roadmap

Vitalik Buterin's 2021 note [2] and Dankrad Feist's companion article [1] laid out how Verkle trees apply to Ethereum's state: a 256-ary tree keyed by 32-byte keys, with inner nodes as KZG commitments over their 256 children and leaf-level "extension and suffix" nodes holding up to 256 values. EIP-6800 [4] specifies the concrete state layout — unified tree keys derived via Pedersen-style elliptic-curve hashing, packed account headers, and chunkified contract code — while EIP-7612 [6] describes the transition strategy: an overlay tree that leaves the historical MPT frozen. More recent benchmarking work [5] compares Verkle witnesses against binary Merkle trees with SNARKs, framing the central engineering trade-off of the roadmap.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a theoretical and systems-level study; no new implementation or benchmark campaign was conducted. The methodology is *reconstructive and comparative*:

1. **Cryptographic reconstruction.** We restate the KZG commitment scheme [7] and the multi-point aggregation technique of Boneh, Drake, Fisch, and Gabizon [8] in notation suited to the Verkle setting, then reconstruct the Verkle node algebra (internal nodes, extension nodes, suffix trees) from the Feist and Buterin expositions [1][2] and the EIP-6800 specification [4].
2. **Witness accounting.** We derive the asymptotic witness size of Verkle multiproofs from first principles — counting commitments, openings, and the single aggregated proof — and compare against the MPT witness formula and the measured numbers reported in the benchmarking literature [5].
3. **Roadmap analysis.** We trace the EIP-6800 state layout, the EIP-7612 overlay transition, and the later pivot discussion toward binary trees, evaluating each design decision against the statelessness objective: minimizing witness size subject to prover feasibility and consensus-layer simplicity.
4. **Source verification.** Every factual claim about the construction is grounded in the primary sources — Kuszmaul's paper [3], the Feist and Buterin notes [1][2], the EIPs [4][6], the KZG and batching papers [7][8], and the Oberst benchmarking study [5].

> **Theorem:** *Security reduction sketch.* If the KZG commitment scheme is binding and the underlying vector commitment is position-binding, then the Verkle tree is a secure authenticated data structure: no probabilistic polynomial-time adversary can produce a valid multiproof for a key–value pair that was never inserted, except with negligible probability.

> **Proof:** Any forged Verkle proof contains, for each level, an opening of a commitment at a claimed child index. By the position-binding property, each such opening fixes the committed child digest; chaining from the root, the adversary must therefore open some commitment at a position whose true value differs from the claimed one, contradicting binding of KZG. The multiproof's Fiat–Shamir challenges are derived from the commitments themselves, so the standard forking argument for the KZG batching scheme [8] applies. ∎

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 KZG polynomial commitments as vector commitments

The KZG scheme [7] operates over a bilinear group setting with a structured reference string *(g, g^τ, g^{τ^2}, …, g^{τ^d})* for a secret *τ*. To commit to a polynomial *φ* of degree at most *d*, the committer computes *C = g^{φ(τ)}*; to prove that *φ(z) = y*, the prover computes the quotient polynomial *q(X) = (φ(X) − y) / (X − z)* and publishes *π = g^{q(τ)}*. Verification is a single pairing check:

```
e(C − g^y, g) = e(π, g^{τ − z})
```

For Verkle trees, the vector *(v_0, …, v_{255})* of child digests is encoded as the evaluations of *φ* at the 256-th roots of unity or at small integer points, so *C* commits to all children and an opening at index *i* costs one group element — 48 bytes on BLS12-381 — regardless of the arity. This is the precise sense in which a polynomial commitment is a *good* vector commitment where a hash is a poor one [1].

The scheme's homomorphic property is what makes aggregation possible: given commitments *C_j = g^{φ_j(τ)}*, the linear combination *∑ r_j C_j* commits to *∑ r_j φ_j*, so proofs about many polynomials can be merged into one. Boneh et al. [8] show how a single group element can serve as an opening proof for multiple polynomials evaluated at arbitrary point sets, which is the algebraic engine of the Verkle multiproof.

```rust
// Sketch: KZG opening proof verification (BLS12-381 style pseudocode)
fn verify_opening(c: G1, z: Fr, y: Fr, pi: G1, srs: &SRS) -> bool {
    // e(C - [y]g, g2) == e(pi, [tau]g2 - [z]g2)
    let lhs = pairing(&(c - srs.g1 * y), &srs.g2);
    let rhs = pairing(pi, &(srs.g2_tau - srs.g2 * z));
    lhs == rhs
}
```

### 4.2 The Verkle tree construction: stems, extension nodes, and suffix trees

Ethereum's Verkle tree [1][2][4] is keyed by 32-byte keys split into a 31-byte *stem* and a 1-byte *suffix*. The 31-byte stem defines a path through 256-ary internal nodes — each byte of the stem selects one of 256 children, so the tree has at most 31 levels but is in practice very shallow and wide. Internal nodes store a KZG commitment over the 256 child digests, where the digest of a child is a 32-byte field-element encoding of its commitment.

When many keys share a stem, the node at the end of the stem path is an *extension node* whose commitment covers up to 256 *suffix* values — one per possible suffix byte. This two-level structure (stem path, then suffix tree) means that values sharing a stem are committed together, which is exploited by EIP-6800's key layout to keep related state (an account's header, its first storage slots, its code chunks) under adjacent suffixes of the same stem [4].

Key derivation in EIP-6800 abandons keccak for tree keys and instead uses a Pedersen-style vector commitment over the address and tree index, with a domain separator, taking the first 31 bytes and appending the sub-index byte [4]. The tree key for address state is therefore:

```python
def get_tree_key(address: bytes, tree_index: int, sub_index: int) -> bytes:
    # Pedersen-style commitment: 5 scalars, domain separator 2 + 256*64
    c = pedersen_commit(
        domain=2 + 256 * 64,
        scalars=[address[0:16], address[16:32],
                 tree_index.to_bytes(16, 'little'),
                 tree_index.to_bytes(16, 'little')],
    )
    return c[0:31] + bytes([sub_index])
```

Account data is packed into 32-byte values at fixed suffixes: the header (version, balance, nonce, code size, code hash) at suffix 0, the first 64 storage slots at suffixes 64 through 127, and contract bytecode — *chunkified* into 31-byte payload chunks with a pushdata-prefix byte — at suffixes 128 and above [4].

### 4.3 Multiproof aggregation: one proof for all paths

A naive Verkle inclusion proof would contain, for each level of each accessed path, the child commitment plus a KZG opening showing it is the correct child — logarithmic in the tree size and linear in the number of accessed keys. The multiproof collapses all of this into a constant-sized proof [2].

The reduction works as follows. Every parent–child link to be proven is an equation of the form *A_i(x_i) = y_i*, where *A_i* is the polynomial committed at some node, *x_i* is the child index, and *y_i* is the child's digest. The prover forms the random linear combination

*g(X) = r_0 (A_0(X) − y_0) / (X − x_0) + r_1 (A_1(X) − y_1) / (X − x_1) + … + r_n (A_n(X) − y_n) / (X − x_n)*

with Fiat–Shamir challenges *r_i* derived from all commitments. Each quotient *(A_i(X) − y_i) / (X − x_i)* is a genuine polynomial precisely because *A_i(x_i) = y_i* — this is where the truth of every claimed link is enforced — and *g* is committed as a single KZG opening proof. The verifier checks one pairing equation against the aggregated commitment *∑ r_i C_i* and the aggregated evaluation structure. The bulk of the work is computing *g*, which requires multi-scalar multiplications linear in the total number of committed values, but the resulting proof is a single opening plus a handful of group elements — independent of the number of keys proven [2][8].

The practical consequence is striking: a block witness contains the accessed key–value pairs, the intermediate commitments along their paths (deduplicated where paths overlap), and exactly one multiproof. Witness size grows with the number of *distinct node commitments* touched, not with the tree depth, and the proof component is constant.

| Component | MPT witness (approx.) | Verkle witness (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| Per-level data | up to 15 siblings × 32 B × ~6 levels | 1 commitment (48 B) per level |
| Proof for one key | ~3 KB | ~200 B (amortized) |
| Block witness (5k keys) | tens of MB | ~0.8–1 MB |
| Proof size growth | logarithmic in state size | constant (multiproof) |

### 4.4 Witness composition and stateless verification

In the stateless model, a block proposer (or a witness-serving full node) assembles an *execution witness*: all state values the block's transactions will read or write, the intermediate Verkle node commitments needed to connect them to the pre-state root, and the multiproof.

EIP-6800's layout optimizations directly serve this flow: because an account's header, its first 64 storage slots, and its first 128 code chunks live under one stem, a typical transaction's accesses share commitments and the witness stays compact [4].

```python
def verify_block_witness(pre_root, witness, txs):
    # 1. Check the single multiproof against the pre-state root
    assert verify_multiproof(pre_root, witness.commitments,
                             witness.openings, witness.multiproof)
    # 2. Execute transactions against witnessed state only
    state = WitnessedState(pre_root, witness.values)
    post_root = execute(txs, state)
    # 3. The post-state root is recomputed from the state diff
    return post_root
```

### 4.5 Comparison with binary Merkle trees and the evolving roadmap

Verkle trees were long the consensus choice for Ethereum statelessness, but the roadmap has evolved.

EIP-7612 [6] charts the transition mechanics independent of that debate: the MPT is frozen, an overlay Verkle tree absorbs all new writes and copies of accessed state, and the historical tree is eventually migrated off-chain.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Asymptotic analysis

For a Verkle tree of arity 256 over *n* leaves, a single inclusion proof requires one commitment per level — 48 bytes each on BLS12-381 — plus an amortized share of the constant-sized multiproof. The depth is *log_256 n*, so a single-key proof is *O(log n)* group elements with a tiny constant, versus *O(log n)* hashes with a large constant for Merkle trees; crucially, the multiproof makes the *marginal* cost of each additional key nearly constant rather than logarithmic, because all parent–child equations are folded into the same aggregated quotient polynomial [2][8]. Kuszmaul's original analysis [3] gives the general trade: branching factor *k* yields *O(k n)* construction and *O(log_k n)* proof size, with the bandwidth reduction independent of depth.

### 5.2 Measured witness sizes

The benchmarking literature puts numbers on the theory. Oberst [5] assumes a worst case of 5,000 proven keys per block and reports Verkle proving and verification times on the order of seconds with proof sizes on the order of one megabyte, against MPT witnesses in the tens of megabytes. The dominant cost in multiproof generation is the multi-scalar multiplication computing the aggregated quotient polynomial *g(X)*, which is linear in the number of committed values across all touched nodes but highly parallelizable. Verification is dominated by a constant number of pairings plus linear work in the number of distinct commitments — fast enough for per-block consensus verification.

### 5.3 Security arguments

The security of the construction rests on three pillars. First, KZG binding under the *q*-strong Diffie–Hellman assumption: no adversary can open a commitment to two different values at the same point [7]. Second, the batching soundness of the multiproof: the Fiat–Shamir challenges bind the prover to all claimed equations before the quotient is formed, so a false equation makes the quotient a non-polynomial and the pairing check fail except with negligible probability [8]. Third, the tree-level reduction sketched in Section 3: position-binding at every level lifts commitment binding to authenticated-data-structure security. The trusted setup ceremony (the Ethereum KZG ceremony, shared with EIP-4844's blob commitments) ensures no single party knows the trapdoor *τ*.

## 6 Limitations

**Trusted setup and pairing assumptions.** KZG requires a structured reference string; while Ethereum's large-scale ceremony distributes trust, the assumption remains qualitatively stronger than hash-based security. More seriously, KZG relies on discrete-logarithm hardness in pairing-friendly groups and falls to quantum attacks — unlike hash-based Merkle trees. Any post-quantum migration would need FRI- or lattice-based vector commitments, which have logarithmic rather than constant openings [5].

**Prover cost and witness generation latency.** Multiproof generation requires multi-scalar multiplications over all values in every touched commitment — seconds of compute per block in current implementations [5]. Block proposers must generate witnesses within the slot time, which constrains how large the touched-state set can be and motivates the gas-schedule reforms of EIP-4762.

**Complexity of the state layout.** EIP-6800's stem/suffix packing, code chunkification, and Pedersen-based key derivation are a substantial departure from the keccak-based MPT, complicating client implementations, testing (the verkle devnets and execution-spec fixtures), and formal verification of the state transition [4][6].

**Roadmap uncertainty.** The exploration of binary trees with SNARK compression [5] means the Verkle transition, while far advanced in specification and testing, is no longer the uncontested end state. Engineering effort split across two candidate trees risks delaying statelessness itself.

## 7 Conclusion

Verkle trees resolve the fundamental tension that made stateless Ethereum validation seem out of reach: by replacing hashes with vector commitments at every internal node, they convert the *(d − 1)* sibling penalty that dooms high-arity Merkle trees into a constant-sized opening proof, and multiproof aggregation then folds every parent–child link of a block's entire access set into a single proof. The result is a reduction of block witnesses from tens of megabytes to roughly one megabyte — the difference between infeasible and deployable [1][2][5]. EIP-6800's carefully packed state layout and EIP-7612's overlay transition turn the cryptography into an executable roadmap [4][6]. The remaining challenges — trusted setup, quantum vulnerability, prover latency, and the binary-tree counterproposal — are real, but they are engineering and cryptanalytic problems within a solved architectural frame. The deeper lesson of Verkle trees generalizes beyond Ethereum: wherever bandwidth dominates and computation is cheap, vector commitments beat hashing, and the right commitment scheme can change what a protocol dares to attempt.
