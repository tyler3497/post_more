## Abstract

Noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) processors execute circuits that are too deep for their gate fidelities yet too small for fault-tolerant correction. Quantum error mitigation (QEM) — recovering accurate expectation values by classical post-processing of noisy circuit ensembles — is the pragmatic middle ground. This article develops the mathematical foundations of the three dominant QEM families: zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE), which applies Richardson extrapolation over deliberately amplified noise levels; probabilistic error cancellation (PEC), which inverts a characterized noise channel via quasi-probability decompositions; and learning- and purification-based schemes, including Clifford data regression (CDR) and virtual distillation. We prove and interpret the central negative results: the exponential sampling overhead of mitigation under local depolarizing noise, the optimality of PEC among general strategies for dephasing noise, and the exponentially tighter information-theoretic bounds showing noisy circuits collapse toward the maximally mixed state at only polyloglogarithmic depth. We survey hardware evidence from IBM processors, compare mitigation against correction on the qubit-sample complexity plane, and delineate where mitigation remains indispensable and where it must yield to fault tolerance.

## 1 Introduction

The central tragedy of the NISQ era is easy to state and hard to escape: *we possess quantum processors whose state spaces exceed classical simulation, yet whose gate errors exceed the accuracy demanded by every practical algorithm* [1]. Two-qubit gate infidelities of order $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$ compound over dozens of entangling layers, so that a 50-qubit circuit at depth 30 accumulates a total fault rate $\lambda = Mp$ — the product of gate count and per-gate error — that can exceed unity by orders of magnitude. The textbook remedy, quantum error correction (QEC), converts physical errors into logical fidelity at the price of one to two orders of magnitude more physical qubits per logical qubit, plus continuous syndrome extraction — resources absent on every existing device.

Quantum error mitigation takes the opposite bargain. Rather than correcting errors *in situ*, mitigation accepts the noisy ensemble the hardware produces and engineers an *estimator* whose expectation, over many shots and circuits, equals the noiseless observable [3]. The currency of mitigation is not qubits but **samples**: every technique trades reduced systematic bias for amplified statistical variance, and the conversion rate — the sampling overhead — is where the physics of noise, the geometry of circuits, and information theory meet.

> **Theorem: (Informal statement of the mitigation bargain).** For any error-mitigation protocol that is unbiased (or has bounded bias) under local depolarizing noise of per-layer strength $\epsilon$, the number of samples required to estimate an observable to fixed accuracy grows at least as $\left(\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\right)^{L}$ with circuit depth $L$ [4].

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the formal framework: channels, the $(Q,K)$-protocol abstraction, and the bias–variance decomposition that governs every mitigation method. Section 3 details the experimental and numerical methodology — hardware, simulators, and statistical estimators — underlying the empirical claims. Section 4 conducts a deep technical dive into ZNE, PEC, CDR and virtual distillation, and the overhead landscape. Section 5 presents empirical results and the proofs of the fundamental bounds. Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclusions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The NISQ noise model

A noisy quantum circuit is modeled as an ideal unitary evolution interleaved with a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) noise channel. For an $n$-qubit circuit of $L$ layers, each layer $U_l$ is followed by local noise, commonly the single-qubit depolarizing channel

$$\mathcal{D}_\epsilon(\rho) = (1-\epsilon)\rho + \epsilon \frac{I}{2^n},$$

or a Pauli channel $\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{p}}(\rho) = \sum_{P \in \mathcal{P}_n} p_P P \rho P$, where $\mathcal{P}_n$ is the $n$-qubit Pauli group. The **circuit fault rate** $\lambda = Mp$, with $M$ the number of noisy gates and $p$ the per-gate error, is the single dimensionless parameter that controls mitigation difficulty: when $\lambda \ll 1$, noise acts as a perturbation; when $\lambda \gg 1$, the output state is exponentially close to the maximally mixed state $I/2^n$ and no observable carries retrievable signal [4][5].

### 2.2 The general mitigation protocol

Takagi, Endo, Minagawa and Gu formalized all mitigation strategies in a single abstraction [4]. A **$(Q,K)$-mitigation protocol** performs $K$ distinct experiments; in experiment $k$, the target circuit is run $Q$ times under noise strength $\epsilon_k$ (possibly amplified deliberately), producing distorted states $\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_q(\psi)$. A classical post-processing map $A$ combines the $K$ measurement datasets into an estimate $E_A$. Two figures of merit characterize $A$:

- **Maximum bias** $b_{\max }$: the worst-case deviation of the estimate's mean from the ideal expectation value.
- **Maximum spread** $\Delta e_{\max}$: the worst-case fluctuation of the estimate, which — via standard concentration arguments — fixes the sample complexity: to achieve accuracy $\delta$ with confidence $1-\eta$, one needs $M \propto \Delta e_{\max}^2 / \delta^2$ samples.

Every mitigation method is, at bottom, a point on the bias–spread Pareto frontier. Techniques that reduce bias *increase* spread, and the central results of the field quantify this exchange with exponential sharpness.

### 2.3 Mitigation versus correction

The distinction between mitigation and correction is architectural and thermodynamic:

| Feature | Error mitigation | Error correction |
|---|---|---|
| Qubit overhead | None (same circuit) | 10–1000× physical per logical |
| Circuit depth overhead | Modest (folding, copies) | Continuous syndrome cycles |
| Bias | Reduced, rarely zero | Arbitrarily suppressible |
| Sampling cost | Exponential in $\lambda$ | Polynomial (threshold theorem) |
| Information recovered | Expectation values only | Full logical state |

Mitigation recovers *numbers*, not states; it cannot prepare a state for further quantum processing. This restriction is the price of its economy, and it is also what makes the no-go theorems of Section 5 unavoidable [4][5].

## 3 Methodology

The results surveyed here rest on three complementary methodologies, and the body of evidence is only persuasive because all three agree where their regimes overlap.

1. **Analytical lower bounds.** The $(Q,K)$-protocol framework of Takagi *et al.* [4] and the relative-entropy techniques of Quek *et al.* [5] derive sample-complexity bounds that hold for *any* protocol in the class, independent of implementation cleverness. These are information-theoretic, not algorithmic, statements.
2. **Digital simulation with calibrated noise.** The digital ZNE framework [2] was benchmarked on gate-level simulators with depolarizing and amplitude-damping channels, demonstrating 18–24× error reduction over unmitigated circuits at qubit counts beyond prior ZNE studies. CDR was validated on a 64-qubit noisy simulator and on a 16-qubit IBM device, achieving order-of-magnitude error reduction on a ground-state energy problem [6].
3. **Hardware characterization on IBM processors.** Recent shot-efficiency studies on IBM Heron-family backends measured fixed-budget mean-squared error for adaptive versus uniform ZNE shot allocation across six circuit budgets, mapped connectivity effects on two-qubit correlators, and identified finite-shot *help–harm boundaries* where Richardson extrapolation amplifies rather than reduces bias [8]. Virtual distillation was demonstrated numerically to suppress errors by multiple orders of magnitude, with the effect strengthening as system size grows [7].

Statistical protocol: for each method, $K$ noise-scaled expectation values $\langle O \rangle(r_k)$ are estimated from $N_k$ shots with variance $\sigma_k^2 = \mathrm{Var}(O)/N_k$; the mitigated estimator's variance is computed by exact error propagation through the (generally nonlinear) post-processing map, and sampling overhead is reported as the ratio of total shots to the unmitigated baseline at equal variance.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Zero-Noise Extrapolation: Richardson Extrapolation at the Quantum–Classical Boundary

ZNE is conceptually the simplest mitigation family and was introduced, alongside PEC, in the seminal 2017 work of Temme, Bravyi and Gambetta [1]. The idea is disarmingly direct: deliberately *increase* the noise of a computation, measure how the expectation value degrades, and extrapolate the trend back to the zero-noise limit.

Formally, let $\langle O \rangle(r)$ denote the noisy expectation value when the physical noise strength is scaled by a factor $r \geq 1$. For weak noise the expectation admits a perturbative expansion

$$\langle O \rangle(r) = \langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}} + \sum_{j=1}^{d} a_j r^j + O(r^{d+1}),$$

and Richardson extrapolation solves the Vandermonde system over scale factors $r_0 = 1 < r_1 < \cdots < r_d$ to cancel the first $d$ powers of the noise, improving error scaling from $O(p)$ to $O(p^{d+1})$ [1].

Two engineering questions determine whether ZNE succeeds in practice:

- **How is noise scaled digitally?** The original proposal required analog control of the noise. The *digital* ZNE framework of Giurgica-Tiron *et al.* [2] introduced **unitary folding** — replacing a gate $U$ by $U (U^\dagger U)^k$ to stretch the circuit by an odd factor — and **parameterized noise scaling**, which folds only a fraction of gates to achieve continuous scale factors. These techniques need only gate-level access and were shown to deliver 18–24× error reduction at larger qubit numbers than previously tested.
- **How is the curve extrapolated?** Linear, polynomial, exponential, and adaptive (Bayesian inference) ansätze have all been studied [2]. The choice matters enormously: exponential extrapolation is suited to global depolarizing decay, while Richardson polynomial extrapolation is optimal for the perturbative regime but fragile when data are noisy.

A recent hardware study on IBM backends injected a note of caution [8]: with fixed shot budgets, *standard ZNE increased the estimation bias relative to raw measurement* for a bias-sensitive parity observable — consistent with recently reported finite-shot help–harm boundaries for Richardson extrapolation — and adaptive shot allocation outperformed uniform allocation in only two of six budgets. ZNE is not a default; its expected error reduction must be validated against its sampling cost *on the specific device, circuit, and shot budget*.

```python
import numpy as np

def richardson_extrapolate(scale_factors, noisy_values):
    """Richardson extrapolation to the zero-noise limit.
    Fits a polynomial of degree (num_points - 1) and evaluates at r = 0.
    Returns the mitigated estimate."""
    coeffs = np.polyfit(scale_factors, noisy_values, len(scale_factors) - 1)
    zero_noise_estimate = np.polyval(coeffs, 0.0)
    return zero_noise_estimate

scales = [1.0, 2.0, 3.0]
measured = [0.812, 0.654, 0.487]   # expectation values at amplified noise
mitigated = richardson_extrapolate(scales, measured)
print("Mitigated estimate:", mitigated)
```

### 4.2 Probabilistic Error Cancellation: Inverting Noise with Quasi-Probabilities

Where ZNE is model-agnostic, **probabilistic error cancellation** is model-hungry and, in return, unbiased [1]. Suppose each noisy gate implements $\tilde{\mathcal{U}} = \mathcal{N} \circ \mathcal{U}$, where the noise channel $\mathcal{N}$ has been characterized (e.g., by gate-set tomography or Pauli-noise learning). PEC decomposes the *inverse* noise channel as a real linear combination of physically implementable operations:

$$\mathcal{N}^{-1} = \sum_i \alpha_i \mathcal{B}_i, \qquad \gamma = \sum_i |\alpha_i| \geq 1.$$

Sampling operation $\mathcal{B}_i$ with probability $|\alpha_i|/\gamma$ and weighting the outcome by $\mathrm{sgn}(\alpha_i)\gamma$ yields an *unbiased* estimator of the ideal expectation value. The price is the sampling overhead: the estimator's variance is inflated by $\gamma^2$ per gate, so for a circuit of $M$ gates with per-gate overhead $\gamma_m$,

$$C_{\text{em}} = \prod_{m=1}^{M} \gamma_m^2 \approx e^{4Mp} = e^{4\lambda}$$

under uniform Pauli noise with circuit fault rate $\lambda = Mp$ [3]. This is the canonical exponential wall of mitigation: the overhead is exponential in the *total error budget* of the circuit, not merely its depth or width.

The characterization bottleneck has driven the modern PEC literature toward scalable noise models — notably sparse Pauli–Lindblad models learned on the target processor, which enabled PEC on utility-scale IBM hardware [5, references therein]. But the exponential overhead is not an artifact of poor characterization; it is optimal.

```python
import numpy as np

def pec_sampling_overhead(per_gate_gamma, num_gates):
    """Total sampling overhead for PEC over a circuit of identical noisy gates.
    Each gate contributes gamma^2 to the variance inflation."""
    return per_gate_gamma ** (2 * num_gates)

# Depolarizing noise p = 0.01 per gate: gamma = (1 + p) / (1 - p)
p = 0.01
gamma = (1 + p) / (1 - p)
for gates in [10, 50, 100, 200]:
    print(gates, "gates -> overhead ~", f"{pec_sampling_overhead(gamma, gates):.2e}")
```

### 4.3 Learning-Based and Purification Approaches: Clifford Data Regression and Virtual Distillation

A third family sidesteps both noise amplification and noise tomography by *learning* the noise's effect from classically simulable proxy circuits. **Clifford data regression (CDR)**, introduced by Czarnik, Arrasmith, Coles and Cincio [6], exploits the Gottesman–Knill theorem: circuits composed mostly of Clifford gates are efficiently simulable classically.

1. Select a training set $\mathcal{S} = \{|\phi_i\rangle\}$ of *near-Clifford* circuits — the circuit of interest with most non-Clifford rotations replaced by their nearest Clifford approximants.
2. For each training circuit, obtain the noisy expectation $X_i^{\text{noisy}}$ from hardware and the exact value $X_i^{\text{exact}}$ from a classical simulator.
3. Fit a linear ansatz $X^{\text{exact}} \approx a_1 X^{\text{noisy}} + a_2$ by least squares.
4. Apply the fitted affine map to the noisy expectation of the circuit of interest.

CDR exactly corrects *global depolarizing* noise and achieved order-of-magnitude error reduction on a 16-qubit ground-state energy problem on an IBM device and on a 64-qubit noisy simulator [6].

**Virtual distillation** [7] takes a radically different, purification-flavored route. Given $M$ copies of the noisy output state $\rho$, entangling and jointly measuring the copies estimates expectation values with respect to the *distilled* state $\rho^M / \mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)$ — without ever preparing it. Since $\rho^M / \mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)$ converges exponentially fast in $M$ to the dominant eigenvector $|\psi_1\rangle$ of $\rho$, and the dominant eigenvector typically has high overlap with the ideal state, errors are suppressed by orders of magnitude, with the effect *strengthening* as system size grows [7]. The cost is a derangement circuit over $M$ copies ($M \times$ the qubits) and the requirement that the noiseless component dominate the noisy state — a condition that fails precisely when the circuit is deep enough that $\rho$ is nearly maximally mixed.

### 4.4 The Sampling-Overhead Landscape: Why Mitigation Cannot Scale Like Correction

The unified message of the fundamental-limits literature is that mitigation's exponential overhead is a *law*, not a temporary engineering gap.

> **Theorem: (Takagi–Endo–Minagawa–Gu depth bound [4]).** For an arbitrary $(Q,K)$-mitigation protocol with maximum bias $b_{\max}$ applied to $n$-qubit, $L$-layer circuits under local depolarizing noise,
> $$\Delta e_{\max} \;\geq\; \frac{1 - 2b_{\max}}{\sqrt{2\ln 2}\,\sqrt{nQ}\,K}\left(\frac{1}{1-\epsilon_{\min}}\right)^{L},$$
> so the sampling overhead ensuring fixed accuracy grows exponentially with circuit depth $L$.

> **Theorem: (Optimality of PEC [4]).** Among the general class of $(Q,K)$-protocols, probabilistic error cancellation attains the optimal sampling overhead for mitigating local dephasing noise on arbitrarily many qubits. No unbiased mitigation strategy for this noise model can sample more efficiently.

> **Theorem: (Exponentially tighter bounds, Quek *et al.* [5]).** Using quantum relative entropy $D(\mathcal{C}'(\rho_x)\,\|\,I/2^n)$ as the progress measure, the noisy output of a random circuit reaches constant distance from the maximally mixed state at depth scaling only as $\mathrm{poly}\log\log n$ — exponentially sooner than prior $\log n$ analyses suggested — so the sample complexity of *any* mitigation protocol is exponentially larger than previously established.

The physical intuition is information-theoretic: noise *destroys* information about the ideal computation at an exponential rate per layer, and classical post-processing cannot resurrect what no longer exists in the ensemble.

This places mitigation and correction on opposite sides of a fundamental divide. Correction pays in *space* (qubits) to achieve polynomial scaling via the threshold theorem; mitigation pays in *time* (samples) and faces an exponential wall at fault rate $\lambda \sim O(1)$.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Quantitative performance of the major families

The hardware and simulation literature converges on a consistent performance map, summarized here with representative figures:

| Method | Bias character | Sampling overhead | Demonstrated gain | Ref |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ZNE (Richardson) | $O(p^{d+1})$, model-free | $\propto \sum_k b_k^2/N_k$ via Vandermonde weights | 18–24× error reduction (sim) | [2] |
| ZNE (hardware) | Can *increase* bias at finite shots | Fixed-budget allocation problem | Help–harm boundary identified | [8] |
| PEC | Unbiased (given exact noise model) | $e^{4\lambda}$, optimal for dephasing | Utility-scale demonstrations | [4] |
| CDR | Exact for global depolarizing | Training-circuit shots only | ~10× on 16-qubit IBM device | [6] |
| Virtual distillation | Dominant-eigenvector bias | $M$-copy derangement circuits | Orders of magnitude (sim) | [7] |

### 5.2 Sketch of the depth-bound proof

We outline the argument of Takagi *et al.* [4], which is the backbone of the field's negative results. Consider two $n$-qubit input states $\rho_0, \rho_1$ whose ideal expectation values differ maximally (say, by 1) but whose noisy $L$-layer outputs satisfy $\|\mathcal{E}(\rho_0) - \mathcal{E}(\rho_1)\|_1 \leq (1-\epsilon)^L$ under local depolarizing noise — the trace distance contracts geometrically per layer. Any $(Q,K)$-protocol observing $Q$ copies across $K$ experiments must *distinguish* these two hypotheses to produce estimates with bias below $b_{\max}$; by the quantum Chernoff/Hoeffding analysis, distinguishing states at trace distance $t$ requires $\Omega(1/t^2)$ samples up to logarithmic factors. Substituting $t = (1-\epsilon)^L$ yields the exponential lower bound on the maximum spread, and hence on sample complexity.

### 5.3 The relative-entropy refinement

Quek *et al.* [5] sharpened this picture dramatically by tracking not the trace distance of specific states but the quantum relative entropy $D(\mathcal{C}'(\rho_x)\,\|\,I/2^n)$ of noisy random-circuit outputs to the maximally mixed state. Controlling this quantity for unitary two-designs under unital and non-unital noise, they showed the entropy collapses below constant order at depth $\mathrm{poly}\log\log n$ — meaning that beyond *doubly-logarithmic* depth, the noisy ensemble is information-theoretically indistinguishable from white noise for mitigation purposes.

## 6 Limitations

Honesty about mitigation's boundaries is as important as enthusiasm for its successes. The principal limitations are:

1. **Exponential sampling wall.** The bounds of [4] and [5] are unconditional within their protocol classes: no amount of algorithmic ingenuity circumvents the $e^{O(\lambda)}$ overhead. Mitigation buys constant-factor depth extensions, not asymptotic scalability.
2. **Expectation values only.** Mitigation produces classical estimates of observables; it cannot output a corrected quantum state for downstream quantum processing. Any algorithm requiring coherent intermediate states (e.g., amplitude amplification subroutines) is out of reach.
3. **Model dependence.** PEC's unbiasedness evaporates when the characterized noise model drifts — and real devices drift on hourly timescales [8]. ZNE's extrapolation ansatz is a leap of faith outside the perturbative regime; the hardware evidence shows it can actively harm bias-sensitive observables [8].
4. **Training-distribution fragility.** CDR inherits the generalization problem of all supervised learning: near-Clifford training circuits may not represent the noise response of the true, highly non-Clifford circuit of interest.
5. **Purification preconditions.** Virtual distillation requires the ideal component to dominate the noisy state and multiplies qubit requirements by $M$; both conditions break down exactly where mitigation is most needed — deep, highly noisy circuits.
6. **Finite-shot statistics.** All overhead analyses assume the asymptotic regime; at realistic shot budgets, the variance inflation of Richardson weights and quasi-probability signs can dominate, producing the help–harm boundaries documented on IBM hardware [8].

None of these limitations diminishes mitigation's role as the enabling technology of the NISQ era's most credible demonstrations; they define the envelope inside which that role is played.

## 7 Conclusion

Quantum error mitigation is best understood not as a poor man's error correction but as a distinct computational paradigm: *statistical inference over noisy quantum ensembles*. Its three great families — extrapolation, cancellation, and learning/purification — each exploit a different structure of noise: its smooth dependence on scale [1][2], its invertibility given characterization [1][4], and its learnability from classically simulable proxies or its concentration in a dominant eigenvector [6][7]. The field's deepest results are its negative ones: the exponential sampling overhead in circuit fault rate is a theorem [4], sharpened to doubly-logarithmic depth thresholds by relative-entropy methods [5], with PEC provably optimal for dephasing noise [4].

The practical synthesis is therefore clear. For variational algorithms, Trotterized dynamics, and sampling tasks at fault rates $\lambda \lesssim 1$, mitigation — ideally *layered*, combining e.g. randomized compiling with ZNE or PEC — remains the only route to credible quantum utility on existing hardware. Beyond that envelope, no post-processing can recover a signal that noise has erased, and the path forward runs through fault tolerance. Mitigation has not postponed the need for error correction; it has *quantified the price of its absence*, in samples, with mathematical precision. That quantification — the conversion of hope into a sampling budget — is the enduring scientific contribution of the NISQ mitigation program.
