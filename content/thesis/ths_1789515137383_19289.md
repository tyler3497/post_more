# High-Temperature Superconductivity: the Hubbard Model, RVB Theory, and Cuprate Phase Diagrams

## Abstract

High-temperature superconductivity in the copper oxides, discovered by Bednorz and Müller in 1986 in Ba-doped La2CuO4 with Tc near 35 K [1], remains the defining unsolved problem of strongly correlated electron physics. This thesis presents a unified exposition of the theoretical edifice built to explain it: the single-band Hubbard model and its strong-coupling reduction to the *t-J* model via the Zhang–Rice singlet construction [3]; Anderson's resonating valence bond (RVB) theory, in which doping a Mott insulator liberates preformed singlet pairs that condense into a *d*-wave superconductor [2]; and the resulting universal cuprate phase diagram spanning the antiferromagnetic Mott insulator, the pseudogap, the strange metal, and the superconducting dome. We review numerical evidence from determinant quantum Monte Carlo, density matrix renormalization group, and cluster dynamical mean-field theory [4][5][8], together with the competing stripe and charge-density-wave orders that intertwine with pairing [7]. We close by assessing the persistent quantitative gaps — the strange-metal transport, the pseudogap's ground-state identity, and the pairing glue — that keep the field open four decades on.

## 1 Introduction

No single discovery has reshaped condensed matter physics more decisively than the observation, in the spring of 1986, that a ceramic copper oxide — barium-doped lanthanum copper oxide, La2−xBaxCuO4 — could carry electrical current without resistance at a transition temperature near 35 K [1]. The prevailing phonon-mediated theory of superconductivity, the BCS theory of 1957, predicted an effective ceiling around 30 K, and the previous record holder, Nb3Ge at 23.2 K, had stood for over a decade. Within months, related cuprates shattered the new record: YBa2Cu3O7 at 93 K, above the boiling point of liquid nitrogen; Bi- and Tl-based compounds beyond 100 K; and HgBa2Ca2Cu3O8 at 135 K, a record at ambient pressure that stood for decades. Bednorz and Müller received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1987, the fastest such recognition in the prize's history.

What makes the cuprates theoretically profound is not merely the magnitude of Tc but the *normal state from which superconductivity condenses*. The parent compounds are antiferromagnetic Mott insulators: materials whose band theory predicts metallic conduction, but in which the on-site Coulomb repulsion U overwhelms the kinetic energy t and freezes the electrons into a Néel-ordered lattice of localized spins. Superconductivity emerges upon chemical doping of this Mott insulator — an arena where Fermi-liquid theory, perturbation theory, and the quasiparticle concept all break down simultaneously. The doped Mott insulator is the central object of modern strong-correlation physics [5], and its theoretical description has forced the invention of entirely new languages: resonating valence bonds, spin–charge separation, emergent gauge fields, and intertwined orders.

This thesis develops the canonical theoretical framework. **Section 2** reviews the cuprate crystal structure and the discovery, introduces the Hubbard model as the minimal Hamiltonian, and explains the Mott transition. **Section 3** derives the strong-coupling limit, the *t-J* model, and the Zhang–Rice singlet reduction of the three-band problem to a single band. **Section 4** gives a deep technical treatment of Anderson's RVB theory, the pseudogap, the strange metal, and *d*-wave pairing symmetry. **Section 5** surveys the empirical and numerical evidence — from angle-resolved photoemission to determinant quantum Monte Carlo — that constrains every candidate theory. **Section 6** confronts the limitations and open problems, and **Section 7** concludes.

> **Thesis claim:** The single-band Hubbard model, projected to the *t-J* model at strong coupling, contains the essential physics of cuprate superconductivity — antiferromagnetism, the pseudogap, *d*-wave pairing, and competing stripe order — and Anderson's RVB theory provides its most conceptually complete interpretation, even though quantitative, controlled solution of the model in two dimensions remains out of reach.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The discovery and the cuprate families

The original material, La2−xBaxCuO4, adopts the K2NiF4 (T-phase) structure: two-dimensional CuO2 sheets separated by LaO/BaO spacer layers, with copper in an elongated octahedral coordination [1]. The essential architectural motif shared by *all* cuprate families is the **CuO2 plane**. Charge reservoir layers — HgO, TlO, BiO, or the CuO chains of YBa2Cu3O7 — donate holes (or electrons) into these planes, and the superconducting transition temperature tracks the doping level of the plane with remarkable universality.

| Family | Representative compound | Max Tc (K) | Planes per unit cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| La-based | La2−xSrxCuO4 | ~38 | 1 |
| Y-based | YBa2Cu3O7−δ | ~93 | 2 |
| Bi-based | Bi2Sr2Ca2Cu3O10 | ~110 | 3 |
| Tl-based | Tl2Ba2Ca2Cu3O10 | ~125 | 3 |
| Hg-based | HgBa2Ca2Cu3O8 | ~135 | 3 |

*Table 1: Selected hole-doped cuprate families and their maximum Tc values at optimal doping.*

The empirical correlation is striking: Tc is maximized at three CuO2 planes per unit cell, and *all* families share the same qualitative temperature-versus-doping phase diagram, differing only in energy scales [6]. This universality is the strongest evidence that the physics resides in the CuO2 plane itself — and therefore in a single, material-independent model.

### 2.2 The Hubbard model

The minimal model capturing the competition between kinetic energy and Coulomb repulsion is the single-band Hubbard Hamiltonian:

```python
# The Hubbard Hamiltonian on a square lattice:
# H = -t Σ_<ij>,σ (c†_{iσ} c_{jσ} + h.c.) + U Σ_i n_{i↑} n_{i↓} - μ Σ_i n_i
#
# Parameters for cuprates (typical):
t  = 0.35   # nearest-neighbour hopping (eV)
U  = 3.5    # on-site Coulomb repulsion (eV)  ->  U/t ≈ 8–10
J  = 4*t**2/U  # superexchange scale ≈ 0.12–0.15 eV (~1300–1700 K)
```

Formally,

$$H = -t \sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma} \left(c^\dagger_{i\sigma} c_{j\sigma} + \mathrm{h.c.}\right) + U \sum_i n_{i\uparrow} n_{i\downarrow}$$

where the first term hops electrons between neighbouring sites and the second penalizes double occupation with energy U. At half filling (one electron per site) and U ≫ t, the ground state is not a metal: the charge gap U freezes charge fluctuations, producing a **Mott insulator**, while virtual hopping generates antiferromagnetic superexchange of strength J = 4t²/U between neighbouring spins, ordering the parent compound into a Néel state with staggered magnetization near 0.6 μB per copper [4]. The cuprate parent La2CuO4 is precisely such a spin-1/2 Heisenberg antiferromagnet with J ≈ 1300 K — an enormous magnetic energy scale that foreshadows the high pairing scale.

> **Theorem (Mott):** At half filling, for U exceeding a critical value of order the bandwidth, the Hubbard model has a finite charge gap and vanishing compressibility — the system is an insulator despite a half-filled band.

Doping x holes per copper site removes electrons and restores charge mobility, but each hole's motion is frustrated by the antiferromagnetic background: every hop displaces a spin and leaves a trail of misalignments. This **spin-polaron** physics, where charge motion and spin order are inextricably coupled, is the heart of the doped Mott insulator problem [5].

### 2.3 Why BCS theory fails

Conventional BCS theory rests on three pillars: a well-defined Fermi surface, weak electron–phonon coupling amenable to perturbation theory, and a retarded attractive interaction overcoming Coulomb repulsion. In the cuprates, all three collapse. The normal state above Tc at optimal doping is not a Fermi liquid — its resistivity grows *linearly* with temperature over hundreds of kelvin, defying the T² law of Fermi-liquid theory [8]. The pairing symmetry is not *s*-wave but *d*-wave, with gap nodes along the Brillouin-zone diagonals, as established by phase-sensitive SQUID experiments. And the relevant energy scale is magnetic (J ≈ 0.13 eV), an order of magnitude larger than any phonon Debye energy. A new theoretical language was required — and Anderson supplied it within a year of the discovery [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 From three bands to one: the Zhang–Rice singlet

A realistic CuO2 plane contains both copper 3dx²−y² and oxygen 2px, 2py orbitals, described by the three-band (Emery) model with parameters Δ (charge-transfer energy), tpd (Cu–O hopping), and Ud, Up (Coulomb terms). Zhang and Rice made the decisive simplification [3]: a doped hole, residing primarily on the four oxygens surrounding a copper, forms a **spin singlet** with the copper hole — the Zhang–Rice singlet — whose internal structure is frozen at low energies. The singlet then propagates as a single effective entity on the copper lattice, and the three-band model maps onto the single-band Hubbard model, and hence onto the *t-J* model.

The *t-J* Hamiltonian is the strong-coupling projection of the Hubbard model into the subspace with no double occupancy:

$$H_{t-J} = -t \sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma} \mathcal{P}\, c^\dagger_{i\sigma} c_{j\sigma}\, \mathcal{P} + J \sum_{\langle ij\rangle} \left(\mathbf{S}_i \cdot \mathbf{S}_j - \frac{n_i n_j}{4}\right)$$

where P is the Gutzwiller projector forbidding double occupation. The first term moves holes with amplitude t; the second encodes the antiferromagnetic superexchange J that survives projection. This Hamiltonian, with the constraint of no double occupancy *built in*, is the canonical starting point of cuprate theory [5].

### 3.2 Slave-particle methods and emergent gauge theory

The no-double-occupancy constraint is handled analytically by **slave-particle** representations. In the slave-boson scheme, the electron operator is factorized as c†iσ = f†iσ bi, where f† creates a neutral spin-1/2 *spinon* and b† creates a spinless charged *holon*, subject to the local constraint f†iσ fiσ + b†i bi = 1. Mean-field decoupling of this representation yields the RVB order parameters:

1. **Hopping amplitude** χij = Σσ ⟨f†iσ fjσ⟩ — spinon kinetic energy;
2. **Pairing amplitude** Δij = ⟨fi↑ fj↓ − fi↓ fj↑⟩ — the singlet RVB order parameter.

Crucially, the factorization introduces a local U(1) (indeed SU(2) at half filling) **gauge redundancy**: physical states must be gauge singlets, and fluctuations of the gauge field mediate interactions between spinons and holons. The pseudogap regime emerges naturally as a phase where the spinon pairing amplitude Δij is nonzero — singlets have formed — but the holons have not Bose-condensed, so there is no superconducting phase coherence [5]. Superconductivity arrives only when the holons condense, locking the gauge field and producing a true *d*-wave superconductor. This two-stage picture — *pairing without coherence* — is the RVB theory's signature prediction and its most contested claim.

### 3.3 Numerical methodology

Because the *t-J* and Hubbard models have no exact solution in two dimensions, the field relies on controlled numerics, each with complementary strengths and blind spots:

- **Determinant quantum Monte Carlo (DQMC):** numerically exact at finite temperature, but crippled by the fermion *sign problem* at low temperature and finite doping; restricted to T ≳ J/10 in the doped regime.
- **Density matrix renormalization group (DMRG):** essentially exact in quasi-one-dimensional geometries (ladders, cylinders); reveals stripe ground states in the Hubbard model [8].
- **Cluster dynamical mean-field theory (CDMFT) and the dynamical cluster approximation (DCA):** embed a small cluster in a self-consistent medium; the DCA provided the first unbiased numerical evidence for *d*-wave superconductivity in the Hubbard model.
- **Variational Monte Carlo with Gutzwiller-projected wavefunctions:** implements the RVB ansatz directly; energies compete with the best DMRG results.

No single method spans the full phase diagram; convergence of *all* of them on the same qualitative picture — antiferromagnetism at half filling, *d*-wave pairing upon doping, and stripe competition — is the strongest argument that the Hubbard model is the right model [4][8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Anderson's resonating valence bond theory

Anderson's 1987 proposal [2] begins with a radical claim about the *undoped* Mott insulator: its ground state is not the classical Néel antiferromagnet but a **quantum spin liquid** — a superposition of all possible coverings of the lattice by nearest-neighbour spin singlets (valence bonds), resonating quantum-mechanically between configurations. Long-range magnetic order in La2CuO4 is then a weak instability of this spin liquid, and doping the liquid liberates mobile singlet pairs: the preformed pairs of the spin liquid become the Cooper pairs of the superconductor. Superconductivity is thus *already present* in the insulator, hidden by the absence of mobile charge.

The Gutzwiller-projected BCS wavefunction makes this concrete:

$$|\Psi_{\mathrm{RVB}}\rangle = \mathcal{P}_G \, |\Psi_{\mathrm{BCS}}\rangle, \qquad \mathcal{P}_G = \prod_i (1 - n_{i\uparrow} n_{i\downarrow})$$

Projecting a *d*-wave BCS state into the no-double-occupancy subspace yields, at half filling, a state with excellent overlap with the Heisenberg ground state — and upon doping, a superconductor whose gap has *d*x²−y² symmetry, Δk = Δ0(cos kx − cos ky), with nodes along the zone diagonals [5]. The theory predicts, with no adjustable parameters, the *d*-wave symmetry confirmed a decade later by phase-sensitive experiments, and the doping dependence of the superfluid density, which vanishes linearly as x → 0 because only the doped holes carry supercurrent.

> **Theorem (RVB pairing):** In the slave-boson mean-field theory of the *t-J* model, the energetically favoured spinon pairing has *d*x²−y² symmetry; the superconducting order parameter is proportional to the hole doping x, vanishing at the Mott insulator.

The deepest consequence is **spin–charge separation**: the electron fractures into a spinon and a holon as elementary excitations. While true separation is confined to one dimension, its two-dimensional shadow dominates the underdoped phase diagram.

### 4.2 The pseudogap

Below a doping-dependent temperature T*(x), underdoped cuprates develop a suppression of low-energy spectral weight — the **pseudogap** — visible in NMR Knight shift, specific heat, ARPES, and tunnelling, with T* rising as doping falls, reaching ~300 K near the insulator [5][6]. The pseudogap has the *same d-wave momentum structure* as the superconducting gap, suggesting it is a pairing gap without phase coherence — exactly the RVB prediction.

But the pseudogap's true identity is fiercely debated. Competing proposals include:

- **Preformed pairs** (RVB/emery-Kivelson): singlets form at T*, phase coherence at Tc;
- **Competing order:** a *d*-density wave, loop-current order, or charge order that gaps the antinodal Fermi surface;
- **Mott physics:** the pseudogap as the finite-doping descendant of the Mott gap, with Fermi arcs as the surviving nodal quasiparticles.

ARPES observes truncated Fermi surfaces — **Fermi arcs** — rather than closed pockets, consistent with a nodal metal whose antinodal states are gapped. Whether the arcs are the visible half of hidden hole pockets remains one of the sharpest experimental questions in the field [6].

### 4.3 The strange metal

At and above optimal doping, the normal state exhibits resistivity linear in temperature, ρ ∝ T, from Tc up to the highest measured temperatures — sometimes over 1000 K — with a slope per CuO2 plane approaching the **Planckian bound**, where the scattering time saturates the uncertainty limit τ ≈ ħ/(kBT) [6][8]. No Fermi-liquid mechanism produces T-linear resistivity over such a range; the strange metal appears to be a quantum-critical fan emanating from a putative quantum critical point near optimal doping, where the pseudogap line T*(x) extrapolates to zero temperature.

Holographic (AdS/CFT) models and SYK-inspired theories reproduce Planckian dissipation naturally, but a derivation of the strange metal *from the Hubbard model* remains elusive — DQMC sees the linear-T regime but cannot reach low enough temperatures to identify its ground state, and DMRG is confined to geometries too narrow to host a two-dimensional metal.

### 4.4 *d*-wave pairing and the pairing glue

The superconducting order parameter transforms as *d*x²−y²: the gap changes sign under 90° rotation, with four nodes along the Brillouin-zone diagonals where gapless quasiparticles survive.

1. Phase-sensitive tricrystal SQUID experiments showing half-flux quanta;
2. ARPES mapping of the gap's momentum dependence;
3. The linear-T dependence of the penetration depth at low T, from nodal quasiparticles.

What mediates the pairing?

The dissenting view notes the ubiquitous electron–phonon couplings observed in modern ARPES — sharp mode couplings at ~40 and ~70 meV present at all dopings and temperatures — and argues the glue is composite.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The theoretical framework above is constrained by an unusually rich experimental record. The table below summarizes the key energy scales at optimal doping.

| Scale | Symbol | Value | Probe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Superexchange | J | ~0.13 eV (~1500 K) | Neutron scattering, Raman |
| Pseudogap onset | T* | ~150–300 K (underdoped) | NMR, ARPES, specific heat |
| Max Tc | Tc,max | 135 K (Hg-1223) | Resistivity, susceptibility |
| Gap maximum | Δ0 | ~30–40 meV | ARPES, tunnelling |
| Resonance mode | Er | ~5 kBTc | Inelastic neutron scattering |
| Upper critical field | Hc2 | >100 T | Transport, Nernst |

*Table 2: Characteristic energy scales of the cuprates at optimal doping.*

**Numerical results.** The past decade has seen landmark unbiased computations. A large-scale DMRG and constrained-path Monte Carlo collaboration found that the ground state of the doped two-dimensional Hubbard model exhibits **stripe order** — unidirectional charge and spin density waves — with *d*-wave superconductivity as a close competitor, nearly degenerate in energy [8]. This validates the "intertwined orders" paradigm [7]: antiferromagnetism, charge order, and superconductivity are not rival phases but different facets of a single strongly correlated state, with the balance tipped by material details.

Cluster DMFT and DCA studies find a *d*-wave superconducting dome in the Hubbard model with maximum Tc of order 0.02t–0.05t and a pseudogap regime above it with momentum-selective antinodal gapping [4]. DQMC at finite temperature confirms the linear-T resistivity and the growth of antiferromagnetic correlations upon cooling toward the dome.

**Experimental confirmations of RVB phenomenology.** (i) The *d*-wave gap symmetry, predicted by RVB theory in 1987–88 before phase-sensitive confirmation. (ii) The superfluid density's linear vanishing with underdoping, consistent with doping-controlled carrier count. (iii) Fermi arcs and the nodal–antinodal dichotomy, matching the momentum-selective destruction of quasiparticles. (iv) The neutron resonance mode, whose energy tracks the superconducting gap.

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires stating what the framework does *not* deliver.

1. **No controlled solution.** The two-dimensional Hubbard model at finite doping has no exact solution, and the sign problem blocks unbiased low-temperature simulation. Every analytic approach — slave-boson mean field, large-N expansions, DMFT — is uncontrolled in the physical regime. We do not possess a derivation of Tc from first principles with reliable error bars.
2. **The pseudogap ground state is unknown.** Whether the T = 0 endpoint of the pseudogap is a spin liquid, a competing order, or a phase-fluctuating superconductor determines the entire interpretation — and high-field experiments that kill superconductivity reveal a Fermi-liquid-like ground state with quantum oscillations, complicating every scenario [6].
3. **Material specificity.** The Hubbard model is universal, but Tc varies by a factor of four between families. Apical-oxygen distance, next-nearest-neighbour hopping t′, disorder in the charge reservoirs, and interlayer coupling all matter quantitatively — and are absent from the minimal model.
4. **The glue debate is unresolved.** Spin fluctuations explain the symmetry and scale but have not produced a quantitatively verified Tc; phonon contributions are observed but theoretically insufficient alone. A composite mechanism remains possible.
5. **Electron-doped cuprates differ.** The electron-doped side (Nd2−xCexCuO4) shows a narrower dome, more robust antiferromagnetism, and arguably *s*-wave-compatible behaviour — a particle–hole asymmetry the simplest *t-J* picture does not naturally explain.
6. **Competing orders may be essential, not incidental.** If stripes and superconductivity are truly intertwined [7], then a theory of pairing alone — RVB or otherwise — is incomplete by construction.

## 7 Conclusion

Four decades after Bednorz and Müller's ceramic shocked the world [1], high-temperature superconductivity remains unsolved in the quantitative sense — and yet the theoretical progress is immense. We possess a canonical minimal model, the Hubbard and *t-J* Hamiltonians, whose strong-coupling physics organizes the entire phenomenology: the Mott insulator and its antiferromagnetism, the Zhang–Rice singlet reduction to a single band [3], the RVB spin liquid and its doped *d*-wave superconductor [2][5], the pseudogap as pairing without coherence, the strange metal as quantum-critical dissipation, and the intertwined stripe and charge orders revealed by modern numerics [7][8]. The universal phase diagram — AF insulator, pseudogap, strange metal, superconducting dome — is reproduced in outline by every controlled numerical method brought to bear [4][6].

What is missing is control: an unbiased low-temperature solution of the doped two-dimensional Hubbard model, a definitive identification of the pseudogap's ground state, and a first-principles calculation of Tc. Whether these arrive via sign-problem-free algorithms, quantum simulation with ultracold atoms, or a conceptual breakthrough on the order of Anderson's, the cuprates will continue to serve as the sternest test of our understanding of quantum matter.
