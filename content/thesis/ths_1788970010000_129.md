# Threshold Cryptography at Scale: Distributed Key Generation, FROST, and Proactive Secret Sharing

## Abstract

Threshold cryptography distributes trust in a private key across *n* parties so that any *t* of them can jointly authorize an operation while any coalition of fewer than *t* learns nothing. This thesis surveys the three pillars of practical threshold systems at scale: (i) distributed key generation (DKG), which eliminates the trusted dealer, from Pedersen's 1991 protocol through the Gennaro–Jarecki–Krawczyk–Rabin (GJKR) fully secure construction that defeats the public-key bias attack; (ii) FROST, the Flexible Round-Optimized Schnorr Threshold signature scheme that reduces signing to two rounds with identifiable abort, now standardized as RFC 9591; and (iii) proactive secret sharing, pioneered by Herzberg, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Yung, which refreshes shares across epochs so that a *mobile* adversary who gradually compromises parties can never assemble a full quorum of stale shares. We analyze round and communication complexity, formal security reductions to the discrete logarithm problem, the ROAST wrapper that adds asynchrony and robustness for hundred-signer deployments, and the open gap in post-quantum threshold signatures.

## 1 Introduction

Every high-value private key is a single point of failure. Threshold cryptography replaces this monolith with *distributed* trust. A secret *s* is split into *n* shares *s₁, …, sₙ* such that any *t* shares reconstruct the capability — signing a message, decrypting a ciphertext — while any *t − 1* or fewer shares reveal nothing, in a precise information-theoretic or computational sense [1][7].

Three sub-problems must be solved before such systems can be deployed at scale:

1. **Key generation without a trusted dealer.** Classical Shamir secret sharing [7] assumes a dealer who knows the secret. In a threshold system, *no single party* may ever learn the full key; the key must be born distributed. This is the distributed key generation (DKG) problem, first attacked by Pedersen [3] and put on rigorous footing by Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Rabin [2].
2. **Threshold use of the key.** Once shares exist, parties must cooperate to produce signatures or decryptions whose output is indistinguishable from single-party operation. FROST [1] brought Schnorr threshold signing down to two rounds with a preprocessing optimization to a single online round, and its standardization in RFC 9591 [8] marks the technology's arrival as infrastructure.
3. **Long-term share hygiene.** Shares sitting on servers for years are vulnerable to gradual compromise. Proactive secret sharing (PSS) [4] periodically re-randomizes shares without changing the underlying secret, defeating adversaries who compromise parties *over time* rather than all at once.

This thesis develops each pillar formally, compares their complexity, presents the security theorems that justify deployment, and evaluates the empirical evidence — from Blockstream's ROAST experiments at 100 signers to FROST micro-benchmarks — that these protocols now scale.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Shamir Secret Sharing

Let $\mathbb{F}_q$ be a finite field with $q$ prime. To share a secret $s \in \mathbb{F}_q$ with threshold $t$ among $n$ parties, the dealer samples a random polynomial $f(x) = s + a_1 x + \cdots + a_{t-1} x^{t-1}$ of degree $t-1$ and gives party $i$ the share $s_i = f(i)$. Because a degree-$(t-1)$ polynomial is determined by $t$ points, any $t$ shares recover $f$ — and hence $f(0) = s$ — via Lagrange interpolation:

$$s = f(0) = \sum_{i \in S} \lambda_i \, f(i), \qquad \lambda_i = \prod_{j \in S, j \neq i} \frac{j}{j - i} \pmod q,$$

for any qualified set $S$ with $|S| = t$. Fewer than $t$ shares leave $s$ uniformly random over $\mathbb{F}_q$: the scheme is *perfect*, leaking zero information regardless of computational power [7]. The construction is, however, not *verifiable* — a malicious dealer can hand out inconsistent shares — which motivates verifiable secret sharing (VSS).

### 2.2 Feldman and Pedersen Verifiable Secret Sharing

**Feldman VSS** [6] augments Shamir sharing with public commitments $C_j = g^{a_j}$ for each coefficient, where $g$ generates a prime-order group $\mathbb{G}$. A party receiving share $s_i$ verifies

$$g^{s_i} = \prod_{j=0}^{t-1} C_j^{i^j} = g^{f(i)},$$

and broadcasts a *complaint* against a dealer whose shares fail the check; the dealer must then publicly reveal the disputed share or be disqualified. Hiding is only *computational* (it rests on the discrete logarithm assumption), but binding is perfect.

**Pedersen VSS** replaces $g^{a_j}$ with commitments $C_j = g^{a_j} h^{b_j}$ over a *second* random polynomial $b(x)$, using a generator $h$ whose discrete log relative to $g$ is unknown. The binding remains computational, but *hiding becomes information-theoretic*: the commitment reveals nothing about $a_j$ even to an unbounded adversary. This asymmetry — Pedersen VSS hides perfectly, Feldman VSS binds publicly — is exactly what the GJKR DKG exploits in §4.2.

### 2.3 Schnorr Signatures

FROST is a threshold *Schnorr* scheme, so we fix notation. Key pair $(x, Y = g^x)$; to sign message $m$, the signer samples a nonce $k$, computes $R = g^k$, $c = H(R \,\|\, Y \,\|\, m)$, and outputs $(R, z)$ with $z = k + c x$. Verification checks $g^z = R \cdot Y^c$. Security reduces tightly to the discrete logarithm problem in the random oracle model. 

### 2.4 Adversary and Network Models

We distinguish dimensions that determine which protocol is applicable:

| Dimension | Variants | Typical assumption in this thesis |
|---|---|---|
| Corruption | static (fixed before execution) vs. adaptive vs. mobile (changes over time) | static, active |
| Network | synchronous (bounded delay, broadcast) vs. asynchronous | synchronous broadcast for DKG; asynchronous for ROAST signing |
| Fault tolerance | $t < n/2$ (honest majority) vs. $t < n/3$ (asynchronous consensus) | $t < n/2$ |
| Liveness | robustness (honest parties always terminate with output) vs. identifiable abort (abort allowed, culprit named) | identifiable abort for FROST |

> **Theorem:** (folklore) No DKG can be both *robust* (always terminates) and *unbiased* against $t \ge n/3$ active adversaries in an asynchronous network without setup, by the FLP impossibility result applied to the agreement on the qualified set. All practical DKGs therefore assume synchrony or a broadcast channel.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *synthesis* rather than a single new protocol: we reconstruct the major constructions from their primary sources [1]–[8] in a unified notation, tabulate their costs, and evaluate the empirical record of deployments. Our method has three components:

1. **Formal reconstruction.** Each protocol is presented as an explicit round-by-round specification with its verification equations, so that the security arguments can be checked mechanically against the cited reductions.
2. **Complexity accounting.** We count broadcast rounds, point-to-point messages, and exponentiations per party, and relate these to published benchmarks (FROST micro-benchmarks in secp256k1 [8]; the ROAST 100-signer evaluation [6]).
3. **Comparative analysis.** Protocols are compared on four axes — round complexity, communication, assumption strength (synchrony, static vs. mobile adversary), and liveness guarantees — to derive deployment guidance.

**Notation.** $\mathbb{G}$ is a prime-order group of order $q$ with generator $g$; $H, H_1, H_2$ are hash functions modeled as random oracles; $[n] = \{1, \dots, n\}$; a $(t, n)$-threshold scheme tolerates $n - t$ missing parties and up to $t - 1$ corruptions (we follow the cryptographic convention where *t* is the reconstruction threshold).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Pedersen's DKG and the Bias Attack

Pedersen's 1991 DKG [3] runs $n$ parallel instances of Feldman VSS, one per party acting as dealer. Party $P_i$ samples $z_i \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{F}_q$, shares it via a degree-$(t-1)$ polynomial $f_i(x)$ with $f_i(0) = z_i$, broadcasts commitments $C_{i,j} = g^{a_{i,j}}$, and sends share $f_i(j)$ privately to $P_j$. Complaints and disqualifications prune misbehaving dealers, yielding a qualified set $\mathsf{QUAL}$. The joint secret is

$$x = \sum_{i \in \mathsf{QUAL}} f_i(0), \qquad x_j = \sum_{i \in \mathsf{QUAL}} f_i(j) \text{ (party } j\text{'s share)},$$

and the public key $Y = \prod_{i \in \mathsf{QUAL}} C_{i,0} = g^x$ is computable by everyone. No party ever learns $x$; the protocol is *robust* under $t < n/2$.

The flaw, identified by Gennaro et al. [2], is subtle and devastating for applications that need the key *uniformly random*: an active adversary controlling the *last* dealer to broadcast can choose its contribution $z_{\text{adv}}$ *after seeing* the honest parties' commitments, thereby *biasing the distribution* of the final public key $Y$. Feldman VSS's computational hiding is precisely what gives the rushing adversary this power: commitments leak enough for an adaptive choice.

### 4.2 GJKR: The Fully Secure DKG

Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Rabin [2] repair the bias with a two-phase design whose key insight is to exploit Pedersen VSS's *information-theoretic* hiding during the sharing phase:

- **Phase 1 (sharing).** Each party shares a random $z_i$ using *Pedersen* VSS (perfect hiding). Complaints and disqualification proceed as before, fixing $\mathsf{QUAL}$. At this point the adversary has learned *nothing* about the honest parties' contributions — the final key's distribution is already fixed and uniform, though no one knows it.
- **Phase 2 (extraction).** Each $P_i \in \mathsf{QUAL}$ now re-shares its $z_i$ using *Feldman* VSS (computationally hiding, but that no longer matters — the key distribution is already committed) and broadcasts Feldman commitments. Parties combine these into the joint commitment vector for $x$, and the public key $Y = g^x$ is published.

Because Phase 1 is perfectly hiding, a rushing adversary gains no information with which to bias its choice, and the resulting public key is *statistically* close to uniform — secure even against a computationally *unbounded* adversary [2].

The price is steep: the protocol needs up to five broadcast rounds, $O(n^3)$ communication in the worst case (every complaint triggers broadcast revelations), and $O(n)$ exponentiations per party. Later work reduced the broadcast rounds to two [2, follow-ups] and moved toward asynchronous DKG, but GJKR remains the reference construction for *provably unbiased* key generation under a static active adversary with $t < n/2$.

### 4.3 Proactive Secret Sharing: Defeating the Mobile Adversary

Static-adversary models assume the set of corrupt parties is fixed. In long-lived deployments — a certificate authority operating for a decade — an attacker can compromise parties *sequentially*: server 1 this year, server 2 next year, and so on, eventually collecting $t$ shares from *different epochs*. Herzberg, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Yung [4] introduced *proactive* secret sharing to defeat this *mobile* adversary.

Time is divided into epochs (e.g., one day). At each epoch boundary, the parties run a **share-refresh protocol** that re-randomizes every share while leaving the secret unchanged. The core trick: each party $P_i$ shares the *zero secret* via a random degree-$(t-1)$ polynomial $\delta_i(x)$ with $\delta_i(0) = 0$, sending $\delta_i(j)$ to $P_j$. Party $P_j$ updates its share as

$$s_j^{\text{new}} = s_j^{\text{old}} + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta_i(j) \pmod q.$$

Since $\sum_i \delta_i(0) = 0$, the underlying secret $s$ is unchanged, but the new shares are a *fresh, independent* $(t,n)$-sharing of $s$: shares from the previous epoch are now useless, and the mobile adversary's collection resets to zero each epoch [4]. The protocol further provides **share recovery**: a party whose share was corrupted or erased can have it reconstructed by a quorum of honest parties without exposing the secret, using the same zero-sharing machinery in reverse.

### 4.4 FROST: Two-Round Schnorr Threshold Signing

FROST [1] (Flexible Round-Optimized Schnorr Threshold signatures) assumes a $(t,n)$ key sharing already exists (e.g., from a DKG) and produces a *standard* Schnorr signature $(R, z)$ verifiable under the group key $Y$ — verifiers cannot tell threshold signing was used. Let $S \subseteq [n]$, $|S| = t$, be the signing set with Lagrange coefficients $\lambda_i$.

**Round 1 — commitment.** Each $P_i$ samples nonces $d_i, e_i \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{F}_q$, computes $(D_i, E_i) = (g^{d_i}, g^{e_i})$, and sends them to the coordinator (or broadcasts). 

**Round 2 — signing.** The coordinator forms the signer list $L = \{(i, D_i, E_i)\}_{i \in S}$ and each party computes the *binding factor*

$$\rho_i = H_1(i, m, L),$$

then the group commitment $R = \prod_{i \in S} D_i \cdot E_i^{\rho_i}$ and challenge $c = H_2(R \,\|\, Y \,\|\, m)$. Each party responds with

$$z_i = d_i + e_i \rho_i + \lambda_i \, s_i \, c,$$

and the coordinator aggregates $z = \sum_{i \in S} z_i$, outputting $(R, z)$.

Two mechanisms make FROST secure where earlier two-round Schnorr schemes failed. First, the binding factor $\rho_i$ ties each signer's nonce contribution to the *message and the full signer set*, defeating the Drijvers–et-al. concurrent-session forgery that broke naive schemes: an adversary cannot reuse a commitment $(D_i, E_i)$ across sessions because $\rho_i$ differs per session [1]. Second, each share $z_i$ is *individually verifiable*:

$$g^{z_i} = D_i \cdot E_i^{\rho_i} \cdot Y_i^{\lambda_i c},$$

where $Y_i = g^{s_i}$ is party $i$'s public share — so a malformed share identifies its culprit, giving **identifiable abort**: the protocol may abort on misbehavior, but the cheater is named and excluded from future sessions. This weaker liveness notion is what buys the round efficiency: two rounds total, or *one* online round with preprocessing. Security reduces to the discrete logarithm problem: FROST is existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack (EUF-CMA) against $t-1$ corruptions [1], and it is now standardized as RFC 9591 with ciphersuites for secp256k1, P-256, Ed25519, and ristretto255 [8].

```rust
/// Lagrange coefficient lambda_i for signer set S (0-indexed ids), mod q.
fn lagrange(i: u64, signers: &[u64], q: u128) -> u128 {
 let mut num = 1u128;
 let mut den = 1u128;
 for &j in signers {
 if j == i { continue; }
 num = num * j % q; // product of x_j
 den = den * ((j + q - i) % q) % q; // product of (x_j - x_i)
 }
 num * modinv(den, q) % q
}
```

### 4.5 ROAST: Robustness and Asynchrony at Hundred-Signer Scale

FROST's identifiable abort is not *robustness*: one disruptive signer can force an abort, and in an asynchronous network the coordinator cannot distinguish "malicious" from "slow." ROAST [5] (Robust Asynchronous Schnorr Threshold signatures) wraps any FROST-like scheme — semi-interactive, with identifiable aborts — in a coordinator-driven loop: the coordinator launches many *concurrent* signing sessions over responsive signers; each session that completes yields a signature, and each session that aborts *identifies and blacklists* at least one disruptor. Since every failed session eliminates a malicious party, after at most $n - t + 1$ sessions a session composed entirely of honest signers succeeds — guaranteed termination with a valid signature even under full asynchrony, provided at least $t$ honest signers exist.

The empirical results are striking: Blockstream's evaluation [5] ran a **67-of-100** FROST+ROAST deployment with the coordinator and signers spread across continents, and with **33 actively malicious** signers attempting to block progress (sending invalid shares or going silent), the honest 67 still produced a valid signature *within a few seconds*.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Complexity Comparison

| Protocol | Broadcast rounds | Communication | Computation / party | Liveness | Adversary |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pedersen DKG [3] | 3 | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n)$ exp. | robust | static active, $t < n/2$ |
| GJKR DKG [2] | 3–5 | $O(n^3)$ worst-case | $O(n)$ exp. | robust | static active, $t < n/2$ |
| PSS refresh [4] | 2 (+ recovery) | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n)$ exp. | robust | mobile, honest majority/epoch |
| FROST signing [1] | 2 (1 w/ preprocessing) | $O(t)$ | $O(t)$ exp. | identifiable abort | static, $t-1$ corruptions |
| ROAST wrapper [5] | $O(n)$ sessions worst-case | $O(n \cdot t)$ | $O(t)$ exp. | **robust** | static active, $\ge t$ honest |

### 5.2 Security Theorems

> **Theorem:** (GJKR [2]) Under a static active adversary corrupting $t < n/2$ parties, the GJKR DKG outputs a public key whose distribution is statistically indistinguishable from uniform over $\mathbb{G}$, and no coalition of fewer than $t$ parties learns anything about the secret key beyond the public key. This holds even against a computationally unbounded adversary.

> **Theorem:** (Komlo–Goldberg [1]) FROST is EUF-CMA secure in the random oracle model assuming the hardness of the discrete logarithm problem in $\mathbb{G}$, against any adversary corrupting fewer than $t$ signers and scheduling concurrent signing sessions arbitrarily, provided honest signers never reuse a preprocessing nonce pair across sessions.

> **Theorem:** (Ruffing et al. [5]) The ROAST wrapper around a FROST-like scheme with identifiable aborts terminates with a valid signature whenever at least $t$ signers are honest, regardless of network asynchrony and of the behavior of the remaining $n - t$ signers, within at most $n - t + 1$ signing sessions.

### 5.3 Concrete Performance

FROST's per-signer work is dominated by two exponentiations for nonce generation and $O(t)$ multiplications for share aggregation; optimized implementations using multi-scalar multiplication report single-digit-millisecond signing latency on commodity hardware for $t \le 10$ on secp256k1 [8]. The dominant cost at scale is *coordination*, not cryptography: ROAST's cross-continent 67-of-100 experiment shows total wall-clock time of a few seconds, nearly all of it network round-trips [5]. For DKG, the one-time setup cost of GJKR ($O(n^3)$ worst-case messages) remains the practical bottleneck for $n$ in the hundreds, which is why many deployments use a *trusted-dealer* or *simple* DKG for keygen and reserve the heavy machinery for signing — an explicit, documented trade-off rather than a free lunch.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Synchrony and broadcast for DKG.** Pedersen's and GJKR's DKGs assume a synchronous network with a broadcast channel — expensive to realize among mutually distrusting parties spread across the internet. Asynchronous DKG constructions exist but add substantial complexity and typically assume $t < n/3$.
2. **Static adversaries.** The headline theorems assume the corrupt set is fixed before execution. Adaptive security (corruptions during the protocol) is achievable with erasures but complicates implementations; PSS handles *mobile* adversaries only under per-epoch honest majorities plus true secure erasure — a notoriously fragile assumption on real servers.
3. **Identifiable abort is not liveness.** FROST alone lets one malicious signer deny service; ROAST repairs this but adds a coordinator, which becomes a liveness-critical (though not trust-critical) component.
4. **Key-generation trust gap.** FROST standardizes *signing*, not key generation. Pairing FROST with a biased Pedersen/Joint-Feldman DKG reintroduces the bias attack of §4.1; deployments must use GJKR-style or audited DKG ceremonies.
5. **Post-quantum gap.** All results here rest on the discrete logarithm problem. Lattice- and hash-based threshold signatures exist but are far from FROST's compactness and round efficiency; a quantum adversary breaks every scheme surveyed.

## 7 Conclusion

Threshold cryptography has crossed from theory to infrastructure. Pedersen's DKG showed that keys could be born distributed; GJKR showed they could be born *uniformly*; proactive secret sharing showed shares could *age safely* against a mobile adversary; FROST showed that threshold Schnorr signing costs only two rounds and produces signatures indistinguishable from single-party ones; and ROAST showed that a hundred signers on different continents can robustly co-sign in seconds despite a third of them being malicious. The standardization of FROST as RFC 9591 cements this stack as the reference architecture for distributed custody, threshold CAs, and multi-party wallets.

The frontier is now threefold: *asynchronous* DKG that matches GJKR's security without synchrony assumptions; *adaptive* security without heroic erasure assumptions; and *post-quantum* threshold signatures with FROST-like round complexity. Until those arrive, the disciplined combination surveyed here — unbiased DKG, proactive refresh, FROST signing, ROAST coordination — is the state of the art for keeping high-value keys alive, distributed, and unforgeable at scale.
