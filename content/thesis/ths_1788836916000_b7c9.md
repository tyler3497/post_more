# Quantum Error Mitigation for NISQ Devices: Zero-Noise Extrapolation, Probabilistic Error Cancellation with Quasi-Probability Decomposition, Clifford Data Regression, and Virtual Distillation

## Abstract

Quantum error mitigation (QEM) is the NISQ-era counterpart to fault-tolerant quantum error correction: rather than encoding logical qubits, it suppresses the *bias* that noise induces in the expectation values estimated from noisy quantum circuits, typically at the cost of increased sampling overhead or classical post-processing. This article develops the theory, overhead scaling, and experimental status of four leading mitigation protocols: **zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE)**, which fits a noise-scaling curve to measurements at amplified noise levels and extrapolates to the zero-noise limit; **probabilistic error cancellation (PEC)**, which inverts well-characterized noise channels by sampling from a quasi-probability decomposition over operations, paying a sampling cost that scales exponentially in the circuit fault rate; **Clifford data regression (CDR)**, a learning-based method that fits an error model on classically simulable Clifford circuits and applies it to the target circuit; and **virtual distillation (VD)**, a purification-based scheme that suppresses errors exponentially in the number of state copies using a shallow derangement circuit. We prove or review the central scaling results — including the fundamental exponential lower bound on sampling overhead in the circuit fault rate — analyze noise-learning protocols such as sparse Pauli–Lindblad fitting, and survey hardware demonstrations on superconducting and trapped-ion platforms, from the foundational 2017–2018 proposals to utility-scale experiments with 127 qubits. We conclude by mapping the hard limits of mitigation, and why it complements, rather than replaces, the road to fault tolerance.

## 1. Introduction

Near-term quantum processors — the so-called noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices — suffer gate errors of order $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$, limited coherence times, and measurement imperfections, yet lack the thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit that fault-tolerant quantum error correction (QEC) demands [1][5]. Since almost all NISQ algorithms (variational eigensolvers, Hamiltonian simulation, sampling tasks) ultimately report *expectation values* of observables, a pragmatic question arises: can we extract unbiased expectation values from noisy hardware without full QEC?

**Quantum error mitigation (QEM)** answers in the affirmative, trading bias for variance: it removes the *systematic* distortion of $\langle O \rangle$ at the price of more circuit executions or heavier classical post-processing [5]. Crucially, mitigation does not produce a better *state* — it produces a better *estimator*. This article surveys the four most influential mitigation families:

1. **Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE)** [1]: amplify noise by factors $\lambda \ge 1$, measure $\langle O \rangle(\lambda)$, extrapolate to $\lambda = 0$.
2. **Probabilistic error cancellation (PEC)** [1,2]: decompose the *inverse* of the noise channel into operations, sampled with quasi-probabilities; overhead $\gamma^2$ per shot.
3. **Clifford data regression (CDR)** [5]: learn a noise-to-signal map on classically simulable Clifford circuits, apply it to the target.
4. **Virtual distillation (VD)** [3,4]: purify the dominant eigenvector via multi-copy derangement circuits, suppressing errors as $\mathcal{O}(Q^n)$ with $n$ copies.

We structure the discussion around three lenses: the mathematical theory of each method, the *sampling overhead* scaling that determines feasibility at scale, and the noise-learning and experimental demonstrations that have moved these methods from theory to practice. A recurrent theme is the **fundamental exponential barrier**: QEM overhead grows exponentially in the circuit fault rate $\lambda = \sum_k p_k$ (total error probability), as formalized by information-theoretic lower bounds [6]. Mitigation extends the computational reach of NISQ devices, but it cannot repeal this scaling.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The NISQ noise model

Consider an ideal unitary circuit $U = U_d \cdots U_1$ applied to $|\psi_0\rangle$, followed by measurement of observable $O$. On real hardware, each gate layer $i$ is followed (or preceded) by a noise channel $\mathcal{E}_i$, so the executed circuit prepares the noisy state $\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}$ and the experimenter estimates $\langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{noisy}} = \mathrm{Tr}[O\,\rho_{\mathrm{noisy}}] \ne \langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{ideal}}$. Under gate-independent Markovian noise with per-gate error rate $\epsilon$, the *circuit fault rate* $\lambda = \sum_i \epsilon_i \approx d\, \epsilon$ (for depth $d$) controls how far the output distribution drifts; the probability of a fault-free execution is approximately $e^{-\lambda}$ [5].

Two distinct sources of inaccuracy afflict the estimator:

- **Bias:** $b = |\langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{noisy}} - \langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{ideal}}|$, growing monotonically with $\lambda$ (typically linearly at small $\lambda$).
- **Statistical (shot) noise:** variance $\sigma^2/N$ from $N$ finite measurement shots.

QEM attacks the bias while paying for it in sampling overhead $\Gamma \ge 1$: the mitigated estimator satisfies $|\mathbb{E}[\hat{O}_{\mathrm{em}}] - \langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{ideal}}| \approx 0$ but requires $\Gamma N$ shots for the same variance [5][6].

### 2.2 The general QEM estimator

Cai *et al.* [5] unify many protocols under a single framework. Given the input circuit $\mathbf{A}$ and a set of *response circuits* $\{\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{rsp},i}\}$ — variants of $\mathbf{A}$ obtained by adding/replacing gates, rescaling noise, or adding measurements — the mitigated expectation value is

$$
E_{\mathrm{em}}(\mathbf{A}) = f\big(\{E(\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{rsp},i})\}; \vec{\theta}\big),
$$

for a classical post-processing function $f$ with parameters $\vec{\theta}$. ZNE's response circuits are noise-amplified variants; PEC's are circuits with extra sampled gates; CDR's are classically simulable Clifford training circuits; VD's are multi-copy derangement circuits. The framework's power lies in exposing the common currency: **sampling overhead**, defined as the factor by which the variance of the mitigated estimator is inflated relative to the ideal measurement, $\Gamma = \mathrm{Var}[\hat{O}_{\mathrm{em}}]/\mathrm{Var}[\hat{O}_{\mathrm{ideal}}]$ [5].

### 2.3 Fault tolerance vs. mitigation

Fault-tolerant QEC detects and corrects errors *during* the computation using redundant encoding, suppressing logical error rates exponentially in code distance with polynomial overhead. QEM, by contrast, uses no additional qubits (mostly) and no real-time correction; it is purely an *estimation* strategy. The two are complementary: QEM can improve the performance of early fault-tolerant circuits, and every QEM method ultimately fails once $\lambda \gg 1$, whereas QEC's threshold theorem promises arbitrarily long computations [5].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Zero-noise extrapolation

ZNE, introduced by Temme, Bravyi, and Gambetta [1] (and independently in a variational context by Li and Benjamin), assumes the noisy expectation value is a smooth function of a tunable noise-scaling parameter $\lambda$:

$$
E(\lambda) = E(0) + \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k \lambda^k + \mathcal{O}(\lambda^{n+1}).
$$

**Noise amplification** (the experimental primitive) is achieved by several strategies [1,5]:

- **Gate (unitary) folding:** replace each gate $G$ by $G\,G^\dagger\,G$, scaling the noise of that layer by odd factors $1, 3, 5, \dots$ (local folding) or folding entire circuits (global folding).
- **Pulse-level stretching:** scale control-pulse durations, amplifying both coherent and incoherent errors proportionally.
- **Identity insertion:** insert $G G^\dagger$ pairs, an approach particularly suited to two-qubit gates [1].

Given measurements $\{\hat{E}(\lambda_j)\}_{j=1}^{M}$ at scaled noise levels, one fits an extrapolation model and evaluates it at $\lambda = 0$:

- **Richardson extrapolation:** exact cancellation of the first $n$ orders using the identity
  $$
  E_{\mathrm{Rich}} = \sum_{j=1}^{n+1} \gamma_j\, E(\lambda_j), \quad \sum_j \gamma_j \lambda_j^k = \delta_{k,0},\;\; k = 0, \dots, n,
  $$
  yielding bias $\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{n+1})$.
- **Linear / polynomial / exponential fits:** lower-order models trade residual bias for variance robustness when only few data points exist.

The price is variance inflation. For Richardson extrapolation with $M$ points, the sampling overhead scales as $\Gamma_{\mathrm{ZNE}} \sim \mathcal{O}(\gamma^2)$ with $\gamma = \sum_j |\gamma_j|$, growing exponentially in the number of data points and hence in $\lambda$ when deep extrapolation is needed [5].

```python
# Zero-noise extrapolation with unitary folding (pseudocode)
import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial.polynomial import polyfit

def fold(circuit, scale):            # global unitary folding
    folded = circuit.copy()
    for _ in range(int((scale - 1) // 2)):
        folded = folded + circuit.inverse() + circuit
    return folded

def zne_extrapolate(circuit, observable, scales, shots):
    E = [measure(fold(circuit, s), observable, shots) for s in scales]
    coeffs = polyfit(scales, E, deg=len(scales) - 1)   # Richardson ~ full-degree fit
    return coeffs[0]                                   # evaluate at scale 0
```

### 3.2 Probabilistic error cancellation

PEC inverts noise *probabilistically*. Suppose a noisy gate implements $\tilde{\mathcal{G}} = \mathcal{E} \circ \mathcal{G}$ with known noise channel $\mathcal{E}$. If the *inverse* channel admits a decomposition into implementable operations $\{\mathcal{B}_i\}$,

$$
\mathcal{E}^{-1} = \sum_i \eta_i \mathcal{B}_i, \qquad \gamma = \sum_i |\eta_i| \ge 1,
$$

then $\mathcal{E}^{-1}$ — unphysical as a channel — can be realized *in expectation* by sampling $\mathcal{B}_i$ with probability $p_i = |\eta_i|/\gamma$ and weighting the measurement outcome by $\gamma \cdot \mathrm{sgn}(\eta_i)$ [1]. This is a **quasi-probability decomposition**: negative weights are allowed, and $\gamma$, the *one-norm* of the quasi-probability, is the per-gate sampling overhead. The variance of the PEC estimator is inflated by $\gamma^2$, and for a circuit of $G$ noisy gates the total overhead compounds multiplicatively:

$$
\Gamma_{\mathrm{PEC}} = \prod_{k=1}^{G} \gamma_k^{\,2} \approx e^{2\sum_k (\gamma_k - 1)}.
$$

Since $\gamma_k - 1 \approx c\,p_k$ for gate error $p_k$, PEC overhead scales as $e^{\mathcal{O}(\lambda)}$ with the circuit fault rate $\lambda = \sum_k p_k$ [5][6]. PEC is unbiased *if the noise model is exact*; model mismatch introduces bias, making **noise learning** (Section 4.4) the practical bottleneck.

### 3.3 Clifford data regression

CDR is a *learning-based* protocol [5]: it exploits the Gottesman–Knill theorem by generating a training set of near-Clifford circuits — variants of the target circuit in which most non-Clifford gates are replaced by Clifford gates — whose ideal expectation values $x_i^{\mathrm{exact}}$ are classically computable and whose noisy values $x_i^{\mathrm{noisy}}$ are measured on hardware. A simple ansatz, typically affine,

$$
x^{\mathrm{exact}} = a\, x^{\mathrm{noisy}} + b,
$$

is fit by least squares to the training data and then applied to the target circuit's noisy expectation value. Variants use multiple ansatz features (e.g., adding a constant-response circuit) or nonlinear models. CDR requires no explicit noise model and is robust to certain model violations, but its accuracy depends on how well the near-Clifford training circuits probe the same noise regime as the target [5].

### 3.4 Virtual distillation

Virtual distillation (VD), proposed independently by Huggins *et al.* [3] and Koczor [4] (as "error suppression by derangements"), is a *purification-based* method. Given $n$ copies of the noisy state $\rho$ with spectral decomposition $\rho = \sum_k \lambda_k |\psi_k\rangle\langle\psi_k|$ ($\lambda_0 > \lambda_1 \ge \cdots$), the mitigated estimator is

$$
\langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{VD}}^{(n)} = \frac{\mathrm{Tr}[\rho^n O]}{\mathrm{Tr}[\rho^n]}.
$$

Because $\rho^n = \sum_k \lambda_k^n |\psi_k\rangle\langle\psi_k|$, the weight of the dominant eigenvector grows as $(\lambda_0/\lambda_k)^n$ relative to sub-dominant ones — errors are suppressed **exponentially in the number of copies** $n$ [3,4]. The ratio is estimated without physically preparing $\rho^n$ by a shallow circuit implementing a *derangement* $D_n$ (a permutation with no fixed points, generalizing the SWAP test):

$$
\frac{\mathrm{Tr}[\rho^n O]}{\mathrm{Tr}[\rho^n]} = \frac{\mathrm{Tr}[D_n (\rho^{\otimes n} O)]}{\mathrm{Tr}[D_n \rho^{\otimes n}]},
$$

where the numerator uses a controlled derangement with the observable measured on one copy. For $n = 2$, the circuit is a transversal Bell-basis measurement — dramatically simpler than the $n > 2$ case [3].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Sampling-overhead scaling and fundamental limits

The single most important quantitative fact about QEM is the **exponential scaling of sampling overhead** in the circuit fault rate. Takagi *et al.* [6] derived information-theoretic lower bounds: any unbiased QEM protocol achieving accuracy $\delta$ for circuits with fault rate $\lambda$ must incur sampling overhead

$$
\Gamma \ge e^{2\lambda}\, /\, \mathrm{poly}(1/\delta),
$$

up to constants, and moreover the *maximum* circuit fault rate that can be mitigated with sub-exponential resources satisfies $\lambda = \mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta))$. Concretely:

| Method | Sampling overhead $\Gamma$ | Bias | Requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| ZNE (Richardson, $n$ orders) | $\exp(\mathcal{O}(n))$ effective | $\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{n+1})$ | Small $\lambda$; smooth $E(\lambda)$ |
| PEC | $\exp(2c\lambda)$, $c \ge 1$ model-dependent | 0 (exact model) | Accurate noise model |
| CDR | $\mathcal{O}(1)$ extra shots for training | Model-fit residual | Representative training set |
| VD ($n$ copies) | $\mathcal{O}(1/\mathrm{Tr}[\rho^n]^2)$ variance cost | $\mathcal{O}((\lambda_1/\lambda_0)^n)$ | $\lambda_0$ dominant; $n$-copy circuit |

*Table 1: Asymptotic comparison of the four mitigation families. $\lambda$ is the circuit fault rate.*

The bound is not merely academic: it explains why every successful QEM demonstration to date operates at modest fault rates ($\lambda \lesssim 1$–$5$) and why QEM is an *enabler of early utility*, not a substitute for fault tolerance [5][6].

> **Theorem (Takagi *et al.* [6], informal):** For the task of estimating $\langle O \rangle$ to accuracy $\delta$ under unknown local noise of total strength $\lambda$, the sampling overhead of any unbiased mitigation protocol is lower-bounded by $\Omega(e^{2\lambda})$. Mitigation is thus asymptotically efficient only for circuits with $\lambda = \mathcal{O}(1)$.

### 4.2 Richardson extrapolation: bias–variance calculus

For ZNE with $M$ noise levels $\{\lambda_j\}$, the Richardson coefficients $\gamma_j$ satisfy the Vandermonde system $\sum_j \gamma_j \lambda_j^k = \delta_{k0}$. With equally spaced levels $\lambda_j = 1, 2, \dots, M$, one finds $\sum_j |\gamma_j| \sim e^{\mathcal{O}(M)}$; the variance of the extrapolated estimator is $\Gamma = \sum_j \gamma_j^2$ times the single-point variance. Hence increasing the extrapolation order *reduces* bias as $\lambda^{n+1}$ but *inflates* variance exponentially in $n$ — a bias–variance trade-off controlled by the available shot budget.

### 4.3 Quasi-probability decomposition in detail

The core PEC identity for a Pauli noise channel $\mathcal{E}(\rho) = \sum_P q_P P\rho P$ is particularly clean: its inverse decomposes as $\mathcal{E}^{-1}(\rho) = \sum_P \eta_P P\rho P$ with $\gamma = \sum_P |\eta_P| = \prod_P (2q_P - 1)^{-1}$ for the relevant support. After **Pauli twirling** (randomized compiling), arbitrary Markovian gate noise can be *tailored* into stochastic Pauli noise, making this decomposition applicable to realistic devices [2]. The full-circuit estimator is a Monte Carlo average over $N$ randomly sampled circuit instances:

$$
\hat{O}_{\mathrm{PEC}} = \frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}}{N} \sum_{s=1}^{N} \sigma_s\, o_s, \qquad \Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}} = \prod_k \gamma_k,
$$

where $\sigma_s = \pm 1$ is the sign of the sampled quasi-probability branch and $o_s$ the measured eigenvalue. The estimator is unbiased; its standard deviation is $\Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}$ times the shot-noise floor — so halving the per-gate error (or the number of gates) *squares* the affordable circuit volume, the exponential leverage that makes noise learning so valuable [2,5].

### 4.4 Noise learning: the sparse Pauli–Lindblad model

PEC's Achilles' heel is model accuracy. Full process tomography scales exponentially; the breakthrough of van den Berg *et al.* [2] is a **sparse Pauli–Lindblad model** that captures correlated and crosstalk errors with $\mathcal{O}(n)$ parameters on an $n$-qubit device. The model writes each noisy gate layer as $\tilde{\mathcal{U}} = \mathcal{E} \circ \mathcal{U}$ with

$$
\mathcal{E}(\rho) = \exp\!\Big(\sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} \lambda_k (P_k \rho P_k - \rho)\Big),
$$

where $\mathcal{K}$ contains all weight-1 Paulis and weight-2 Paulis on connected qubit pairs — a *sparse* subset of the $4^n$ possible generators. The rates $\{\lambda_k\}$ are fit by nonnegative least squares to Pauli fidelities measured via cycle benchmarking at multiple depths, and the model inverts *in closed form*: because the Pauli generators commute, each Pauli channel inverts independently and the total quasi-probability norm factorizes as

$$
\gamma = \prod_{k \in \mathcal{K}} e^{2\lambda_k} = e^{2\sum_k \lambda_k}.
$$

This protocol enabled the first PEC demonstration at utility scale — 127-qubit circuits with crosstalk — on superconducting hardware [2]. ### 4.5 Multi-copy purification: VD circuits and the derangement primitive

For $n = 2$ copies, virtual distillation reduces to a **destructive SWAP test**: prepare $\rho \otimes \rho$, apply transversal CNOTs and Hadamards (diagonalizing SWAP into the Bell basis), and post-process. The derangement $D_n$ for general $n$ is a cyclic shift of the copies with no fixed points; Koczor [4] showed it can be implemented with a shallow circuit of depth $\mathcal{O}(1)$ per copy, The residual bias after $n$-copy VD is governed by the **coherent mismatch**: if the dominant eigenvector $|\psi_0\rangle$ of $\rho$ differs from the ideal state $|\psi_{\mathrm{ideal}}\rangle$ by angle $\theta$, no amount of distillation removes the resulting bias $\sim \sin^2\theta$; VD suppresses only the *incoherent* error weight [4]. Dual-state purification [5] later reduced the qubit overhead of VD by recycling a single ancilla register.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Foundational demonstrations

- **ZNE on superconducting qubits (2017–2019):** Following the Temme–Bravyi–Gambetta proposal [1], Kandala *et al.* demonstrated ZNE-enhanced variational eigensolvers, showing that extrapolation extends the *computational reach* — the largest simulable molecule — on noisy superconducting processors (Nature 2019). - **PEC on trapped ions (2020):** Zhang *et al.* implemented PEC for single- and two-qubit gates in a trapped-ion system, achieving **error-mitigated gate fidelities exceeding the physical fidelities** of the underlying operations (Nat. Commun. 11, 587 (2020)) — a landmark proof that probabilistic inversion works on real hardware with learned noise models.
- **VD/ESD numerical validation (2021):** Koczor [4] demonstrated error suppression below $10^{-6}$ for circuits of several hundred noisy gates (2-qubit error $0.5\%$) using at most $n = 4$ copies in simulation, and Huggins *et al.* [3] showed comparable suppression on Heisenberg-model ground-state estimation.

### 5.2 Utility-scale experiments

The modern era of QEM is defined by experiments at 100+ qubits where classical verification is still possible via tensor networks or Clifford-adjacent methods:

- **127-qubit PEC with sparse Pauli–Lindblad models [2]:** van den Berg *et al.* learned crosstalk-inclusive noise models on an IBM superconducting processor and applied PEC to kicked-Ising dynamics, recovering bias-free magnetization estimates at circuit volumes far beyond what unmitigated hardware could deliver. The measured sampling overhead tracked the model's predicted $\gamma = e^{2\sum_k \lambda_k}$ closely, validating the theory end-to-end.
- **127-qubit ZNE for quantum utility [5]:** Kim *et al.* (Nature 618, 500 (2023)) used ZNE with noise-amplified Trotter circuits to estimate observables of 2D spin models at volumes where brute-force classical simulation fails, with matrix-product-state verification — widely cited as the first *evidence for utility* of pre-fault-tolerant quantum computing.

### 5.3 Overhead measurements vs. theory

Across these experiments, the measured sampling overhead consistently follows the exponential-in-$\lambda$ prediction: doubling the circuit depth at fixed gate error roughly *squares* the required shots for PEC and ZNE alike, while CDR's overhead stays near-constant (it pays in training-circuit count and model bias instead) and VD's overhead is dominated by the purity $\mathrm{Tr}[\rho^2]$ — which itself decays as $e^{-\mathcal{O}(\lambda)}$ [3,5,6]. This convergence of theory and experiment is the empirical content of the fundamental limits: mitigation works precisely in the regime where $\lambda$ is small enough that $e^{2\lambda}$ shots are affordable.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Exponential sampling wall.** The lower bound $\Gamma = \Omega(e^{2\lambda})$ [6] is the field's central constraint: every order-of-magnitude increase in circuit volume demands an exponential increase in shots. QEM extends NISQ reach by a *constant factor* in $\lambda$, not asymptotically.
2. **Noise-learning bottleneck (PEC).** PEC is only as good as its noise model. Non-Markovian effects, drift, leakage, and strongly correlated errors violate the sparse Pauli–Lindblad ansatz; model mismatch reintroduces bias that no amount of sampling removes [2,5].
3. **Extrapolation instability (ZNE).** High-order Richardson extrapolation is exponentially sensitive to statistical fluctuations and device drift; the smooth-$\lambda$ assumption fails for coherent errors that oscillate with amplification factor.
4. **Training-set representativeness (CDR).** Near-Clifford training circuits may not probe the same error channels as the target circuit (e.g., non-Clifford gate errors), and the affine ansatz can underfit structured noise [5].
5. **Coherent mismatch floor (VD).** Virtual distillation cannot correct a rotated dominant eigenvector; it suppresses incoherent weight only [4]. It also doubles (or $n$-tuples) qubit requirements and demands high-fidelity inter-copy operations.
6. **Estimator-only mitigation.** No QEM method produces a better quantum *state*; algorithms needing coherent output states (e.g., quantum phase estimation with coherent readout, fault-tolerant subroutines) gain nothing.
7. **Measurement and SPAM errors.** Readout errors require separate treatment (matrix-inversion, TREX, or M3 methods); most gate-focused QEM analyses assume readout is independently mitigated [5].

## 7. Conclusion

Quantum error mitigation has matured from two 2017 proposals [1] into a quantitative engineering discipline with proven utility-scale demonstrations [2,5]. Its four pillars partition the design space cleanly: **ZNE** needs no noise model but pays in extrapolation variance; **PEC** is unbiased given a learned model but pays the exponential quasi-probability tax; **CDR** learns the noise-to-signal map empirically from Clifford data at the cost of model bias; and **VD** purifies the dominant eigenvector with multi-copy circuits at the cost of qubit overhead and a coherent-mismatch floor. The unifying law — sampling overhead exponential in the circuit fault rate [6] — both explains the successes and draws the boundary: mitigation buys a constant-factor extension of NISQ reach, while only fault tolerance buys arbitrary scaling.

The research frontier reflects this understanding: *combining* methods (ZNE+PEC, CDR+VD, symmetry verification as a pre-filter), *learning* noise more accurately (sparse Pauli–Lindblad and beyond [2]), *composing* mitigation with early error correction, and *certifying* mitigated estimates. For the NISQ era and the early fault-tolerant transition, mitigation is not a detour from error correction — it is the bridge that makes noisy processors scientifically useful while the long road to fault tolerance is walked.
