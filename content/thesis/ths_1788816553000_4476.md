# Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra: Oblivious Subspace Embeddings, Leverage-Score Sampling, and the Blendenpik Solver for Overdetermined Least Squares

## Abstract

Randomized numerical linear algebra (RandNLA) compresses a large matrix by a random linear map before solving, and this thesis develops its theory and practice for overdetermined least squares $\min_x \|Ax - b\|_2$ with $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, $n \gg d$. We begin with *oblivious subspace embeddings* — random $\Pi \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ preserving every vector of a fixed $d$-dimensional subspace to $1 \pm \epsilon$ — surveying Gaussian, SRHT, CountSketch, and near-optimal sparse sign constructions and their dimension–sparsity trade-offs. We then treat *leverage-score sampling*, the non-oblivious dual, which draws rows proportionally to $\|U_{i,:}\|_2^2$ for $(1+\epsilon)$ relative error from $O(d \log d/\epsilon^2)$ rows. Both paradigms converge in *sketch-and-precondition*, whose flagship is **Blendenpik**: randomized unitary de-cohering, uniform sampling, QR preconditioning, and LSQR convergence in a handful of iterations — up to 4× faster than LAPACK's direct dense solver [2]. We close with the LSRN Gaussian solver [3], sharp sparse-embedding lower bounds, and the optimal dimension–sparsity frontier.

## 1 Introduction

The overdetermined least squares problem

$$\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^d} \|Ax - b\|_2, \qquad A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d},\ n \gg d$$

is arguably the single most important computational primitive in data science. Classical direct methods — QR factorization of $A$ at $O(nd^2)$ cost, or the singular value decomposition at roughly twice that — are optimal in the deterministic sense but leave an uncomfortable question: must we really pay a cost proportional to *every* row of $A$ when the answer $x$ lives in a space of only $d$ dimensions?

The randomized answer is *no*. A random sketching matrix $\Pi \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with $m \ll n$ can compress $(A, b)$ to $(\Pi A, \Pi b)$ such that the sketched regression $\min_x \|\Pi A x - \Pi b\|_2$ yields a $(1+\epsilon)$-approximation to the optimum, at a sketching cost that is frequently *sublinear* in the naive $nd^2$ work — in the celebrated input-sparsity regime, just $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A))$ operations, proportional to the number of nonzero entries [4].

Two distinct intellectual traditions feed this result:

1. **Oblivious embeddings**, inherited from the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma [1] and the sparse JL work of Clarkson–Woodruff: the random map is drawn *before* seeing $A$, and works for any fixed subspace.
2. **Leverage-score sampling**, inherited from the statistics of regression diagnostics and the relative-error CUR work of Drineas–Mahoney–Muthukrishnan: the map is *adaptive*, sampling rows with probability tied to their influence on the fit.

This thesis unifies both, proves the core distortion theorems, and culminates in **Blendenpik** [2] — the first randomized linear algebra code to beat LAPACK in production settings — and its Gaussian-preconditioned sibling **LSRN** [3]. The ideas are no longer theoretical curiosities: they are the foundation of modern scalable linear algebra [1][5].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Johnson–Lindenstrauss seed

The field germinates from a deceptively simple fact.

> **Theorem:** *Johnson–Lindenstrauss.* For any finite set $X \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ of $N$ points and any $\epsilon \in (0,1)$, there exists a linear map $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ with $m = O(\epsilon^{-2} \log N)$ such that $(1-\epsilon)\|x-y\|_2 \le \|f(x)-f(y)\|_2 \le (1+\epsilon)\|x-y\|_2$ for all $x, y \in X$.

The proof is *constructive and randomized*: $f(x) = m^{-1/2} G x$ with $G$ an $m \times n$ matrix of i.i.d. sub-Gaussian entries succeeds with high probability. Ailon–Chazelle showed the map can be *fast*: the subsampled randomized Hadamard transform (SRHT) applies the map in $O(n \log n)$ time rather than $O(mn)$.

### 2.2 From finite sets to subspaces: Sarlós's insight

Least squares cares not about finitely many vectors but about the *entire column space* of $A$ — infinitely many vectors. Sarlós (2006) observed that a JL map with an $\epsilon$-net argument over the unit sphere of a $d$-dimensional subspace yields a *subspace* embedding: one map works uniformly for the whole subspace. Clarkson–Woodruff then showed the map can be *extremely sparse* — even a single nonzero per column (CountSketch) — provided we accept larger sketch dimension [4].

### 2.3 Leverage scores and regression diagnostics

Given the thin QR factorization $A = QR$ (or SVD $A = U\Sigma V^\top$), the *leverage score* of row $i$ is

$$\ell_i = \|U_{i,:}\|_2^2 = \|Q_{i,:}\|_2^2,$$

the squared norm of the $i$-th row of the orthonormal basis. These scores satisfy $\sum_i \ell_i = d$ and $0 \le \ell_i \le 1$. In classical statistics, $\ell_i$ measures how much the $i$-th observation influences its own fitted value. In RandNLA, they become *sampling probabilities*: drawing $s = O(d \log d / \epsilon^2)$ rows with probability $p_i \propto \ell_i$ and rescaling by $1/\sqrt{s p_i}$ preserves the entire subspace [6].

### 2.4 Coherence

The *coherence* of $A$,

$$\mu(A) = \frac{n}{d} \max_{i} \ell_i \in [1, n/d],$$

quantifies the worst-case non-uniformity of leverage. Low coherence ($\mu \approx 1$) means uniform sampling is nearly as good as leverage sampling; high coherence means a few rows dominate. Blendenpik's masterstroke is to *randomly rotate* the row space to flatten coherence *before* sampling — restoring the power of cheap uniform draws [2].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology mirrors the three-layer architecture of the field:

1. **Sketching primitives.** Construct random maps $\Pi$ with provable distortion guarantees, and benchmark sketch-apply cost: dense Gaussian ($O(mn)$), SRHT ($O(n \log n)$), CountSketch ($O(\mathrm{nnz}(A))$), sparse sign ($O(s \cdot \mathrm{nnz}(A))$).
2. **Sampling primitives.** Estimate leverage scores — exactly via QR at $O(nd^2)$, or *approximately* via a preliminary sketch at $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A) \log n) + \mathrm{poly}(d)$ — and sample rows accordingly.
3. **Solver assembly.** Combine sketches into two meta-algorithms:
   - **Sketch-and-solve**: solve the sketched problem directly (one shot, $(1+\epsilon)$ relative error).
   - **Sketch-and-precondition**: use the sketch only to build a preconditioner $R$, then iterate (LSQR/CG) to machine precision. This is the Blendenpik/LSRN paradigm and the practical workhorse [2][3].

The theoretical spine is the *subspace embedding distortion bound*: if $\Pi$ is an $\epsilon$-embedding for $\mathrm{col}(A)$, then both meta-algorithms inherit clean guarantees, and the preconditioned normal equations have condition number at most $(1+\epsilon)/(1-\epsilon)$.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Oblivious Subspace Embeddings: Definition and the Construction Zoo

> **Theorem:** *Oblivious subspace embedding (OSE).* A distribution over random matrices $\Pi \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is an $(\epsilon, \delta)$-OSE for dimension $d$ if, for *every* fixed $d$-dimensional subspace $T \subset \mathbb{R}^n$,
>
> $$\Pr_{\Pi}\Big[\forall x \in T:\ (1-\epsilon)\|x\|_2 \le \|\Pi x\|_2 \le (1+\epsilon)\|x\|_2\Big] \ge 1 - \delta.$$

The quantifier order is crucial: $\Pi$ is drawn *obliviously*, before $T$ is known. The parameter landscape is governed by three axes — embedding dimension $m$, column sparsity $s$, and sketch-apply time:

| Construction | Sketch dim. $m$ | Nonzeros / column $s$ | Apply to $A$ | Oblivious? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian (i.i.d. $N(0,1/m)$) | $O(d/\epsilon^2)$ | $m$ (dense) | $O(m\,\mathrm{nnz}(A))$ | ✓ |
| SRHT ($PHD$) | $O(\epsilon^{-2} d \log d \log(n/\delta))$ | dense-in-transform | $O(nd \log n)$ | ✓ |
| CountSketch | $O(d^2/(\delta\epsilon^2))$ | $1$ | $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A))$ | ✓ |
| Sparse sign [6][7] | $O(d \log d / \epsilon^2)$ | $O(\log d / \epsilon)$ | $O(s\,\mathrm{nnz}(A))$ | ✓ |
| OSNAP (Nelson–Nguyen) | $O(d \log d / \epsilon^2)$ | $O(\log d / \epsilon)$ | $O(s\,\mathrm{nnz}(A))$ | ✓ |

The Gaussian construction is dimension-optimal ($m = \Theta(d/\epsilon^2)$ is a lower bound) but dense; CountSketch is sparsity-optimal ($s = 1$) but pays a quadratic $d^2$ dimension; sparse sign matrices thread the needle, achieving near-optimal *both* — $m = O(d \log d/\epsilon^2)$ with $s = O(\log d/\epsilon)$ nonzeros per column — via the matrix-Chernoff trace-inequality analysis of Cohen [6], recently sharpened to essentially optimal sparsity by decoupling/cumulant arguments [7].

A unifying modern abstraction is the **oblivious subspace injection (OSI)** of Tropp et al. [5]: a random matrix that preserves lengths *on average* and annihilates no subspace vector with high probability. OSI is strictly weaker than OSE yet suffices for low-rank approximation and least squares, and it explains *why* structured maps (sparse signs, trigonometric transforms, tensor sketches) all work: algorithm analysis factors into (i) correctness under OSI, and (ii) verifying OSI for each construction.

### 4.2 Leverage-Score Sampling: The Non-Oblivious Dual

Where OSEs ignore $A$, leverage-score sampling exploits it. Let $A = U\Sigma V^\top$ be the thin SVD. Sample $s$ rows i.i.d. with probabilities $p_i \ge \beta \ell_i / d$ (overestimates suffice) and rescale row $i$ by $1/\sqrt{s p_i}$ to form $SA$.

> **Theorem:** *Leverage-score subspace embedding.* If $s = O(d \log(d/\delta)/(\beta \epsilon^2))$, then with probability $\ge 1-\delta$,
>
> $$(1-\epsilon)\|Ax\|_2 \le \|SAx\|_2 \le (1+\epsilon)\|Ax\|_2 \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}^d.$$

The proof couples a matrix-Bernstein concentration argument with the identity $\sum_i \ell_i = d$. Three consequences follow:

1. **Relative-error regression.** The sketched solution $\hat{x} = \arg\min_x \|SAx - Sb\|_2$ satisfies $\|A\hat{x} - b\|_2 \le (1+\epsilon)\min_x \|Ax-b\|_2$ — *relative* error against the true optimum, not merely additive [6].
2. **Approximate leverage scores suffice.** Exact scores cost $O(nd^2)$, defeating the purpose. But a *first* cheap sketch (e.g., SRHT or CountSketch) yields a constant-distortion embedding $\Pi A$; its QR factor's row norms approximate the true leverage scores up to constants, at cost $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A)\log n) + \mathrm{poly}(d)$ [4].
3. **Lewis weights generalize to $\ell_p$.** For $p \ne 2$, leverage scores generalize to *Lewis weights*, giving input-sparsity $(1+\epsilon)$ $\ell_p$ regression in $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A)\log n) + \mathrm{poly}(d)$ time [4].

The fundamental tension: leverage sampling is *data-adaptive* (better constants, optimal sample complexity $O(d\log d/\epsilon^2)$) but requires a preprocessing pass; OSEs are *one-shot* but pay in sketch dimension or density. Practical solvers pick per regime — which is exactly the design space Blendenpik navigates.

### 4.3 Sketch-and-Precondition: From Rokhlin–Tygert to LAPACK-Beating Codes

Sketch-and-solve gives $(1+\epsilon)$ error — excellent for data analysis, unacceptable for scientific computing, where users demand residuals matching direct QR. The *sketch-and-precondition* framework of Rokhlin–Tygert (2008) resolves this:

1. Sketch: $\tilde{A} = \Pi A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$, $m \approx 2d$–$5d$.
2. Factor: compute the thin QR, $\tilde{A} = \tilde{Q}\tilde{R}$.
3. Precondition: run LSQR (or CG on the normal equations) on $A\tilde{R}^{-1}$ — whose singular values all lie in $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$.

> **Theorem:** *Preconditioned convergence.* If $\Pi$ is an $\epsilon$-OSE for $\mathrm{col}(A)$, then $\kappa(A\tilde{R}^{-1}) \le (1+\epsilon)/(1-\epsilon)$, and LSQR converges to machine precision in $O(\log(1/u))$ iterations, each costing $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A))$.

The sketch is used *only* to discover the geometry (the $R$ factor); the iteration then refines against the *true* $A$, recovering full backward stability. This is the template behind both Blendenpik and LSRN — differing only in the sketch $\Pi$ and the factorization of the sketch.

### 4.4 The Blendenpik Algorithm: Randomized Coherence Reduction

Blendenpik (Avron–Maymounkov–Toledo, 2010) [2] targets *dense, highly overdetermined* systems — LAPACK's home turf — and wins by exploiting a structural observation: after a randomized unitary transform, row norms become uniform with high probability, so *uniform* sampling (the cheapest possible sketch) behaves like leverage sampling.

**Phase 1 — Random mixing.** Apply a randomized orthogonal transform, e.g. $F = D H$ where $H$ is a Walsh–Hadamard or discrete cosine transform and $D$ is a random sign diagonal:

$$\hat{A} = F A, \qquad \hat{b} = F b.$$

The transform costs $O(nd \log n)$ via the fast transform, and with high probability reduces the coherence to $\mu(\hat{A}) = O(\log n)$ — nearly minimal [2].

**Phase 2 — Uniform sampling with adaptive resampling.** Sample $\gamma d$ rows of $\hat{A}$ uniformly ($\gamma \approx 3$–$5$ in practice), forming $A_s$. Because coherence is now low, uniform sampling is a valid subspace embedding. Blendenpik then *verifies* quality cheaply: it estimates $\kappa(A_s)$ and, if the sample is degenerate, doubles the sample — an adaptive loop that converts a probabilistic guarantee into a practically certain one.

**Phase 3 — Precondition and iterate.** Compute $A_s = Q_s R_s$ (thin QR of a $\gamma d \times d$ matrix — cheap), then solve

$$\min_y \|A R_s^{-1} y - b\|_2$$

with LSQR, recovering $x = R_s^{-1} y$. Since $\kappa(AR_s^{-1}) \approx 1$, LSQR converges in a handful of iterations.

The engineering details matter as much as the math: Blendenpik uses LAPACK's own QR kernels for the small factor, BLAS-3 blocked transforms for the mixing step, and careful memory layout — which is why it beats LAPACK's `DGELS` by roughly **4×** on large tall matrices rather than merely matching it asymptotically [2]. Its name — German for " lightning-fast " in spirit, a play on *blenden* (to dazzle) — reflects the authors' ambition: not a prototype, but a drop-in replacement.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import qr
from scipy.sparse.linalg import lsqr
from scipy.fft import dct

def blendenpik(A, b, gamma=4.0, seed=0):
    """Sketch-and-precondition least squares (Blendenpik-style).
    A: (n, d) dense tall matrix, b: (n,) rhs."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n, d = A.shape
    # Phase 1: randomized orthogonal pretransform (random signs + DCT)
    D = rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=n)
    FA = dct((D[:, None] * A), type=2, norm='ortho', axis=0)
    Fb = dct(D * b, type=2, norm='ortho')
    # Phase 2: uniform row sampling (coherence is now ~log n)
    s = int(gamma * d)
    idx = rng.choice(n, size=s, replace=False)
    As = FA[idx]
    # Phase 3: QR of the sketch -> preconditioner, then LSQR
    _, Rs = qr(As, mode='economic')
    y, *_ = lsqr(A @ np.linalg.inv(Rs), b, atol=1e-14, btol=1e-14)
    return np.linalg.solve(Rs, y)
```

### 4.5 LSRN: Gaussian Sketching with Parallel SVD Preconditioning

LSRN (Meng–Saunders–Mahoney, 2014) [3] takes a different point in the design space, optimized for *parallelism* and *rank deficiency* rather than raw dense speed:

1. **Gaussian sketch:** $G \in \mathbb{R}^{s \times n}$ with i.i.d. $N(0,1)$ entries, $s = \gamma d$; compute $GA$ — embarrassingly parallel matrix multiplication, no structured transform needed.
2. **Compact SVD:** $GA = \hat{U}\hat{\Sigma}\hat{V}^\top$; form the preconditioner $N = \hat{V}\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}$.
3. **Iterate:** LSQR on $AN$, then $x = Ny$.

> **Theorem:** *LSRN conditioning (informal).* For $s = \lceil \gamma d \rceil$ with oversampling $\gamma > 1$, with high probability $\kappa(AN) \le \frac{1+\sqrt{\gamma^{-1}} + \tau}{1-\sqrt{\gamma^{-1}} - \tau}$ for small $\tau$, so LSQR converges in $O(\log(1/u))$ iterations independent of $\kappa(A)$ [3].

LSRN's virtues: it handles *rank-deficient* problems (the SVD exposes numerical rank), works *symmetrically* for over- and underdetermined systems, and parallelizes trivially since $GA$ needs no communication-heavy transform. In head-to-head benchmarks on $m \times 1000$ dense problems, the speed ordering for large $m$ is **Blendenpik > LSRN > DGELS/DGELSD > DGELSY > backslash**, with LSRN pulling ahead decisively on sparse and underdetermined problems where Blendenpik's transform-based design is less at home [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 The distortion theorem (proof sketch)

The workhorse guarantee — that an $\epsilon$-OSE yields a $(1+\epsilon)$-relative-error regression — rests on two lemmas.

> **Theorem:** *Structural regression lemma.* Let $\Pi$ be an $\epsilon$-OSE for $\mathrm{col}([A\; b])$ with $\epsilon < 1/\sqrt{2}$. Let $x^\star = \arg\min_x \|Ax - b\|$ and $\hat{x} = \arg\min_x \|\Pi A x - \Pi b\|$. Then
>
> $$\|A\hat{x} - b\|_2 \le \frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\,\|Ax^\star - b\|_2.$$

*Proof sketch.* Write $b = Ax^\star + r$ with $r \perp \mathrm{col}(A)$. Optimality of $\hat{x}$ on the sketch gives $\|\Pi A \hat{x} - \Pi b\| \le \|\Pi A x^\star - \Pi b\| = \|\Pi r\|$. Decompose $A\hat{x} - b = A(\hat{x} - x^\star) - r$. Since $\Pi$ embeds $\mathrm{col}([A\; b])$, norms of both the in-subspace component and $r$ are preserved to $1\pm\epsilon$; the Pythagorean decomposition in the sketched space transfers back, yielding the bound after algebra. ∎

Sharpening $(1+\epsilon)/(1-\epsilon)$ to $1+O(\epsilon)$ uses the *affine embedding* refinement, but the qualitative message stands: **subspace embedding ⇒ relative-error regression** [1][4].

### 5.2 Sketch-dimension lower bounds

The constructions of §4.1 are nearly tight. Nelson–Nguyen proved that any OSE with column sparsity $s$ requires $m = \Omega(d^2/(\epsilon^2 s^{1+o(1)}))$ in the sparse regime; Li–Liu sharpened the sparse OSE lower bounds, showing $m = \Omega(d^2/(\epsilon^2\delta))$ for $s = 1$ (CountSketch is optimal) [8]. The 2025 breakthrough of Liu–Cohen [7] gives $m = \Theta(d/\epsilon^2)$ with sparsity $\tilde{O}(1/\epsilon)$ — nearly matching the Nelson–Nguyen conjecture for the sparsity of an optimal-dimension OSE, and introducing *leverage-score-sparsified* embeddings that bridge the oblivious/non-oblivious divide.

### 5.3 Empirical performance portrait

Reported benchmark results (representative, from [2][3]):

| Solver | $10^5 \times 10^3$ dense, full rank | Sparse $10^5 \times 10^3$ | $10^3 \times 10^5$ underdetermined |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blendenpik | **fastest** (~4× vs DGELS) | ≈ LAPACK | degrades |
| LSRN | runner-up | **leads by large margin** | **symmetric, fastest** |
| LAPACK DGELS/DGELSD | baseline | no sparse path | slow |
| MATLAB backslash | slowest dense | beats Blendenpik, loses to LSRN | slow |

LSRN's accuracy matches DGELSD to $\sim 10^{-14}$ relative residual in quad-precision-verified tests [3]. The residual achieved by Blendenpik is *indistinguishable* from LAPACK's direct QR — the sketch-and-precondition paradigm pays no accuracy tax [2].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Dense tall matrices are Blendenpik's kingdom; sparse ones are not.** The randomized transform densifies sparse $A$, destroying sparsity that a direct sparse QR (e.g., SuiteSparseQR) would exploit. On sparse problems Blendenpik performs no better than LAPACK, and LSRN — which touches $A$ only through matvecs — dominates [3].
2. **Coherence pathology.** Blendenpik's guarantees assume the pretransform flattens coherence; adversarial matrices with extreme row-norm concentration can force repeated resampling rounds, degrading the constants [2].
3. **Oversampling and the $\epsilon$-dependence.** Gaussian/CountSketch embeddings carry $1/\epsilon^2$ dimension dependence; high-accuracy sketch-and-solve is expensive, which is why production codes use sketching only for *preconditioning* and iterate for accuracy.
4. **Randomness and reproducibility.** Sketch-and-precondition solvers are randomized: runtimes and iteration counts vary across seeds. The failure probability $\delta$ can be driven down by oversampling, but the algorithm is Monte Carlo, not Las Vegas — a philosophical shift for numerical libraries that prize determinism.
5. **Rank deficiency.** Blendenpik assumes full column rank; rank-deficient problems need the SVD-based treatment of LSRN [3].
6. **Lower bounds bite.** The $\Omega(d^2)$ dimension barrier for ultra-sparse ($s=1$) embeddings [8] means input-sparsity-time algorithms inherently use larger sketches; the "holy grail" $m = O(d/\epsilon^2)$ *and* $O(\mathrm{nnz}(A))$ apply time remains conjectural up to log factors.

## 7 Conclusion

Randomized numerical linear algebra has crossed the chasm from theory to practice. The intellectual arc — from the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma, through Sarlós's subspace embedding and the sparse sketching revolution of Clarkson–Woodruff and Cohen, to the leverage-score sampling of Drineas–Mahoney–Muthukrishnan — converges on a single practical recipe: **sketch to learn the geometry, precondition, and iterate to machine precision**. Blendenpik proved this recipe beats LAPACK on its own turf [2]; LSRN extended it to parallel, sparse, and rank-deficient regimes [3]; and the modern OSI abstraction [5] plus near-optimal sparse constructions [7] are still tightening the constants. The frontier now lies in closing the dimension–sparsity gap, in derandomization without performance loss, and in carrying the sketch-and-precondition paradigm to eigenvalue problems, tensor decompositions, and nonlinear optimization. Randomness, once anathema to numerical analysis, has become its most reliable accelerator.
