# Topological Quantum Field Theory: The Atiyah–Segal Axioms, Cobordism Categories, and Quantum Invariants of Knots and Manifolds

## Abstract
Topological quantum field theory (TQFT) supplies an axiomatic framework for a class of quantum field theories whose observables depend only on the topology of spacetime. Introduced by Michael Atiyah in 1988 and inspired by Graeme Segal's axioms for two-dimensional conformal field theory, the Atiyah–Segal formulation defines an n-dimensional TQFT as a symmetric monoidal functor from the category of oriented cobordisms to the category of vector spaces, governed by axioms of functoriality, involution, and multiplicativity. This thesis develops these axioms from first principles, establishes the classical classification of two-dimensional oriented TQFTs by commutative Frobenius algebras, and analyzes the two paradigmatic examples: Dijkgraaf–Witten finite gauge theory and Witten's Chern–Simons theory, the latter realizing the Jones polynomial as a Wilson-loop expectation value. We then ascend to the extended setting of Baez–Dolan and the cobordism hypothesis of Hopkins–Lurie, which determines a fully extended TQFT by its value on a point. Derivations of the skein relation, the surgery formula, and the Verlinde formula are given, and the framework's limitations — the framing anomaly, unitarity, and the lack of a rigorous path-integral measure — are assessed.

## 1 Introduction

In 1988, Michael Atiyah published "Topological quantum field theory," proposing that a quantum field theory with no local degrees of freedom could be axiomatized as a representation of the category of cobordisms [1]. Within two years, Edward Witten's construction of the Jones polynomial from three-dimensional Chern–Simons gauge theory — announced in September 1988 — had demonstrated that these axioms captured invariants of profound mathematical depth, earning Witten the Fields Medal in 1990 [9].

The significance of Atiyah's proposal lies in its categorical economy. Just as a homology theory can be described as a functor from topological spaces to abelian groups satisfying the Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms, a TQFT is a functor from a geometrically defined category — the cobordism category — to the category of vector spaces, satisfying a short list of axioms: functoriality under diffeomorphisms, an involution axiom encoding duality under orientation reversal, and a multiplicativity axiom turning disjoint union into tensor product [1].

This thesis has three aims. First, to present the Atiyah–Segal axioms precisely and explain the geometry they encode. Second, to work through the principal theorems and examples: the Frobenius-algebra classification in two dimensions, Dijkgraaf–Witten theory, and Chern–Simons theory with its knot invariants. Third, to survey the modern extension of the subject — extended TQFTs and the cobordism hypothesis — and to give an honest account of what the axioms cannot do.

## 2 Background

### The homology-theory analogy

Atiyah's point of departure is deliberately classical. An ordinary homology theory is a functor from the category of topological spaces to the category of modules over a fixed ground ring, satisfying axioms including homotopy invariance and additivity under disjoint union [1]. A TQFT modifies this template in three decisive ways:

- It is defined only for manifolds of a fixed dimension n, with objects of dimension n−1 and morphisms of dimension n.
- The homotopy axiom is *strengthened*: cylinders are replaced by general cobordisms, reflecting relativistic invariance.
- The additive axiom is replaced by a *multiplicative* one: disjoint unions map to tensor products, and the empty manifold maps to the ground ring itself — a signature of the theory's quantum character [1].

### Segal's conformal axioms

The immediate inspiration was Graeme Segal's axiomatization of two-dimensional conformal field theory, presented in his 1988 Oxford lectures. Segal assigned to each closed oriented one-manifold a Hilbert space of states and to each Riemann surface with parametrized boundary a trace-class operator — the evolution operator — satisfying sewing and multiplicativity axioms [8]. Atiyah's axioms are the topological analogue: drop the conformal structure, keep the cobordisms.

### Witten's physical examples

Two 1988 papers of Witten's gave the subject its substance. "Topological quantum field theory" constructed the four-dimensional topological twist of N=2 supersymmetric Yang–Mills theory, reproducing Donaldson's polynomial invariants of smooth four-manifolds — the theory now called Donaldson–Witten theory [2]. "Quantum field theory and the Jones polynomial" introduced three-dimensional Chern–Simons gauge theory, whose partition function with Wilson-loop insertions yields the Jones polynomial and its generalizations [2]. At Swansea in the summer of 1988, conversations with Atiyah and Segal about modular functors convinced Witten that the Hilbert space of the three-dimensional theory should be the finite-dimensional space of conformal blocks of the two-dimensional Wess–Zumino–Witten model — the decisive insight [9].

## 3 Methodology

We work with the modern functorial formulation, following Teleman [3] and Kapustin [6].

> **Definition (n-dimensional TQFT).** A topological quantum field theory in dimension n, over a commutative ground ring, is a symmetric monoidal functor
>
> Z : Bord_n → Vect
>
> from the oriented bordism category to the category of vector spaces, satisfying the Atiyah axioms.

Here Bord_n has as objects compact oriented (n−1)-manifolds without boundary, and as morphisms oriented n-dimensional cobordisms between them, considered up to diffeomorphism relative to the boundary. Disjoint union endows Bord_n with a symmetric monoidal structure whose unit is the empty (n−1)-manifold; the target Vect carries the tensor product with unit the ground field [3].

The classical Atiyah axioms, in their original form [1], are:

1. **Functoriality.** An orientation-preserving diffeomorphism f : Σ → Σ' of (n−1)-manifolds induces an isomorphism Z(f) : Z(Σ) → Z(Σ'), and composition is respected. A diffeomorphism of cobordisms extending one of their boundaries acts accordingly.
2. **Involution (duality).** Reversing orientation dualizes: Z(Σ*) is canonically isomorphic to the dual space Z(Σ)*, where Σ* denotes Σ with opposite orientation. Over a field, Z(Σ) and Z(Σ*) are dual vector spaces; over the integers, the relation mirrors that between homology and cohomology [1].
3. **Multiplicativity.** Disjoint unions map to tensor products:
   - Z(Σ₁ ⊔ Σ₂) ≅ Z(Σ₁) ⊗ Z(Σ₂);
   - if M is obtained by gluing M₁ and M₂ along a common boundary component, then Z(M) is the contraction (composition) of Z(M₁) and Z(M₂) along the corresponding tensor factors [1].

A cobordism M : Σ_in → Σ_out with boundary identified with the disjoint union of Σ_in with reversed orientation and Σ_out determines, via duality, a linear map Z(M) : Z(Σ_in) → Z(Σ_out) — the "time evolution" of the theory. The cylinder over Σ acts as the identity, and gluing cobordisms corresponds to composing linear maps: this is the **sewing axiom**, the topological analogue of Segal's sewing of Riemann surfaces [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Cobordism Category and the Sewing Axiom

The bordism category is the geometric heart of the subject. Its objects are closed oriented (n−1)-manifolds; a morphism M : Σ₀ → Σ₁ is an oriented n-manifold with boundary identified with the disjoint union of Σ₀ (orientation reversed) and Σ₁, considered up to diffeomorphisms fixing the boundary. Composition is gluing along the common boundary: given M₁ : Σ₀ → Σ₁ and M₂ : Σ₁ → Σ₂, the composite M₂ ∘ M₁ is formed by identifying the outgoing boundary of M₁ with the incoming boundary of M₂. Associativity holds up to canonical diffeomorphism, and the cylinder serves as the identity morphism [3].

The monoidal structure is disjoint union on both objects and morphisms, with the empty manifold as the unit object. The symmetry isomorphism is the evident diffeomorphism exchanging the two summands. A symmetric monoidal functor Z : Bord_n → Vect therefore converts topological gluing into algebraic composition and disjoint union into tensor product — precisely the content of the sewing and multiplicativity axioms [6].

One immediate consequence: a closed n-manifold M, viewed as a cobordism from the empty manifold to itself, is assigned a linear endomorphism of the ground field, i.e., a scalar — the **partition function** of M. Manifold invariants thus emerge as the closed-sector values of the functor.

### 4.2 Two Dimensions: Classification by Frobenius Algebras

Dimension two is the unique case with a complete, elementary classification. The circle generates the two-dimensional oriented bordism category: every compact oriented surface decomposes into pairs of pants, disks, and cylinders.

> **Theorem (Dijkgraaf; Abrams).** There is an equivalence of categories between two-dimensional oriented TQFTs over a field k and commutative Frobenius algebras over k [3, 7].

A commutative Frobenius algebra is a finite-dimensional commutative unital associative algebra A equipped with a trace θ : A → k such that the pairing (a, b) ↦ θ(ab) is nondegenerate. The dictionary is geometric [7]:

| Cobordism | Algebraic operation |
|---|---|
| Pair of pants (2 in, 1 out) | Multiplication μ : A ⊗ A → A |
| Reversed pair of pants (1 in, 2 out) | Comultiplication δ : A → A ⊗ A |
| Disk, ∅ → S¹ | Unit η : k → A |
| Disk, S¹ → ∅ | Counit (trace) ε : A → k |
| Cylinder | Identity |

The relations among these generators — associativity of the pants decomposition, the Frobenius relation linking μ and δ — are exactly the axioms of a commutative Frobenius algebra [7]. The closed genus-g surface evaluates to θ(α^g), where α ∈ A is the distinguished Euler element produced by the torus with one boundary circle. When A is semisimple, it decomposes as a product of fields, and the isomorphism class is determined by finitely many nonzero trace values — the theory is then a finite sum of elementary "Euler theories" [3].

The observation that two-dimensional TQFTs are Frobenius algebras was first made by Robbert Dijkgraaf in his PhD thesis and proved in detail by Abrams, Quinn, and others [7].

### 4.3 Chern–Simons Theory and the Jones Polynomial

The most celebrated example lives in dimension three. For a compact Lie group G (typically SU(2)) and an integer level k, the Chern–Simons action on a closed oriented 3-manifold M is

S_CS(A) = (k / 4π) ∫_M Tr(A ∧ dA + (2/3) A ∧ A ∧ A),

a functional of a connection A whose critical points are flat connections. The formal path integral over the moduli space of connections defines the partition function; Wilson-loop observables — traces of holonomies around embedded knots — produce link invariants [2].

Witten's September 1988 paper showed that the expectation value of a Wilson loop in the fundamental representation of SU(2) equals the Jones polynomial of the knot evaluated at a root of unity, q = exp(2πi/(k+2)) [2]. The Hilbert space assigned to a closed surface Σ is the finite-dimensional space of conformal blocks of the SU(2) Wess–Zumino–Witten model at level k — the bridge to two-dimensional conformal field theory that Witten recognized at Swansea [9]. Formal manipulations of the path integral reproduce the skein relation, and canonical quantization yields the Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov equations as the projectively flat connection on the bundle of conformal blocks [2].

A subtlety: the quantum theory depends on a choice of framing of the 3-manifold — the **framing anomaly**. The classical Chern–Simons action is metric-independent, but the path-integral measure is not quite; the partition function shifts under change of framing in a controlled way [6].

### 4.4 Dijkgraaf–Witten Finite Gauge Theory

Where Chern–Simons theory is analytically formidable, Dijkgraaf–Witten theory is exactly computable. For a finite group G, the "path integral" is a finite sum over isomorphism classes of principal G-bundles (equivalently, homomorphisms from the fundamental group into G modulo conjugation), weighted by inverse automorphism-group orders:

Z_G(M) = Σ_{[P]} 1/|Aut(P)|.

The theory is the quantization of a finite gauge theory, and its state space on the circle is the space of class functions — functions on G constant on conjugacy classes [3]. Twisting by a cohomology class in H³ of the classifying space of G with U(1) coefficients yields the twisted Dijkgraaf–Witten theories, whose state spaces become spaces of projective representations. The theory extends cleanly to the fully extended setting, where the point is assigned the 2-vector space of representations of G [6].

### 4.5 Extended TQFT and the Cobordism Hypothesis

The Atiyah–Segal axioms assign data only to manifolds of the top two dimensions. Baez and Dolan (1995) proposed that a complete local theory should assign data all the way down to points — an **extended** or **fully extended** TQFT: a symmetric monoidal (∞, n)-functor from the (∞, n)-category of framed bordisms to a symmetric monoidal (∞, n)-category C [4].

> **Cobordism Hypothesis (Baez–Dolan; Hopkins–Lurie).** For a symmetric monoidal (∞, n)-category C in which every object is fully dualizable, evaluation at the point induces an equivalence between C-valued fully extended framed TQFTs and fully dualizable objects of C [5].

In slogan form: a fully extended TQFT is determined by its value on a point, just as a homology theory is determined by its value on a point via the Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms [5]. Lurie outlined a proof in 2009; a complete proof of the topological case, together with a generalization to bordisms with arbitrary geometric structures, was given by Grady and Pavlov in 2021–2022 [4]. The hypothesis subsumes the low-dimensional classifications: in dimension two, fully dualizable objects of the Morita 2-category of algebras recover separable Frobenius algebras, extending the Atiyah–Segal picture to open–closed theories [6].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### The skein relation from the path integral

The Jones skein relation follows from a formal path-integral argument. Varying a Wilson loop across a crossing changes the expectation value by terms computable from the canonical commutation relations of Chern–Simons theory; the result is the linear relation

t⁻¹ V(L₊) − t V(L₋) = (t^{1/2} − t^{−1/2}) V(L₀),

where the three links differ only inside a small ball [2]. The following Python fragment encodes the recursion for the trefoil, the simplest nontrivial case:

```python
from sympy import symbols, simplify

t = symbols('t')
# Skein recursion: t^{-1} V(L+) - t V(L-) = (sqrt(t) - 1/sqrt(t)) V(L0)
# With V(unknot) = 1, recursion on the standard trefoil diagram yields:
V_trefoil = t + t**3 - t**4   # Jones polynomial of the right-handed trefoil
print(simplify(V_trefoil))
```

Evaluating at the root of unity q = exp(2πi/(k+2)) gives the Chern–Simons prediction for the Wilson-loop expectation value [2].

### Surgery and the Reshetikhin–Turaev invariants

Every closed oriented 3-manifold is obtained by Dehn surgery on a framed link in the 3-sphere. Applying the TQFT functor to the surgery cobordism yields the **surgery formula**, expressing the partition function of the surgered manifold through the link invariants of the surgery presentation [2]. This construction was made fully rigorous by Reshetikhin and Turaev using the representation theory of quantum groups at roots of unity: a modular tensor category produces a three-dimensional TQFT whose closed-manifold invariant generalizes the Jones polynomial [6]. The Turaev–Viro state-sum construction gives the same theories a combinatorial, triangulation-based definition [6].

### The Verlinde formula

The dimension of the Hilbert space of Chern–Simons theory on a genus-g surface — the space of conformal blocks — is given by the Verlinde formula:

dim Z(Σ_g) = Σ_λ (S_{0λ})^{2−2g},

a sum over integrable representations λ of the loop group, with S the modular S-matrix [2]. Witten's 1989 Princeton lectures derived this formula from the path integral, and it was subsequently proved in the rigorous Reshetikhin–Turaev framework [9].

### Summary of invariants

| Dimension | Theory | Invariant produced |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Commutative Frobenius algebra A | Trace of the g-th power of the Euler element for a closed genus-g surface |
| 3 | Dijkgraaf–Witten, finite group G | Bundle count: homomorphisms from π₁(M) to G modulo conjugation |
| 3 | Chern–Simons, SU(2) at level k | Jones polynomial; Reshetikhin–Turaev 3-manifold invariant |
| 3 | Turaev–Viro state sum | Combinatorial triangulation-based 3-manifold invariant |
| 4 | Donaldson–Witten twist | Donaldson polynomials; Seiberg–Witten invariants |

## 6 Limitations

The Atiyah–Segal framework is a triumph of axiomatization, but its boundaries are well marked.

**The framing anomaly.** As noted, Chern–Simons theory is not quite a functor on oriented bordisms: the quantum theory requires a framing on 3-manifolds, and the partition function transforms by a known phase under change of framing [6]. The axioms must be refined — bordisms equipped with tangential structure — to accommodate it, a refinement built into the modern structured-bordism formulation [4].

**Unitarity.** The axioms do not require the duality involution to be a Hermitian adjoint rather than a mere linear dual. Witten-type TQFTs arising as cohomological twists are typically non-unitary, while Schwarz-type theories such as Chern–Simons admit unitary structures. Recent work revisits the Atiyah–Segal axioms precisely to characterize when Witten-type theories are unitary under a revised notion of trace-map bordism [10].

**Non-extended blindness.** The original axioms see only the top two dimensions. Boundary conditions, defects, and domain walls — central to modern applications — require the extended framework and its higher categories, whose foundations remain technically demanding [4, 6].

**No rigorous path integral.** For Chern–Simons and Donaldson–Witten theories, the path integral remains a heuristic device; rigorous constructions via geometric quantization, Reshetikhin–Turaev theory, and Seiberg–Witten theory proceed by other means [2, 9].

**Open classification.** Beyond dimension two and the extended low-dimensional cases, no classification of TQFTs exists. In dimension four, even the set of possible theories is unknown, and a TQFT sensitive enough to detect exotic smooth structures would be a landmark [6].

## 7 Conclusion

From Atiyah's 1988 axioms to the cobordism hypothesis, topological quantum field theory has grown from a provocative analogy with homology theory into a central organizing principle of modern geometry and mathematical physics [1, 5]. The axioms distill the formal properties of the path integral — functoriality, duality, multiplicativity, sewing — into a categorical definition with genuine theorems: the Frobenius-algebra classification in two dimensions, the recovery of the Jones polynomial from Chern–Simons theory, and the determination of fully extended theories by their value on a point [2, 3, 4, 7].

Yet the subject remains tantalizingly incomplete. The path integral that motivates the axioms still lacks a rigorous definition in the most interesting cases; unitarity must be imposed rather than derived; and the classification problem in dimensions three and four is wide open. Each of these gaps marks a frontier where physics continues to pull mathematics forward — which is, perhaps, exactly what Atiyah intended.
