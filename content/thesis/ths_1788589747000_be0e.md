# Time-Series Databases at Fleet Scale: Gorilla's In-Memory Blocks, Delta-Delta Compression, Columnar Chunks in Prometheus and M3, and Downsampling Retention Architectures

## Abstract

Operational observability at the scale of a global fleet demands a storage engine that can ingest tens of millions of measurements per second, retain them for months, and answer aggregate queries in milliseconds. This thesis unifies three landmark architectures that define the modern time-series database (TSDB): Facebook's **Gorilla**, an in-memory TSDB that trades write-path durability for extreme query performance through *delta-delta timestamp* and *XOR floating-point* compression [1]; **Prometheus TSDB**, whose head block, write-ahead log, inverted index, and immutable columnar chunks bring Gorilla's codec to a single-node monitoring engine with log-structured compaction [4]; and Uber's **M3**, which shards M3DB across a fleet and couples it with a coordinator that performs streaming downsampling and long-window retention [7]. We formalize the bit-level encoding schemes, derive their worst-case and expected storage bounds, compare block and index organizations with a common cost model, and analyze downsampling pyramids that reconcile raw fidelity with bounded retention cost. Grounded in published systems papers, vendor documentation, and a classical survey of column-oriented storage [3], the thesis closes with open problems in high-cardinality indexing, out-of-order ingestion, and semantically correct rollups.

---

## 1. Introduction

A time series is among the simplest data structures in computing — an ordered sequence of *(timestamp, value)* pairs — yet at fleet scale it becomes one of the hardest storage problems. A single datacenter fleet can emit tens of millions of metric samples per second; every host, container, switch, and application reports CPU, memory, latency, error counters, and queue depths on fixed cadences. The *operational* value of this data is concentrated in two directions: the most recent minutes, which drive alerting and incident triage, and long historical windows, which drive capacity planning, regression detection, and post-mortem analysis [1].

These two directions pull in opposite directions. Alerting demands **low-latency point and range reads over hot data**, while history demands **cheap, dense, immutable storage over cold data**. A further structural observation, made explicit by the designers of Gorilla at Facebook, simplifies the design space: users of monitoring systems rarely care about individual points; they care about *aggregate analysis* — rates, quantiles, correlations — and recent points are far more valuable than old ones [1]. This insight justifies lossy-in-spirit (but lossless-in-fact) compression, tiered retention, and even the deliberate sacrifice of small amounts of write-path data in exchange for availability.

This thesis studies how three production systems resolve the tension:

1. **Gorilla** (Facebook, 2015): an *in-memory* TSDB where 26 hours of data live in RAM across a shard of hosts, made possible by a 10x compression codec [1].
2. **Prometheus TSDB**: a single-node engine combining a WAL-backed mutable *head block* with immutable on-disk blocks of columnar chunks and an inverted label index [4].
3. **M3** (Uber): a *distributed* metrics platform in which M3DB shards storage across nodes while an M3 Coordinator provides a global query interface, Prometheus remote-write compatibility, and streaming downsampling [7].

We contribute (i) bit-level formalizations of the delta-delta and XOR codecs with exact grammars and storage bounds, (ii) a unified block-and-index cost model, (iii) an analysis of downsampling pyramids as bounded-cost fidelity schedules, and (iv) a catalogue of open problems.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The time-series data model

A time series pairs non-decreasing 64-bit timestamps with IEEE-754 double values. Fleet monitoring series share two statistical regularities that every codec in this thesis exploits:

- **Near-periodic timestamps.** Scrapes and heartbeats occur on fixed intervals (e.g., every 15 s), so successive deltas $d_i = t_i - t_{i-1}$ are nearly constant and the *delta-of-delta* $\delta_i = d_i - d_{i-1}$ is usually zero.
- **Slowly varying values.** Gauges such as temperature or CPU utilization change gradually; consecutive doubles share most of their 64 bits, so $v_i \oplus v_{i-1}$ has long runs of leading zeros.

### 2.2 Columnar storage

TSDB chunks are *columnar*: timestamps and values live in separate compressed columns, the layout Abadi et al. show dominates scan/aggregate workloads through selective reads, homogeneous-column compression, and late materialization [3]. Prometheus chunks and M3 blocks inherit this directly [4][6].

### 2.3 Design space of TSDBs

Surveys and practitioner taxonomies divide TSDBs along three axes [5][6]:

- **Ingest path:** in-memory ring buffers (Gorilla), WAL-backed head blocks (Prometheus), or distributed commit logs (M3DB).
- **Encoding:** delta-based timestamp codecs and XOR/floating-point value codecs, frequently descended from Gorilla [1].
- **Retention:** fixed-window in-memory stores, log-structured compaction with time/size retention, and explicit downsampling pyramids.

BTrDB (Andersen & Culler, FAST '16) explored a complementary corner — a copy-on-write tree with multiresolution aggregates precomputed at ingest for *statistical* range queries — demonstrating that the block format and the query algebra can be co-designed [2]. We return to this idea in §4.4.

> **Theorem (informal):** For any append-only time series with bounded timestamp jitter $J$ and bounded per-step value bit-flip width $w$, there exists a lossless encoding whose amortized cost per point is $O(\log J + w)$ bits. *The Gorilla-family codecs are constructive instances of this bound.*

## 3. Methodology

We proceed by **comparative architecture analysis**: the published evaluations of these production systems are taken as ground truth [1][4][7]. Three steps:

1. **Codec formalization.** We reconstruct the exact bit grammars of the delta-delta and XOR schemes from the Gorilla paper, the Prometheus chunk format, and M3's M3TSZ variant, and derive per-point bit budgets under regular and adversarial inputs.
2. **Common cost model.** We evaluate each system on four normalized dimensions — *ingest throughput*, *hot-read latency*, *bytes per point at rest*, and *operational complexity* — distinguishing architectural consequences from implementation tuning.
3. **Retention calculus.** We model downsampling as a function $R: (\text{resolution}, \text{age}) \to \text{retention}$ and analyze the storage-vs-fidelity frontier it induces.

A reference Python implementation of the two codecs is given in §4.1 to make the bit manipulation concrete and testable.

## 4. Deep Technical Dive

### 4.1 Gorilla: in-memory blocks and the delta-delta / XOR codec

Gorilla's central bet is that the last ~26 hours of monitoring data can be kept **entirely in RAM** if compression is aggressive enough, yielding order-of-magnitude query wins over disk-backed stores [1]. Each host in a Gorilla cluster owns a shard of the series keyspace; a series maps to exactly one host, so scaling is a matter of adding hosts and re-sharding — no cross-host coordination on the write path.

#### 4.1.1 Timestamp compression: delta-of-delta

Let deltas $d_i = t_i - t_{i-1}$ and delta-deltas $\delta_i = d_i - d_{i-1}$. For a perfectly periodic series $\delta_i = 0$ for all $i$. The codec emits a variable-length code per $\delta_i$:

| Control bits | Payload | $\delta$ range |
|---|---|---|
| `0` | — | 0 |
| `10` | 7 bits | $-63 \le \delta \le 64$ |
| `110` | 9 bits | $-255 \le \delta \le 256$ |
| `1110` | 12 bits | $-2047 \le \delta \le 2048$ |
| `1111` | 32 bits | full range |

Each block stores its base timestamp at full width and covers a fixed two-hour window, so readers binary-search block headers by time [1].

> **Theorem:** For a series scraped at nominal interval $\Delta$ with jitter bounded by $J$ seconds, every timestamp after the first costs at most $1 + \lceil\log_2(2J+1)\rceil + 2$ bits, and exactly 1 bit when the interval is perfectly regular. *Proof sketch:* $\delta_i \in [-J, J]$; the bin table above assigns the shortest sufficient code; the perfectly regular case emits `0`. ∎

A worked illustration from a university lecture on the paper shows the power of the scheme: ten points whose raw representation occupies 48 bytes compress to just 21 bytes (167 bits) once timestamps and values are jointly encoded [5].

#### 4.1.2 Value compression: XOR of consecutive doubles

For values, Gorilla XORs consecutive IEEE-754 doubles and suppresses leading and trailing zeros of the result:

```
x = v_{i-1} XOR v_i
if x == 0:            emit '0'
elif leading(x) >= leading_prev and trailing(x) == trailing_prev:
                      emit '10'; emit meaningful bits of x   # reuse window
else:                 emit '11'; emit 5 bits leading zeros;
                      emit 6 bits meaningful-bit length;
                      emit meaningful bits of x              # new window
```

The `'10'` fast path — the key TSZ refinement adopted by Prometheus and M3 — costs only a 2-bit header plus meaningful bits (often < 10 bits total) when the change pattern is stable, as it is for smooth gauges and small-increment counters [4][5].

#### 4.1.3 Reference implementation

```python
def encode_timestamps(ts):
    """Delta-of-delta encode a list of int timestamps. Returns (base, bitstring)."""
    out = []
    prev_delta = 0
    for i, t in enumerate(ts):
        if i == 0:
            base = t
            continue
        if i == 1:
            prev_delta = t - ts[0]
            out.append(("delta14", prev_delta))  # block stores first delta wider
            continue
        d = t - ts[i - 1]
        dd = d - prev_delta
        prev_delta = d
        if dd == 0:
            out.append(("0",))
        elif -63 <= dd <= 64:
            out.append(("10", dd & 0x7F, 7))
        elif -255 <= dd <= 256:
            out.append(("110", dd & 0x1FF, 9))
        elif -2047 <= dd <= 2048:
            out.append(("1110", dd & 0xFFF, 12))
        else:
            out.append(("1111", dd & 0xFFFFFFFF, 32))
    return base, out

def xor_encode(values):
    """Gorilla-TSZ float compression. Returns list of (ctrl, payload) tokens."""
    import struct
    def bits(f):
        return struct.unpack(">Q", struct.pack(">d", f))[0]
    toks, prev, lz_prev, tz_prev = [], None, 0, 0
    for v in values:
        b = bits(v)
        if prev is None:
            toks.append(("raw64", b)); prev = b; continue
        x = prev ^ b
        prev = b
        if x == 0:
            toks.append(("0",)); continue
        lz = (x.bit_length() ^ 63)  # leading zeros in 64-bit word
        lz = 63 - (x.bit_length() - 1)
        tz = (x & -x).bit_length() - 1  # trailing zeros
        meaningful = 64 - lz - tz
        if lz >= lz_prev and tz == tz_prev and meaningful > 0:
            toks.append(("10", (x >> tz) & ((1 << meaningful) - 1)))
        else:
            toks.append(("11", lz, meaningful, (x >> tz) & ((1 << meaningful) - 1)))
            lz_prev, tz_prev = lz, tz
    return toks
```

#### 4.1.4 Availability over durability

Gorilla optimizes for *write and read availability even under failures, at the expense of possibly dropping small amounts of data on the write path* [1]: replication is asynchronous, so a lost block costs at most a small recent window — tolerable because aggregates over slightly incomplete data remain actionable. This workload-driven relaxation of durability is the ancestor of every best-effort remote-write design since.

### 4.2 Prometheus TSDB: head block, WAL, and columnar chunks

Prometheus keeps Gorilla's codec but rehouses it in a **log-structured, single-node** engine designed for operational simplicity: no cluster coordination, crash recovery via write-ahead log, and immutable on-disk blocks [4].

#### 4.2.1 Write path: WAL → head block → chunks

1. **WAL.** Samples are appended to the write-ahead log (`wal/`) for crash durability; record compression halves WAL size at small CPU cost (default since v2.20) [4].
2. **Head block.** The head is an in-memory structure of the most recent ~2 hours: series live in memory-mapped chunk files (`chunks_head/`) and are indexed by a mutable inverted index.
3. **Block cut and compaction.** Every two hours the head is cut into an immutable block (`chunks/`, `index`, `tombstones`, `meta.json`); background compaction merges blocks up to **10% of retention or 31 days, whichever is smaller** [4].

#### 4.2.2 The inverted index

Each label name/value pair maps to a **postings list** of series IDs, and each series ID to its chunk references. Label matchers resolve to postings lists, which are intersected to yield the series set before the engine walks chunk references in the time range [6] — so the expensive step is postings intersection, not data scanning.

#### 4.2.3 Chunk format

Each chunk holds ~120 samples as two columns: timestamps as deltas from the chunk's first timestamp, values in the Gorilla XOR `'0'/'10'/'11'` TSZ form [6]. Chunks are immutable after the head cut (lock-free reads); deletions use per-block **tombstones** applied at query time.

#### 4.2.4 Retention as configuration

Prometheus exposes retention as two flags: `--storage.tsdb.retention.time` (default **15 days**) and `--storage.tsdb.retention.size` (oldest blocks deleted first; the WAL and memory-mapped head chunks count toward the budget and peak every 2 hours) [4]. Retention is thus *deletion-based*: there is no built-in downsampling — a design decision that pushes long-term, low-resolution storage to external systems.

### 4.3 M3: sharded retention and long-term storage

Where Prometheus is deliberately single-node, **M3** (Uber) is a distributed metrics platform: M3DB shards series across storage nodes, and the **M3 Coordinator** acts as a sidecar giving Prometheus a global query and storage interface, including a Prometheus remote-write/read backend [7].

#### 4.3.1 Architecture

- **M3DB nodes** own shards of the series space (consistent hashing), each with configurable retention namespaces — e.g., short-retention raw alongside long-retention aggregated namespaces.
- **M3 Coordinator** fans queries across shards and regions and exposes PromQL-compatible endpoints, so Prometheus/Grafana tooling works unchanged.
- **M3 Aggregator** performs *streaming downsampling*: raw namespaces expire after days while 1-minute and 1-hour rollups persist for months [7].

#### 4.3.2 Design principles

Uber's M3 principles are ordered revealingly: *maximum storage per hardware; maximum compression; low access time; avoid compaction where possible; minimal operational attention at high write volume* [7]. M3DB's time-partitioned immutable blocks make expiry a file deletion rather than an LSM-style merge — a direct reaction to compaction's operational cost at fleet scale.

M3's value codec, **M3TSZ**, is a tuned descendant of the Gorilla TSZ scheme, keeping the `'0'/'10'/'11'` grammar while optimizing the bit-packing for M3DB's block layout — evidence that the Gorilla codec has become the *de facto* standard wire format for float series [7].

### 4.4 Downsampling and retention architectures

Downsampling reframes retention as a *fidelity schedule*: a stepwise function mapping series age to resolution:

```
age < 2 days    → raw resolution      (e.g., 15 s scrape)
age < 30 days   → 1-minute rollups
age < 1 year    → 1-hour rollups
age < 5 years   → 1-day rollups
```

With per-tier ratios of 60–100x, a four-tier pyramid keeps five years of history for little more than one month of raw-data cost. The rollups themselves are computed by streaming aggregation (sums and counts for counters — never averages of averages; min/max/last for gauges; histogram bucket merges for latency distributions).

Three architectural placements exist:

1. **Write-time rollup** (M3 Aggregator; BTrDB's precomputed aggregates [2]): bounded ingest cost, but the rollup set is fixed at write time.
2. **Compact-time rollup** (Thanos/Cortex-style): flexible, but compactor lag delays cold-tier availability.
3. **No rollup** (vanilla Prometheus): simplest operations, but retention is bounded by raw-data economics — the gap M3 and remote storage fill [4][7].

> **Theorem (retention cost):** Under a geometric pyramid with per-tier size ratio $r < 1$ and tier count $k$, total storage is bounded by $S_{raw} \cdot (1 + r + r^2 + \dots) < S_{raw}/(1-r)$, independent of $k$. *Downsampling thus converts unbounded retention growth into a bounded multiple of raw cost.*

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 Published measurements

| System | Reported result | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Gorilla | 10x storage reduction vs. uncompressed; 73x lower query latency, 14x higher throughput vs. HBase-backed TSDB | [1] |
| Gorilla codec (worked example) | 10 points: 48 bytes raw → 21 bytes (167 bits) encoded | [5] |
| Prometheus WAL | Record compression halves WAL size; default since v2.20 | [4] |
| Prometheus blocks | 2 h head blocks compacted to ≤ min(10% retention, 31 d) | [4] |
| Prometheus retention default | 15 days (`--storage.tsdb.retention.time`) | [4] |
| Columnar analytics | Column stores outperform row stores by an order of magnitude on scan/aggregate benchmarks (SSBM) | [3] |

### 5.2 Formal bit-budget argument

For $N$ series at interval $\Delta$ with jitter $J \ll \Delta$: timestamps cost 1 bit/point when regular (≤ 6 bits for $J=2$ s); values cost 1 bit when unchanged, typically $2+w$ bits with $w \approx 8$–12 under the `'10'` fast path. Total **≈ 12–18 bits/point** (≈ 1.5–2.25 bytes) vs. 16 bytes uncompressed — a **7–10x** ratio reproducing Gorilla's reported 10x from first principles [1]. The adversarial worst case (32-bit delta-delta + full 64-bit payload) is ≤ 101 bits/point, below the 128-bit baseline: *losslessness is never more expensive than the naive layout*, so the codec is safe to apply unconditionally.

### 5.3 Index and query cost

For a query matching $m$ label matchers over a corpus of $S$ series, postings-list intersection costs $O(\sum_i |P_i|)$ where $|P_i|$ is the postings length per matcher; the most selective matcher dominates [6]. Chunk reads are sequential within a series, so range scans are I/O-optimal up to the compression ratio. This is why cardinality — the number of distinct series $S$ — is the true scaling variable of Prometheus-class systems, not the sample count: every series pays index and chunk-header overhead regardless of its sample density.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Cardinality explosion.** The inverted index grows with distinct label combinations; a single high-cardinality label (e.g., `user_id`) can multiply $S$ by orders of magnitude and OOM the head block. Current mitigations are operational (label dropping, cardinality limits), not principled. A *cardinality-aware* index with approximate postings remains open.
2. **Out-of-order and backfill writes.** The delta-delta codec assumes append-only, near-ordered arrival. Late or backfilled points force either expensive chunk rewrites or sidecar "overflow" structures; Prometheus historically bounded out-of-order tolerance tightly, and distributed systems must reconcile replicas that observed different arrival orders.
3. **Semantic correctness of rollups.** Downsampling counters requires `sum`/`count` pairs, never averaged rates; histograms need bucket-wise merges; counter resets and stale markers must survive aggregation. No formal algebra of rollup-safe aggregations exists yet.
4. **Deletion and compliance.** Immutable chunks plus tombstones make point deletion (e.g., GDPR erasure) a compaction-time concern with unpredictable latency.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Three systems, one lineage. **Gorilla** proved that an in-memory TSDB with a 10x codec could serve a global fleet's hot path [1]. **Prometheus** took Gorilla's bit grammar, embedded it in a WAL-backed head block with an inverted index and log-structured compaction, and made it operable by a single team [4]. **M3** sharded the design across a fleet, added a coordinator for global PromQL, and closed the retention loop with streaming downsampling [7]. Underneath all three lies the same columnar insight surveyed by Abadi et al. [3]: store homogeneous columns, compress them with domain-aware codecs, and let the query engine scan less.

The enduring lesson is methodological: each system *co-designed codec, block format, and query algebra* around two empirical facts — periodic timestamps, slowly varying values — and relaxed a traditional contract its workload did not need (durability in Gorilla, distribution in Prometheus, fixed resolution in M3). The Gorilla bit grammar will likely survive another decade; the open frontier is the *index* (taming cardinality) and the *algebra* (provably correct rollups).
