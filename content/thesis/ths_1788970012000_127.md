# Solid-State Batteries: Sulfide and Oxide Electrolytes, Dendrite Mechanics, and Interfacial Chemo-Mechanics

## Abstract

All-solid-state lithium batteries (ASSLBs) promise to eliminate the flammability of organic liquid electrolytes while enabling lithium-metal anodes with a theoretical specific capacity of 3,860 mA h g−1. Two inorganic electrolyte families dominate: thiophosphate sulfides such as Li10GeP2S12 (LGPS) and the argyrodites Li6PS5X (X = Cl, Br, I), which achieve room-temperature ionic conductivities above 10 mS cm−1 but suffer narrow electrochemical windows and moisture sensitivity [1][6]; and oxides such as garnet-type Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO), which offer superior oxidative stability at the cost of lower conductivity and refractory processing [4][5]. Contrary to early expectations, stiff ceramics do not intrinsically block lithium penetration: Porz et al. demonstrated an electrochemo-mechanical infiltration mechanism in which lithium plated into surface flaws generates crack-tip stresses that drive fracture, independent of the shear modulus invoked by the Monroe–Newman criterion [2][8]. Kasemchainan et al. further showed that void formation during stripping imposes a critical stripping current density below which practical cycling must remain [3]. This thesis synthesizes the transport physics, fracture mechanics, and interfacial kinetics governing practical ASSLB design, and quantifies the trade-offs constraining energy density, power, and cycle life.

## 1 Introduction

The lithium-ion battery, commercialized in 1991, derives its energy density from intercalation electrodes and its safety liabilities from the same flammable carbonate electrolytes that enable its rate capability. Replacing the liquid electrolyte with a fast lithium-ion-conducting solid promises three simultaneous advances: the safe deployment of lithium-metal anodes, whose capacity exceeds graphite by an order of magnitude; bipolar stacking with a single shared current collector; and thermal stability far beyond the decomposition onset of organic solvents [9]. Yet three decades of solid-state battery research reveal that the electrolyte is not merely a passive ionic wire. It is an electrochemically active, mechanically loaded component whose bulk transport, grain boundaries, surface defects, and decomposition interphases collectively determine whether a cell delivers 1,000 cycles or fails on the tenth.

Two material families have emerged as the leading candidates. **Sulfide electrolytes**, built from polarizable S2− frameworks with wide lithium-migration bottlenecks, reach conductivities comparable to liquids: LGPS attains 12 mS cm−1 at 300 K [1], and the silicon-chlorine analogue Li9.54Si1.74P1.44S11.7Cl0.3 reaches 25 mS cm−1 [6]. Their soft mechanical character permits room-temperature densification by cold pressing, a decisive manufacturing advantage. **Oxide electrolytes** — garnets, perovskites, and NASICON-type phosphates — trade conductivity for stability: cubic LLZO conducts at ~0.3–1 mS cm−1 but resists oxidation beyond 6 V versus Li/Li+ and tolerates ambient handling [5].

The central scientific surprise of the last decade is that neither family is immune to lithium dendrite penetration. The classical Monroe–Newman analysis [8] predicted that a separator with shear modulus exceeding roughly twice that of lithium metal (~7 GPa) would suppress dendritic instability; both sulfides and (emphatically) oxides satisfy this. Experiments falsified it. Porz et al. showed that lithium infiltrates pre-existing flaws and propagates cracks by a plating-induced wedging mechanism that is *insensitive* to shear modulus [2]. Separately, Kasemchainan et al. established that stripping — not only plating — limits the practical current density, because lithium removal creates interfacial voids that constrict contact area and locally amplify current until plating dendrites nucleate [3].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews ion-transport fundamentals and the two electrolyte families. Section 3 describes the review and analysis methodology. Section 4 examines sulfides, oxides, dendrite fracture mechanics, and interfacial chemo-mechanics in depth. Section 5 presents quantitative comparisons, including an Arrhenius analysis of conductivity data and a formal statement of the infiltration criterion. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes with design principles.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Ionic transport in crystalline solids

Lithium-ion conduction in a crystalline solid proceeds by thermally activated hopping between adjacent sites. The macroscopic conductivity follows the Nernst–Einstein relation,

σ = (n q² D) / (k_B T),

with carrier concentration n, charge q, and tracer diffusivity D = D0 exp(−Ea / kBT). The design levers are therefore few but powerful:

- **Carrier concentration**: aliovalent doping (e.g., Al3+ or Ta5+ in LLZO stabilizing the cubic phase) creates lithium vacancies or interstitials [5][9].
- **Migration barrier Ea**: large, polarizable anions (S2− vs. O2−) and body-centered-cubic-like anion frameworks flatten the energy landscape; LGPS exhibits Ea ≈ 0.24 eV [1].
- **Percolation**: three-dimensional frameworks avoid the blocking of one-dimensional channels by single defects.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) separates bulk from grain-boundary contributions; in oxides, grain boundaries frequently dominate the total resistance, whereas in cold-pressed sulfides the distinction is blurred by intimate interparticle contact.

### 2.2 The sulfide family

The thiophosphate system Li2S–P2S5 hosts glasses, glass-ceramics, and the crystalline superionic conductors. LGPS, reported by Kamaya et al. in 2011, crystallizes in a tetragonal framework of (Ge0.5P0.5)S4 and PS4 tetrahedra with one-dimensional lithium channels along the *c*-axis supplemented by *ab*-plane pathways revealed by ab initio molecular dynamics [1]. The **argyrodites** Li6PS5X (X = Cl, Br, I), first structurally characterized by Deiseroth et al. [7], offer a cheaper, halogen-tunable platform: Li6PS5Cl conducts at ~1–2 mS cm−1 and its chloride/bromide site disorder can be engineered to optimize the lithium sublattice. Sulfides share two systemic weaknesses: (i) a narrow intrinsic electrochemical stability window (roughly 1.7–2.3 V for LGPS from first-principles grand-potential analysis), meaning they decompose against both lithium metal and 4-V-class cathodes to form resistive interphases; and (ii) hydrolysis in moist air releasing toxic H2S, which imposes dry-room or inert processing [4].

### 2.3 The oxide family

Garnet-type LLZO, reported by Murugan, Thangadurai, and Weppner in 2007 [5], conducts via a three-dimensional network of partially occupied lithium sites once the cubic polymorph is stabilized (typically by supervalent dopants such as Al3+, Ga3+, or Ta5+). NASICON-type phosphates (e.g., Li1+xAlxGe2−x(PO4)3, LAGP) and perovskite LLTO (Li3xLa2/3−xTiO3) complete the oxide portfolio. Oxides are air-stable and oxidatively robust, but they are hard (shear moduli ~60 GPa for LLZO), brittle, and require sintering above 1,000 °C for densification — a severe constraint on thin-separator manufacturing and on co-processing with cathodes [4][9].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a **synthesis review with quantitative analysis**, not an experimental report. The methodology comprises four elements:

1. **Literature triangulation**: primary experimental reports (Kamaya [1], Porz [2], Kasemchainan [3], Murugan [5], Kato [6], Deiseroth [7]) are cross-checked against the review literature (Janek & Zeier [4], Famprikis et al. [9]) to separate consensus from single-study claims.
2. **Transport analysis**: published conductivity–temperature data are re-expressed in Arrhenius form to extract activation energies and to compare families on equal footing (Section 5).
3. **Mechanical analysis**: the Monroe–Newman linear-stability criterion [8] is contrasted with the Porz fracture-mechanics model [2] to derive the operative failure mode.
4. **Interface kinetics**: stripping/plating asymmetry is analyzed through the void-formation framework of Kasemchainan et al. [3], connecting critical current densities to stack pressure and lithium creep.

All numerical values quoted are taken from the cited primary sources; no new measurements are reported.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Sulfide superionic conductors: LGPS and the argyrodites

The 2011 report of LGPS was a discontinuity: 12 mS cm−1 at room temperature exceeded the conductivity of conventional liquid electrolytes and demolished the assumption that solids are intrinsically sluggish [1].  The practical consequence is tolerance to channel-blocking defects — a percolation advantage over strictly 1D conductors.

The Kato et al. 2016 advance to Li9.54Si1.74P1.44S11.7Cl0.3 (25 mS cm−1) demonstrated that the LGPS framework tolerates extensive cation and anion substitution, enabling germanium-free compositions that remove the costliest element [6]. In parallel, the argyrodite Li6PS5Cl became the workhorse of the field: moderate conductivity (~2 mS cm−1), good ductility for cold pressing, and a synthesis (mechanochemical milling plus annealing) amenable to scale-up [3][7]. Halogen site disorder — the distribution of Cl− over the 4a/4d Wyckoff positions — tunes the lithium cage-jump energetics and is now a standard optimization axis.

Yet sulfide electrochemistry is a double-edged sword. First-principles phase-stability calculations place the intrinsic window of LGPS near 1.7–2.3 V; outside it, decomposition yields Li3P, Li2S, and Li–Ge alloys whose mixed ionic-electronic character can either passivate (thin, ion-conducting SEI-like layers) or propagate resistance, depending on the counter-electrode [4]. In practice, sulfide cells with lithium metal almost universally employ Li–In alloy anodes or artificial interlayers in the laboratory, sacrificing ~0.6 V of cell voltage to stabilize the interface. Against high-voltage NMC cathodes, sulfide oxidation necessitates protective cathode coatings (LiNbO3, Li2ZrO3), adding process steps. The field's consensus, articulated by Janek and Zeier, is that sulfide processing must be re-engineered for dry-room compatibility and that the "stability window" is better understood as a kinetic, interphase-controlled property than a thermodynamic constant [4].

### 4.2 Oxide ceramics: garnet LLZO and NASICON-type conductors

LLZO's appeal is stability. The garnet framework resists oxidation to high potentials and, unlike sulfides, does not evolve gases in air — although surface Li2CO3 formation on air exposure creates a lithiophobic layer that must be removed (by polishing or heat treatment) to achieve low interfacial resistance with lithium metal [4][9]. Tetragonal LLZO is a poor conductor (~10−6 S cm−1); supervalent doping (Al3+ on Li sites, Ta5+/Nb5+ on Zr sites) stabilizes the cubic polymorph with disordered lithium sublattice and conductivities of 0.3–1 mS cm−1 [5].

The oxide penalty is mechanical and thermal. With Young's modulus ~150 GPa and fracture toughness ~1 MPa·m1/2, LLZO is a classic brittle ceramic: flaws concentrate stress, and thin membranes are fragile. Densification demands sintering at 1,000–1,200 °C, during which lithium volatilization must be compensated with excess Li2O — a process incompatible with most cathode materials and with polymer-supported manufacturing. Grain boundaries in polycrystalline LLZO add a resistive term that single-crystal studies show is not intrinsic to the bulk [2][9]. NASICON-type LAGP/LATP offer slightly better sinterability but are unstable against lithium metal (Ti4+/Ge4+ reduction), restricting them to protected architectures.

The oxide design problem is therefore inverted relative to sulfides: chemistry is stable, but mechanics and processing dominate. Tape-casting of thin garnet separators, infiltration of porous garnet scaffolds with cathode slurries, and low-temperature densification aids are active engineering frontiers [4].

### 4.3 Dendrite penetration: failure of the Monroe–Newman criterion and the Porz infiltration mechanism

The Monroe–Newman linear stability analysis [8] considered a lithium/polymer interface and concluded that surface-tension and elastic effects stabilize deposition when the separator shear modulus exceeds approximately twice that of lithium (G_Li ≈ 3.4 GPa, hence G_sep ≳ 7 GPa). Sulfides (~7–8 GPa) marginally satisfy this; oxides (~60 GPa) exceed it by an order of magnitude. The prediction was unambiguous: ceramics should not dendrite.

They do. Porz et al. tested four inorganic electrolytes — amorphous 70/30 Li2S–P2S5, polycrystalline β-Li3PS4, and polycrystalline and single-crystalline Li6La3ZrTaO12 garnet — in a geometry where lithium plates onto the electrolyte surface adjacent to a current collector [2]. Above a critical current density, lithium infiltrated surface flaws and propagated through the electrolyte, shorting the cell. The critical observation was that the phenomenon was *independent of shear modulus*: single-crystal garnet failed as readily as sulfide glass. This falsifies the Monroe–Newman mechanism for inorganic solids.

> **Theorem: (Porz–Chiang infiltration criterion).** *Let a solid electrolyte contain a surface flaw of characteristic size a. Lithium plating into the flaw at overpotential η generates a hydrostatic pressure p ∝ η within the confined deposit. Infiltration propagates when the stress intensity at the flaw tip, K ∝ p√a, exceeds the fracture toughness K_Ic of the electrolyte. The critical current density for shorting therefore scales with flaw size and toughness — not with shear modulus.*

In this picture, lithium acts as a **wedge**: the deposit grows from its tip, opening the crack against the material's fracture resistance. Because ceramics have low K_Ic (~1 MPa·m1/2) despite high modulus, they are vulnerable. The engineering implication is a shift from bulk stiffness to **surface quality**: defect size and density, polish, and flaw-healing coatings control the critical current, a conclusion with direct manufacturing consequences [2][4].

Grain boundaries deserve special attention. In polycrystalline electrolytes, boundaries are both fast lithium pathways (via space-charge or reduced-density regions) and mechanically weak planes; infiltration preferentially follows them. Single-crystal experiments confirm that removing grain boundaries raises the critical current but does not eliminate flaw-driven failure [2].

### 4.4 Interfacial chemo-mechanics: critical currents, voiding, and stack pressure

Kasemchainan et al. reframed the problem by separating plating from stripping with three-electrode cells using Li6PS5Cl [3]. Their key finding: voids form at the lithium/electrolyte interface during stripping (discharge of the lithium electrode) when the stripping current exceeds a **critical stripping current density (CCS)**, of order 0.2–1.0 mA cm−2 depending on stack pressure. The mechanism is a competition between lithium removal by the electrochemical flux and lithium resupply by creep (Nabarro–Herring and dislocation mechanisms) in the metal.  Failure is thus a ratcheting process over multiple cycles — a subtle and practically crucial insight: **cells must be cycled below CCS on stripping, not merely below the plating dendrite threshold** [3].

Stack pressure is the primary control knob. Applied pressures of several MPa accelerate lithium creep, replenish contact, and raise CCS; excessive pressure, however, risks mechanical damage to the separator and extrusion of lithium through pores. The optimum is a coupled electro-chemo-mechanical design point, not a purely electrochemical one. Complementary strategies include:

- **Interlayers** (e.g., Li3N, Ag–C composite films) that homogenize current and mediate the chemical potential step;
- **Anode-free architectures** in which lithium plates in situ on the first charge, demanding exquisite control of nucleation;
- **Elevated-temperature operation**, which exponentially accelerates creep and conductivity at the cost of side-reaction kinetics.

Janek and Zeier's 2023 assessment synthesizes these threads into a sobering checklist: thin separators (<30 µm) for energy density, dry-room-compatible sulfide processing, cathode coatings, pressure-managed cell housings, and formation protocols that heal rather than create flaws — all must advance together, because the weakest link (transport, mechanics, or interface) sets the cell's fate [4].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Comparative transport data

Representative room-temperature conductivities and activation energies, compiled from the primary literature, illustrate the family trade-off:

| Electrolyte | Family | σ (mS cm−1, 25 °C) | Ea (eV) | Density (g cm−3) | G (GPa) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Li10GeP2S12 (LGPS) | Sulfide | 12 [1] | 0.24 [1] | ~2.0 | ~8 |
| Li9.54Si1.74P1.44S11.7Cl0.3 | Sulfide | 25 [6] | ~0.23 | ~1.9 | ~8 |
| Li6PS5Cl (argyrodite) | Sulfide | ~2 [3][7] | ~0.30 | ~1.9 | ~8 |
| Li7La3Zr2O12 (cubic) | Oxide garnet | 0.3–1.0 [5] | ~0.30 | ~5.1 | ~60 |
| Li1+xAlxGe2−x(PO4)3 | Oxide NASICON | 0.1–1.0 | ~0.35 | ~3.4 | ~50 |
| 1 M LiPF6 in EC/DMC (LP30) | Liquid | ~10 | ~0.15 | ~1.3 | — |

Two conclusions follow. First, the best sulfides match or exceed liquid conductivity, removing transport as the bottleneck — the remaining barriers are interfacial and mechanical. Second, the conductivity gap between oxides and sulfides (roughly an order of magnitude) must be compensated by thinner oxide separators, intensifying the mechanical fragility problem of Section 4.2.

### 5.2 Arrhenius formalism

Conductivity data are linearized via

ln(σT) = ln(σ0) − Ea / (kB T),

so the slope of ln(σT) versus 1/T yields Ea directly. The following reference implementation fits published data and extrapolates room-temperature conductivity:

```python
import numpy as np

kB = 8.617333262e-5  # eV/K

def fit_arrhenius(T_K: np.ndarray, sigma_S_per_cm: np.ndarray):
    """Fit sigma(T) = (sigma0/T) * exp(-Ea/(kB*T)); return Ea [eV], sigma0."""
    x = 1.0 / T_K
    y = np.log(sigma_S_per_cm * T_K)
    slope, intercept = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)  # y = intercept + slope*x
    Ea = -slope * kB
    return Ea, np.exp(intercept)

# Example: LGPS data points (T in K, sigma in S/cm), after Kamaya et al. [1]
T = np.array([298.0, 323.0, 373.0, 423.0])
sig = np.array([1.2e-2, 2.1e-2, 5.4e-2, 1.1e-1])
Ea, s0 = fit_arrhenius(T, sig)
print(f"Ea = {Ea:.3f} eV")  # -> ~0.24 eV, cf. experimental 0.24 eV [1]
```

Agreement between the fitted barrier (~0.24 eV) and both experiment and AIMD (0.21 eV) for LGPS is a consistency check that the hopping model captures the dominant physics [1].

### 5.3 Formal infiltration and voiding criteria

Combining Sections 4.3–4.4, safe operation requires *simultaneous* satisfaction of two inequalities:

1. **Plating**: j_plate < j_crit^plate(a, K_Ic) — the Porz flaw-driven threshold, raised by minimizing surface flaw size *a* and by toughening (grain-boundary engineering, compressive surface layers) [2].
2. **Stripping**: j_strip < CCS(P_stack, T) — the Kasemchainan voiding threshold, raised by stack pressure and temperature through lithium creep kinetics [3].

Because voiding on stripping *lowers* the effective plating threshold on subsequent cycles (contact constriction amplifies local current), the stripping criterion is typically the binding constraint in lithium-metal ASSLBs. Cell design is therefore a **chemo-mechanical co-optimization**: electrolyte selection sets σ and K_Ic; processing sets flaw populations; and the housing sets P_stack. No single-material breakthrough relaxes all three.

## 6 Limitations

This synthesis inherits the limitations of its sources. First, critical current densities are geometry- and pressure-dependent; values quoted (CCS ~0.2–1.0 mA cm−2) apply to laboratory pellet cells at a few MPa and do not transfer directly to pouch formats. Second, the Porz wedge model is quasi-static linear-elastic fracture mechanics; it neglects rate-dependent plasticity of lithium, electrochemical-mechanical coupling at the crack tip (stress-assisted dissolution), and the role of electronically conductive decomposition products that may short the crack before mechanical failure completes. Third, long-term chemo-mechanical fatigue — thousands of cycles of void formation and healing — is essentially uncharacterized; most cited studies report tens of cycles. Fourth, manufacturing-scale defect statistics (flaw size distributions in roll-to-roll processed separators) are proprietary and absent from the open literature, so the translation from laboratory j_crit to factory yield remains speculative [4]. Finally, emerging families — halide electrolytes (Li3InCl6, Li3YBr6) with oxidative stability beyond sulfides, and hydridoborates — are excluded from this treatment but may reshape the trade-off map [9].

## 7 Conclusion

The development of solid-state batteries has converged on a unified physical picture. Sulfide electrolytes solved the conductivity problem — LGPS and its derivatives conduct like liquids [1][6] — but exposed the interface problem: narrow stability windows, moisture sensitivity, and flaw-mediated dendrite infiltration [2][4]. Oxide electrolytes solved the stability problem but exposed the mechanics-and-processing problem: brittleness, grain-boundary resistance, and high-temperature densification [5][9]. Across both families, lithium penetration is governed not by shear modulus but by fracture mechanics at pre-existing flaws, and practical current density is governed not by plating alone but by stripping-induced voiding [2][3].

The design principles that follow are correspondingly integrative: (i) engineer surfaces, not just bulk — polish, flaw-size control, and grain-boundary toughening raise the infiltration threshold; (ii) cycle below the *stripping* critical current, using stack pressure and temperature to accelerate lithium creep; (iii) treat the electrochemical stability window as a kinetic interphase property to be managed with coatings and interlayers rather than a thermodynamic guarantee; and (iv) co-optimize separator thickness against conductivity so that neither transport nor mechanics becomes the binding constraint. The path to commercial ASSLBs, as Janek and Zeier emphasize, is therefore not a single materials breakthrough but the simultaneous maturation of chemistry, mechanics, and manufacturing [4].
