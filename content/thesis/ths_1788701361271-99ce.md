# NCCL and RDMA Collective Communication for Distributed Deep Learning: Ring and Tree All-Reduce Algorithms, GPUDirect RDMA, InfiniBand Congestion Control, and Scaling Laws to Ten Thousand GPUs

## Abstract

Modern large-scale deep learning is bounded not by floating-point throughput but by the collective communication that synchronizes gradients across thousands of accelerators. This thesis examines the algorithmic and systems foundations of that synchronization: NVIDIA's NCCL collective library and its ring and double-binary-tree all-reduce algorithms, the GPUDirect RDMA mechanism that moves data directly between GPU memory and the network adapter without CPU staging, the InfiniBand fabric that carries it, and the DCQCN congestion-control scheme that keeps multi-thousand-GPU fabrics from collapsing under incast. We derive the bandwidth-optimal lower bound of 2M(N-1)/N per rank, formalize the latency-bandwidth tradeoff between ring and tree schedules, analyze zero-copy data paths and ECN-driven rate control, and extend a cost model to ten thousand GPUs. The result is a coherent account of why bandwidth-optimal collectives, lossless fabrics, and explicit congestion control together determine the scaling efficiency of frontier AI training clusters.

---

## 1. Introduction

Frontier models contain hundreds of billions of parameters and train on trillions of tokens — a computational appetite no single accelerator can satisfy [6]. The industry response is *data-parallel distributed training*: thousands of GPUs each process a data shard, compute local gradients, and synchronize them so every rank applies an identical update before the next iteration.

That synchronization step is an **all-reduce** collective — every one of *N* processes contributes a vector of *M* elements, and every process receives the element-wise sum. In a 10,000-GPU run synchronizing a 1-terabyte gradient tensor, each iteration moves roughly *2M(N−1)/N* ≈ 2 TB through the fabric while GPUs sit idle. The all-reduce is therefore the pacing operation of the entire training loop: its latency sets a hard floor on step time, and its bandwidth efficiency determines how much expensive silicon sits idle waiting [5].

This thesis unifies four layers of this problem that are usually treated separately:

1. **Algorithms** — the ring and tree schedules implemented by NVIDIA's NCCL library, and the optimality theory that governs them [1][2].
2. **The data path** — GPUDirect RDMA, which eliminates CPU staging from the transfer path [8].
3. **The fabric** — InfiniBand's lossless transport and its credit-based flow control.
4. **Congestion control** — DCQCN, the ECN-driven rate-control scheme that keeps RDMA fabrics fair and stable at scale [4].

We compose these into a quantitative scaling model extended to ten thousand GPUs.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Data parallelism and the all-reduce bottleneck

In synchronous data-parallel SGD, each of *N* workers holds a full model replica and processes a distinct mini-batch. After the backward pass, gradients are averaged across all workers — exactly the gradient of the concatenated batch — via a single all-reduce over the gradient vector of size *M* [7].

The communication cost of the all-reduce has two components, classically captured by the **α–β model**: a per-message *latency* term α and a per-byte *bandwidth* term β (where 1/β is the link bandwidth). For an algorithm that sends *s* messages of total byte-volume *m* per rank, the time is approximately

$$T = s\alpha + m\beta$$

### 2.2 NCCL and the GPU collective ecosystem

The **NVIDIA Collective Communications Library (NCCL)** is the de facto standard for GPU-resident collectives. Unlike CPU-centric MPI, NCCL kernels execute on the GPU itself: communication primitives are fused into CUDA kernels so reductions are computed on data as it streams through, without intermediate host buffers [2]. NCCL discovers machine topology at initialization and maintains a *tuning model* selecting among several algorithms per call based on message size, rank count, and measured latency [2].

Key NCCL concepts include:

- **Channels**: NCCL runs multiple independent instances of an algorithm in parallel over distinct physical connections; more channels raise aggregate bandwidth until the physical links saturate.
- **Algorithms**: `Ring`, `Tree` (double binary tree), `CollNetDirect`/`CollNetChain` (in-network reduction via NVIDIA SHARP), `NVLS` (NVSwitch hardware reduction), and `PAT` (a Bruck-style schedule for all-gather/reduce-scatter) [2].
- **Protocols**: Simple, LL (low-latency), and LL128, trading payload efficiency against per-message latency.

### 2.3 InfiniBand and RDMA

InfiniBand (IB) is a switched fabric architecture designed from the ground up for remote direct memory access. Its link layer implements **hop-by-hop credit-based flow control**: a receiver grants the sender a finite number of credits, and the sender may only transmit when it holds a credit, guaranteeing that no packet is ever dropped due to buffer overflow [4]. This *lossless* property lets the IB transport protocol (L4) be simple and entirely NIC-resident — there is no kernel TCP stack, no software retransmission logic, and no CPU involvement in the steady-state data path.

The IB verbs interface exposes *one-sided operations* (RDMA write, RDMA read, atomics): a process registers a memory region with its NIC and a remote peer can read or write it without any involvement of the remote CPU. Modern GPU clusters use 200–400 Gb/s links (HDR/NDR), with multi-rail configurations per node.

### 2.4 GPUDirect RDMA

Ordinarily, moving data from GPU to network requires staging through host memory: `cudaMemcpy` to a pinned host buffer, then NIC DMA from host memory. **GPUDirect RDMA** eliminates the CPU from this path entirely: GPU device memory is exposed through the PCIe Base Address Register (BAR) window, and the NIC's DMA engine reads and writes GPU memory directly, peer-to-peer over PCIe [8].

### 2.5 DCQCN congestion control

Lossless flow control solves buffer overflow at a single hop but creates a new problem: **congestion spreading**. When many senders target one receiver (an *incast*, exactly what all-reduce traffic patterns produce), the receiver's ingress queue fills, credit backpressure propagates upstream, and innocent flows sharing those upstream buffers are paused — a *PFC storm* that can collapse fabric throughput [4].

**DCQCN** (Data Center Quantized Congestion Notification) is the standard end-to-end congestion-control scheme for RDMA fabrics:

1. Switches mark packets with the **ECN** bit when queue occupancy exceeds a threshold.
2. The receiver reflects these marks back as **CNPs** (Congestion Notification Packets).
3. The sender's NIC applies a quantized rate controller: on each CNP it multiplicatively decreases its rate, then probes for available bandwidth through fast-recovery, active-increase, and hyper-active-increase phases [4].

DCQCN runs entirely in NIC hardware, requires no host CPU, and is the mechanism that lets thousands of RDMA queue pairs share a fabric without persistent PFC pauses.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is a synthesis and analysis study. We do not report new cluster measurements; instead we derive the governing cost models from first principles, ground every constant and structural claim in published literature, and compose the layers into a scaling analysis. Our method has three parts:

1. **Optimality analysis.** We restate the bandwidth lower bound for all-reduce [1] and derive the α–β cost expressions for ring and double-binary-tree schedules, identifying the message-size crossover.
2. **Mechanism decomposition.** We trace the GPUDirect RDMA data path and the DCQCN control loop, quantifying where each removes a classical bottleneck (CPU copies, PFC head-of-line blocking).
3. **Scaling extrapolation.** We extend the α–β model with hierarchy (intra-node vs. inter-node bandwidth asymmetry) and evaluate it analytically at N = 10, 100, 1,000, and 10,000 ranks.

Where quantitative claims require empirical grounding, we cite published measurement studies: NCCL's performance model and tuning [2], the DCQCN evaluation at SIGCOMM [4], and distributed-training bottleneck analyses [5][6][7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The ring all-reduce: bandwidth optimality and the 2(N−1) step structure

The ring algorithm arranges the *N* ranks in a logical ring and partitions the *M*-element buffer into *N* chunks. Two phases follow [1]:

- **Reduce-scatter (N−1 steps):** each rank sends its reduction chunk to its successor, receives from its predecessor, and accumulates. After *N−1* steps, rank *r* holds the fully reduced chunk *r*.
- **All-gather (N−1 steps):** the reduced chunks circulate for another *N−1* steps, so every rank ends with all *N* chunks.

Per rank, the cost is therefore:

$$T_{ring} = 2(N-1)\alpha + \frac{2(N-1)M}{N}\beta$$

The bandwidth term *2M(N−1)/N* is not an accident of this schedule — it is a **lower bound**. Patarasuk and Yuan proved that in any all-reduce over *N* processes with an associative, commutative operator, some process must send and some process must receive at least *2M(N−1)/N* bytes [1]. The ring achieves the bound, so it is *bandwidth-optimal*: no algorithm can move fewer bytes per rank.

> **Theorem: (Bandwidth optimality of the ring [1]).** Let *M* be the total number of elements reduced over *N* processes. Any correct all-reduce requires at least one process to communicate ≥ *2M(N−1)/N* elements. The ring schedule communicates exactly *2M(N−1)/N* elements per process and is therefore bandwidth-optimal.

The price of optimality is latency: *2(N−1)* sequential steps — 19,998 message rounds at *N = 10,000*. But the ring pipelines naturally: each step's chunk is independent, so NCCL's multiple channels overlap chunks across steps and the *β* term dominates for large messages. This is why the ring is NCCL's default for large gradient tensors: gradient synchronization in LLM training is a large-message regime, and bandwidth is the scarce resource [2][7].

### 4.2 The double binary tree: logarithmic latency with near-ring bandwidth

The tree algorithm attacks the latency term. A single binary tree performs all-reduce by *reducing up* to the root then *broadcasting down*, in *2·⌈log₂N⌉* steps [2]:

$$T_{tree} = 2\lceil\log_2 N\rceil\,\alpha + 2M\beta$$

At *N = 10,000* that is ~28 steps instead of ~20,000 — but a naive tree is bandwidth-poor, since the root's links become a hotspot and per-rank traffic approaches *2M*.

NCCL's `Tree` is a **double binary tree** [2]: two binary trees over the same ranks with the invariant that *no rank is interior in both trees, and at most one rank is a leaf in both*. Each tree carries half the data (*M/2*) — Tree 1 reduces/broadcasts the first half while Tree 2 simultaneously handles the second half.

Because interior ranks in one tree are leaves in the other, every rank is active in exactly one tree at every level, giving per-rank traffic ≈ *2M(N−1)/N* with *2·log₂N* steps [2]:

$$T_{dbt} \approx 2\lceil\log_2 N\rceil\,\alpha + 2M\beta$$

The crossover between ring and double-binary-tree is governed by message size [9]:

| Regime | Dominant term | Preferred algorithm |
|---|---|---|
| Small messages (*Mβ ≪ α*) | Latency | Double binary tree |
| Large messages (*Mβ ≫ α*) | Bandwidth | Ring |

For gigabyte-scale gradient tensors the ring wins; for small control collectives and optimizer-state shards, the tree wins decisively [2][7].

### 4.3 GPUDirect RDMA: the zero-copy data path

The cost models above count *bytes on the wire* — but bytes must first reach the wire.

GPUDirect RDMA restructures this into a single device-resident pipeline [8]:

1. **Registration.** GPU memory is pinned and registered with the NIC, which obtains the GPU's PCIe BAR mapping to address device memory directly.
2. **Descriptor posting.** The NCCL kernel posts RDMA write descriptors whose source addresses are GPU virtual addresses.
3. **Peer-to-peer DMA.** The NIC's DMA engine pulls data directly from GPU memory over PCIe — no host staging, no CPU copies — and injects it into the InfiniBand fabric as RDMA writes targeting the peer GPU's memory.
4. **Completion.** The receiving NIC writes directly into the destination GPU's memory and posts a completion event the CUDA stream synchronizes against.

The consequences are threefold: lower small-message *latency* (no CPU round trips), higher *bandwidth* (limited by PCIe/NIC, not host memory), and — most important at scale — *overlap*: stream-ordered completions let frameworks overlap the all-reduce of layer *l*'s gradients with the backward pass of layer *l−1*, hiding much of the communication cost behind computation [5].

A minimal illustration of the cost-model reasoning used throughout this thesis:

```python
import math

def t_ring(N, M_GB, alpha_us=3.0, bw_GBs=25.0):
    steps = 2 * (N - 1)
    return steps * alpha_us * 1e-6 + 2 * (N - 1) / N * M_GB / bw_GBs

def t_dbt(N, M_GB, alpha_us=3.0, bw_GBs=25.0):
    steps = 2 * math.ceil(math.log2(N))
    return steps * alpha_us * 1e-6 + 2 * M_GB / bw_GBs

for N in (8, 128, 1024, 10000):
    for M in (0.004, 4.0):  # 4 MB control msg vs 4 GB gradient tensor
        print(f"N={N:5d} M={M:>6}GB  ring={t_ring(N,M)*1e3:9.2f} ms"
              f"  tree={t_dbt(N,M)*1e3:9.2f} ms")
```

The output reproduces the textbook crossover: the tree dominates at 4 MB for large *N* (latency-bound), while the two converge at 4 GB (bandwidth-bound) — exactly the behavior NCCL's tuning model encodes [2][9].

### 4.4 InfiniBand fabrics and DCQCN: keeping 10,000 endpoints honest

Lossless flow control solves buffer overflow at a single hop but creates *congestion spreading*: all-reduce phases produce many-to-one incast bursts that fill switch buffers, credit backpressure propagates upstream, and innocent flows sharing those buffers are paused — a *PFC storm* that can collapse fabric throughput [4].

- **Detection:** switches mark the ECN bit on packets when the egress queue exceeds a threshold *K_min* (probabilistically up to *K_max*).
- **Notification:** the receiver's NIC generates a Congestion Notification Packet (CNP) for each marked packet (rate-limited).
- **Reaction:** on each CNP, the sender cuts its rate: *R ← R·(1 − α/2)*, where the gain *α* is itself adapted — increased when CNPs keep arriving, decayed toward zero in quiet periods.
- **Recovery:** after a quiet interval, the sender enters *fast recovery* (5 rounds holding rate), then *active increase* (additive, ~5 Mb/s per 55 µs by default), then *hyper-active increase* if no CNPs arrive — rapidly reclaiming freed bandwidth [4].

Three properties make DCQCN load-bearing for large training: the entire loop runs in NIC hardware (microsecond-scale response, no host CPU); the quantized rate table bounds oscillation and guarantees convergence to fairness [4]; and PFC pauses are reserved as a safety net for severe transient overload while DCQCN's ECN loop handles the steady state — directly raising all-reduce goodput by eliminating head-of-line blocking of innocent flows [4].

> **Theorem: (Congestion control as a scaling prerequisite).** In a lossless RDMA fabric, without end-to-end congestion control every incast episode becomes credit backpressure propagating through shared buffers. DCQCN's ECN→CNP→rate-cut loop is therefore a *correctness mechanism for performance*: without it, all-reduce bandwidth at thousands of ranks collapses toward the PFC-limited rate regardless of link capacity [4].

Complementing DCQCN at the largest scales is **in-network reduction**: NVIDIA SHARP (exposed through NCCL's `CollNet` algorithms) performs the element-wise sum *inside the InfiniBand switches* rather than at endpoints, halving endpoint traffic and changing the machine model so the *2M(N−1)/N* bound no longer applies [2].

### 4.5 Scaling laws to ten thousand GPUs

We now compose the layers into a single scaling model. A hierarchical all-reduce — reduce-scatter intra-node, all-reduce across the *N/8* node-leaders, all-gather intra-node — has cost

$$T_{hier} \approx \underbrace{\frac{2M}{B_{intra}}}_{\text{intra-node}} + \underbrace{2\frac{(K-1)}{K}M\beta_{inter} + 2(K-1)\alpha_{inter}}_{\text{inter-node ring over } K \text{ nodes}}$$

where *K = N/8*. The *bandwidth* term is asymptotically independent of *N*, while the *latency* term grows linearly in *K* for the ring and logarithmically for the tree. At *N = 10,000* (*K = 1,250* nodes): the ring needs *2(K−1) = 2,498* inter-node rounds (~7.5 ms at 3 µs/hop), while the double-binary-tree needs only *2·⌈log₂K⌉ = 22* rounds (~66 µs).

Hence the practical scaling law of frontier training: **bandwidth-optimal hierarchical rings for gradient tensors, latency-optimal trees for everything else, DCQCN holding the fabric fair, and GPUDirect RDMA keeping the CPU out of the path** [2][4][5].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The bandwidth lower bound (proof sketch)

> **Theorem: (Patarasuk–Yuan bandwidth lower bound [1]).** Any all-reduce over *N* processes and *M* elements with an associative, commutative operator requires some process to send ≥ *2M(N−1)/N* elements and some process to receive ≥ *2M(N−1)/N* elements.

*Proof sketch.* Each process must *receive* at least *M(N−1)/N* elements it did not initially hold, and its own data must reach the other *N−1* processes, contributing at least *M(N−1)/N* sent. The reduce phase (computing the sum, requiring inbound flow) and the broadcast phase (disseminating it, requiring outbound flow) are information-theoretically independent, giving the factor of two: *2M(N−1)/N* in each direction. ∎

The ring achieves exactly this bound (§4.1); recursive-doubling/halving also achieves it when *N* is a power of two; the double binary tree approaches it within the *(N−1)/N* factor (§4.2) [1][9].

### 5.2 Latency-bandwidth crossover (analytical)

Setting *T_ring = T_dbt* in the α–β model:

$$2(N-1)\alpha + \frac{2(N-1)M}{N}\beta = 2\log_2 N\,\alpha + 2M\beta$$

Solving for the crossover message size:

$$M^* = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot\frac{(N-1) - \log_2 N}{\frac{N-1}{N} - 1} \approx \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot\frac{N}{\log_2 N}\quad\text{for large } N$$

With *α = 3 µs* and *β⁻¹ = 25 GB/s*, *α/β = 75 KB*; at *N = 10,000*, *M* ≈ 56 MB*. Messages below ~56 MB favor the tree; above it, the ring — consistent with NCCL's empirical tuning tables [2][9].

### 5.3 Published measurement evidence

- **NCCL tuning and bus bandwidth.** NCCL's *bus bandwidth* normalization — *B = S/t · 2(N−1)/N* for all-reduce — is precisely the Patarasuk–Yuan bound used as a yardstick: measured bus bandwidth near the hardware peak proves the schedule is bandwidth-optimal in practice [2].
- **The network is not always the bottleneck — until it is.** A widely cited measurement study found scaling efficiency plateaus once network bandwidth exceeds ~25 Gb/s *for the models and stacks tested* — because the software stack, not the wire, became the limiter [5]. GPUDirect RDMA and kernel-fused NCCL collectives attack exactly this regime: removing the software from the path lets the wire become the limit again, at which point the bounds of §5.1–5.2 bind.
- **Communication surveys.** Comprehensive surveys confirm that gradient/parameter synchronization dominates training time as systems scale, and catalog the mitigations — compression, reduced synchronization frequency, topology-aware collectives, in-network aggregation — as the central research program of the field [6][7].

## 6. Limitations

1. **The α–β model is a simplification.** Real collectives incur PCIe transfer costs, kernel-launch overhead, NIC descriptor processing, multi-hop switch serialization, and protocol-dependent overheads (NCCL's LL protocol sends 8-byte payloads per 16-byte slot). The model's predictions are order-of-magnitude guides, not performance contracts [2][9].
2. **Topology is not a ring or a tree.** Production fabrics are multi-stage Clos networks with oversubscription and adaptive routing. Embedding a logical ring into a Clos introduces hop-count variance and contention that the analysis of [1] assumes away; NCCL mitigates this with topology detection and channel placement, but pathological job placements still degrade performance.
3. **DCQCN assumes well-behaved endpoints.** Its fairness guarantees rest on all endpoints honestly implementing the rate controller. Misconfigured or non-compliant flows can capture unfair shares, and extreme incast can still produce transient PFC storms — an active research area with proposals like HPCC and receiver-driven schemes [4].
4. **Overlap has limits.** Computation-communication overlap hides all-reduce latency only when the backward pass produces gradients in an order matching the communication schedule; the final buckets and the optimizer step remain exposed [5].
5. **Beyond all-reduce.** Frontier training increasingly relies on all-to-all for mixture-of-experts routing and reduce-scatter/all-gather pairs for ZeRO-style sharding — each with its own optimality theory, not analyzed here [7].
6. **Power and reliability at 10k GPUs.** At ten thousand accelerators, mean-time-between-failures is measured in hours; checkpoint-restart and elastic training dominate wall-clock efficiency as much as collectives do.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The all-reduce is the narrow waist of distributed deep learning: every data-parallel training job funnels its synchronization through this single collective. This thesis has shown that its performance is governed by a clean stack of ideas — the *2M(N−1)/N* bandwidth lower bound and the ring schedule that achieves it [1]; the double binary tree trading negligible bandwidth for logarithmic latency [2]; GPUDirect RDMA's removal of the CPU from the data path [8]; InfiniBand's lossless transport; and DCQCN's ECN-driven control loop that keeps the fabric stable under incast [4].

The scaling law that emerges is simple to state and demanding to realize: make the bandwidth term independent of *N* (bandwidth-optimal schedules, hierarchical decomposition, in-network reduction where available), make the latency term logarithmic (trees for small messages), and keep the fabric fair (DCQCN, tuned ECN thresholds, PFC as last resort). As clusters push past ten thousand GPUs, the frontier lies in pushing computation into the network itself — switch-resident reduction and faster congestion control. The mathematics of the lower bound will not change; everything else is engineering.
