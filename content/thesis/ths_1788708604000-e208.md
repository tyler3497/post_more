# Omnigenous by Design: Quasi-Symmetry, Neoclassical Transport, and the Stellarator Route to Magnetic Fusion

## Abstract

The stellarator offers the only established route to steady-state magnetic fusion confinement without reliance on a large, potentially disruptive plasma current, at the price of surrendering axisymmetry and with it the automatic confinement guarantees of the tokamak. This thesis develops the modern theoretical and computational framework by which that price is managed: the theory of quasi-symmetry, omnigenity, and quasi-isodynamicity as *hidden symmetries* of the magnetic field strength that restore tokamak-like orbit confinement in three-dimensional geometry; the kinetic theory of neoclassical transport in the low-collisionality **1/&nu;** regime and its control through the effective ripple &epsilon;<sub>eff</sub>; and the two-stage numerical optimization paradigm — equilibrium shaping followed by modular-coil synthesis — implemented in codes such as **STELLOPT**, **ROSE**, and **SIMSOPT**. The framework is confronted with experiment through Wendelstein 7-X, whose 2021 campaign demonstrated neoclassically optimized confinement at record stellarator triple product, and with the emerging generation of compact, precisely quasi-symmetric configurations that challenge the historical assumption that stellarators must be large, expensive, and coil-limited [1][3].

## 1 Introduction

Magnetic confinement fusion rests on a deceptively simple requirement: hold a hundred-million-degree plasma long enough, at high enough density, that fusion power exceeds the power needed to sustain it. The tokamak pursues this with *axisymmetry*. In an axisymmetric torus the toroidal canonical momentum

$$p_\phi = m v_\phi R - Z e \psi$$

is an exact invariant of guiding-centre motion, and confinement of collisionless orbits follows almost for free. The cost is equally fundamental: the poloidal field that provides the rotational transform &iota; must be generated by a toroidal **plasma current**, which must be driven non-inductively for steady-state operation, which in turn drives kink instabilities, and whose sudden loss — a *disruption* — can deposit megajoules on the wall in milliseconds [1].

The stellarator, proposed by Spitzer in 1951, inverts the trade. The confining field is produced **entirely by external coils**, so the plasma is intrinsically steady-state and disruption-free. But without axisymmetry, nothing guarantees that field lines trace out nested flux surfaces, that trapped-particle orbits are confined, or that cross-field transport is tolerable. Early stellarators confirmed the pessimistic reading: confinement was poor, and the concept was widely regarded as an elegant dead end. The modern stellarator program is the story of how that verdict was overturned — not by restoring symmetry, which is impossible for a non-axisymmetric vacuum field in the strict sense, but by discovering *which* symmetries actually matter, and by developing the computational machinery to impose them on real coil sets [1][2].

Three ideas structure this thesis:

1. **Hidden symmetry**: only the *magnitude* |**B**|, not the vector field, must be symmetric (in suitable coordinates) to recover tokamak-like orbit confinement. This yields quasi-symmetry, omnigenity, and quasi-isodynamicity.
2. **Neoclassical control**: in 3D geometry the dominant non-turbulent loss channel is the collisionless **1/&nu;** regime, whose strength is captured by a single geometric figure of merit, the effective ripple &epsilon;<sub>eff</sub>.
3. **Optimization as design**: the space of 3D equilibria is vast enough that numerical optimization — from target physics to manufacturable coils — is not a refinement but the *enabling technology* of the concept [4][6].

## 2 Background: From Axisymmetry to Three-Dimensional Confinement

### 2.1 The tokamak bargain and its limits

The tokamak's virtues are real and hard-won: the axisymmetric field guarantees nested flux surfaces (absent islands), the bootstrap current partially self-drives the required plasma current, and decades of scaling studies culminating in ITER's physics basis give credible extrapolations to burning plasmas. Yet the bargain has sharp edges:

- **Disruptions** remain an existential engineering risk; ITER's mitigation systems are among its most complex subsystems.
- **Steady-state current drive** is energetically expensive — recirculating power fractions of tens of percent erode the economics of a reactor.
- **Density and beta limits** (Greenwald, Troyon) constrain the operating window.

These are not arguments against the tokamak; they are the motivation for a parallel path whose failure modes are *different*.

### 2.2 The stellarator's degrees of freedom

In a stellarator the rotational transform is produced by the external field's 3D shaping, conventionally decomposed into *toroidal*, *helical*, and *vertical* field components in classical (heliotron/torsatron) designs, or into fully **modular non-planar coils** in the modern Helias line. Because the plasma current can be near zero:

- Operation is **intrinsically steady-state** — no current-drive recirculating power.
- There is **no current-driven kink**, and no disruption in the tokamak sense.
- The density limit is a radiative/operational boundary, not a hard MHD one.

The price is paid in two currencies: *coil complexity* (the coils that produce a precision 3D field are geometrically intricate and expensive) and *neoclassical transport* (the 3D ripple opens loss channels with no tokamak analogue). Both are addressed by optimization, but only if the right targets are chosen — which requires the kinetic theory reviewed next [1].

> **Theorem (Boozer, 1983 — transport and isomorphic equilibria):** In Boozer magnetic coordinates (&psi;, &theta;, &phi;), the guiding-centre drift equations depend on the magnetic field *only* through |**B**|(&psi;, &theta;, &phi;) and the rotational transform. Consequently, two equilibria with the same |**B**| in Boozer coordinates have identical collisionless orbit topology and identical neoclassical transport, regardless of how differently their vector fields are shaped [1].

This theorem is the foundation of everything that follows: it tells us that the design variable that matters is a *scalar field* on flux surfaces, not the full vector geometry.

## 3 Methodology: The Two-Stage Optimization Paradigm

Modern stellarator design proceeds in two stages, a decomposition pioneered for Wendelstein 7-X and now standard in **SIMSOPT**, **STELLOPT**, and **ROSE** workflows [4]:

**Stage 1 — Physics optimization.** The plasma boundary is parametrized (typically a Fourier series in poloidal and toroidal angles with stellarator symmetry) and varied to minimize a weighted objective:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_k w_k \left(\frac{f_k - f_k^{\mathrm{target}}}{\sigma_k}\right)^2$$

where the $f_k$ include quasi-symmetry error measures, the effective ripple &epsilon;<sub>eff</sub>, MHD stability proxies (magnetic well, Mercier criterion), the bootstrap current, fast-particle loss fractions, and coil-plasma separation. Each evaluation requires a 3D MHD equilibrium — usually **VMEC**, which assumes nested flux surfaces and minimizes the MHD energy functional — followed by post-processing (e.g., **BOOZ_XFORM** to Boozer coordinates, **NEO**/**SFINCS**/**DKES** for neoclassical coefficients) [4].

**Stage 2 — Coil synthesis.** Given the optimized target boundary, find filamentary (or finite-build) coil shapes whose Biot–Savart field reproduces the target normal field on the boundary, minimizing the *quadratic flux* while penalizing coil curvature, length, coil–coil and coil–plasma proximity. Regularization is essential: the inverse problem is severely ill-posed, and unregularized solutions produce unbuildable coils [5].

```python
# SIMSOPT-style stage-2 coil optimization (simplified)
from simsopt.geo import SurfaceRZFourier, create_equally_spaced_curves
from simsopt.field import BiotSavart, Current, coils_via_symmetries
from simsopt.objectives import SquaredFlux, LeastSquaresProblem

# ...target plasma boundary from a stage-1 VMEC optimization...
surf = SurfaceRZFourier.from_vmec_input("input.LandremanPaul_QA",
                                        range="half period", nphi=64, ntheta=64)

# ...initial guess: equally spaced circular coils, then optimize shapes...
base_curves = create_equally_spaced_curves(n_base=4, n_coils=16, r_major=5.5)
coils = coils_via_symmetries(base_curves, [Current(1e6)] * 4)
bs = BiotSavart(coils)
J = SquaredFlux(surf, bs)          # normal-field error on target surface
prob = LeastSquaresProblem(J)      # + curvature/length penalties in practice
prob.solve()                       # trust-region / L-BFGS over curve dofs
print("max |B*n| error:", J.J())
```

Gradient-based optimization with adjoint or finite-difference gradients (MPI-parallelized in SIMSOPT) routinely handles hundreds of degrees of freedom, and the 2022 demonstration that *precisely* quasi-symmetric fields can be reproduced by coils of only moderate complexity removed the last conceptual objection to the two-stage approach [5][4].

## 4 Deep Dive: Hidden Symmetries and the 1/&nu; Regime

### 4.1 Quasi-symmetry: symmetry where it counts

A field is **quasi-symmetric** if, in Boozer coordinates, the field strength depends on the angles only through a single helical combination:

$$|**B**|(\psi, \theta, \phi) = B(\psi, M\theta - N\phi)$$

for integers $(M, N)$. Three families result:

| Family | $(M, N)$ | Character | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quasi-axisymmetry (QA) | $N = 0$ | Contours close toroidally | NCSX design |
| Quasi-helical symmetry (QH) | $M = 1, N \ne 0$ | Contours close helically | HSX experiment |
| Quasi-poloidal symmetry (QP) | $M = 0$ | Contours close poloidally | (no compact realization) |

Quasi-symmetry implies an *approximate* conserved canonical momentum, so trapped-particle orbits are confined exactly as in a tokamak, neoclassical transport is intrinsically ambipolar, and the bootstrap current is tokamak-like [1]. Exact quasi-symmetry is impossible globally (a theorem of Garren and Boozer), but the 2022 result of Landreman and Paul — configurations quasi-symmetric to *orders of magnitude* better precision than previously achieved, with alpha-particle losses below 0.04% at reactor scale — showed that the approximation can be made essentially perfect for practical purposes [2]. The follow-up coil study demonstrated these fields are realizable with realistic modular coils, closing the loop from theory to hardware [5].

### 4.2 Omnigenity and quasi-isodynamicity

Quasi-symmetry is sufficient for good orbit confinement but not necessary. The weaker, more general condition is **omnigenity**: the bounce-averaged radial drift of every trapped particle vanishes, i.e., drift surfaces coincide with flux surfaces (Cary & Shasharina, 1997). A particularly reactor-attractive subclass is the **quasi-isodynamic (QI)** field (Gori et al., 1994; Helander & N&uuml;hrenberg, 2009):

- Contours of constant |**B**| **close poloidally** on each flux surface;
- Maxima and minima of |**B**| are aligned so that all trapped particles precess in the same direction;
- The **bootstrap current is minimal** — a decisive advantage, since bootstrap-driven transform evolution complicates island-divertor operation.

Wendelstein 7-X is an approximately QI configuration, and the newest single-field-period QI designs achieve this at aspect ratios far below the historical $A \gtrsim 10$, pointing toward *compact* stellarator reactors [6].

> **Theorem (Helander & N&uuml;hrenberg, 2009):** In a perfectly quasi-isodynamic stellarator, the bootstrap current vanishes identically and the radial neoclassical current is intrinsically ambipolar at all collisionalities — the stellarator recovers the two most economically valuable properties of the tokamak (current-free steady state, no $E_r$-driven transport bifurcations) without axisymmetry [1].

### 4.3 Neoclassical transport and the 1/&nu; regime

In a rippled 3D field, helically trapped particles execute superbanana orbits with large radial excursions. In the low-collisionality **1/&nu; regime** — the regime that matters for reactors, where the collision frequency &nu; is smaller than the effective ripple-trapping frequency — the diffusion coefficient *increases* as collisionality *decreases*:

$$D_{1/\nu} \propto \frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3/2} v_{th}^2}{\nu_{\mathrm{eff}}}$$

a scaling with no tokamak analogue and catastrophic if uncontrolled: it grows as $T^{7/2}$, so that a reactor-hot stellarator plasma would leak energy faster the hotter it gets. The geometric factor &epsilon;<sub>eff</sub>, the **effective ripple**, distills the entire 3D shaping problem into a single scalar: it measures the radial drift of ripple-trapped particles averaged over the trapped population, and it became the *primary* optimization target of the W7-X design [3][1].

Optimization for small &epsilon;<sub>eff</sub> must simultaneously control the **radial electric field** $E_r$, because stellarator neoclassical particle fluxes are not intrinsically ambipolar (except in quasi-symmetry): $E_r$ adjusts itself to enforce ambipolarity, and the resulting "electron root" vs "ion root" solutions have very different confinement. Recent theory shows that sufficiently optimized configurations can even sustain a *positive* (outward) $E_r$ in the core, expelling high-Z impurities — turning a long-standing liability into a design feature [7].

### 4.4 Coil synthesis: from target field to manufacturable magnets

Stage-2 optimization is where physics meets engineering reality. The design variables are the 3D curves of dozens of coils; the constraints are brutal:

1. **Field accuracy**: reproduce the target $|B\cdot n|$ on the plasma boundary to $\sim 10^{-3}$ relative error, or islands and error fields destroy the optimized physics.
2. **Curvature and torsion limits**: superconducting cable-in-conduit conductors cannot bend below a minimum radius.
3. **Coil–plasma and coil–coil separation**: needed for blanket, shielding, and assembly access.
4. **Forces and tolerances**: EM loads on non-planar coils reach hundreds of tonnes; manufacturing tolerances must be held against the field-error budget.

Modern approaches — regularized least-squares in SIMSOPT, adjoint-based shape gradients, and increasingly **permanent-magnet** augmentation that replaces complex coil segments with rare-earth magnet arrays — have steadily relaxed these constraints. The PNAS coil study for precise quasi-symmetry showed that with modest curvature and generous coil–coil spacing, quasi-symmetry error can be driven down to the level of Earth's magnetic field relative to the 1 T confining field [5][4].

## 5 Empirical Validation: Wendelstein 7-X and the Compact Renaissance

### 5.1 Wendelstein 7-X: optimization tested at scale

Wendelstein 7-X at Greifswald is the largest stellarator ever built and the first designed by large-scale numerical optimization: major radius $R = 5.5$ m, aspect ratio $A \approx 10$, five field periods, 50 non-planar plus 20 planar superconducting coils, with a HELIAS equilibrium targeting reduced neoclassical transport, small bootstrap current, and MHD stability at finite &beta; [3].

The decisive test came in the 2017–2018 campaigns and was reported in *Nature* in 2021: high-temperature discharges achieved **record stellarator triple products**, and — crucially — the measured energy confinement was shown to be *consistent with neoclassical predictions only if* the magnetic configuration possessed the optimized low &epsilon;<sub>eff</sub>. Recomputing the same plasma profiles in less-optimized configurations (W7-X high-mirror variant, LHD geometries) yields neoclassical energy fluxes *exceeding the available heating power* — physically impossible, hence proof by contradiction that the optimization is what makes the observed performance possible [3]. Complementary studies confirmed the predicted magnetic-configuration dependence of confinement and validated neoclassical flow and $E_r$ calculations against charge-exchange spectroscopy [1][3].

### 5.2 The compact stellarator renaissance

W7-X proved the concept but also the cost: large aspect ratio, 70 superconducting coils, exquisite tolerances. The second empirical wave attacks the economics:

- **Precise quasi-symmetry** (Landreman & Paul, 2022) demonstrated that QA fields can be optimized orders of magnitude beyond NCSX/HSX levels, with reactor-scaled alpha losses of 0.04% — and realized with as few as 16 modular coils [2][5].
- **Single-field-period QI** configurations (Goodman et al.) showed quasi-isodynamicity at low aspect ratio, collapsing the historical requirement $A \gtrsim 10$ and with it the machine size [6].
- **Turbulence-optimized QI** designs (Goodman et al., 2024, *PRX Energy*) simultaneously minimized ITG-driven turbulent transport, addressing the loss channel that dominates once neoclassical transport is optimized away.
- **Permanent-magnet stellarators** replace portions of the coil set with optimized rare-earth magnet arrays, potentially simplifying the most complex coils dramatically.

Together these results reframe the stellarator from "the expensive steady-state alternative" to a concept whose *design space is still being mapped* — with the mapping itself now automated [2][4][6].

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

Intellectual honesty requires stating what optimization has *not* solved:

1. **Turbulent transport** remains the dominant loss channel in optimized devices; gyrokinetic turbulence optimization is a decade behind neoclassical optimization in maturity, and nonlinear ITG/TEM/ETG physics in 3D geometry still holds surprises.
2. **The divertor and exhaust**: the island divertor of W7-X works, but extrapolating edge physics, detachment, and tungsten impurity control to a reactor remains an open experimental program.
3. **Coil cost and tolerance**: even "simple" modular coils are precision superconducting hardware; the tolerance budget that preserves quasi-symmetry under as-built errors is still being quantified, though a-posteriori tolerance optimization is now part of the toolkit [5].
4. **Finite-&beta; and MHD**: most precise-QS optimizations are vacuum or low-&beta;; self-consistent finite-&beta; equilibria with bootstrap currents and island healing (SPEC) add cost and fragility to the optimization.
5. **The $E_r$ bifurcation risk**: non-ambipolar neoclassical fluxes admit multiple $E_r$ roots; transitions between them are poorly understood experimentally and could degrade confinement in unprobed regimes [7].

None of these is a no-go theorem. All are active research fronts — which is precisely the point: the stellarator program has moved from questioning *whether* 3D fields can confine to engineering *how well*.

## 7 Conclusion

The stellarator's journey from Spitzer's figure-eight to Wendelstein 7-X's record triple products is, at bottom, a triumph of *applied mathematics over geometry*. The key realizations — that only |**B**| in Boozer coordinates governs orbit confinement [1]; that quasi-symmetry, omnigenity, and quasi-isodynamicity form a hierarchy of achievable hidden symmetries; that the 1/&nu; regime's $T^{7/2}$ catastrophe is controlled by a single scalar &epsilon;<sub>eff</sub>; and that the two-stage optimization pipeline can convert these abstractions into windable copper and steel [4][5] — have converted the stellarator from a qualitative idea into a quantitative design discipline.

The experimental ledger now includes demonstrated neoclassical optimization at reactor-relevant temperatures [3], precisely quasi-symmetric fields realizable with modest coils [2][5], and compact quasi-isodynamic configurations that break the large-aspect-ratio paradigm [6]. The remaining challenges — turbulence, exhaust, cost — are shared with every magnetic fusion concept, while the stellarator's unique gifts, steady-state operation and disruption immunity, remain unmatched. If fusion's hardest problems are engineering problems, the concept that *designs its physics* rather than *tolerating its physics* deserves its place on the path to a reactor.
