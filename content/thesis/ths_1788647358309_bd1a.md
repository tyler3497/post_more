# Probabilistic Programming and Differentiable Inference: Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, NUTS, ADVI, and the Semantics of Conditioned Models

## Abstract

Probabilistic programming languages promise to separate the specification of statistical models from the mechanics of inference, yet this separation rests on two subtly different foundations: the *meaning* of a conditioned program and the *algorithms* that approximate its posterior. This thesis develops a unified treatment of differentiable inference for conditioned models. We begin with the measure-theoretic semantics of conditioning: programs denote unnormalized densities, and normalization is an act of disintegration that is ill-posed at zero-probability observations without further structure [6]. We then analyze the four pillars of modern gradient-based inference: Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with symplectic leapfrog integration [1][2], the No-U-Turn Sampler with recursive trajectory doubling and dual-averaging step-size adaptation [3], automatic differentiation variational inference with reparameterization gradients [4], and normalizing flows as arbitrarily flexible variational families [8]. Finally we examine compositional inference — effect handlers, sequential Monte Carlo, and programmable inference in systems such as Gen and Pyro [7][9][10] — and ground the theory in production systems, notably Stan [5]. We state precise correctness conditions, exhibit failure modes, and map open problems in conditioning semantics and differentiable inference.

## 1 Introduction

A probabilistic program is an ordinary program in which randomness is first-class: `sample` draws a value from a distribution and `observe` conditions on data. Executed naively, such a program describes a *generative process*; conditioned on observations, it describes a *posterior* over latent variables. The promise of probabilistic programming is that a practitioner writes the model once, and the system derives inference automatically. This raises two coupled questions: *what does a conditioned program mean?* and *how do we compute with that meaning?*

These questions are deeper than they appear. The meaning of conditioning on a zero-probability event requires disintegration, and its interaction with program structure exposes genuine paradoxes [6]. Meanwhile, the workhorse algorithms for continuous models exploit gradients of the log density: Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) [1][2], its adaptive extension NUTS [3], and variational methods based on automatic differentiation (ADVI) [4] and normalizing flows [8].

This thesis contributes a unified account in five movements: the semantics of conditioning and the zero-probability observation problem; HMC from Hamiltonian mechanics, emphasizing why symplectic leapfrog integration is load-bearing; NUTS as slice sampling on a recursively doubled tree with dual-averaging adaptation; ADVI's reparameterization gradients and normalizing-flow variational families; and compositional inference via effect handlers, SMC, and programmable inference in Gen and Pyro [7][9][10]. Throughout, we anchor claims in the primary literature and in Stan, where NUTS and ADVI meet real models [5].

## 2 Background

We consider a Bayesian model with latent variables $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and observed data $y$. The model specifies a prior density $p(\theta)$ and a likelihood $p(y \mid \theta)$; inference targets the posterior

$$p(\theta \mid y) = \frac{p(\theta)\,p(y \mid \theta)}{p(y)}, \qquad p(y) = \int p(\theta)\,p(y \mid \theta)\,d\theta.$$

The normalizing constant $p(y)$ — the *marginal likelihood* or *evidence* — is intractable for essentially all models of interest, which forces approximate inference. Two families dominate.

**Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)** constructs a chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior. The Metropolis–Hastings kernel proposes $\theta'$ from $q(\cdot \mid \theta)$ and accepts with probability $\min(1, \alpha)$, where $\alpha$ involves only *ratios* of the unnormalized density $\gamma(\theta) = p(\theta)p(y\mid\theta)$ — the intractable $p(y)$ cancels. Random-walk proposals, however, explore high-dimensional posteriors diffusively: traversal time grows quadratically with dimension.

**Variational inference (VI)** turns integration into optimization: posit a tractable family $\mathcal{Q} = \{q_\phi\}$, minimize $\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi \,\|\, p(\cdot \mid y))$, equivalently maximize the *evidence lower bound* (ELBO)

$$\mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\!\left[\log p(\theta, y) - \log q_\phi(\theta)\right] \le \log p(y),$$

with equality iff $q_\phi$ equals the posterior. VI is biased by the family choice but scales to large data and deep models.

**Probabilistic programs as densities.** A straight-line program with `sample` and `observe` denotes a joint density over its random choices: each `sample` a prior factor, each `observe(y)` a likelihood factor at that value. Branches, loops, and higher-order functions complicate the picture — the set of random variables may itself be random — which is why the semantics literature reaches for measure theory [6].

**Automatic differentiation.** Modern systems evaluate $\nabla_\theta \log \gamma(\theta)$ by reverse-mode AD through the program's density computation [5]. Every gradient-based method here — HMC, NUTS, ADVI, flow-based VI — consumes this gradient, which is why "differentiable inference" is the right umbrella term.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is synthetic and analytic: we reconstruct conditioning semantics from the denotational literature [6], derive the inference algorithms from their primary sources [1][2][3][4][8][10], and stress-test each against known pathologies (Neal's funnel, multimodality, zero-probability conditioning). Notation is fixed once: $\theta$ latents, $p$ momentum, $U(\theta) = -\log \gamma(\theta)$ potential energy, $K(p)$ kinetic energy, $H = U + K$ the Hamiltonian, $\epsilon$ step size, $L$ leapfrog steps, $\phi$ variational parameters, $\mathcal{L}$ the ELBO.

We adopt a running example — a hierarchical normal model with group-level means — written in Stan-like syntax:

```python
# hierarchical model: J groups, group j has n_j observations
# parameters: mu (global mean), tau (global scale), theta[j] (group means)
for j in 1..J:
    theta[j] ~ Normal(mu, tau)      # sample: prior factor
    y[j]     ~ Normal(theta[j], sigma)  # observe: likelihood factor
```

This model is small enough to reason about exactly and rich enough to exhibit funnel geometry that defeats naive samplers.We evaluate each method on four criteria: (i) asymptotic correctness, (ii) geometric robustness, (iii) degree of automation, and (iv) compositionality with other inference strategies.
## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Semantics of Conditioning: Densities, Disintegration, and Zero-Probability Observations

Operationally, `observe(y_obs)` multiplies the current execution weight by the likelihood density at the observed value. A program denotes an *unnormalized* measure $\gamma$ over execution traces; the posterior is $\gamma / \gamma(\Omega)$. This density-based semantics is clean for finitely many random choices — the trouble starts with continuous observations.

Conditioning on $\{Y = y\}$ when $P(Y = y) = 0$ — always the case for continuous $Y$ — is undefined by the elementary ratio $P(A\cap B)/P(B)$. Measure theory repairs this with *disintegration*: a kernel $\kappa(y, \cdot)$ such that the joint equals $\int \kappa(y,\cdot)\,\mu_Y(dy)$. Disintegrations exist under mild conditions but are unique only up to $\mu_Y$-null sets — the *Borel paradox*: conditioning on different null-set representations of "the same" observation yields genuinely different posteriors.

The semantics literature has sharpened this progressively. Early work modeled programs as s-finite kernels in a Kleisli category, with `score` (soft conditioning) as density multiplication — soft conditioning sidesteps the paradox because multiplying by a likelihood *function* is always well-defined [6]. Vákár, Kammar, and Staton gave an adequate denotational semantics for higher-order languages with recursion and continuous distributions via *quasi-Borel spaces*, which repair the failure of cartesian closure in measurable spaces and validate equivalences such as commutativity of independent samples [6].

```haskell
-- A probabilistic program denotes a kernel from environments to measures
type Prob a = Env -> Measure a
sample  :: Dist a -> Prob a        -- prior factor
observe :: Double -> Prob ()       -- multiply by likelihood density at v
(>>=)   :: Prob a -> (a -> Prob b) -> Prob b   -- sequencing = integration
```

> **Theorem:** In the quasi-Borel model, independent `sample` statements commute: `do { a <- sample dx; b <- sample dy; return (a,b) }` denotes the same measure as the swapped program. Commutativity is a theorem, not an implementation detail [6].

The practical upshot: every inference algorithm below operates on the *density* a program denotes, and is only as meaningful as that denotation. Stan sidesteps the paradox by construction — a Stan program *is* a log-density function [5]. Universal PPLs (Pyro, Gen) inherit the full subtlety, which is why their semantics papers matter to practitioners, not just theorists.

### 4.2 Hamiltonian Monte Carlo: Symplectic Integrators, Leapfrog, and Energy Conservation

HMC [1][2] augments the target with fictitious *momentum* $p \in \mathbb{R}^d$, defining $\pi(\theta,p) \propto \exp(-H(\theta,p))$ with $H = U(\theta) + K(p)$, $U(\theta) = -\log \gamma(\theta)$, $K(p) = \tfrac{1}{2}p^\top M^{-1}p$. The $p$-marginal is exactly the target. Hamilton's equations conserve $H$ exactly along continuous trajectories — proposing distant states with acceptance probability one, in the idealized limit.

Three properties of the flow make HMC a valid MCMC kernel: (i) **reversibility** — negating $p$ and integrating backward returns to the start; (ii) **volume preservation** — the flow is symplectic, so no Jacobian correction enters the Metropolis ratio; (iii) **energy conservation** — $H$ is invariant, so acceptance is exactly 1. The miracle is that a *discrete* integrator preserves (i) and (ii) exactly, leaving only (iii) approximate. That integrator is the **leapfrog** (Störmer–Verlet) scheme:

```python
def leapfrog(theta, p, eps, grad_U, L):
    p = p - 0.5 * eps * grad_U(theta)   # half-step momentum
    for _ in range(L):
        theta = theta + eps * p          # full-step position
        p = p - eps * grad_U(theta)      # full-step momentum
    p = p - 0.5 * eps * grad_U(theta)   # half-step momentum
    return theta, -p                     # negate for reversibility
```

Each sub-step is a shear with unit Jacobian, so leapfrog is reversible and volume-preserving by construction, committing only $O(\epsilon^2)$ energy error [2]. The Metropolis correction $\min(1, \exp(-\Delta H))$ converts discretization error into rejection rather than bias. Momentum resampling between trajectories is essential — without it, near-conservation of $H$ traps the chain on one energy level set [2].

The mass matrix $M$ acts as a preconditioner — Stan adapts a diagonal or dense $M$ during warmup, targeting roughly 65–80% acceptance per optimal-scaling theory [5]. HMC's remaining weakness is the trajectory length $L$: too short and the chain random-walks; too long and it wastes computation orbiting the typical set. Removing that burden is NUTS's entire purpose.

### 4.3 NUTS: Recursive Doubling, the U-Turn Criterion, and Dual-Averaging Adaptation

NUTS [3] eliminates the trajectory-length parameter while preserving detailed balance: it grows the trajectory *adaptively* in both directions and stops when the path begins to retrace itself.

**Recursive doubling.** From the current state, NUTS builds a balanced binary tree of leapfrog states by repeated doubling — at iteration $j$ it extends $2^j$ steps in a randomly chosen time direction. A *slice variable* $u \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0, \exp(-H(\theta_0,p_0)))$ defines the acceptable set $\mathcal{C}$; the next state is drawn from the tree with probability proportional to $\exp(-H)$.

**The U-turn criterion.** Let $(\theta^-,p^-)$ and $(\theta^+,p^+)$ be the tree's endpoints; doubling stops when $(\theta^+ - \theta^-)\cdot p^- < 0$ or $(\theta^+ - \theta^-)\cdot p^+ < 0$ — continued integration would move the endpoints toward each other. The criterion is checked on every subtree; subtrees containing a U-turn or a *divergence* (excessive energy error) are discarded. Maximum tree depth (typically 10) caps cost.

**Dual-averaging adaptation.** During warmup, NUTS adapts $\epsilon$ toward a target acceptance statistic $\delta$ (Stan: 0.8) via Nesterov-style dual averaging on the smoothed statistic $H_t = \delta - \alpha_t$ [3]. With mass-matrix adaptation, NUTS is effectively tuning-free — the "turnkey" sampler behind Stan's practicality [3][5].

Reversibility is delicate but holds: tree-building probabilities are direction-independent, and slice plus multinomial selection are reversible moves on the augmented space; stopping rules depend only on the tree, never the selected state [3]. Empirically, NUTS matches or beats *well-tuned* HMC on effective sample size per gradient evaluation — about $1.5$–$2\times$ better on a 250-dimensional normal and a stochastic volatility model — with no hand-tuning [3].

### 4.4 ADVI and Normalizing Flows: Reparameterization Gradients and Flexible Variational Families

ADVI [4] automates variational inference as NUTS automates HMC. Given only a model and data, it (i) transforms constrained latents $\theta$ to an unconstrained space $\zeta = T(\theta)$ with Jacobian corrections, (ii) posits a Gaussian variational family on $\zeta$ (mean-field or full-rank), and (iii) maximizes the ELBO by stochastic gradient ascent.

The engine is the **reparameterization gradient**. Writing $\zeta = \mu + L\eta$ with $\eta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$, the ELBO becomes an expectation over the *fixed* distribution of $\eta$, so gradients pass inside the expectation and are estimated unbiasedly with a single Monte Carlo sample — computed entirely by automatic differentiation [4]. ADVI's contribution is the automatic constraint transformation, bringing black-box VI to any differentiable Stan model [4][5].

The Gaussian family is also ADVI's ceiling: it cannot capture multimodality or heavy tails, and mean-field ADVI systematically underestimates variance. **Normalizing flows** [8] lift this ceiling. A flow pushes a simple base density $q_0$ through invertible maps $z_K = f_K \circ \cdots \circ f_1(z_0)$, with exact density from the change of variables:

$$\log q_K(z_K) = \log q_0(z_0) - \sum_{k=1}^K \log\left|\det \frac{\partial f_k}{\partial z_{k-1}}\right|.$$

Planar flows $f(z) = z + u\,h(w^\top z + b)$ [8] have $O(D)$ determinants via the matrix determinant lemma; stacking $K$ of them yields arbitrarily flexible posteriors with a tractable ELBO. Later architectures (inverse autoregressive flows, coupling layers) trade expressiveness against determinant cost. In short: *flows turn the variational family from a modeling assumption into a computational budget*, and reparameterization gradients apply unchanged since every flow is a differentiable reparameterization.

### 4.5 Compositional Inference: Effect Handlers, SMC, and Programmable Inference

MCMC and VI are monolithic: one algorithm consumes the whole model. Compositional inference treats inference as a *program* built from combinators — and PPLs are the natural host, because `sample` and `observe` can be reinterpreted as *effects*.

**Effect handlers.** In Pyro, every `pyro.sample` site is an effectful operation intercepted by *poutine* handlers [9]: `trace` records execution, `replay` forces values, `condition` binds data, `block` hides sites, `scale` reweights. An inference algorithm is a handler stack — SVI composes `trace` (computing the ELBO over model and guide) with an optimizer — and new strategies are ordinary library code, which is what makes Pyro "universal" rather than just a VI library [9].

**Sequential Monte Carlo.** SMC [10] is the compositional workhorse for sequential models: it maintains $N$ *particles* (execution traces) with weights; each `observe` multiplies weights by the likelihood factor, and when the effective sample size $\mathrm{ESS} = (\sum w_i)^2/\sum w_i^2$ drops below a threshold, particles are resampled proportional to weight. SMC is asymptotically exact as $N \to \infty$, unbiased for the marginal likelihood, and embarrassingly parallel; its weakness is weight degeneracy in high dimensions, mitigated by better — possibly learned — proposals.

**Programmable inference in Gen.** Gen makes the model/inference interface explicit through the *generative function interface* (GFI): `simulate`, `generate`, `assess`, and `update` [7]. Inference combinators — Metropolis–Hastings over address selections, particle filters, custom kernels — are written against the GFI, so one model region can use SMC, another HMC, and a third a hand-designed proposal, all inside one inference program [7].

| Method | Exactness | Tuning | Geometry | Composition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HMC | asymptotically exact | $\epsilon, L, M$ | needs gradients | monolithic kernel |
| NUTS | asymptotically exact | $\delta$ only | + adaptive length | monolithic kernel |
| ADVI (Gaussian) | biased, mode-seeking | learning rate | unimodal approx. | objective-based |
| Flow VI | biased, budget-controlled | depth, architecture | flexible | objective-based |
| SMC | exact as $N\to\infty$ | $N$, ESS threshold | sequential structure | combinator |

The last column is the field's forward direction: the future is not a single better sampler but *languages for composing samplers* — handlers and combinators that let model structure dictate inference structure [7][9].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Correctness sketches.** Each method's guarantee has a characteristic shape:

1. *HMC detailed balance.* The leapfrog proposal is an involution with unit Jacobian; the Metropolis–Hastings ratio reduces to $\min(1, \exp(H_{\text{old}} - H_{\text{new}}))$, and the standard MH reversibility argument applies [2]. Momentum resampling is a Gibbs update on $p \mid \theta$, hence invariant.
2. *NUTS reversibility.* Doubling probabilities are direction-independent; slice sampling on the acceptable set $\mathcal{C}$ and multinomial selection weighted by $\exp(-H)$ are reversible moves on the augmented space $(\theta, p, u)$ [3]. Stopping rules depend only on the tree, not the selected state, so they cannot break symmetry.
3. *ELBO bound.* By Jensen's inequality, $\log p(y) \ge \mathcal{L}(\phi)$, with gap exactly $\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi \,\|\, p(\cdot\mid y))$. Reparameterization gradients are unbiased because differentiation commutes with the expectation over the fixed base distribution [4].
4. *SMC unbiasedness.* The particle estimate of the marginal likelihood is unbiased for any $N$ under multinomial resampling [10]; this underwrites particle MCMC and SMC-based model comparison.

**Reported empirical behavior.** Hoffman and Gelman showed NUTS matches or beats the *best* hand-tuned HMC on effective sample size per gradient evaluation, with the dual-averaging target $\delta = 0.6$–$0.65$ near-optimal throughout [3]. Kucukelbir et al. compared ADVI to NUTS across hierarchical GLMs, nonconjugate matrix factorization, and a mixture model on 250,000 images [4]: ADVI reached comparable predictive accuracy orders of magnitude faster, but posterior variances were systematically underestimated — the expected price of the Gaussian family.

**A unifying observation.** All gradient methods here fail *diagnosably* when geometry defeats them (divergences, low ESS, loose ELBO) — except where bias is structural (mean-field VI). The practical workflow this suggests — run NUTS, inspect divergences and $\hat{R}$, reparameterize as needed, and use ADVI/flows for scale — is exactly what Stan's documentation encodes [5].

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

**Differentiability is a hard requirement.** HMC, NUTS, ADVI, and flow VI all need $\nabla_\theta \log \gamma(\theta)$. Discrete latents — mixture assignments, changepoints, branches — must be marginalized analytically or handled by another kernel, which is why compositional systems matter: Gen lets a discrete SMC sweep coexist with a continuous HMC kernel [7].

**NUTS's adaptivity has costs.** Recursive doubling evaluates up to $2^{10} = 1024$ leapfrog steps per iteration worst-case, and the U-turn criterion can stop *too early* on highly curved posteriors. The criterion is defined in the *unconstrained* parameterization, so a poor variable transform degrades it silently [3][5].

**Variational bias is structural.** Even with flows, VI minimizes the *reverse* KL, which is mode-seeking: it would rather miss a mode than cover one poorly. The ELBO gap is unobservable without ground truth, so flow-based VI can be confidently wrong; amortized inference adds an *amortization gap* on top [8][9].

**Conditioning semantics remains open.** Quasi-Borel spaces validate commutativity and adequacy for higher-order programs [6], but open problems persist: (i) *nested inference* — programs that run inference internally lack a settled denotation; (ii) *disintegration at scale* — the Borel paradox means textually different but "equivalent" conditioning statements can denote different posteriors, and no mainstream PPL warns the user; (iii) *differentiable resampling* — resampling is not differentiable, blocking gradient-based learning of proposals inside particle filters [10].

There is a genuine tension: Stan's monolithic NUTS+ADVI stack is the most automated and the least extensible [5]; Gen's GFI is the most extensible and demands the most expertise [7]; Pyro's handlers sit in between [9]. No current system offers both turnkey reliability *and* first-class inference programming — closing that gap is the central engineering challenge of the next decade.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Probabilistic programming rests on two achievements that are often conflated: a *semantics* giving conditioned programs mathematical meaning, and *inference algorithms* computing with that meaning at scale. Conditioning is density multiplication plus disintegration, with zero-probability observations as the sharp edge where quasi-Borel semantics restores rigor [6]; HMC's power flows from leapfrog's exact reversibility and volume preservation [1][2]; NUTS converts trajectory-length tuning into reversible slice sampling with dual-averaging adaptation [3]; ADVI reduces VI to stochastic optimization of a reparameterized ELBO [4], with normalizing flows extending the variational family arbitrarily [8]; and effect handlers plus programmable combinators — SMC foremost — compose these primitives into inference programs in Pyro and Gen [7][9][10]. Stan shows the monolithic path already works in production [5]; the compositional path is where the field is going.
