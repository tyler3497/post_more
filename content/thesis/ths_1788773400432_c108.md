# HotStuff, Narwhal, and Tusk: Chained Linear BFT Consensus, Rotating Leaders, and DAG-Based Mempool Separation for High-Throughput Byzantine Transaction Ordering

## Abstract

This thesis presents a unified treatment of the two dominant architectural revolutions in Byzantine fault-tolerant (BFT) state machine replication of the last decade: the linear, responsive consensus discipline of HotStuff, and the separation of transaction dissemination from transaction ordering embodied by the Narwhal–Tusk DAG framework. We begin with HotStuff's quorum-certificate framework, which yields optimistic responsiveness and *O(n)* communication per view under partial synchrony, and its chained variant, which pipelines prepare, pre-commit, commit, and decide phases into a single propose–vote pattern governed by the three-chain commit rule [1][2]. We then examine how this leader-centric design suffers under faults and liveness failures, motivating the Narwhal mempool: an asynchronous, worker-parallelizable directed acyclic graph (DAG) in which every validator reliably disseminates certified batches with 2f+1 inter-round edges [3]. On top of this DAG, Tusk executes asynchronous consensus with zero additional messages via waves of three rounds and a shared-coin leader election [3][4]. We conclude with the partially synchronous refinements of Bullshark and the latency-optimized Mysticeti, and survey open problems in garbage collection, censorship resistance, and multi-leader DAGs [5][6].

## 1 Introduction

State machine replication (SMR) under Byzantine faults is the theoretical backbone of permissioned blockchains, cross-datacenter coordination services, and the ordering layers of modern layer-1 networks. Classical protocols such as PBFT [8] demonstrated feasibility but carried *O(n²)* communication even in the common case, and their leader-failover machinery—nested view-change protocols with quadratic all-to-all exchanges—made rotating the leader expensive. For nearly two decades, this combination was accepted as the practical price of Byzantine agreement.

Two structural insights broke the impasse. The first, from Yin, Malkhi, Reiter, Gueta, and Abraham, is that threshold signatures can compress each voting phase into a single aggregate certificate, and that a uniform propose–vote phase can be *chained*: every view both extends the previous proposal and acts as a phase of earlier views' pipelines [1]. The result is HotStuff, the first partially synchronous BFT protocol to combine optimistic responsiveness—progress at the speed of the actual network delay δ rather than the worst-case bound Δ—with linear per-view communication, and the basis of Facebook's DiemBFT (formerly LibraBFT) [2].

The second insight, from Danezis, Kokoris-Kogias, Sonnino, and Spiegelman, is that *dissemination* and *ordering* should not share a protocol [3]. In leader-centric designs, the leader is both a bandwidth bottleneck and a liveness hazard: if it fails, the mempool stalls. Narwhal restructures the mempool as a DAG of certified batches, built once and reused by any consensus logic; Tusk then layers a randomized, zero-message asynchronous consensus over that DAG. Measurements on a wide-area network report Narwhal-HotStuff sustaining over 130,000–170,000 transactions per second at sub-3-second latency—roughly two orders of magnitude above HotStuff alone—scaling linearly with worker parallelism to 600,000 tx/s [3].

This thesis develops both systems formally, proves their safety arguments from first principles, analyzes their complexity and fault-tolerance profiles, and evaluates their empirical performance and remaining limitations.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 System model and fault assumptions

We consider a fixed committee of *n = 3f + 1* replicas, at most *f* of which may be Byzantine (arbitrary, possibly colluding). Correct replicas follow the protocol; faulty replicas may send arbitrary messages but cannot forge cryptographic signatures. Communication is *partially synchronous* [8] for HotStuff and Bullshark: there exists an unknown global stabilization time (GST) after which message delays are bounded by a known Δ, with no timing guarantees before it. Narwhal and Tusk assume a purely *asynchronous* network, obtaining only randomized termination guarantees.

A quorum is any set of *n − f = 2f + 1* replicas. Any two quorums intersect in at least *f + 1* replicas, hence in at least one correct replica—this *quorum intersection* property underpins every safety argument below.

### 2.2 Threshold signatures and quorum certificates

Both protocol families compress votes with *(k, n)*-threshold signatures, where *k = 2f + 1*: partial signatures from any quorum combine into a single compact signature whose verification cost is independent of *n* [1]. A *quorum certificate* (QC) for a block *B* at view *v* is an aggregate of *2f + 1* votes for *B* in *v*; equivalently, it is a cryptographic proof that a quorum approved *B*. QCs are the only objects a leader needs to present, turning view change from an *O(n²)* evidence-dump into an *O(n)* certificate forwarding.

### 2.3 Classical precursors

PBFT established the three-phase (pre-prepare, prepare, commit) discipline with quadratic communication and a complex view-change subprotocol [8]. Tendermint and Casper FFG preserved quadratic or leader-stable structures. The long-standing open problem—simultaneously achieving (a) optimistic responsiveness and (b) linear view change—was resolved by HotStuff [1][2]. Independently, DAG-based dissemination traces a lineage from Reiter and Birman's secure causal broadcast through Hashgraph, Aleph, DAG-Rider, and the unifying "All You Need is DAG" framework [4], systematized as a survey of DAG-based blockchain designs in [7].

## 3 Methodology

We proceed in three layers. First, we give an axiomatic specification of HotStuff's basic and chained variants in a TLA⁺-style notation, and prove safety via quorum intersection applied to QCs. Second, we formalize the Narwhal DAG's construction rules—round structure, 2f+1 inter-round edges, certificate availability—and derive its *completeness*, *validity*, and *integrity* properties [3]. Third, we specify Tusk's wave-based commit rule and its shared-coin election, and show how the combination yields a zero-message-overhead asynchronous total order. Empirical results are drawn from the published WAN evaluations in [1][3][5], renormalized into comparative tables. Our notation: *v* for view (HotStuff), *r* for DAG round (Narwhal), *w* for wave (Tusk).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The HotStuff framework: phases, QCs, and rotating leaders

HotStuff's core contribution is a *framework* rather than a single protocol: every phase is a `propose–vote` pair. In view *v*, the leader broadcasts `propose(b, qc)`; replicas check that *qc* justifies *b* (the *SafeNode* predicate), then send `vote(b)` back to the next leader. The leader of view *v+1* collects *2f+1* votes into a QC and chains it into its own proposal.

The basic protocol runs four phases—**prepare**, **pre-commit**, **commit**, **decide**—before a block is committed and executed. Its skeleton in a TLA⁺-like pseudocode:

```tla
---- MODULE BasicHotStuff ----
CONSTANTS Replicas, F, Views
VARIABLES prepared, lockedQC, committed

Propose(v, leader, b, qc) ==
  /\ SendToAll(leader, [type |-> "propose", view |-> v, block |-> b, qc |-> qc])
  /\ Vote(v, r, b) == r \in Correct =>
       SendTo(leader, [type |-> "vote", view |-> v, block |-> b, sig |-> Sign(r,b)])

FormQC(votes) == AggregateSig({s.sig : s \in votes})  \* |votes| = 2F+1

PhaseTransition(v, phase) ==
  /\ \E votes \in SUBSET ReceivedVotes : Cardinality(votes) = 2*F+1
  /\ LET qc == FormQC(votes)
     IN  leader(v+1).propose(v+1, NewBlock(qc), qc)
```

Four phases times two network hops each gives 8 message rounds per decision in the basic protocol, with *O(n)* messages per phase because the leader alone collects votes and disseminates the aggregate. The crucial subtlety is *optimistic responsiveness*: no phase ever waits for the Δ timeout unless the timer fires for a failed leader, so the protocol advances at *O(δ)*, the actual network speed [1].

> **Theorem (HotStuff safety):** If two QCs are formed for blocks *b* and *b′* at the same view *v*, then *b = b′*.

*Proof sketch.* Each QC contains *2f+1* votes from distinct replicas; by quorum intersection, some correct replica *r* voted for both *b* and *b′*. Correct replicas vote at most once per view. Contradiction. ∎

The leader rotation is deterministic—typically `leader(v) = v mod n`—and because view change is linear, rotating every view is affordable. Frequent rotation yields *chain quality*: no single replica can censor indefinitely, addressing a principal weakness of stable-leader PBFT.

### 4.2 Chained HotStuff: the three-chain commit rule

The basic protocol's four phases are wasteful: each view replicates identical work. Chained HotStuff observes that a proposal for view *v* can simultaneously serve as the pre-commit of *v−1*, the commit of *v−2*, and the decision trigger of *v−3*. All views collapse into one perpetual propose–vote pipeline. The commit rule becomes:

> **Three-chain rule:** A block *b* at view *v* is *committed* if there exist QCs forming a chain *b = B(v) ← B(v+1) ← B(v+2)*—that is, three consecutive views where each QC's parent is the previous block—and additionally one further QC exists for *B(v+3)* extending the chain. Two consecutive rounds plus one non-consecutive round are the necessary skeleton [2].

Fast-HotStuff later showed the same pipeline can be reduced to a *two-chain* rule by attaching proof-of-proposal metadata, at the cost of slightly heavier view-change evidence [2]. Chained HotStuff was adopted verbatim by DiemBFT, Flow, and Cypherium [2], making it arguably the most deployed BFT commit discipline in production blockchains.

Latency analysis: a commit requires three consecutive correct leaders, i.e., 7 network hops in the common case—higher *round* complexity than PBFT's 3 hops, but with *O(n)* messages per hop versus *O(n²)*. The throughput/latency trade-off therefore favors chained HotStuff at *n ≥ 30*, where PBFT's quadratic broadcast saturates links [1].

### 4.3 Narwhal: the mempool as a DAG

Narwhal inverts the architecture: instead of the leader disseminating transactions, *every* validator continuously disseminates, and the DAG itself becomes the shared, certified data structure [3]. Each validator runs a **primary** (which builds DAG vertices) and any number of **workers** (which batch client transactions in parallel). A vertex at round *r* contains: (i) a set of batch digests produced by that validator's workers, and (ii) references to at least *2f+1* vertices of round *r−1*. A vertex is *certified* once *2f+1* validators sign its availability, yielding a certificate of availability.

```
Round r:      V1(r)──┐
              V2(r)──┼──▶ each vertex references ≥ 2f+1 vertices of round r−1
              V3(r)──┤
              V4(r)──┘
                        Causal history of every vertex is transitively available.
```

Three properties are proved in [3]:

| Property | Statement |
|---|---|
| **Integrity** | Every certified vertex contains digests of available batches; Byzantine validators cannot invent data. |
| **Block-Availability** | A certified vertex's full causal history can be retrieved from any honest validator holding it. |
| **Containment** | The causal history of a certified vertex contains at least *2f+1* vertices of every earlier round (by induction on edges). |

Workers scale out linearly: adding workers increases batch production without touching consensus logic, because the DAG's certification work is per-vertex and constant-size regardless of how many batches a vertex references. This is the decisive throughput lever: dissemination parallelism is *embarrassingly* parallel, while ordering remains the sequential bottleneck.

Narwhal tolerates asynchrony during dissemination; a slow or crashed validator simply does not advance its own rounds, while others' rounds proceed. Garbage collection proceeds by round watermarks, discarding vertices older than a configurable depth once consensus has ordered them.

### 4.4 Tusk: zero-message asynchronous consensus over the DAG

Tusk is remarkable because it sends **no messages of its own**: it is a deterministic interpretation function over the locally observed Narwhal DAG [3]. Rounds are grouped into *waves* of three:

- **Round 3w−2**: propose round; every validator's vertex is a candidate.
- **Round 3w−1**: voting round; vertices vote for a candidate by referencing it.
- **Round 3w**: election round; a shared coin (a threshold signature on the wave number) elects a *leader* vertex uniformly among round 3w−2 vertices.

The *direct commit rule*: the elected leader of wave *w* is committed if at least *f+1* vertices of round *3w−1* reference it. The *indirect commit rule*: if a later wave's leader is committed and its causal history contains the wave-*w* leader, the wave-*w* leader is committed transitively. Ordering is then derived deterministically: sort committed leaders by wave, and for each, topologically order its causal history, skipping already-delivered vertices. This yields a total order with agreement, validity, and liveness (with probability 1) under asynchrony.

```python
def tusk_commit(dag, wave):
    leader = shared_coin_elect(wave)          # uniform over round 3w-2 vertices
    supporters = [v for v in dag.round(3*w - 1) if leader in v.parents]
    if len(supporters) >= f + 1:
        return direct_commit(leader)          # order causal history
    # else: wait; a later wave's leader may commit this one indirectly
```

The expected latency is constant waves per commit (expected 3 waves, since a random leader has ≥ f+1 supporters with probability ≥ 1/3 when ≥ 2f+1 vertices exist per round—a standard calculation in [3][4]). Because the DAG construction itself costs ~3 message delays per round, end-to-end commit latency is roughly 9 message delays, higher than HotStuff's 7 hops but achieved *asynchronously* and with zero dedicated consensus messages.

### 4.5 Bullshark, Mysticeti, and the latency frontier

**Bullshark** [5] ports the Narwhal–Tusk stack to partial synchrony, replacing the randomized election with a deterministic, timer-driven leader schedule and steady-state leaders per wave, collapsing commit latency to roughly 2 DAG rounds in the common case. **Mysticeti** [6] goes further: it replaces explicit DAG certification with threshold-clock rounds, restructures the commit rule so blocks can commit without delay (3 message rounds, the latency lower bound), and reports 0.5s WAN commit latency at over 200k tx/s, integrating Mysticeti-C into the Sui blockchain for a 4x latency reduction. Together they trace a clear trajectory: from *asynchronous generality* (Tusk) to *partially synchronous practicality* (Bullshark) to *latency-optimized deployment* (Mysticeti), all riding the same DAG substrate.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Published performance results

The following consolidates the WAN evaluations reported in [1] and [3] (512-byte transactions, geo-distributed validators):

| System | Throughput (tx/s) | Latency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| HotStuff (baseline) | ~1,800 | ~1.0 s | leader bottleneck, no mempool separation |
| Narwhal-HotStuff | ~130,000–170,000 | ~2.0–2.5 s | DAG dissemination + HotStuff ordering |
| Narwhal-HotStuff (multi-worker) | ~600,000 | ~2.5 s | linear worker scale-out, no latency cost |
| Tusk | ~140,000–160,000 | ~3–4 s | zero-message async consensus over DAG |

The order-of-magnitude gap between HotStuff and Narwhal-HotStuff isolates the architectural claim: in leader-centric protocols, *dissemination*—not voting logic—is the throughput bottleneck. Under crash faults, Narwhal-based variants sustain throughput while HotStuff's latency degrades during view changes, because DAG rounds advance independently of any single leader [3].

### 5.2 Complexity comparison

| Dimension | PBFT | HotStuff (chained) | Narwhal–Tusk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Messages, common case | *O(n²)* | *O(n)* | *O(n)* amortized per vertex; consensus adds 0 |
| Rounds per commit | 3 | 7 hops / 3 chained views | ~9 message delays (3 rounds × 3) |
| Responsiveness | no (waits Δ) | yes (*O(δ)*) | n/a (async; randomized termination) |
| Leader rotation cost | *O(n²)* view change | *O(n)* view change | no leaders in mempool |
| Throughput scaling | saturates at n≈30 | saturates at leader NIC | linear in workers |

### 5.3 Safety proof obligations

The safety arguments compose cleanly because the layers are decoupled:

> **Theorem (Narwhal–Tusk agreement):** If two correct validators commit leader *L* of wave *w*, they order the same causal history, hence deliver identical transaction sequences.

*Proof sketch.* Committed leaders are totally ordered by wave; ties within a wave are broken by the deterministic vertex ordering. For a committed leader, every correct validator eventually holds its full causal history by block-availability, and all validators topologically sort the *same* DAG prefix. Two validators therefore produce identical sequences. ∎

> **Theorem (HotStuff liveness after GST):** After GST, a correct leader's proposal is committed within bounded time.

*Proof sketch.* After GST, *2f+1* votes reach the leader within Δ; the leader forms QCs; rotation guarantees a correct leader within *f+1* views; the three-chain rule is then satisfied over three consecutive correct-led views. ∎

Mechanized verification efforts (e.g., in Coq and Ivy) have checked the QC intersection arguments, though full machine-checked proofs of the chained rule with view-change remain an active research frontier.

## 6 Limitations

**Latency floor of DAG consensus.** Tusk's expected ~9 message delays and multi-second latency make it unsuitable for latency-sensitive applications; even Mysticeti's optimizations keep DAG commits above the theoretical 3-delay lower bound for leader-based protocols.

**Censorship and fairness.** A Byzantine majority of a round's vertices can exclude a particular validator's batches from causal histories indefinitely; Narwhal provides availability, not inclusion fairness. Bullshark and later work add explicit fairness gadgets, but they cost latency.

**Garbage collection and storage.** The DAG grows with throughput; vertices must be retained until their rounds are ordered and executed. Aggressive GC risks breaking availability proofs for slow validators, and the watermark tuning in [3] is heuristic rather than principled.

**Common-coin setup.** Tusk's shared coin requires a distributed key generation (DKG) ceremony; a compromised coin lets the adversary bias leader election, degrading expected latency to the adversarial case. Bullshark avoids the coin only by reintroducing timing assumptions.

**Chained HotStuff's consecutive-leader requirement.** Committing needs three consecutive correct leaders; a single Byzantine leader in the chain delays commitment by a full rotation cycle. Fast-HotStuff's two-chain rule mitigates this but complicates view-change evidence.

**Evaluation realism.** Published numbers use 512-byte transactions and generous bandwidth; real deployments (e.g., Sui's Mysticeti) report lower absolute throughput under heterogeneous validators and adversarial network conditions.

## 7 Conclusion

HotStuff and Narwhal–Tusk represent complementary answers to the same question: how should Byzantine agreement be structured when throughput, not raw fault tolerance, is the binding constraint? HotStuff's answer is *protocol economy*—threshold-signature QCs, a uniform propose–vote phase, chained views, and linear view change, achieving responsiveness with *O(n)* communication [1][2]. Narwhal–Tusk's answer is *architectural separation*—disseminate asynchronously and in parallel via a certified DAG, then order with minimal or zero extra messages, scaling throughput linearly with workers [3][4]. Their descendants, Bullshark and Mysticeti, show the DAG substrate absorbing the best of both worlds: deterministic fast paths under synchrony, randomized fallback under asynchrony [5][6]. The open frontier—fair inclusion, principled garbage collection, sub-second DAG finality under asynchrony, and mechanized proofs of the full chained rule—defines the next decade of BFT research.
