# External Consistency at Planetary Scale: TrueTime, Commit-Wait, and the Transactional Architecture of Google Spanner

![Figure 1: TrueTime uncertainty interval and the commit-wait rule.](ths_1789075771000_17281-0.webp)

## Abstract

Prior to 2012, distributed databases faced an apparent trilemma: global scale, ACID transactions, and freedom from specialized clock hardware — choose at most two. Google's Spanner [1] shattered this assumption with **TrueTime**, an API that returns time as an interval `[earliest, latest]` with bounded uncertainty *ε*, and a small set of provably correct rules — chiefly **commit-wait** — that convert clock uncertainty into *latency* rather than *incorrectness*. This thesis reconstructs Spanner from first principles: Paxos groups over tablets, two-phase commit layered on Paxos replication, globally meaningful commit timestamps, the *t-safe* mechanism behind lock-free read-only transactions, and the proof sketch yielding **external consistency**. We distinguish external consistency rigorously from Herlihy and Wing's **linearizability** [2], survey the principal successors — Calvin's deterministic sequencing [3], CockroachDB's hybrid logical clocks [4], Clock-SI's loosely synchronized snapshot isolation [5] — and critically evaluate Spanner's published measurements, including the sawtooth *ε* profile bounding write latency. We close with limitations: GPS/atomic-clock dependence, commit-wait as a fundamental latency floor, and the open problem of replacing physical intervals with logical constructions.

## 1 Introduction

The database literature of the 2000s scaled globally by surrendering transactions: Bigtable abandoned them, Megastore confined them to partitions, Dynamo traded consistency for availability. The received wisdom held that this was *necessary*: clocks cannot be synchronized across a planet, and without shared time, globally ordered transactions seemed to demand precisely the coordination that scale forbids.

Spanner's insight is disarmingly simple: **you do not need to know the exact time; you need to know the bounds on your uncertainty.** TrueTime returns `TT.now() = [earliest, latest]` with the guarantee `earliest ≤ t_abs(now) ≤ latest`, where `latest − earliest = 2ε` [1][6]. With GPS receivers and atomic clocks in every datacenter, *ε* stays below ~10 ms, so waiting out uncertainty is *cheap* — but correctness never depends on *ε* being small. A large *ε* only makes Spanner slow; the invariants hold regardless. This thesis formalizes that claim and traces its consequences through Spanner's architecture, its external-consistency proof, and a decade of systems that adapted, avoided, or attacked the TrueTime idea.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Serializability, linearizability, and external consistency

**Serializability** requires concurrent transactions to behave as if executed in some serial order. It is *not* a real-time property: a serializable history may order *T₂* before *T₁* even when *T₁* finished entirely before *T₂* began. **External consistency** adds the real-time constraint: if the commit of *T₁* precedes the start of *T₂*, the serialization order must place *T₁* before *T₂* [1]. Equivalently, commit timestamps must respect commit-before-start real-time order.

Herlihy and Wing's **linearizability** [2] is analogous but *different*: each operation of a concurrent object must appear to take effect at a **linearization point** between invocation and response, with the resulting sequence legal per the object's sequential specification. Two distinctions matter:

- *Scope.* Linearizability is a **local** property — a system is linearizable iff each object is (the locality theorem) [2]. Serializability is **global** over transactions spanning many objects.
- *Granularity.* Linearizability constrains *operations* (single-object reads/writes); external consistency constrains *transactions* (multi-object, multi-partition units).

> **Theorem:** A system providing external consistency for transactions also provides single-object linearizability for the operations those transactions induce; the converse does not hold.

Spanner's paper calls external consistency "equivalently, linearizability" [1]. This is a slight abuse: what Spanner proves is the real-time-order property for transactions — the transactional analogue of linearizability's real-time clause. We keep the notions distinct.

### 2.2 Time in distributed systems

Classical clocks (Lamport, vector, hybrid logical) construct *causal* order, which cannot distinguish "T1 committed before T2 started" from concurrency when no causal path exists. Spanner instead approximates **absolute time**, quantifying imprecision explicitly. The lineage runs from Marzullo's work on loosely synchronized time servers to the "Distributed Computing Column" exposition of exact-time reasoning [6], which poses the central question: *what does it mean for a protocol to be correct under bounded clock error?* Spanner's answer: protocols must be correct for *all* error bounds, paying the bound only in performance.

### 2.3 Replication and consensus

Spanner partitions its key-space into tablets, each replicated by a **Paxos group** that may span datacenters [1][7]. Two-phase commit (2PC) coordinates atomicity *across* groups; Paxos provides durability and order *within* a group. Crucially, 2PC runs **on top of** Paxos: the coordinator writes its transaction record via Paxos into its own group, and participant leaders log prepare records through their own Paxos rounds, so a participant failure is masked by its quorum rather than blocking the transaction.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a reconstructive, comparative analysis. Our primary artifact is the Spanner OSDI 2012 paper [1], cross-checked against the CMU lecture exposition of its TrueTime semantics [7], the SIGACT column on exact-time reasoning [6], and CockroachDB's design paper [4] for the comparison systems. Formal claims (the commit-wait invariant, the external-consistency proof sketch) are re-derived from the definitions in [1, §3–4] and [6] in a uniform notation with explicit assumptions. Quantitative claims are taken directly from Spanner's §6: the sawtooth *ε* bound, the 2PC latency breakdown, and throughput figures. We do not replicate experiments; §5 is a critical reading of the published numbers and what they imply for the design's generality.

### 3.1 Notation

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `t_abs(e)` | Absolute (true) time of event `e` |
| `TT.now()` | TrueTime read, returning interval `[earliest, latest]` |
| `ε` | Worst-case clock divergence; `latest − earliest = 2ε` |
| `s(T)` | Commit timestamp assigned to transaction `T` |
| `commit(T)` | Event at which the coordinator externalizes `T`'s commitment |
| `t_safe` | Safe time at a replica (see §4.3) |

A **read-write transaction** is assigned `s(T) = TT.now().latest`, followed by **commit-wait**: the coordinator delays until `TT.after(s(T))`, i.e., `TT.now().earliest > s(T)` [1]. Since `t_abs(e) ≥ TT.now().earliest`, commit-wait guarantees `t_abs(commit(T)) > s(T)`: the commit timestamp is *in the past* before any observer can learn of the commit.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 TrueTime: intervals, not instants

TrueTime's API is deliberately minimal [1, §3]:

```python
class TTstamp:
    earliest: int   # t_abs(now) >= earliest, guaranteed
    latest: int     # t_abs(now) <= latest, guaranteed

def after(t: int) -> bool:   # true iff t has definitely passed
    return TT.now().earliest > t
def before(t: int) -> bool:  # true iff t has definitely not arrived
    return TT.now().latest < t
```

The **implementation** is statistical metrology. Each machine runs a *time-master* daemon consulting diverse references — GPS receivers and atomic clocks — and computes, via a variant of Marzullo's algorithm, an interval containing true time with the configured confidence. The daemon advertises a slowly varying *ε*; the paper reports a **sawtooth** where the local clock drifts at most 200 µs/s between re-synchronizations, bounding *ε* in `[0, 6]` ms plus ~1 ms communication delay to time masters [1, §3]. Excursions occur under failures — unreachable time masters inflate *ε* datacenter-wide; overloaded machines or congested links inflate it locally — and *every* TrueTime consumer must degrade gracefully rather than fail.

> **Theorem (TrueTime guarantee):** For any `TT.now()` returning `[earliest, latest]`, `earliest ≤ t_abs(now) ≤ latest`, with `latest − earliest = 2ε` for the daemon's advertised bound at call time.

The content is not that clocks are accurate, but that *the advertised inaccuracy is itself accurate*. Spanner's proofs take the theorem as an axiom; the implementation's only job is keeping *ε* small for performance.

### 4.2 Two-phase commit over Paxos groups

Spanner's universe is divided into **zones**, each with one **spanserver**, each spanserver managing 100–1000 **tablets** [1, §2]. A tablet's state machine is replicated by a **Paxos group** with a long-lived *stable leader* holding timed leases, so reads and writes normally flow through one replica. Groups may stretch across datacenters — the defining departure from conventional single-datacenter consensus [7].

A read-write transaction proceeds as follows [1, §4.1.2]:

1. **Execution and locking.** The client drives reads and buffered writes through the coordinator leader of each participating group, acquiring pessimistic locks (two-phase locking with wound-wait deadlock avoidance).
2. **Coordinator selection.** One participant group is designated coordinator; its leader reads `TT.now()` and sets `s(T) = latest`.
3. **Prepare.** The coordinator sends prepare to each participant leader; each logs a *prepare record* through its Paxos group, recording `s(T)` and the write set.
4. **Commit-wait.** The coordinator waits until `TT.after(s(T))` — the fulcrum of the design.
5. **Commit.** Only then does the coordinator log the commit record via Paxos and apply writes tagged with `s(T)`, visible at every replica once `t_safe ≥ s(T)`.

![Figure 2: Paxos groups spanning datacenters with a 2PC coordinator.](ths_1789075771000_17281-1.webp)

Paxos serializes *all* mutations within a group, and the leader assigns each committed write a timestamp no smaller than any previously assigned — the **monotonicity invariant**. With commit-wait, monotonicity yields the external-consistency lemma:

> **Lemma (commit order):** If `commit(T₁)` precedes the start of `T₂` in absolute time, then `s(T₁) < s(T₂)`.

*Proof sketch.* By commit-wait, `t_abs(commit(T₁)) > s(T₁)`. Since `T₂` starts later, its timestamp satisfies `s(T₂) ≥ TT.now().latest ≥ t_abs` of `T₂`'s read `> t_abs(commit(T₁)) > s(T₁)`. ∎

Commit timestamps thus reflect serialization order in the external, wall-clock sense — exactly external consistency [1, §4.1; 6].

### 4.3 Safe time and lock-free, non-blocking reads

Read-write transactions are expensive: they hold locks, span datacenters, and wait out *ε*. Spanner's second masterstroke makes **read-only transactions** cheap — lock-free and non-blocking — via the same timestamp machinery [1, §4.1.3–4.1.4].

Each replica maintains **`t_safe`**, the largest timestamp `t` such that the replica has applied *all* writes with timestamp ≤ `t`, computed as the minimum of three quantities:

```
t_safe = min( t_Paxos_safe,       # highest timestamp applied from the Paxos log
              t_TC,               # smallest prepare timestamp of prepared-but-uncommitted 2PC txns
              TT.now().latest )   # uncertainty cap: reads may not pass possible futures
```

The first term advances as the Paxos log applies; the second stalls until every prepared transaction commits or aborts (a prepared transaction at `s` blocks `t_safe` at `s − 1`; fine-grained safe time narrows the blast radius [1]); the third prevents reads from overtaking timestamps a not-yet-started transaction might claim. A read-only transaction takes a read timestamp `t_r` (either `TT.now().latest`, or an older timestamp for staleness-bounded reads) and executes at **any sufficiently up-to-date replica** once `t_safe ≥ t_r` — no locks, no blocking on concurrent writers.

![Figure 3: External consistency: wall-clock order preserved in serialization order.](ths_1789075771000_17281-2.webp)

Notably, **snapshot reads need not contact the leader.** Because timestamps are globally meaningful, any replica whose `t_safe` has caught up can serve the read; the cost is only the staleness of waiting for `t_safe ≥ t_r` — bounded by Paxos propagation plus commit-wait latencies, not by per-read cross-datacenter round trips.

![Figure 4: Safe-time architecture: t_safe as the minimum of Paxos progress, prepared-transaction bounds, and the TrueTime horizon.](ths_1789075771000_17281-3.webp)

### 4.4 Three responses to the clock-uncertainty problem

**Calvin: determinism instead of time.** Thomson et al. [3] observe that 2PC's true cost is not latency but *contention footprint*: holding locks while coordinating. Calvin's sequencer batches transactions every ~10 ms into a deterministic total order; every replica then executes the same log in the same order with **deterministic locking** — locks acquired in the agreed order, never held across coordination, no 2PC, no distributed deadlock. Active replication falls out for free: identical inputs plus deterministic execution yield identical states. The price: (i) read/write sets must be known up front, ruling out interactive multi-statement transactions; (ii) real-time order is sacrificed to epoch batching — Calvin is serializable but *not* externally consistent, by design; (iii) hot keys serialize through the deterministic order. It scales near-linearly on TPC-C because that workload's stored procedures have statically known footprints.

**CockroachDB: uncertainty without the hardware.** Taft et al. [4] faced Spanner's problem without Spanner's datacenters: no GPS/atomic clocks, only NTP, so offsets can reach 250 ms or more. Their hybrid-logical-clock scheme assigns each transaction a timestamp and reasons with an **uncertainty interval** `[timestamp, timestamp + max_offset]`. When a transaction encounters a write whose timestamp falls inside its uncertainty interval, the database "must not guess": instead of *waiting* (commit-wait would cost hundreds of milliseconds), it **restarts the transaction above the conflicting write** — a "read refresh" pushing the commit timestamp forward. This converts Spanner's latency tax into an *abort tax*: low contention → lower latency than Spanner; high contention → cascading restarts. It also explains why CockroachDB does not recommend linearizable mode by default: single-key linearizability with a 250 ms offset would impose Spanner-style waits without Spanner-style hardware.

**Clock-SI: physical time, weak guarantees, commodity clocks.** Du et al. [5] use only ordinary clocks to implement **snapshot isolation** (SI) for partitioned stores: transactions read from a start-timestamp snapshot and commit absent write-write conflicts. The authors contrast this with Spanner explicitly: Clock-SI's correctness "does not depend on clock synchrony" — skewed clocks degrade to extra aborts or staler snapshots, never incorrectness — whereas Spanner's *performance* depends on small *ε*. The trade is isolation strength: SI admits write-skew anomalies that strict serializability forbids.

| System | Time source | Guarantee | Cost of uncertainty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spanner [1] | TrueTime (GPS/atomic), *ε* ≲ 10 ms | External consistency | Commit-wait latency (~2ε) |
| Calvin [3] | None (deterministic order) | Serializability, not real-time | Epoch delay; no real-time order |
| CockroachDB [4] | HLC + NTP, offset ≲ 250 ms+ | Serializable; single-key linearizable | Restarts under contention |
| Clock-SI [5] | Commodity clocks | Snapshot isolation | Extra aborts / staleness |

The follow-on literature is thus a taxonomy of *where to pay for uncertainty*: Calvin pays in real-time order, CockroachDB pays in restarts, Clock-SI pays in isolation strength.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation

We read Spanner's §6 critically, as reported in [1] and the lecture-note measurements [7].

**The *ε* sawtooth.** The time-master daemon keeps *ε* in a sawtooth between 0 and 6 ms (200 µs/s drift between synchronizations) plus ~1 ms communication delay [1, §3]. Failures cause excursions: datacenter-wide inflation under time-master unavailability, localized spikes under overload or congestion. The consequence is direct: commit-wait adds approximately `2ε` (≈ 10–14 ms typical) to every read-write transaction. This is the *fundamental* latency floor of external consistency under TrueTime — no network optimization can remove it, because it derives from the uncertainty bound itself.

**Throughput and latency.** A 5-replica US deployment sustains tens of thousands of read-write transactions per second with commit latencies in the tens of milliseconds, while lock-free read-only transactions and snapshot reads — served locally — achieve sub-10 ms latencies at far higher throughput [1, §6]. The Table 2 taxonomy is borne out: workload mix determines performance far more than raw hardware. Under high contention, wound-wait and lock holding across two Paxos rounds dominate, but throughput degrades gracefully because locality-aware directory placement keeps most waits intra-datacenter.

**What the numbers do not prove.** The *ε* profile is a property of *Google's deployment*, not of the algorithm. A reimplementation on NTP inherits the correctness but a 250 ms-class latency floor for commit-wait — precisely why CockroachDB chose restarts over waits. The benchmarks also use F1's advertising-backend workload on 5 US replicas, not geo-distributed OLTP with cross-region write affinity, where 2PC across continental Paxos groups would dominate.

**The verification frontier.** Recent timestamp-based isolation checkers — e.g., Chronos [8], which detects violations online from transaction timestamps — have reproduced clock-skew bugs in production systems (including YugabyteDB) that black-box checkers miss. This validates Spanner's methodological bet from the other direction: when timestamps are first-class, correctness checking becomes *easier*.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Hardware dependence.** Small *ε* requires GPS receivers and atomic clocks in every datacenter plus careful daemon engineering. Correctness survives without the hardware (uncertainty becomes latency), but the resulting latency is impractical — a tension the paper underplays.
2. **Commit-wait is a latency floor.** Every externally consistent write pays ~`2ε`; Spanner offers no per-transaction knob trading external consistency for latency.
3. **Leader bottlenecks.** All writes flow through Paxos-group leaders; poor directory placement turns every write into a cross-continental Paxos round. Automatic tablet migration mitigates but does not eliminate this, and the US-only evaluation flatters the numbers.
4. **Reads are not free.** Lock-free ≠ wait-free: a read at `t_r` waits for `t_safe ≥ t_r`, and one long-prepared 2PC transaction stalls `t_safe` at its prepare timestamp across a whole group. Fine-grained safe time narrows the blast radius, but the head-of-line blocking is fundamental.
5. **The linearizability conflation.** Calling external consistency "equivalently, linearizability" invites the belief that per-key linearizability suffices for transactional external consistency. It does not: the multi-partition case needs the commit-wait machinery.
6. **Correlated failure modes.** When *ε* inflates datacenter-wide, *every* write slows simultaneously — an infrastructure-level latency event with no per-transaction backpressure signal.

## 7 Conclusion

Spanner's enduring contribution is not a database but a **discipline**: treat time as an interval, expose uncertainty honestly, and convert it into latency rather than incorrectness. The commit-wait rule — wait until the commit timestamp is certainly in the past — is a two-line protocol with a one-paragraph proof, yet it buys the strongest transactional guarantee known at a cost measured in milliseconds. The surrounding architecture (Paxos groups as the replication unit, 2PC layered over Paxos, `t_safe` for lock-free reads) is conservative engineering in service of that one radical idea.

Whether future systems can recover external consistency from purely logical constructions — hybrid logical clocks with bounded drift, or deterministic protocols with real-time epochs — remains the central open question. Until it is answered, TrueTime stands as both landmark and challenge: proof that the planet can have ACID transactions, at a price measured in milliseconds and metrology.
