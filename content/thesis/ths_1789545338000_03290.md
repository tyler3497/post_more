# Succinct Zero-Knowledge Arguments: zk-SNARKs, Polynomial Commitment Schemes, and the PLONK–FRI Landscape

## Abstract

Succinct zero-knowledge arguments of knowledge (zk-SNARKs) have moved from theoretical objects to deployed infrastructure securing billions of dollars in digital assets. This thesis presents a unified treatment of the modern succinct-argument landscape: the QAP-based Groth16 scheme with its constant three-element proofs [1], the universal and updatable PLONK framework built on permutation arguments over Lagrange bases [2], the KZG and batched SHPLONK polynomial commitment schemes that underpin pairing-based systems [4], and the hash-based FRI low-degree test powering transparent, plausibly post-quantum STARKs [5]. We compare trusted versus transparent setup models, analyze recursive proof composition via Halo-style accumulation [3], and evaluate deployed systems including Zcash and zero-knowledge rollups using benchmark data from recent surveys [6][7]. We also examine lookup-table optimizations via plookup [8] as a case study in arithmetization innovation. Our analysis shows that no single system dominates: Groth16 remains optimal for on-chain verification cost, PLONK offers circuit agility with a universal reference string, and FRI-based STARKs trade proof size for transparency and quantum resistance. We conclude with open problems in post-quantum succinctness, prover parallelization, and the standardization of arithmetization front-ends.

##1 Introduction

Zero-knowledge proofs allow a *prover* to convince a *verifier* that a computation was performed correctly without revealing the private inputs to that computation [6]. When such proofs are *succinct* — meaning proof size and verification time grow sublinearly in the size of the computation — they become practical for blockchains, where every byte of on-chain data carries a direct economic cost. The resulting objects, **zk-SNARKs** (zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge), are among the most consequential cryptographic primitives of the last decade, deployed in privacy-preserving cryptocurrencies, scaling layers, and identity systems [7].

Three design revolutions shaped the current landscape:

1. **Pairing-based succinctness.** Starting with the QAP (quadratic arithmetic program) paradigm, Groth16 reduced proofs to just three elliptic-curve group elements — a lower bound that is, in a precise sense, optimal for its class of constructions [1]. These systems demand a *trusted setup*: a one-time generation of public parameters whose trapdoor must be destroyed, lest soundness collapse.
2. **Universal arithmetization.** PLONK replaced the circuit-specific preprocessing of Groth16 with a universal and updatable structured reference string (SRS), plus a remarkably simple gate model whose constraints are unified by a single permutation argument [2]. This traded a constant factor in proof size for dramatic gains in developer ergonomics and setup reusability.
3. **Transparent hashing-based systems.** The FRI protocol (Fast Reed–Solomon Interactive Oracle Proof of Proximity) enabled STARKs: proofs whose security rests on collision-resistant hash functions and the random-oracle model rather than pairing-friendly elliptic curves [5]. The price is proof size — tens to hundreds of kilobytes versus hundreds of bytes — and the reward is a *transparent* setup (public randomness only) plus plausible post-quantum security.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the definitional core: arguments of knowledge, arithmetization front-ends, and polynomial commitment schemes. Section 3 formalizes the universal compiler pipeline from computation to proof, with executable illustrations. Section 4 deep-dives into four pillars: Groth16, PLONK, KZG commitments, and the FRI/STARK axis, including recursive composition. Section 5 presents empirical benchmarks and the theoretical proofs that bound each system's efficiency. Section 6 catalogs limitations and open problems. Section 7 concludes with a decision framework for practitioners.

##2 Background

### 2.1 Definitions

A *non-interactive argument of knowledge* for an NP relation R consists of three algorithms — setup, prove, and verify — where the prover demonstrates knowledge of a witness w such that the pair (x, w) belongs to R for a public statement x. Soundness requires that a cheating prover cannot produce an accepting proof for a false statement except with negligible probability; *knowledge* soundness strengthens this by demanding an extractor that recovers a valid witness from any successful prover. Zero knowledge requires that the proof leak nothing beyond the truth of the statement, formalized by the existence of a simulator producing indistinguishable transcripts without the witness.

> **Theorem:** (Succinctness lower bounds, informal.) For pairing-based preprocessing zk-SNARKs over bilinear groups with linear preprocessing, proof size of three group elements is optimal in the generic group model under standard knowledge-of-exponent assumptions [1].

The intuition behind Groth16's optimality is that each proof element must simultaneously commit to the witness-dependent polynomials and permit a single pairing check to verify the QAP divisibility relation; two elements leave an extractable degree of freedom that breaks simulation-extractability, while three is both necessary and sufficient.

*Completeness*, *soundness*, and *zero knowledge* are the three classical properties; succinctness adds the requirement that proof size and verifier complexity be polylogarithmic (ideally constant) in the computation size. Arguments satisfying all four are zk-SNARKs.

### 2.2 Arithmetization front-ends

Every SNARK compiles a computation into an algebraic constraint system over a finite field:

- **R1CS** (rank-1 constraint systems): constraints of the form `<a, w> * <b, w> = <c, w>` over a witness vector w. Groth16, libsnark, and circom target R1CS [1][6].
- **Plonkish / custom-gate tables:** computations are laid out as rows of a trace table; each row's cells are wired to gates selected by selector polynomials. PLONK's great insight was that *copy constraints* — equalities between cells — can be enforced globally by a single permutation argument, making the constraint system both flexible and uniform [2].
- **AIR** (algebraic intermediate representation): used by STARKs, where transition constraints relate consecutive rows of an execution trace and boundary constraints pin specific cells [5].

The front-end choice interacts deeply with the commitment layer: pairing-based systems prefer R1CS and Plonkish encodings amenable to KZG commitments [4], while hash-based systems prefer AIR traces whose polynomial extensions are committed via Merkle trees and tested with FRI.

### 2.3 Polynomial commitment schemes

A polynomial commitment scheme (PCS) lets a prover commit to a polynomial f with a short commitment C, then later open the commitment at a point z, revealing y = f(z) together with a proof that the opening is consistent with C [4]. The modern SNARK is best understood as the composition of a polynomial interactive oracle proof (PIOP) — the *information-theoretic* core — with a PCS that *cryptographically compiles* the oracle into a succinct argument. This "PIOP + PCS" lens, articulated in the systematization literature, explains why PLONK can be instantiated with KZG (pairing-based, tiny proofs), with FRI (transparent, larger proofs), or with IPA/Bulletproof-style inner-product arguments [5][7].

> **Lemma:** (KZG binding.) Under the q-strong Diffie–Hellman assumption, a KZG commitment is computationally binding: no efficient adversary can open the same commitment to two different values at one point [4].

The KZG scheme commits as C = g^{f(tau)} where tau is the setup trapdoor embedded in the SRS as powers g^{tau^i}; an opening proof at z is the commitment to the quotient polynomial q(X) = (f(X) − y) / (X − z), checked by the pairing equation e(C / g^y, g) = e(proof, g^{tau} / g^z). SHPLONK and related batching techniques compress openings of many polynomials at many points into a constant number of group elements and pairings [4].

##3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in three stages, mirroring the engineering pipeline of a real deployment.

### 3.1 The universal proving pipeline

We model every system under study as an instance of the following pipeline, and verify the claims of Sections 4–5 against published benchmarks and primary papers [6][7].

```
computation  ──arithmetize──▶  constraint system
      │
      ▼
polynomial IOP (information-theoretic checks on oracles)
      │
      ▼  (cryptographic compilation via PCS + Fiat–Shamir)
succinct non-interactive argument π
```

The *Fiat–Shamir heuristic* converts the public-coin interactive protocol into a non-interactive one by replacing verifier challenges with hashes of the transcript; security is analyzed in the random-oracle model [5].

### 3.2 Reference models

To make arithmetization concrete, consider proving knowledge of a secret square root modulo a prime: the prover knows x with x² = y for public y. In R1CS this is a single constraint. A minimal Python sketch of the witness-generation layer looks like this:

```python
def prove_sqrt_knowledge(x: int, p: int):
    """Witness generator for the relation x^2 = y (mod p)."""
    y = (x * x) % p
    # R1CS: one multiplication constraint, witness vector w = [1, x, y]
    A = [0, 1, 0]   # selects x
    B = [0, 1, 0]   # selects x
    C = [0, 0, 1]   # selects y
    w = [1, x, y]
    assert (sum(a * wi for a, wi in zip(A, w)) *
            sum(b * wi for b, wi in zip(B, w))) % p == \
           (sum(c * wi for c, wi in zip(C, w))) % p
    return y  # public statement
```

The corresponding Rust-style prover interface against a generic PCS backend illustrates the PIOP + PCS separation:

```rust
// Generic prover over any polynomial commitment scheme.
fn prove_relation<PCS: PolynomialCommitment>(
    srs: &PCS::SRS,
    witness: &Witness,
    statement: &Statement,
) -> Proof {
    let polys = arithmetize(witness, statement);   // front-end: R1CS/Plonkish/AIR
    let mut transcript = Transcript::new(b"snark");
    let coms: Vec<PCS::Commitment> =
        polys.iter().map(|f| PCS::commit(srs, f, &mut transcript)).collect();
    let challenges = transcript.squeeze(3);        // Fiat-Shamir
    let quotient = build_quotient(&polys, &challenges);
    let eval_proof = PCS::open(srs, &quotient, &challenges[0], &mut transcript);
    Proof { coms, eval_proof }
}
```

Finally, a TLA+-style specification fragment captures the verifier's state machine for the batched KZG opening check:

```tla
---- MODULE KZGVerify ----
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES com, claimedY, proof, srsTau, acc
CheckOpening ==
    /\ acc = Pairing(com / G^claimedY, G)
    /\ acc = Pairing(proof, srsTau / G^z)
    /\ PrintT("opening accepted")
====
```

##4 The SNARK Landscape: A Deep Dive

### 4.1 Groth16: the pairing-optimal baseline

Groth16 compiles an R1CS instance into a QAP: matrices A, B, C are interpolated into polynomials evaluated at the constraint indices, and the prover shows that the polynomial identity A(X)·B(X) − C(X) = H(X)·Z(X) holds, where Z is the vanishing polynomial of the constraint domain [1]. The proof is three group elements (two in G1, one in G2); verification is three pairings plus a linear combination of public inputs [1][6].

The cost of this optimality is a *circuit-specific* trusted setup: the common reference string (CRS) encodes the circuit's QAP structure, so every new circuit needs a new ceremony. Zcash's Powers of Tau and subsequent MPC ceremonies (involving dozens of participants, any one of whom could have destroyed their "toxic waste") made Groth16 deployment practical, but the per-circuit ceremony remains a governance and engineering burden [6][7].

> **Theorem:** (Groth16 knowledge soundness.) Under the generic group model and knowledge-of-exponent assumptions, Groth16 is a knowledge-sound zk-SNARK: an extractor recovers a satisfying R1CS witness from any accepting prover [1].

### 4.2 PLONK: universal setup and the permutation argument

PLONK's two headline innovations were a *universal and updatable* SRS — one ceremony serves all circuits up to a bounded size, and security holds as long as one participant is honest — and a gate model in which arbitrary custom gates are expressed through selector polynomials [2]. The arithmetization packs, for each of n gates, five wire values (left, right, output, plus two more in TurboPLONK) and enforces:

- **Gate identity:** q_L·a + q_R·b + q_O·c + q_M·a·b + q_C = 0 per gate, with selector polynomials choosing the gate type.
- **Copy constraints:** a permutation sigma over all wire positions encodes which cells must hold equal values; the prover shows that the permuted wire polynomial matches, via the now-famous permutation product argument.

The gate identity and permutation argument are folded into a single quotient polynomial t(X), committed with KZG, and opened at a Fiat–Shamir challenge [2]. TurboPLONK added custom gates and plookup added *lookup arguments* — proving that wire values belong to a precomputed table — which makes non-native operations like range checks and bitwise logic dramatically cheaper; a plookup table of size 2^k handles all k-bit operations in a constant number of constraints [8].

### 4.3 Polynomial commitments: KZG, batching, and the DARK alternative

The PCS is the fulcrum of the efficiency tradeoff. KZG gives constant-size commitments and constant-size opening proofs at the price of a trusted setup and pairing-friendly curves [4]. SHPLONK-style batching (the Boneh–Drake–Fisch–Gabizon construction) opens arbitrarily many polynomials at many points with proof size independent of the batch size, at the cost of extra G2 SRS elements and verifier pairings — a tradeoff that favors rollups, where thousands of constraints are verified on-chain and prover-side batching amortizes cleanly [4].

At the other end of the spectrum sit *transparent* PCSs: FRI-based commitments (Merkle roots over Reed–Solomon codewords) and DARK-based commitments built on groups of unknown order [5]. DARKs yield logarithmic proof sizes without trusted setup, but their concrete constants and reliance on class groups have limited deployment. The field has largely converged on a binary choice for production: **KZG** for minimal on-chain cost, **FRI** for transparency and quantum resistance.

### 4.4 FRI, STARKs, and recursive composition

FRI is an interactive oracle proof of proximity: the prover commits to evaluations of a degree-d polynomial over a large domain, then repeatedly *folds* — the verifier sends a random challenge alpha, and the prover commits to a degree-halved polynomial combining even and odd coefficients — until a constant remains [5]. A logarithmic number of query rounds then check consistency between adjacent layers; any polynomial far from the Reed–Solomon code is caught with overwhelming probability. Because folding is hash-committed, the whole protocol compiles through Fiat–Shamir into a transparent non-interactive argument whose only cryptographic assumption is a collision-resistant hash function [5].

STARKs instantiate this with AIR arithmetization: the execution trace becomes a polynomial, transition and boundary constraints become a composition polynomial, and FRI certifies its low degree. Proofs are tens to hundreds of kilobytes, but proving is quasi-linear and highly parallelizable, verification is polylogarithmic, and there is no trapdoor anywhere in the system [5][7].

*Recursion* — proving the correctness of a verifier inside another proof — changes the scaling calculus. Halo introduced recursion without trusted setup via an *accumulation* scheme: instead of verifying a full inner proof in-circuit (prohibitively expensive for pairing checks), the verifier's expensive work is deferred into an accumulator that is itself proven later, amortizing cost across the recursion chain [3]. Modern folding schemes (Nova, HyperNova) generalize this to incrementally verifiable computation, and STARK-based recursion (as in StarkWare's SHARP and Polygon's recursion layers) exploits the hash-based verifier's SNARK-friendliness to compress many proofs into one [5][7].

##5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark comparison

The following table consolidates representative figures from primary papers and independent surveys; exact numbers depend on hardware, curve, and implementation maturity [6][7].

| System | Proof size | Verifier time | Setup | Post-quantum | Prover scaling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Groth16 | ~200 bytes (3 group elements) | ~1.5 ms, 3 pairings | Trusted, per-circuit | No | O(n log n) |
| PLONK (KZG) | ~400–900 bytes | ~3–7 ms | Universal, updatable | No | O(n log n) |
| PLONK + plookup | ~400–900 bytes | ~3–7 ms | Universal, updatable | No | O(n log n), fewer n |
| Bulletproofs / IPA | O(log n), ~1.5 KB at 2^20 | O(n) | Transparent | No | O(n) |
| zk-STARK (FRI) | ~80–200 KB | ~10 ms | Transparent | Plausibly yes | O(n log² n), parallel |
| Halo 2 / folding | ~5–20 KB | ~10–50 ms | Transparent | No | O(n log n) |

Two theoretical results frame these numbers. First, Groth16's three-element proof is *optimal* for pairing-based preprocessing SNARKs in the generic group model — no scheme in this class can do with fewer [1]. Second, FRI's soundness error decays exponentially in the number of query repetitions, giving a tunable knob between proof size and security that hash-based systems exploit to reach 100+ bits of conjectured security at the cost of larger proofs [5].

> **Lemma:** (FRI soundness.) If the committed function is delta-far from every Reed–Solomon codeword of the claimed degree bound, then a single FRI query round rejects with probability at least delta, up to small proximity-gap corrections; repetition amplifies this exponentially [5].

### 5.2 Deployments

*Zcash* pioneered production zk-SNARKs, migrating from Pinocchio to Groth16 and ultimately toward Halo 2's recursion-friendly design; its multi-round MPC ceremonies set the template for trustworthy trusted setups [6][7]. *Zero-knowledge rollups* (zkSync, Polygon zkEVM, StarkNet, Scroll) post validity proofs to Ethereum L1, where verifier gas cost dominates economics: PLONK-family proofs with KZG commitments and batched openings minimize on-chain bytes, while STARK-based rollups accept larger proofs in exchange for faster, parallel proving of EVM execution traces [7]. *Filecoin* uses Groth16-family proofs for proof-of-replication at massive scale, demonstrating that per-circuit setup costs amortize when the circuit is fixed [6].

The plookup optimization deserves special mention as an empirical win: by replacing bitwise decomposition gadgets (thousands of constraints per hash) with table lookups, circuits for SHA-256 and similar primitives shrank by an order of magnitude, directly reducing prover time and CRS size [8].

### 5.3 Security arguments

Knowledge soundness for Groth16 and PLONK is proven in the generic or algebraic group model under knowledge-of-exponent-type assumptions [1][2]; simulation-extractability variants (needed when proofs are malleable in adversarial protocols) add randomization or stronger assumptions [6]. FRI-based systems instead reduce to the random-oracle model plus combinatorial proximity gaps — a qualitatively different, and arguably more conservative, assumption profile [5]. The Fiat–Shamir transform is the shared bridge from interactive to non-interactive in all deployed systems, and its concrete security in the presence of grinding attacks remains an active analysis area.

##6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Post-quantum succinctness.** All pairing-based systems (Groth16, PLONK/KZG) fall to Shor's algorithm. Hash-based STARKs are plausibly quantum-resistant but pay a 100–1000x proof-size penalty [5][7]. Lattice- and isogeny-based succinct arguments remain far from practical; closing this gap is the field's central open problem.
2. **Prover scalability.** Proving remains superlinear and memory-hungry: circuits with 2^28 constraints demand hundreds of gigabytes of RAM in current implementations. GPU/FPGA acceleration, distributed proving, and linear-time PIOPs (as in HyperPlonk-style systems) are active mitigations [6].
3. **Trusted setup governance.** Even universal updatable setups require at least one honest ceremony participant *perpetually*; the social layer of MPC ceremonies is under-theorized relative to its security importance [7].
4. **Arithmetization fragmentation.** R1CS, Plonkish, and AIR front-ends are mutually incompatible, forcing developers to bet on an ecosystem. A standardized, backend-agnostic intermediate representation is missing [6].
5. **Recursive verifier costs.** In-circuit pairing verification remains the bottleneck for pairing-based recursion; cycle-of-curves techniques (as in Halo 2) mitigate but do not eliminate it [3].
6. **Formal verification.** Machine-checked proofs of SNARK implementations (as opposed to paper proofs) are rare; bugs in constraint systems have caused real fund losses, and the gap between specification and deployed code is a systemic risk.

##7 Conclusion

The PLONK–FRI landscape is best understood not as a horse race but as a *design space* parameterized by setup trust, proof size, prover cost, and quantum assumptions. Groth16 remains the right choice when on-chain verification cost is paramount and a per-circuit ceremony is acceptable [1]. PLONK dominates the middle ground: one universal setup, expressive custom gates, lookup tables via plookup, and proofs small enough for L1 verification [2][8]. FRI-based STARKs win where transparency and post-quantum plausibility outweigh proof size, and where massive parallel proving amortizes their constants [5]. Recursive composition via Halo-style accumulation is steadily erasing the boundaries, letting systems nest proofs to get the best of several worlds [3].

For practitioners, our decision framework is simple: *count your verifier's budget first*. If every byte costs gas, choose KZG-backed PLONK with batched openings [4]; if your threat model includes quantum adversaries or untrusted setup coordinators, choose FRI [5]; if your proof must be as small as physically possible, Groth16 remains unbeaten [1]. The next decade will likely belong to hybrids — folding-based recursion over transparent commitments — that refuse to choose at all.
