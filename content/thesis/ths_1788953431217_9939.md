# Spectral Sequences in Homological Algebra: Exact Couples, the Grothendieck–Leray–Serre Machinery, Edge Homomorphisms, Transgression, and Classical Cohomology Computations

## Abstract

Spectral sequences are the central computational engine of homological algebra, organizing the passage from local or filtered data to global homological invariants through a succession of pages $E_r^{p,q}$ linked by differentials $d_r$ of controlled bidegree. This thesis develops the theory from its foundational source — Massey's exact couples [7] — through the two canonical constructions (filtered complexes and double complexes), and into the three great functorial spectral sequences of the subject: Grothendieck's spectral sequence for composite derived functors [4], Leray's spectral sequence for sheaf cohomology, and Serre's spectral sequence for fibrations [8], together with the Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre sequence in group cohomology [1]. We treat edge homomorphisms, the five-term exact sequence, transgression, and the degeneration and collapse phenomena that make the machinery decisive in practice. The theory is exercised on worked computations: the integral cohomology ring of complex projective spaces via the Hopf fibration, and the rational and integral structure of the cohomology of Eilenberg–MacLane spaces via the path–loop fibration. A Haskell formalization of pages, differentials, and derived couples accompanies the exposition.

## 1. Introduction

Few devices in mathematics repay study as richly as the spectral sequence. Where an exact sequence relates three objects in a line, a spectral sequence relates an entire bigraded lattice of groups to a single graded object — the *abutment* — through a limiting process of successive approximation. Each page $E_r$ is a bigraded complex whose homology is the next page $E_{r+1}$; the differentials $d_r$ grow longer as $r$ increases, $d_r : E_r^{p,q} \to E_r^{p+r,\,q-r+1}$ in cohomological indexing, until eventually every differential entering or leaving a fixed bidegree vanishes and the pages *stabilize* at $E_\infty$. The stable page is not the abutment itself but its *associated graded* with respect to a filtration, so that the final step is always an *extension problem* [3].

The historical arc runs from Leray's 1940s construction [5] through Serre's 1951 thesis [8], Grothendieck's 1957 Tôhoku unification via composite derived functors [4], and Massey's exact couples [7]; standard modern references are Weibel [1], McCleary [2], and Hatcher's notes [3].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes conventions: cohomological versus homological indexing, pages, differentials, convergence, and the extension problem. Section 3 develops the methodology — exact couples and derived couples, filtered complexes, and double complexes — with a Haskell model of the derivation process. Section 4, the deep dive, treats the four functorial spectral sequences: Grothendieck, Leray, Serre (homology and cohomology), and Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre, including edge maps and transgression. Section 5 carries out the worked computations: $H^*(\mathbb{C}P^n;\mathbb{Z})$ from the Hopf fibration, and $H^*(K(\mathbb{Z},n))$ from the path–loop fibration. Section 6 discusses limitations: non-convergence, the extension problem, multiplicative indeterminacy, and conditional convergence for unbounded filtrations.

> **Theorem:** (Leray, 1946; modern form [4]). Let $X, Y$ be suitable sites and $f : X \to Y$ a morphism. For a sheaf of abelian groups $\mathcal{F}$ on $X$ there is a cohomological spectral sequence $E_2^{p,q} = H^p(Y, R^q f_* \mathcal{F}) \Rightarrow H^{p+q}(X, \mathcal{F})$.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Definitions and indexing conventions

A **cohomological spectral sequence** consists of bigraded abelian groups (or modules, or objects of an abelian category) $E_r^{p,q}$ for integers $r \ge 1$ (sometimes $r \ge 0$ or $r \ge 2$), together with differentials

$$d_r : E_r^{p,q} \longrightarrow E_r^{p+r,\, q-r+1}, \qquad d_r^2 = 0,$$

such that $E_{r+1}^{p,q} \cong H^{p,q}(E_r, d_r)$, the homology at bidegree $(p,q)$. A **homological spectral sequence** has instead $E^r_{p,q}$ with $d^r : E^r_{p,q} \to E^r_{p-r,\, q+r-1}$ [3][5]. The two are dual by reindexing; we work cohomologically except where homology is traditional (Serre's original setting).

The sequence **converges** to a graded object $H^n$ equipped with a decreasing filtration $\cdots \supseteq F^p H^n \supseteq F^{p+1} H^n \supseteq \cdots$ if

$$E_\infty^{p,q} \;\cong\; F^p H^{p+q} \big/ F^{p+1} H^{p+q},$$

written $E_r^{p,q} \Rightarrow H^{p+q}$. Here $E_\infty^{p,q}$ denotes the stable value: for each fixed $(p,q)$, $d_r$ into and out of $E_r^{p,q}$ vanishes for all sufficiently large $r$ (automatic for first-quadrant sequences), so $E_r^{p,q}$ is eventually constant [1][3].

The passage from $E_\infty$ to $H^*$ is the **extension problem**: knowing the successive quotients $F^p/F^{p+1}$ does not determine $H^n$ in general. Over a field, extensions split and $H^n \cong \bigoplus_p E_\infty^{p,n-p}$ as vector spaces; over $\mathbb{Z}$, genuine extension data (e.g., $\mathbb{Z}/4$ versus $\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2$ from quotients $\mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/2$) must be resolved by other means [2].

### 2.2 The landscape of classical spectral sequences

The following table organizes the principal spectral sequences treated in this thesis [1][2][5]:

| Spectral sequence | $E_2$-page | Abutment | Origin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grothendieck (composite functors) | $E_2^{p,q} = (R^p G)(R^q F)(A)$ | $R^{p+q}(GF)(A)$ | Grothendieck 1957 [4] |
| Leray (sheaf, $f : X \to Y$) | $E_2^{p,q} = H^p(Y; R^q f_*\mathcal{F})$ | $H^{p+q}(X; \mathcal{F})$ | Leray 1946 [5] |
| Serre (fibration $F \to E \to B$) | $E_2^{p,q} = H^p(B; H^q(F; G))$ | $H^{p+q}(E; G)$ | Serre 1951 [8] |
| Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre ($1 \to N \to G \to Q \to 1$) | $E_2^{p,q} = H^p(Q; H^q(N; M))$ | $H^{p+q}(G; M)$ | Hochschild–Serre 1953 [1] |
| Hypercohomology (complex $K^\bullet$) | $E_2^{p,q} = R^p F(H^q(K^\bullet))$ | $\mathbb{R}^{p+q}F(K^\bullet)$ | Grothendieck [4] |
| Künneth | $E^2_{p,q} = \bigoplus_{i+j=q} \mathrm{Tor}_p(H_i X, H_j Y)$ | $H_{p+q}(X \times Y)$ | Classical [3] |

All but the last are instances, or corollaries, of the Grothendieck spectral sequence — a fact that exhibits the unity of the subject [1].

## 3. Methodology: Exact Couples, Filtered Complexes, and Double Complexes

### 3.1 Exact couples and derived couples

The most economical construction of spectral sequences is due to Massey [7]. An **exact couple** is a diagram of bigraded objects and homogeneous maps

$$\begin{array}{ccc} D & \xrightarrow{\,i\,} & D \\\\ & \searrow^{j} & \downarrow^{k} \\\\ & & E \end{array}$$

exact at each vertex: $\mathrm{im}\,i = \ker j$, $\mathrm{im}\,j = \ker k$, $\mathrm{im}\,k = \ker i$. The maps have bidegrees $\deg i = (a,b)$, $\deg j = (c,d)$, $\deg k = (e,f)$ with $a+c+e = 0$ in a suitable sense; the standard cohomological convention takes $\deg i = (-1,1)$, $\deg j = (0,0)$, $\deg k = (1,0)$, so that $d = j \circ k : E^{p,q} \to E^{p+1,q}$ has bidegree $(1,0)$ on the $E_1$-page [3][7].

> **Theorem:** (Massey [7]). From an exact couple $\mathcal{C} = (D, E, i, j, k)$ one forms the **derived couple** $\mathcal{C}' = (D', E', i', j', k')$ with $D' = \mathrm{im}\,i$, $E' = H(E, d)$ where $d = jk$, and induced maps $i', j', k'$ making $\mathcal{C}'$ exact. Iterating yields a spectral sequence $E_r$ with $E_{r+1} = H(E_r, d_r)$ and $d_r = j^{(r-1)} k^{(r-1)}$ of bidegree shifting by $r$.

The verification is a diagram chase: $d^2 = jkjk = 0$ because $kj = 0$ by exactness at $E$, and the derived maps are well-defined by exactness at $D$ [7]. The construction is functorial and underlies Matschke's successive spectral systems [9] and the exact-couple approach to persistent homology [10].

We model this derivation in Haskell, making the bidegree bookkeeping explicit:

```haskell
{-# LANGUAGE DataKinds #-}
-- | A page of a cohomological spectral sequence: bigraded groups
--   with a differential of bidegree (r, 1 - r).
data Page (r :: Nat) g = Page
  { eGroup :: (Int, Int) -> g
  , eDiff  :: (Int, Int) -> g -> g   -- d_r : E_r^{p,q} -> E_r^{p+r, q-r+1}
  }

-- | An exact couple (D, E, i, j, k), exact at each vertex.
data ExactCouple d e = ExactCouple
  { iMap :: d -> d   -- degree (-1, 1): the "staircase" endomorphism
  , jMap :: d -> e   -- degree (0, 0)
  , kMap :: e -> d   -- degree (1, 0)
  }

-- | The differential d = j . k satisfies d^2 = 0 because k . j = 0
--   by exactness at E.
coupleDiff :: ExactCouple d e -> e -> e
coupleDiff c = jMap c . kMap c

-- | One derivation step: E_{r+1} = ker(d_r) / im(d_r), with the
--   induced differential lengthened by one step.
derive :: (AbelianGroup g) => Page r g -> Page (r+1) g
derive p = Page
  { eGroup = \(x, y) -> homologyOf (eDiff p (x - r, y + r - 1))
                                    (eDiff p (x, y))
  , eDiff  = inducedDifferential (eDiff p)
  }
  where r = pageIndex p
```

The point of the code is *type-level documentation* of the derivation: the bidegree shift, the homology construction, and the lengthening of the differential are all visible [9].

### 3.2 Filtered complexes

Let $K^\bullet$ be a cochain complex with a decreasing filtration $F^p K^\bullet$ by subcomplexes. The associated exact couple has

$$D_1^{p,q} = H^{p+q}(F^p K), \qquad E_1^{p,q} = H^{p+q}(F^p K / F^{p+1} K),$$

with $i$ induced by inclusion $F^{p+1} \hookrightarrow F^p$, $j$ by projection, and $k$ the connecting homomorphism of the long exact sequence of the pair [1][3]. The resulting spectral sequence satisfies $E_1^{p,q} = H^{p+q}(\mathrm{gr}^p K) \Rightarrow H^{p+q}(K)$ whenever the filtration is *bounded* (or more generally exhaustive and complete, cf. Section 6).

### 3.3 Double complexes

A **double complex** $K^{p,q}$ with commuting differentials $d_h d_v + d_v d_h = 0$ has total complex $\mathrm{Tot}^n = \bigoplus_{p+q=n} K^{p,q}$. Filtering by columns or by rows yields two spectral sequences, $^{I}E$ and $^{II}E$, both converging to $H^*(\mathrm{Tot}\,K)$:

$$^{I}E_2^{p,q} = H^p_h(H^q_v(K)), \qquad ^{II}E_2^{p,q} = H^p_v(H^q_h(K)) \;\; \Rightarrow \;\; H^{p+q}(\mathrm{Tot}\,K).$$

When one filtration collapses (e.g., rows exact except in one degree), the surviving spectral sequence computes the total cohomology directly. This is the mechanism behind the Grothendieck spectral sequence: one builds a double complex from a Cartan–Eilenberg resolution and reads off both pages [1][4].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Grothendieck spectral sequence for composite functors

> **Theorem:** (Grothendieck [4]; Weibel 5.8.3 [1]). Let $\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{B}, \mathcal{C}$ be abelian categories with enough injectives, $F : \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{B}$ and $G : \mathcal{B} \to \mathcal{C}$ left exact functors, and suppose $F$ sends injective objects of $\mathcal{A}$ to $G$-acyclic objects of $\mathcal{B}$. Then for every $A \in \mathcal{A}$ there is a cohomological spectral sequence, functorial in $A$,
>
> $$E_2^{p,q} = (R^p G)(R^q F)(A) \;\; \Longrightarrow \;\; R^{p+q}(GF)(A).$$

*Sketch.* Take an injective resolution $A \to I^\bullet$; then $F(I^\bullet)$ is a complex of $G$-acyclic objects. Choose a Cartan–Eilenberg injective resolution $J^{\bullet,\bullet}$ of the complex $F(I^\bullet)$ and apply $G$ to get a double complex $G(J^{\bullet,\bullet})$. The column-filtered spectral sequence collapses at $E_2$ to $R^*(GF)(A)$ because the columns resolve the $G$-acyclic complex $F(I^\bullet)$; the row-filtered page is $^{II}E_2^{p,q} = R^p G(H^q(F(I^\bullet))) = (R^p G)(R^q F)(A)$ [1][4].

The hypothesis is sharp and checkable: for instance, if $F$ has an exact left adjoint, then $F$ preserves injectives, hence sends them to $G$-acyclics for every $G$. The Grothendieck spectral sequence is the source from which Leray's and Hochschild–Serre's sequences flow as corollaries [1].

### 4.2 The Leray spectral sequence for sheaf cohomology

For a continuous map $f : X \to Y$ and a sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ of abelian groups on $X$, the global section functor factors as $\Gamma(X, -) = \Gamma(Y, -) \circ f_*$. Since $f_*$ sends injective sheaves to flasque sheaves, which are $\Gamma(Y,-)$-acyclic, Grothendieck's theorem applies verbatim [4][5]:

> **Theorem:** (Leray–Grothendieck). There is a spectral sequence $E_2^{p,q} = H^p(Y; R^q f_* \mathcal{F}) \Rightarrow H^{p+q}(X; \mathcal{F})$, where $R^q f_*$ are the higher direct image sheaves.

Geometrically, $R^q f_*\mathcal{F}$ packages the cohomology of the fibers, and the spectral sequence assembles fiberwise cohomology into the cohomology of the total space [5]. The Leray sequence *degenerates* ($E_2 = E_\infty$) whenever $R^q f_*\mathcal{F} = 0$ for $q > 0$ — e.g., for Stein maps in complex geometry — giving $H^n(X; \mathcal{F}) \cong H^n(Y; f_*\mathcal{F})$ [2].

### 4.3 The Serre spectral sequence for fibrations

> **Theorem:** (Serre [8]; Hatcher Ch. 1 [3]). Let $F \to E \xrightarrow{\pi} B$ be a fibration with $B$ path-connected, and let $G$ be abelian with trivial $\pi_1(B)$-action on $H^*(F; G)$ (automatic if $B$ is simply connected). Then there is a cohomological spectral sequence
>
> $$E_2^{p,q} = H^p(B; H^q(F; G)) \;\; \Longrightarrow \;\; H^{p+q}(E; G),$$
>
> and a homological version $E^2_{p,q} = H_p(B; H_q(F; G)) \Rightarrow H_{p+q}(E; G)$.

The construction filters the singular complex of $E$ by preimages of skeleta of $B$ (or, in Hatcher's treatment, builds an exact couple from the resulting staircase diagram and verifies convergence via the finiteness conditions on each column) [3]. With nontrivial monodromy, $H^q(F; G)$ is replaced by the local coefficient system $\mathcal{H}^q(F; G)$ over $B$ [8].

The Serre sequence carries a **multiplicative structure**: if $G$ is a ring, each page $E_r$ is a bigraded algebra and $d_r$ is a derivation, $d_r(xy) = d_r(x)\,y + (-1)^{|x|} x\,d_r(y)$ [3]. This Leibniz rule is what makes the sequence a computational tool: differentials are often forced by multiplicativity from their values on generators.

**Edge homomorphisms.** The bottom row $E_2^{p,0} = H^p(B; G)$ and left column $E_2^{0,q} = H^q(F; G)$ (for simply connected $B$) survive to $E_\infty$ along the edges, giving the *edge maps*

$$H^n(E; G) \;\twoheadrightarrow\; E_\infty^{0,n} \hookrightarrow H^n(F; G), \qquad H^n(B; G) = E_2^{n,0} \twoheadrightarrow E_\infty^{n,0} \hookrightarrow H^n(E; G),$$

which are precisely restriction to the fiber and pullback along $\pi$ [2][3]. Low-degree terms assemble into exact sequences relating the cohomology of $F$, $E$, and $B$.

**Transgression.** Suppose $x \in H^n(F; G) = E_2^{0,n}$ survives to $E_{n+1}$ (i.e., $d_2 x = \cdots = d_n x = 0$). Then $d_{n+1} x \in E_{n+1}^{\,n+1, 0} = H^{n+1}(B; G)$ (modulo earlier indeterminacy) defines the **transgression** $\tau(x) \in H^{n+1}(B; G)$. Dually, elements of $H^n(B)$ that survive transgress *from* the base. In the universal fibration $K(\mathbb{Z},n-1) \to * \to K(\mathbb{Z},n)$, transgression is an isomorphism and is the engine computing $H^*(K(\mathbb{Z},n))$ inductively [8].

### 4.4 The Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre sequence in group cohomology

A group extension $1 \to N \to G \xrightarrow{\pi} Q \to 1$ and a $G$-module $M$ give $\,M^G = (M^N)^Q$, i.e., invariants factor as $(-)^G = (-)^Q \circ (-)^N$. Both functors are left exact, and $(-)^N$ sends injectives to $(-)^Q$-acyclics (injective $G$-modules restrict to injective $N$-modules, and $H^q(N; I) = 0$ for injective $I$) [1]. Grothendieck's theorem therefore yields:

> **Theorem:** (Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre). There is a spectral sequence $E_2^{p,q} = H^p(Q; H^q(N; M)) \Rightarrow H^{p+q}(G; M)$, where $Q$ acts on $H^q(N; M)$ via conjugation in $G$.

The edge maps produce the celebrated **five-term exact sequence**

$$0 \to H^1(Q; M^N) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{inf}} H^1(G; M) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{res}} H^1(N; M)^Q \xrightarrow{\mathrm{tg}} H^2(Q; M^N) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{inf}} H^2(G; M),$$

where $\mathrm{inf}$ is inflation, $\mathrm{res}$ restriction, and $\mathrm{tg}$ transgression. The map $H^2(Q; M^N) \to H^2(G; M)$ classifies which extensions of $Q$ lift through $G$.

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Cohomology of complex projective space via the Hopf fibration

Consider the Hopf fibration $S^1 \to S^{2n+1} \to \mathbb{C}P^n$. The cohomological Serre sequence has

$$E_2^{p,q} = H^p(\mathbb{C}P^n; H^q(S^1; \mathbb{Z})) \;\; \Longrightarrow \;\; H^{p+q}(S^{2n+1}; \mathbb{Z}),$$

with only rows $q = 0, 1$ nonzero. We argue by induction on $n$, knowing $H^*(S^{2n+1})$ is $\mathbb{Z}$ in degrees $0$ and $2n+1$. Suppose $H^*(\mathbb{C}P^{n-1}) \cong \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^n)$ with $|x| = 2$; the cofiber sequence $S^{2n-1} \to \mathbb{C}P^{n-1} \to \mathbb{C}P^n$ shows $H^{2k}(\mathbb{C}P^n) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ for $0 \le k \le n$ and $0$ otherwise (below degree $2n+1$) [3].

On the $E_2$-page, $d_2 : E_2^{p,1} \to E_2^{p+2,0}$ is the only possibly nonzero differential. Since $E_\infty^{p,q} = 0$ for $0 < p+q < 2n+1$ except $(0,0)$, each $d_2 : E_2^{2k,1} \to E_2^{2k+2,0}$ must be an isomorphism for $0 \le k < n$: with $E_2^{2k,1} \cong H^{2k}(\mathbb{C}P^n) \cong \mathbb{Z} \cong H^{2k+2}(\mathbb{C}P^n) = E_2^{2k+2,0}$, exactness of the $d_2$-complex forces the isomorphism [3].

Multiplicativity then determines the ring structure: writing $u$ for the generator of $E_2^{0,1} \cong \mathbb{Z}$ and $x$ for the generator of $E_2^{2,0}$, the Leibniz rule and the isomorphism $d_2 : E_2^{0,1} \xrightarrow{\cong} E_2^{2,0}$ force $x^n \ne 0$ while $x^{n+1} = 0$ (since $H^{2n+2}(\mathbb{C}P^n) = 0$). Hence:

> **Theorem:** $H^*(\mathbb{C}P^n; \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^{n+1})$ with $|x| = 2$. Passing to the colimit, $H^*(\mathbb{C}P^\infty; \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}[x]$, the polynomial ring — i.e., $H^*(K(\mathbb{Z},2); \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}[x]$.

No extension problem arises: every $E_\infty^{p,q}$ is free abelian, so the filtration splits (non-canonically) [2][3].

### 5.2 Cohomology of Eilenberg–MacLane spaces

Serre applied his spectral sequence to the **path–loop fibration** $K(\mathbb{Z}, n-1) \to P \to K(\mathbb{Z}, n)$ with contractible total space $P$ [8]. The $E_2$-page $E_2^{p,q} = H^p(K(\mathbb{Z},n); H^q(K(\mathbb{Z},n-1)))$ converges to $0$ in positive total degree. Knowing $H^*(K(\mathbb{Z},n-1))$ inductively, the differentials are forced: everything must die, and the transgression $d_{n+1} : E_{n+1}^{0,n} \to E_{n+1}^{n+1,0}$ is an isomorphism on the relevant groups. This inductive machine yields, rationally,

$$H^*(K(\mathbb{Z}, n); \mathbb{Q}) \cong \begin{cases} \mathbb{Q}[x], & |x| = n,\ n\ \text{even}, \\\\ \Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}[x], & |x| = n,\ n\ \text{odd}, \end{cases}$$

the polynomial algebra for even $n$ and the exterior algebra for odd $n$ — a consequence of graded commutativity $x^2 = (-1)^{n^2} x^2$ [8]. Integrally the picture is far richer: $H^*(K(\mathbb{Z},n); \mathbb{F}_p)$ is a free unstable algebra over the Steenrod algebra generated by admissible iterated Steenrod operations on the fundamental class $\iota_n$ (the Serre–Cartan computation), e.g. $H^*(K(\mathbb{Z},2); \mathbb{F}_2) \cong \mathbb{F}_2[\mathrm{Sq}^{2^k}\!\cdots\mathrm{Sq}^2\iota_2]$ [8]. The integral $p$-torsion is then detected via the Bockstein spectral sequence [2].

### 5.3 Degeneration and collapse: when the machine stops early

A spectral sequence **collapses** at $E_r$ if $d_s = 0$ for all $s \ge r$, so $E_r = E_{r+1} = \cdots = E_\infty$. Collapse is guaranteed *for degree reasons* whenever the bigrading forces every $d_r$ to land in a zero group — e.g., a first-quadrant sequence concentrated in a single row or column. Important instances [2]:

- **Künneth / trivial fibrations:** for a product $E = B \times F$ the Serre sequence has $d_r = 0$ for $r \ge 2$ on the nose (the product filtration splits), giving $H^*(B \times F) \cong H^*(B) \otimes H^*(F) \oplus \mathrm{Tor}$-terms.
- **Hodge-to-de Rham degeneration:** for a compact Kähler manifold, the Frölicher spectral sequence $E_1^{p,q} = H^q(X, \Omega^p) \Rightarrow H^{p+q}_{dR}(X)$ degenerates at $E_1$ (Deligne–Illusie), so $H^n_{dR} \cong \bigoplus_{p+q=n} H^{p,q}$ — the Hodge decomposition [2].
- **Formality:** if the total complex is formal (quasi-isomorphic to its cohomology), all higher differentials vanish; compact Kähler manifolds are formal (Deligne–Griffiths–Morgan–Sullivan).

Collapse converts the spectral sequence from an approximation scheme into a direct sum decomposition, modulo the extension problem — which itself vanishes over a field or when all groups are free [1][2].

## 6. Limitations

**Convergence is not automatic.** For unbounded or non-exhaustive filtrations the derived couple may fail to converge; the correct framework is Boardman's *conditional convergence*, which identifies the $\lim^1$ obstructions [2].

**The extension problem is genuine.** $E_\infty$ determines only the associated graded of $H^*$. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ this leaves real ambiguity — e.g., a filtration with quotients $\mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/2$ may abut to $\mathbb{Z}/4$ or $\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2$ — resolvable only with extra structure (multiplication, Bocksteins, or comparison maps) [1][2].

**Differentials can be uncomputable in practice.** The formalism guarantees existence of $d_r$ but no algorithm: in the Adams spectral sequence, differentials encode deep unstable homotopy theory and remain unknown in large ranges. Transgression is defined only on the subgroup that survives to $E_{n+1}$, introducing indeterminacy.

**Naturality has limits.** The Serre spectral sequence with local coefficients requires care with the $\pi_1$-action; the Grothendieck sequence demands enough injectives and the acyclicity hypothesis, which fails for arbitrary functor pairs. Massey's exact couple machine, while universal, produces pages whose terms may be unidentifiable without the geometric input of a filtered complex [7][9].

---

## 7. Conclusion

From Massey's exact couples [7] to Grothendieck's functorial synthesis [4], spectral sequences provide a single language in which filtration, derivation, and approximation become one process. The Grothendieck sequence explains *why* composites of derived functors admit stepwise computation; Leray's and Serre's sequences are its geometric incarnations; the Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre sequence carries the same architecture into group cohomology, where edge maps crystallize as the five-term exact sequence. Transgression extracts concrete maps from the abstract machine, while collapse theorems mark the cases where approximation becomes exact. The computations of $H^*(\mathbb{C}P^n)$ and $H^*(K(\mathbb{Z},n))$ show the theory at full power: pages forced by vanishing, differentials forced by multiplicativity, rings emerging from pure homological algebra.
