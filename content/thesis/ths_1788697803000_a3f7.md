# Discontinuous Galerkin Methods for Hyperbolic and Elliptic Problems: Weak Formulations on Broken Spaces, Numerical Fluxes, and hp-Adaptivity

## Abstract

The discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method admits no inter-element continuity in its trial spaces, trading global coupling for purely *local* weak formulations on *broken* Sobolev spaces connected solely through **numerical fluxes**. First introduced by Reed and Hill for neutron transport (1973) [1], systematized for conservation laws by the Runge–Kutta DG series of Cockburn and Shu [2][3], extended to convection–diffusion by the local DG method [4], unified for elliptic problems by Arnold, Brezzi, Cockburn, and Marini [5], and popularized in nodal form by Hesthaven and Warburton [6], DG now underpins high-order simulation of compressible flow, seismology, and electromagnetics. This thesis develops the weak and strong nodal formulations, surveys numerical flux design — upwind, Lax–Friedrichs, interior-penalty, and direct DG fluxes [7] — states key stability and convergence theorems including the cell entropy inequality, covers slope limiters and modern shock-capturing filters [8], and evaluates empirical hp-convergence and application evidence from elastodynamics and computational electromagnetics [9][10].

---

## 1 Introduction

Most numerical methods force a global compromise: *finite volume* schemes gain conservation at the cost of wide reconstruction stencils; *continuous Galerkin* finite elements gain variational structure at the cost of globally coupled mass matrices and brittle adaptivity. The **discontinuous Galerkin method** rejects this compromise. By allowing trial and test spaces that are *discontinuous across element boundaries* — functions in *broken* Sobolev spaces — DG localizes every elemental solve while recovering consistency, conservation, and stability through a single instrument: the **numerical flux** at element interfaces [4][5][6].

The consequences of this architectural choice are far-reaching:

- **Locality**: the mass matrix is *block-diagonal* by construction, trivially invertible element-by-element, yielding fully explicit time marching and near-perfect parallel scalability [6].
- **Arbitrary-order accuracy**: degree-*p* polynomials deliver *p + 1* order convergence on smooth solutions [2][3], with *hp-adaptivity* — simultaneous *h* and *p* refinement, including hanging nodes.
- **Shock capturing**: Riemann-solver machinery from finite volumes plus *TVD/TVB limiters* and artificial-viscosity operators produce sharp, oscillation-free discontinuities [8].
- **Geometric flexibility**: native support for unstructured triangles, tetrahedra, hexahedra, and polytopal meshes [9].

This thesis is self-contained: Section 2 gives the background; Section 3 develops the semi-discrete nodal DG formulation; Section 4 is the technical core — flux design, limiters, hp-adaptivity and error estimates, and comparison with continuous FEM and finite volumes; Section 5 supplies theorems with proofs and empirical evidence; Section 6 is candid about the method's costs; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Origins: neutron transport and hyperbolic conservation laws

The earliest DG scheme was not conceived for fluid mechanics. In 1973, Reed and Hill [1] proposed a *triangular mesh method* for the steady-state neutron transport equation, whose streaming operator is hyperbolic; because transport admits genuinely discontinuous fluxes at material interfaces, they *weakened* the formulation element-by-element and coupled cells through the upwind physical flux. Lesaint and Raviart (1974) supplied the first analysis, proving order *p + 1* convergence for degree-*p* polynomials.

Cockburn and Shu's **Runge–Kutta DG (RKDG)** program — a five-paper sequence [2][3] that remains the canonical reference — covered scalar conservation laws with TVD Runge–Kutta stepping and TVB limiters; the general method-of-lines framework; one-dimensional systems with characteristic decompositions; the multidimensional case; and multidimensional systems including the Euler equations. A consolidating review appeared in the *Journal of Scientific Computing* in 2001 [4].

### 2.2 Extension to diffusion: LDG and the unified analysis

Hyperbolic DG was a triumph, but second-order operators exposed its subtlety: naive central differencing of viscous fluxes is *unstable*. The cure is the **local discontinuous Galerkin (LDG)** method of Cockburn and Shu (1998) [4], which introduces $\mathbf{q} = \nabla u$ and applies DG to each equation of the resulting first-order system with *alternating* upwind/downwind fluxes, restoring stability and order *p + 1*. The parallel **direct DG (DDG)** method of Liu and Yan [7] instead derives diffusive fluxes from interface Taylor expansions, with explicit penalty-parameter admissibility bounds.

For pure elliptic problems, Arnold, Brezzi, Cockburn, and Marini [5] showed that essentially *every* DG method for $-\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla u) = f$ can be written through numerical traces $\hat{u}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}$, and analyzed the entire family — interior-penalty, LDG, Bassi–Rebay, Baumann–Oden — in one framework, proving when each is consistent, conservative, and optimally convergent. This paper [5] is the Rosetta Stone of DG for elliptic problems.

### 2.3 The nodal formulation and modern applications

Hesthaven and Warburton [6] recast DG in **nodal** form: solutions expanded in Lagrange polynomials at optimized interpolation nodes, all operators precomputed on a reference element, quadrature-free evaluation. Their textbook [6] — with accompanying MATLAB codes — democratized the method, and applications exploded into **computational electromagnetics** (time-domain Maxwell, dispersive media, UQ [10]) and **seismology** (acoustic, elastic, and coupled elasto-acoustic waves on complex topography and polytopal meshes [9]).

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The weak formulation on broken spaces

Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ be partitioned into elements $\mathcal{T}_h = \{K\}$, and define the **broken Sobolev space**

$$H^m(\mathcal{T}_h) := \prod_{K\in\mathcal{T}_h} H^m(K), \qquad V_h := \{ v \in L^2(\Omega) : v|_K \in P_p(K)\ \forall K \},$$

with *no continuity imposed across interfaces*. For the scalar conservation law $\partial_t u + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}(u) = 0$ with $u(\cdot,0) = u_0$, multiplying by $v \in V_h$, integrating by parts *per element*, and replacing the boundary flux by a single-valued **numerical flux** $\mathbf{F}^*$ gives the **weak nodal DG formulation** — find $u_h \in V_h$ such that for all $K$ and all $v \in P_p(K)$:

$$\int_K \partial_t u_h\, v\, dx - \int_K \mathbf{F}(u_h)\cdot\nabla v\, dx + \int_{\partial K} \mathbf{F}^*(u_h^-, u_h^+, \mathbf{n})\cdot\mathbf{n}\, v\, ds = 0, \tag{3.1}$$

where $u_h^-$ and $u_h^+$ denote traces from inside and outside $K$. Integration by parts once more gives the **strong form**,

$$\int_K \big(\partial_t u_h + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}(u_h)\big)\, v\, dx + \int_{\partial K} \big(\mathbf{F}^* - \mathbf{F}(u_h^-)\big)\cdot\mathbf{n}\, v\, ds = 0, \tag{3.2}$$

exposing the method's structure: the *PDE residual holds inside each element*, and the *flux defect* at the boundary drives inter-element coupling. The jump $[\![u_h]\!] := u_h^- - u_h^+$ and average $\{\{u_h\}\} := \tfrac{1}{2}(u_h^- + u_h^+)$ on each face are the vocabulary of every DG scheme [5].

### 3.2 The nodal semi-discrete algorithm

Expanding $u_h|_K = \sum_{j=1}^{N_p} \hat{u}_j^K\, \ell_j(\mathbf{x})$ in nodal Lagrange basis functions and collocating (3.2) gives the *method-of-lines* ODE system on each element:

$$\mathbf{M}^K \frac{d\mathbf{u}^K}{dt} + \mathbf{S}^K\cdot\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u}^K) = \sum_{f} \mathbf{L}^{K,f}\, \big(\mathbf{F}^* - \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u}^K)\big)\cdot\mathbf{n}, \tag{3.3}$$

where $\mathbf{M}^K$ is the *local* mass matrix, $\mathbf{S}^K$ the stiffness matrix, and $\mathbf{L}^{K,f}$ the lift operator of face *f*. The crucial implementation facts [6] are: **$\mathbf{M}^K$ is block-diagonal globally** — each block inverted once in preprocessing; all operators are computed on a **reference element** and mapped geometrically; and the only global operation is the **flux evaluation** $\mathbf{F}^*$ at faces, requiring one neighbor exchange per face.

A minimal Python prototype of the one-dimensional nodal assembly illustrates the mechanics:

```python
import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial.legendre import leggauss

def nodal_dg_1d(u0, flux, flux_star, p, K, T, dt):
    """Nodal DG for u_t + f(u)_x = 0 on K uniform elements."""
    xq, wq = leggauss(p + 1)
    xi = np.linspace(-1, 1, p + 1)
    V = np.vander(xi, p + 1, increasing=True)
    Vr = np.stack([np.polyder(np.polyfit(xi, V[:, j], p)) for j in range(p+1)], -1)
    dx = 2.0 / K
    M = np.linalg.inv(V.T) @ np.diag(wq) @ np.linalg.inv(V)
    D = Vr @ np.linalg.inv(V)
    u = u0(xi)                                      # N_p x K nodal values
    for n in range(int(T / dt)):
        rhs = np.zeros_like(u)
        for k in range(K):
            f = flux(u[:, k])
            rhs[:, k] = -(2.0 / dx) * D @ f
            ul, ur = u[0, k-1][-1] if False else u[0, k], u[-1, k]
            # Lax-Friedrichs flux at both faces
            for face, (umin, umax) in enumerate([(u[-1, k-1] if k > 0 else u[0, k], u[0, k]),
                                                 (u[-1, k], u[0, k+1] if k < K-1 else u[-1, k])]):
                rhs[face*p, k] -= (2.0/dx) * (flux_star(umin, umax) - flux(umax if face==0 else umin)) / M[face*p, face*p]
        u = u + dt * rhs  # forward Euler; SSPRK in practice
    return u
```

> **Theorem:** *Block-diagonal mass.* For any tessellation $\mathcal{T}_h$, the global DG mass matrix is block-diagonal with one block per element, hence invertible element-by-element in preprocessing, with no global linear solve for explicit time integration [6].

### 3.3 Time integration

For the canonical choice of time integrator, the **strong-stability-preserving Runge–Kutta** (SSP-RK) family of Shu and Osher preserves the TVD/TVB property of the forward-Euler step under a halved CFL restriction; the low-storage five-stage fourth-order SSPRK(5,4) is the workhorse for RKDG [2][4]. For stiff parabolic terms, implicit–explicit (IMEX) splittings treat advection explicitly and diffusion implicitly.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Numerical fluxes: the soul of the method

Everything distinguishing one DG scheme from another lives in $\mathbf{F}^*$ — subject to *consistency*, *conservation* (single-valuedness), and *stability* (an entropy or energy inequality):

| Flux | Formula (scalar, face normal *n*) | Character | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central | $\mathbf{F}^* = \{\{\mathbf{F}(u_h)\}\}\cdot\mathbf{n}$ | Non-dissipative, energy-conserving | Maxwell, acoustics [10] |
| Upwind / Godunov | exact Riemann solution $R(u^-,u^+)$ | Minimal dissipation, sharpest | Hyperbolic systems [2] |
| Lax–Friedrichs | $\{\{\mathbf{F}\}\}\cdot\mathbf{n} + \tfrac{\alpha}{2}[\![u_h]\!]$ | Robust, simple | General hyperbolic [4] |
| HLL / HLLC | two- or three-wave approximate Riemann solver | Cheaper than Godunov | Euler equations [3] |
| LDG (alternating) | $\hat{u} = u^-, \hat{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{q}^+$ (or swapped) | Stable diffusion | Convection–diffusion [4] |
| Interior penalty (SIPG) | $\{\{\kappa\nabla u_h\}\} - \eta\,[\![u_h]\!]/h$ | Penalty-stabilized | Elliptic problems [5] |
| Direct DG (DDG) | Taylor-expansion-based diffusive flux | Parameter-tuned | Diffusion [7] |

> **Theorem:** *Cell entropy inequality.* Let $(U, \mathbf{\Psi})$ be an entropy pair for $\partial_t u + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}(u) = 0$ and let $\mathbf{F}^*$ be any *E-flux* (e.g., Godunov or Lax–Friedrichs). Then the semi-discrete DG solution of (3.1) satisfies, on every element $K$,
> $$\frac{d}{dt}\int_K U(u_h)\,dx + \int_{\partial K} \Psi^*\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds \le 0$$
> for a consistent numerical entropy flux $\Psi^*$. Summing over $K$ telescopes interior contributions, yielding global entropy stability and convergence to the entropy solution [2].

The practical import is enormous: **the flux, not the basis, controls shock behavior**. An eighth-order DG scheme with a monotone E-flux captures shocks in one or two elements without oscillations [2], while the same scheme with a central flux will ring catastrophically. For elliptic problems, the unified analysis [5] classifies fluxes by their numerical traces $\hat{u}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}$: *stability is a property of the traces*, and the zoo — IP, LDG, Bassi–Rebay, Baumann–Oden — reduces to trace choices plus (for IP) a penalty parameter $\eta$ exceeding an explicit threshold ($\eta_e > 3$ on triangles [5]).

The flux kernel — the hot loop of any DG code — stays fast because of locality:

```rust
/// Lax-Friedrichs numerical flux across one face: the DG coupling kernel.
fn lax_friedrichs_flux<const M: usize>(
    u_minus: &[f64; M], u_plus: &[f64; M],
    normal: &[f64; 3], alpha: f64,
    phys_flux: impl Fn(&[f64; M]) -> [[f64; M]; 3],
) -> [f64; M] {
    let fm = phys_flux(u_minus);
    let fp = phys_flux(u_plus);
    let mut fstar = [0.0; M];
    for i in 0..M {
        let n_dot = |f: &[[f64; M]; 3]| f[0][i]*normal[0] + f[1][i]*normal[1] + f[2][i]*normal[2];
        fstar[i] = 0.5*(n_dot(&fm) + n_dot(&fp)) + 0.5*alpha*(u_minus[i] - u_plus[i]);
    }
    fstar
}
```

### 4.2 Slope limiters and shock capturing

High-order DG is linearly stable but *nonlinearly oscillatory* near discontinuities. The classical remedy is the **generalized slope limiter** of Cockburn and Shu [2]: after each RK stage, higher moments in each element are limited by comparing a limited linear reconstruction against neighbor cell averages through a TVB-corrected *minmod* function, preserving formal accuracy at smooth extrema. Modern supplements include **moment limiters** (Krivodonova, hierarchical and p-robust), **artificial viscosity** (Persson–Peraire, driven by a smoothness sensor $S_K$ that retains high order in smooth regions), and **jump filters** (2024), provably entropy-dissipative for hyperbolic conservation laws [8].

The empirical verdict from the RKDG literature [2][4]: *sharp shock transitions, usually within one or two elements, with no spurious oscillations*, at formal order *p + 1* in smooth regions.

### 4.3 hp-adaptivity and error estimates

DG's discontinuity is a gift to adaptivity: *hanging nodes are legal*, neighbors may differ in both *h* and *p*, and refinement is purely local. The standard loop — **solve → estimate → mark → refine** — uses residual-based *a posteriori* estimators $\eta_K$, refining elements where $\eta_K$ exceeds a Dörfler fraction. The *hp* decision (split the element vs. raise its degree) is guided by smoothness estimation: analytic regions earn *p*-enrichment with its *exponential* convergence; singular regions earn *h*-refinement with algebraic rates.

> **Theorem:** *A priori hp-error estimate (linear hyperbolic).* Let $u$ be sufficiently smooth and $u_h \in V_h$ the DG solution of degree $p$ on a shape-regular mesh. Then
> $$\|u - u_h\|_{L^2(\Omega)} \le C\, \frac{h^{\min(p+1,s)-1/2}}{p^{\,s-1}}\, |u|_{H^s(\mathcal{T}_h)},$$
> i.e., order $h^{p+1/2}$ — half an order suboptimal in *h* but with explicit, favorable *p*-dependence [4][6]. For elliptic problems, the unified analysis [5] recovers full *h*-optimality ($h^{p+1}$) for the stable trace choices (SIPG, LDG, Bassi–Rebay II) and characterizes exactly which choices lose half an order.

The half-order gap for hyperbolic problems is *sharp* for upwind DG but not a practical defect: the constant is small, and superconvergence of order $2p+1$ holds at Radau points and for cell averages.

### 4.4 DG vs. continuous FEM vs. finite volume

| Property | Continuous Galerkin | Discontinuous Galerkin | Finite Volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inter-element continuity | Enforced ($H^1$-conforming) | None (broken space) | Cell averages only |
| Mass matrix | Global, sparse, must be solved | Block-diagonal, local | Diagonal (lumped) |
| Order of accuracy | *p + 1*, arbitrary *p* | *p + 1* (hyperbolic), arbitrary *p* | 2nd typical; high-order needs wide WENO stencils |
| Shock capturing | Requires stabilization (SUPG, VMS) | Limiters / Riemann fluxes, 1–2 cells | Natural (TVD/WENO), 2–3 cells |
| hp-adaptivity / hanging nodes | Constrained (mortar, constraints) | Native and trivial | Native in *h*; *p* awkward |
| Parallel communication | Global assembly + solve | One face exchange | One face exchange |
| DOF count (same *h*, *p*) | Fewest | Most (duplicated faces) | Fewest per order |

The table clarifies DG's bargain: it **pays in degrees of freedom and time-step size** for **locality, arbitrary order, and adaptivity**. On modern many-core hardware, where flops are cheap and communication is dear, that bargain increasingly favors DG [6][10].

### 4.5 Applications: compressible CFD, seismology, electromagnetics

- **Compressible CFD.** The RKDG program's original target [2][3]: Euler and Navier–Stokes with HLLC fluxes, characteristic limiters, and curved boundary treatment.
- **Seismology.** DG for acoustic, elastic, and coupled **elasto-acoustic** waves on realistic topography and polytopal meshes, with hp-error analyses proving optimal rates [9].
- **Electromagnetics.** Nodal DG for time-domain **Maxwell equations** with upwind ($\alpha > 0$) or central ($\alpha = 0$) fluxes, dispersive media, and cluster-scale performance [10].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The linear stability and convergence skeleton

> **Theorem:** *$L^2$-stability of semi-discrete DG (linear advection).* For $\partial_t u + \mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla u = 0$ with upwind flux, the DG solution of (3.1) satisfies
> $$\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u_h\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}_h} \int_e |\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{n}|\, [\![u_h]\!]^2\, ds = 0,$$
> with dissipation *exactly* equal to the sum of squared jumps weighted by the normal advection speed [4][6].

*Proof sketch.* Take $v = u_h$ in (3.1) and sum over elements; volume terms telescope into the time derivative and interface terms collapse to the non-negative jump dissipation. The LDG extension [4] proves the same stability for convection–diffusion via a *symmetrized* energy argument on the auxiliary variable $\mathbf{q}_h$, giving $L^2$-stability in the fully nonlinear scalar case.

### 5.2 Measured convergence: the hp signature

The canonical verification — smooth advection $u_t + u_x = 0$, $u(x,0) = \sin(2\pi x)$, periodic — yields the rates reported across [2][4][6]:

| *p* | $N=10$ | $N=20$ | $N=40$ | $N=80$ | Observed order |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3.1e-2 | 8.2e-3 | 2.1e-3 | 5.3e-4 | 2.0 |
| 2 | 2.4e-3 | 3.1e-4 | 3.9e-5 | 4.9e-6 | 3.0 |
| 3 | 1.9e-4 | 1.2e-5 | 7.6e-7 | 4.8e-8 | 4.0 |
| 4 | 1.1e-5 | 3.6e-7 | 1.1e-8 | 3.6e-10 | 5.0 |

Clean *p + 1* rates, with error constants *decreasing* rapidly in *p* — the hp signature. For the Sod shock tube, the same schemes resolve the shock in 1–2 elements and the contact in 3–4, with monotone profiles under the TVB limiter [2]. For elliptic benchmarks, SIPG and LDG attain the optimal $h^{p+1}$ in $L^2$ predicted by [5], while the original Bassi–Rebay I formulation exhibits the predicted half-order loss — a textbook confirmation of the unified theory.

### 5.3 Application evidence

- **Elasto-acoustics:** Antonietti, Bonaldi, and Mazzieri [9] prove *a priori* hp-error estimates in a mesh-dependent energy norm on polygonal/polyhedral meshes and validate optimal 2D rates, including solid–fluid transmission conditions of ocean-bottom seismology.
- **Electromagnetics:** the nodal DG Maxwell solver [10] shows spectral convergence on scattering benchmarks, with energy behavior controlled by the flux parameter $\alpha \in [0,1]$.
- **Shock capturing:** jump-filter DG [8] gives entropy-stable, oscillation-free capture of strong Euler shocks with *provable* discrete-entropy dissipation.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what DG costs.

1. **Degrees of freedom.** Duplicating unknowns at every face inflates DOF counts relative to continuous FEM. For diffusion-dominated problems at low *p* this is pure overhead; the method earns its keep at high *p* and in hyperbolic regimes.
2. **CFL restriction.** Explicit DG suffers $\Delta t \le C\, h/((2p+1)|\lambda|_{\max})$, tightening linearly with *p*, demanding high-order SSPRK or IMEX time stepping.
3. **Limiter fragility.** TVB limiters need a problem-dependent parameter; artificial viscosity needs sensor tuning; over-limiting destroys the high-order accuracy that motivates DG. Provably entropy-stable filters [8] are recent and not yet universal.
4. **Elliptic inefficiency.** For pure Poisson problems, multigrid-preconditioned CG is simply faster per DOF. **Hybridizable DG (HDG)** — statically condensing to face unknowns — is the active remedy.
5. **Implementation complexity.** Curved elements, non-conforming interfaces, and positivity preservation each add genuine engineering burden [6].

None of these is fatal; all are active research fronts. But a thesis that omitted them would be advertising, not scholarship.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The discontinuous Galerkin method is best understood not as a variant of finite elements but as a *design philosophy*: **keep everything local, and pay for global coherence with a single, carefully engineered interface object — the numerical flux**. From Reed and Hill's neutron transport cells [1] through the Runge–Kutta DG program [2][3][4], the LDG treatment of diffusion [4][7], the unified elliptic analysis [5], and the nodal formulation that carried the method into electromagnetics [10] and seismology [9], the history of DG is the history of discovering how much can be built from this one principle.

The ledger is clear. Credits: arbitrary-order accuracy with compact stencils, block-diagonal mass matrices, native hp-adaptivity, provable entropy stability and sharp shock capturing, near-ideal parallel scalability. Debits: inflated degree-of-freedom counts, restrictive CFL conditions, limiter tuning, and implementation complexity — costs that hybridization, entropy-stable fluxes [8], and modern hardware are steadily amortizing.

The decision rule this thesis defends: *if the problem is hyperbolic or convection-dominated, geometrically complex, or adaptivity-hungry, DG is the default high-order choice; if it is elliptic and smooth on simple geometry, continuous methods remain competitive.*
