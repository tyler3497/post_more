# DAG-Based Byzantine Consensus: Narwhal, Tusk, and Bullshark Mempools with Pipelined Commit

## Abstract

Leader-based Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus, exemplified by HotStuff, couples transaction dissemination with ordering and inherits the leader bottleneck: a single replica's bandwidth, CPU, and liveness bound the throughput of the entire system. This article develops the DAG-based paradigm pioneered by Narwhal and Tusk, which cleanly separates reliable data dissemination from ordering. We formalize the Narwhal mempool as an asynchronous protocol that constructs a certified directed acyclic graph of causally linked vertex blocks, and we show how Tusk embeds an asynchronous, zero-message-overhead consensus protocol inside this DAG through randomized leader election and a causal commit rule. We then present Bullshark's partially synchronous fast path, which cuts commit latency during synchronous intervals without any view-change machinery, and we analyze the fairness guarantees obtained through DAG inclusion, including the garbage-collection tension that Bullshark resolves. We survey throughput and latency measurements from the original evaluations, sketch formal safety and liveness arguments, describe production deployments in Sui and Aptos, and close with open problems including adaptive timeouts and principled DAG pruning.

## 1 Intro

Byzantine fault-tolerant state machine replication (BFT SMR) is the consensus core of permissioned blockchains. For two decades the field was dominated by leader-based protocols in the lineage of PBFT: a designated leader proposes a batch of transactions, replicas vote, and a quorum certificate seals the decision. HotStuff modernized this design with a three-phase core, threshold signatures for linear communication, and optimistic responsiveness, becoming the de facto standard for blockchain consensus [3]. Yet every leader-based protocol shares a structural weakness: **the leader is the bottleneck**. Transaction dissemination and ordering are fused in the leader's proposal path, so system throughput is capped by one replica's network bandwidth and CPU. When the leader is faulty or the network degrades, the protocol pays a view-change tax — liveness stalls while replicas synchronize on a new leader — and a Byzantine leader can censor transactions outright by simply omitting them.

The Narwhal and Tusk papers [1] propose a decisive architectural break: *separate data dissemination from ordering*. Dissemination becomes the job of a high-throughput mempool, Narwhal, which reliably broadcasts batches and weaves them into a certified directed acyclic graph (DAG) of causal histories. Ordering becomes a lightweight interpretation layered on top of that DAG — either an embedded consensus protocol such as Tusk or Bullshark [2], which adds **zero extra messages** beyond DAG construction, or a classical protocol such as HotStuff run on top of the mempool, which is freed from carrying bulk data. This separation is what unlocks the headline numbers: two orders of magnitude more throughput than HotStuff alone, scaling that grows linearly with added worker machines, all while tolerating asynchrony and faults.

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews leader-based BFT, reliable broadcast, and the DAG consensus lineage. Section 3 describes our methodology. Section 4 develops the deep technical content: the Narwhal DAG construction, Tusk's randomized commit rule, Bullshark's partially synchronous fast path, the fairness and garbage-collection analysis, and the pipelined-commit performance argument. Section 5 presents empirical results and formal safety and liveness sketches. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes with open problems.

## 2 Background

**Leader-based BFT and its limits.** HotStuff [3] organizes consensus around views, each with a designated leader that proposes a block extending the highest quorum certificate (QC) it knows. Three voting phases — *prepare*, *pre-commit*, *commit* — produce a QC per phase; a three-chain of QCs commits a block. Threshold signatures compress each phase's votes into one certificate, giving linear message complexity, and the protocol is optimistically responsive: after the global stabilization time (GST), progress tracks actual network delay rather than a worst-case bound. Chained HotStuff pipelines the phases so every view both proposes and votes.

The price is centralization of work. The leader must receive all transactions, batch them, and broadcast the full block; every replica's throughput depends on the leader's uplink. View changes, though simpler than in PBFT, still interrupt the pipeline: a faulty leader means a timeout, a new view, and retransmission of uncommitted data. And censorship is trivial for a Byzantine leader — omit a transaction and no replica can force its inclusion. These are not implementation artifacts; they are consequences of fusing dissemination with ordering [1].

**Reliable broadcast and quorum certificates.** The DAG protocols build on Byzantine reliable broadcast (RBC), which guarantees *validity* (a broadcast by a correct sender is delivered by all correct parties), *agreement* (delivery by one correct party implies delivery by all), and *integrity* (no duplication, no forgery). With n = 3f + 1 validators tolerating f Byzantine faults, a quorum certificate of 2f + 1 signatures proves that a value is durably stored: any two quorums intersect in at least one correct validator, the workhorse fact behind every safety argument below.

**The DAG consensus lineage.** Hashgraph first proposed ordering from a gossip DAG. Aleph and DAG-Rider [4] added round structure and rigor: DAG-Rider is the first asynchronous Byzantine atomic broadcast with optimal resilience, optimal amortized communication, and optimal expected time, and it is post-quantum safe because its safety argument needs no number-theoretic assumptions beyond the coin. Remarkably, once the DAG exists, DAG-Rider's entire ordering logic is a local interpretation of under thirty lines of pseudocode — the pattern that Narwhal, Tusk, and Bullshark all inherit.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Narwhal: the certified DAG mempool

Narwhal [1] turns the mempool into a first-class distributed protocol. Time is divided into numbered *rounds*. Each validator runs a *primary* and any number of *workers*. Workers disseminate transaction batches; the primary assembles a *vertex* for each round containing (a) digests of available batches and (b) certificates for **at least 2f + 1 vertices of the previous round**. The primary reliably broadcasts its vertex and advances to round r + 1 only after collecting 2f + 1 certified vertices of round r. Edges point from a vertex to the previous-round vertices it references, so every vertex commits its author to a *causal history*: the transitive closure of referenced vertices, each of which is itself reliably broadcast and therefore retrievable.

```python
def may_advance(dag, validator, r, f):
    """Narwhal primary: advance to round r only over a certified quorum."""
    refs = dag.references(validator, r)          # vertices of round r-1 referenced
    certified = [v for v in refs if dag.has_certificate(v)]
    return len(certified) >= 2 * f + 1
```

Three properties make this design powerful. First, it is fully *asynchronous*: no timeouts, no view changes, no leader — a slow validator simply produces its vertex late, and the DAG routes around it. Second, it *scales out*: workers are embarrassingly parallel, so adding machines raises throughput without touching the consensus-critical primary path. Third, the DAG is *self-certifying*: because every vertex embeds 2f + 1 certificates of the prior round, any two correct validators' DAG views share a massive common core, which is what lets consensus be decided by local interpretation rather than extra messaging.

> **Definition:** The *causal history* of a vertex v is the set of all vertices reachable from v by following reference edges, including v itself. A vertex is *committed* when a consensus rule selects it; committing v commits its entire causal history in one deterministic step.

### 4.2 Tusk: randomized leader election and the commit rule

Tusk [1] is an asynchronous consensus protocol with **zero message overhead**: it adds no messages beyond Narwhal's DAG construction and instead interprets the DAG. Execution is grouped into *waves* of three consecutive rounds. At the end of each wave, validators invoke a shared perfect *common coin* — a distributed randomness beacon — to elect a *leader* retroactively: the leader of wave w is the vertex authored by the elected validator in the *first* round of w. Because the coin is revealed only after the wave's vertices are fixed, the adversary cannot target the leader in advance.

The commit rule is purely structural:

- **Direct rule.** The leader of wave w is *directly committed* if at least f + 1 vertices in the *second* round of w reference it. The f + 1 bound is tight: among the 2f + 1 vertices any correct validator observes in a round, f + 1 supporters guarantee that every other correct validator's quorum intersects the support set, so all correct validators will eventually see the leader as supported.
- **Indirect rule.** If the leader of wave w is not directly committed, it can still be committed *indirectly*: when a later wave w' > w directly commits its leader, the deterministic traversal of that leader's causal history commits every uncommitted ancestor leader it contains, preserving a single total order.

Ordering is then mechanical: sort the committed leader's causal history by (round, author) and append unseen vertices. The sketch below captures the rule in TLA+:

```tla
--------------------------- MODULE TuskCommit ---------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Validators, F, Waves
VARIABLES dag, committedLeaders
Supports(w, leader) ==
    Cardinality({ v \in RoundVertices(dag, SecondRound(w)) :
                  leader \in References(dag, v) }) >= F + 1
DirectCommit(w) ==
    LET leader == CoinElect(Waves[w]) IN
    IF Supports(w, leader) THEN committedLeaders' = committedLeaders \cup {leader}
    ELSE UNCHANGED committedLeaders
=============================================================================
```

Tusk's expected latency is constant — a leader is elected in every wave and the direct rule succeeds with probability bounded away from zero — while throughput inherits Narwhal's scale-out: the evaluation reports roughly 160,000 tx/sec at about 3 seconds of WAN latency [1].

### 4.3 Bullshark: the partially synchronous fast path

Tusk optimizes for asynchrony and pays for it in latency: every commit waits for a full wave plus coin revelation. Bullshark [2] observes that real networks are *usually* synchronous and adds a fast path that commits in **2 round-trips during synchrony**, a 75 percent improvement over DAG-Rider, while keeping a 6-round-trip expected fallback in asynchrony.

Bullshark designates *anchors* — vertices that play the role of leaders — at regular intervals (every two rounds in the original design; every round after Shoal's pipelining [5]). There are two anchor types per wave: *steady-state* anchors proposed at preassigned odd rounds, and a *fallback* anchor elected by common coin when the steady-state anchors fail. To keep the two modes from interfering, every vertex carries a *voting type* recording whether its author votes the steady-state or the fallback leader of the current wave, and the type is determined deterministically by what happened in the previous wave. A steady-state anchor commits as soon as f + 1 vertices of the next round reference it — no coin, no extra round-trips. If it is skipped, the fallback path engages with the stronger 2f + 1 support requirement.

The subtle theoretical contribution is *timeouts inside the DAG*. In partially synchronous Bullshark, a validator advances rounds upon seeing 2f + 1 vertices **or** upon receiving the preassigned anchor's vertex **or** upon a timeout — the first DAG protocol to embed synchrony assumptions directly into round advancement rather than bolting on a view-change subprotocol. The result needs no view changes at all after a faulty leader: the DAG simply routes around the missing anchor and the fallback coin elects a new one. The eventually synchronous variant is famously tiny — about 200 lines of code atop Narwhal, versus roughly 4,000 for HotStuff-over-DAG — yet it sustains 125,000 tx/sec at 2 seconds latency with 50 validators [2].

### 4.4 Fairness, censorship resistance, and garbage collection

Leader-based protocols suffer *censorship by omission*: a Byzantine leader drops transactions it dislikes, and clients have no recourse except waiting for an honest leader. DAG-based protocols obtain fairness structurally. Every correct validator continuously adds its own vertex to each round, and the commit rules order the **entire causal history** of committed leaders — not just the leader's own batch. Consequently every vertex produced by a correct validator is eventually included in the total order. This is the *validity* property of Byzantine atomic broadcast: all proposals of correct processes are eventually delivered [4]. Censorship would require suppressing a validator's vertex from the DAG itself, which reliable broadcast prevents.

There is a catch: *garbage collection*. A DAG that grows forever exhausts memory, but discarding old rounds can strand slow validators' vertices and break fairness — the exact tension Narwhal left unresolved. Bullshark's answer is a bounded GC rule with a fairness guarantee during synchronous periods: vertices below a committed round can be discarded once every correct validator has had a synchronous chance to include them, and the paper proves that after GST, fairness holds despite collection [2]. This reconciliation of GC with fairness was an open problem that Bullshark closed, and it is what makes the design deployable rather than merely publishable.

### 4.5 Pipelined commit and throughput analysis

The deepest performance claim of the DAG paradigm is that **consensus adds no messages**. In Tusk and Bullshark, ordering is a deterministic function of the locally observed DAG; validators never send a single vote, prepare, or view-change message for ordering purposes. All communication is Narwhal's reliable broadcast of vertices — bulk data transfer that parallelizes across workers. HotStuff can also run *over* Narwhal (Narwhal-HotStuff), where the leader proposes only tiny vertex digests while workers move the bytes; this variant already multiplies throughput by two orders of magnitude [1].

| Protocol | Network model | Commit latency | Reported throughput | Extra ordering messages |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HotStuff [3] | Partially synchronous | 3 phases | ~1,800 tx/sec | O(n) per phase |
| Narwhal-HotStuff [1] | Partially synchronous | ~2 s (WAN) | 130,000+ tx/sec | O(n) on digests only |
| Tusk [1] | Asynchronous | ~3 s (WAN) | ~160,000 tx/sec | **Zero** |
| Bullshark [2] | Partially synchronous | 2 round-trips (sync) | 125,000 tx/sec, 50 nodes | **Zero** |
| Shoal [5] | Partially synchronous | −40% vs Bullshark | Matches Bullshark | **Zero** |

The numbers are from the original WAN evaluations with 512-byte transactions and are reproduced here for comparison, not as universal constants: absolute throughput depends on bandwidth, batching, and validator count, but the *ratios* — the two-order-of-magnitude gap between monolithic HotStuff and the DAG family — are structural.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

**Safety.** All three protocols reduce safety to quorum intersection over the DAG.

> **Theorem:** No two correct validators commit conflicting leaders for the same wave.

> *Proof sketch.* Suppose validator p directly commits leader L of wave w via f + 1 supporting vertices in round 2 of w, while validator q commits a different leader L' of the same wave. In Tusk there is a single coin-elected leader per wave, so L = L'; in Bullshark the voting-type discipline guarantees that correct validators vote for the same anchor type in a wave, and the f + 1 support for a steady-state anchor intersects every 2f + 1 quorum in at least one correct validator, whose vote is consistent across views. The deterministic traversal then yields identical total orders of each committed leader's causal history. ∎

> **Theorem:** Committed causal histories are consistent: if correct validators p and q both commit leader L, they order L's causal history identically.

> *Proof sketch.* Reliable broadcast gives agreement on vertex contents and references, so p and q observe the same causal history of L (modulo vertices not yet delivered, which are not ordered yet). The ordering function is a deterministic sort, hence identical inputs give identical outputs. ∎

**Liveness.** Tusk's liveness is probabilistic: each wave elects an unpredictable leader after vertices are fixed, and with at least 2f + 1 correct validators authoring round-1 vertices, the elected leader is correct with probability at least (2f + 1) / (3f + 1) > 2/3, and a correct leader with 2f + 1 correct supporters satisfies the f + 1 direct rule — so commits occur in expected constant waves [1]. Bullshark's partially synchronous variant is deterministically live after GST: timeouts force round advancement, the preassigned steady-state anchor of an honest validator commits in two round-trips, and the fallback coin covers the faulty-anchor case [2]. The DAG-Rider lineage additionally shows that optimal expected time is achievable without any timing assumptions at all [4].

**Empirical notes.** The Narwhal evaluation scales throughput *linearly* with workers — from 130,000 to 600,000 tx/sec in the original experiments — with no latency increase, confirming that the primary's certification work, not bandwidth, was the bottleneck and that workers remove it [1]. Under crash faults, both Narwhal-HotStuff and Tusk maintain throughput; only the HotStuff variant's latency degrades, because view-change timeouts reappear while Tusk's coin-based election does not stall. Shoal's three optimizations — per-round anchors (pipelining), reputation-based anchor selection, and eliminating timeouts in the common case (*prevalent responsiveness*) — cut latency up to 40 percent without faults and 80 percent with faults relative to vanilla Bullshark [5]. Shoal++ pushes further to 4.5 message delays end-to-end using staggered parallel DAGs [6].

## 6 Limitations

**Latency floor.** Even the fast paths commit in seconds on a WAN, not milliseconds. The DAG's round structure imposes a fundamental pipeline depth: a vertex must be broadcast, certified, and referenced before it can anchor a commit. For latency-sensitive applications this remains a real cost relative to optimistic single-leader protocols.

**Memory and the GC frontier.** Bullshark's garbage collection is safe only with its synchrony-conditioned fairness argument; under prolonged asynchrony, unbounded DAG growth returns. Principled *DAG pruning* — deciding what may be discarded while preserving provable fairness under the weakest possible network assumptions — is still open, and recent work such as Sharpedo's uncertified DAGs [7] and staggered-DAG designs [6] suggests the solution space is far from exhausted.

**Timeouts and adaptivity.** Bullshark embeds fixed timeouts in round advancement, and Shoal++ reintroduces small timeouts to keep replicas in lock-step [6]. Fixed timeouts are fragile: too short and slow honest anchors are skipped; too long and faulty anchors stall progress. *Adaptive* timeouts that track observed network conditions without breaking the safety case are an active research direction, as is the leader-reputation scheduling of HammerHead-style mechanisms that bias anchor selection toward fast, well-connected validators.

**Cryptographic and model assumptions.** DAG-Rider's post-quantum safety [4] does not automatically transfer: production DAG stacks use threshold signatures for the common coin and ed25519-style signatures for reliable broadcast, reintroducing quantum vulnerability in practice. TEE-based reductions of the resilience bound [8] trade hardware trust for fewer replicas — a different point in the design space, not yet integrated with the DAG mempool architecture.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Narwhal, Tusk, and Bullshark mark the moment Byzantine consensus stopped being a leader's job. By separating dissemination from ordering, Narwhal turns the mempool into an asynchronous, scale-out broadcast fabric whose certified DAG is a reusable substrate; by interpreting that DAG locally, Tusk and Bullshark achieve consensus with zero additional messages — the former with pure asynchrony and randomization, the latter with a partially synchronous fast path that needs no view changes. The fairness that falls out of DAG inclusion, the garbage-collection discipline that makes it deployable, and the production deployments at Sui and Aptos [2] together show that this is not a theoretical curiosity but the architecture that high-throughput blockchains are converging on. The open problems — adaptive timeouts, principled pruning, reputation-aware anchor scheduling — are refinements of a paradigm that has already won, not rescue missions for one that has failed.
