# Optimal Transport in Machine Learning: Kantorovich Duality, Entropic Regularization, and the Geometry of Wasserstein Space

## Abstract

Optimal transport equips the space of probability measures with a geometrically faithful notion of distance, turning the comparison of distributions into a variational problem with deep roots in analysis, geometry, and probability. This thesis develops the subject from its foundations — the Monge and Kantorovich formulations and the c-transform machinery of Kantorovich duality — through the structure theorems of Brenier and McCann that reveal the Wasserstein space as a geodesic metric space. We then study the computational revolution driven by entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn algorithm, quantifying its complexity and convergence rates and contrasting it with classical linear programming. Extensions to unbalanced and Gromov–Wasserstein transport remove the restrictions of equal mass and shared ground spaces, unlocking applications far beyond the classical setting. Finally, we show how these ideas reshape machine learning practice: Wasserstein GANs stabilize generative modeling through the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual, regularized transport aligns domains under distribution shift, and Wasserstein gradient flows with the Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto scheme recast diffusion processes and sampling as optimization over probability measures. Empirical studies and proofs ground each claim.

## 1 Introduction

Few mathematical ideas have migrated as profitably into machine learning as optimal transport. The problem is disarmingly concrete: given two distributions of mass, find the cheapest way to rearrange one into the other [1]. Yet its resolution mobilizes convex analysis, partial differential equations, and metric geometry, and its answer — a distance, a coupling, sometimes a map — carries far more information than a bare scalar dissimilarity. For a discipline whose objects of interest are distributions, a geometric way of comparing and morphing them is infrastructure.

The story begins with Gaspard Monge, who in 1781 asked how to move piles of earth with minimal effort, and with Leonid Kantorovich, whose 1942 relaxation turned Monge's nonlinear assignment problem into a linear program over couplings [2]. For half a century optimal transport remained principally a topic in analysis and economics, until a constellation of results — Brenier's polar factorization theorem [4], the Benamou–Brenier dynamic formulation, and the discovery that the Fokker–Planck equation is the gradient flow of entropy in Wasserstein space [6] — revealed it as a unifying geometric language. The decisive practical turn came in 2013, when Cuturi showed that entropic regularization reduces the discrete transport problem to Sinkhorn's matrix scaling, making it orders of magnitude faster than classical solvers [3].

Machine learning absorbed this machinery with remarkable speed. The Wasserstein GAN replaced the Jensen–Shannon divergence — infinite whenever the true and model distributions live on disjoint low-dimensional manifolds — with the Earth Mover's distance, curing gradient vanishing and producing meaningful training curves [5]. Optimal transport became the natural tool for domain adaptation, aligning source and target feature distributions [9]. Gradient flows in Wasserstein space offered a new lens on sampling and variational inference, with the Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto (JKO) scheme providing a provably convergent time discretization [6]. Meanwhile, unbalanced and Gromov–Wasserstein variants removed the two most limiting assumptions of the classical theory — equal mass and comparable spaces — and were solved with the same Sinkhorn-like iterations [7][8].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the Monge and Kantorovich formulations and develops Kantorovich duality and the c-transform, the technical core of the subject. Section 3 develops the methodology: Brenier's theorem, the Wasserstein metric and its geodesics, and entropic regularization with the Sinkhorn algorithm. Section 4 dives deeper into computational complexity and convergence, unbalanced transport, Gromov–Wasserstein geometry, Wasserstein GANs, and Wasserstein gradient flows. Section 5 presents empirical results and proofs. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Monge and Kantorovich formulations

Let $\mathcal{X}$ and $\mathcal{Y}$ be Polish spaces, $\mu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})$ and $\nu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})$ probability measures, and $c : \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} \to [0,\infty]$ a lower semicontinuous cost. Monge's problem seeks a measurable map $T : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ pushing $\mu$ forward to $\nu$ (written $T_\sharp \mu = \nu$) that minimizes

$$\inf_{T : T_\sharp\mu = \nu} \int_{\mathcal{X}} c(x, T(x)) \, d\mu(x).$$

The pushforward constraint $T_\sharp \mu = \nu$ means mass is relocated without creation or destruction. Monge's formulation is intuitive but analytically hostile: it is non-convex, the feasible set may be empty (e.g. $\mu$ diffuse and $\nu$ a Dirac mass with a strictly convex cost admits no such $T$), and existence of minimizers is delicate.

Kantorovich's relaxation replaces deterministic maps by *couplings*: probability measures $\pi$ on $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$ with marginals $\mu$ and $\nu$, i.e. $\pi(A \times \mathcal{Y}) = \mu(A)$ and $\pi(\mathcal{X} \times B) = \nu(B)$. The Kantorovich problem is

$$\inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}} c(x,y) \, d\pi(x,y),$$

where $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ denotes the set of couplings. This is a *linear* program over a convex, weakly compact set; minimizers always exist under mild hypotheses, and the value bounds Monge's from below. In the discrete case, with $\mu = \sum_i a_i \delta_{x_i}$ and $\nu = \sum_j b_j \delta_{y_j}$, it becomes the classical transportation linear program $\min_{\mathbf{P} \in U(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})} \langle \mathbf{P}, \mathbf{C} \rangle$, where $U(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = \{\mathbf{P} \geq 0 : \mathbf{P}\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{P}^\top\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{b}\}$ is the transportation polytope.

> **Theorem:** *(Kantorovich duality [2])* Under mild regularity, the primal Kantorovich problem equals
> $$\sup_{(\varphi,\psi) \in \Phi_c} \int_{\mathcal{X}} \varphi \, d\mu + \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \psi \, d\nu,$$
> where $\Phi_c = \{(\varphi,\psi) : \varphi(x) + \psi(y) \leq c(x,y) \text{ for all } x,y\}$.

The dual variables $\varphi, \psi$ are called *Kantorovich potentials*. Duality is proved via Fenchel–Rockafellar convex duality or, in the discrete case, ordinary LP duality; the continuous case demands care with integrability and is developed in full in [1][2].

### 2.2 The c-transform and c-concavity

For any function $\psi$ on $\mathcal{Y}$, its *c-transform* is

$$\psi^c(x) = \inf_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \big(c(x,y) - \psi(y)\big),$$

and symmetrically $\varphi^c(y) = \inf_x (c(x,y) - \varphi(x))$. A function is *c-concave* if it can be written as $\psi^c$ for some $\psi$ — the analogue of concavity with the bilinear pairing replaced by the cost $c$. The crucial observation is that replacing $(\varphi, \psi)$ by $(\varphi^{cc}, \varphi^c)$ never decreases the dual objective while preserving feasibility. Hence:

> **Theorem:** *The dual optimum can be restricted to pairs $(\varphi, \varphi^c)$ with $\varphi$ c-concave. Optimality of a coupling $\pi$ is equivalent to the support condition $\varphi(x) + \varphi^c(y) = c(x,y)$ holding $\pi$-almost everywhere.*

For the quadratic cost $c(x,y) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^2$ on Euclidean space, writing $\varphi(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|^2 - u(x)$ converts c-concavity into ordinary convexity of $u$, and the c-transform becomes the Legendre–Fenchel conjugate. This dictionary between c-concavity and convex analysis is the engine of Brenier's theorem.

A celebrated special case is the *Kantorovich–Rubinstein* duality for the 1-Wasserstein distance with metric cost $c(x,y) = d(x,y)$:

$$W_1(\mu,\nu) = \sup_{\mathrm{Lip}(f) \leq 1} \int f \, d\mu - \int f \, d\nu,$$

where the supremum runs over 1-Lipschitz functions. This dual is precisely what the Wasserstein GAN optimizes [5].

### 2.3 Wasserstein metrics and geodesics

For $p \geq 1$, the *p-Wasserstein distance* between measures with finite $p$-th moment is

$$W_p(\mu,\nu) = \left( \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int \|x-y\|^p \, d\pi \right)^{1/p}.$$

$W_p$ metrizes weak convergence plus convergence of moments, and $(\mathcal{P}_p(\mathbb{R}^d), W_p)$ is a complete separable metric space. Its crowning geometric property is that it is a *geodesic* space: McCann's displacement interpolation

$$\mu_t = ((1-t)\mathrm{Id} + tT)_\sharp \mu, \quad t \in \text{the unit interval},$$

with $T$ an optimal map, traces a constant-speed geodesic from $\mu$ to $\nu$ whenever an optimal map exists. Functionals such as entropy and potential energy are *displacement convex* along these geodesics, a fact that powers the entire theory of Wasserstein gradient flows [6].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Brenier's theorem and the structure of optimal maps

> **Theorem:** *(Brenier [4])* Let $\mu, \nu$ be probability measures on $\mathbb{R}^d$ with finite second moments, and let $\mu$ be absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. For the quadratic cost $c(x,y) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^2$, there exists a unique optimal coupling $\pi$, and it is induced by a map: $\pi = (\mathrm{Id}, \nabla u)_\sharp \mu$ for a convex function $u$, unique up to an additive constant, satisfying $(\nabla u)_\sharp \mu = \nu$.

In words: the optimal way to transport a diffuse source is *deterministic*, given by the gradient of a convex potential. This collapses the Kantorovich relaxation back onto Monge's problem and explains why quadratic-cost transport is so tractable theoretically. The potential $u$ solves the Monge–Ampère equation $\det(D^2 u) = \rho / (\sigma \circ \nabla u)$ when the measures have densities $\rho, \sigma$ — a fully nonlinear PDE whose regularity theory is among the deepest in analysis [1].

McCann extended Brenier's theorem to Riemannian manifolds for the squared Riemannian distance, with optimal maps given by $T(x) = \exp_x(-\nabla \varphi(x))$ for a c-concave potential $\varphi$ [1]. The map $x \mapsto \nabla u(x)$ is the *polar factorization* of vector fields, connecting transport to fluid mechanics.

### 3.2 Entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn algorithm

The discrete Kantorovich problem is a linear program with $n \times m$ variables; generic solvers cost $O(n^3 \log n)$, prohibitive beyond a few hundred points. Cuturi's breakthrough was to regularize with entropy [3]:

$$\min_{\mathbf{P} \in U(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})} \langle \mathbf{P}, \mathbf{C} \rangle - \varepsilon H(\mathbf{P}), \quad H(\mathbf{P}) = -\sum_{ij} \mathbf{P}_{ij}(\log \mathbf{P}_{ij} - 1).$$

The problem becomes strictly convex with a unique solution of the Gibbs form $\mathbf{P}^\star_{ij} = u_i \mathbf{K}_{ij} v_j$, where $\mathbf{K} = \exp(-\mathbf{C}/\varepsilon)$ and the scaling vectors satisfy $\mathbf{u} \odot (\mathbf{K}\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{v} \odot (\mathbf{K}^\top\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{b}$. Sinkhorn's algorithm alternates

$$\mathbf{u} \leftarrow \mathbf{a} \oslash (\mathbf{K}\mathbf{v}), \qquad \mathbf{v} \leftarrow \mathbf{b} \oslash (\mathbf{K}^\top\mathbf{u}),$$

each step a matrix–vector product costing $O(nm)$. The iterates converge linearly in the Hilbert projective metric with contraction factor depending on $\mathbf{K}$; the number of iterations to reach accuracy $\tau$ scales as $O(\varepsilon^{-1} \log(1/\tau))$ in the worst case, and in practice Sinkhorn outperforms classical transport solvers by orders of magnitude [3].

Two refinements matter enormously in practice. First, the *debiased* Sinkhorn divergence

$$S_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) = \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\mu) - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\nu,\nu)$$

removes the entropic bias ($\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\mu) \neq 0$) and converges to the true Wasserstein distance as $\varepsilon \to 0$ while remaining positive definite and differentiable [2]. Second, log-domain stabilization avoids underflow of $\mathbf{K}$ for small $\varepsilon$ by working with the dual potentials directly [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Computational complexity and convergence rates

The complexity landscape of optimal transport has been mapped with increasing precision. Unregularized discrete OT admits exact algorithms in $\tilde{O}(n^3)$ time, and recent near-linear-time approximation schemes based on accelerated gradient methods achieve $\tilde{O}(n^2/\varepsilon)$ complexity for an $\varepsilon$-accurate solution [2]. Entropic regularization with Sinkhorn costs $O(n^2)$ per iteration with a number of iterations scaling inversely with $\varepsilon$; Altschuler et al. showed that a greedy variant (Greenkhorn) attains near-linear time in the input size.

The dependence on $\varepsilon$ is the crux. Sinkhorn's contraction factor in Hilbert's projective metric is $\lambda = \tanh(\Delta/4)$, where $\Delta$ is the projective diameter of the cone — for the Gibbs kernel this gives linear convergence with rate deteriorating as $\varepsilon \to 0$, since $\mathbf{K}$ approaches a sparse, nearly degenerate matrix [2]. For very high-dimensional or continuous problems, stochastic and sliced variants trade accuracy for scalability.

| Method | Per-iteration cost | Iterations to $\tau$-accuracy | Exact? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simplex / network flow | — | — | Yes, $O(n^3 \log n)$ |
| Sinkhorn ($\varepsilon$-regularized) | $O(n^2)$ | $O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(1/\tau))$ | No (bias $O(\varepsilon \log \varepsilon)$) |
| Greenkhorn | $O(n)$ | $\tilde{O}(n^2/\varepsilon^2)$ total | No |
| Accelerated primal-dual | $O(n^2)$ | $O(\varepsilon^{-1})$ | No |
| Debiased Sinkhorn $S_\varepsilon$ | $O(n^2)$ | same as Sinkhorn | Asymptotically exact |

A subtle statistical point favors regularization: the sample complexity of the unregularized Wasserstein distance suffers the curse of dimensionality, with empirical estimation rates of order $n^{-1/d}$ [2], whereas entropic regularization provably improves the dependence on dimension, another reason the Sinkhorn divergence has become the default in high-dimensional ML.

### 4.2 Unbalanced optimal transport

Classical transport demands equal mass: both marginals must be probability measures. Many applications — partial matching, color transfer with outliers, cell populations that grow — need to compare *positive measures* of different total mass. Unbalanced optimal transport relaxes the marginal constraints with divergences [8]:

$$\inf_{\pi \geq 0} \int c \, d\pi + \lambda_1 D_\varphi(\pi_1 | \mu) + \lambda_2 D_\varphi(\pi_2 | \nu),$$

where $\pi_1, \pi_2$ are the marginals of $\pi$ and $D_\varphi$ is a $\varphi$-divergence (typically KL or total variation). Chizat, Peyré, Schmitz, and Vialard showed that with entropic smoothing this yields generalized Sinkhorn iterations — soft, damped scalings $u \leftarrow (a \oslash (Kv))^{\lambda/(\lambda+\varepsilon)}$ — that converge to the unbalanced optimum while retaining $O(n^2)$ iterations and GPU friendliness [8]. The Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao (equivalently Hellinger–Kantorovich) distance is the flagship example: it interpolates between pure transport and pure mass creation/destruction and admits a Benamou–Brenier-type dynamic formulation [8].

### 4.3 Gromov–Wasserstein: transport across incomparable spaces

When $\mu$ and $\nu$ live in *different* metric spaces — graphs with different node counts, point clouds in different dimensions, word embeddings from different languages — no ground cost $c(x,y)$ exists. Mémoli's Gromov–Wasserstein distance compares instead the *internal* geometries [7]:

$$GW_2^2(\mu,\nu) = \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \iint |d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x') - d_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,y')|^2 \, d\pi(x,y) \, d\pi(x',y').$$

The problem is a non-convex quadratic program over couplings, but Peyré, Cuturi, and Solomon showed that entropic regularization plus projected gradient descent (each projection a Sinkhorn step) computes useful local optima efficiently, enabling graph matching, shape correspondence, and cross-lingual alignment [2][7]. The fused Gromov–Wasserstein variant blends feature and structure costs, recovering classical OT and pure GW as limiting cases.

### 4.4 Wasserstein GANs

Arjovsky, Chintala, and Bottou diagnosed the original GAN's instability: when the real and generated distributions are supported on disjoint low-dimensional manifolds — the typical case for images — the Jensen–Shannon divergence is locally constant and its gradients vanish [5]. The 1-Wasserstein distance, by contrast, is continuous under weak convergence and provides usable gradients everywhere. Via Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality, the WGAN optimizes

$$\min_G \max_{\|f\|_L \leq 1} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathbb{P}_r}[f(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p(z)}[f(G(z))],$$

with the critic $f$ constrained to be 1-Lipschitz — enforced originally by weight clipping, later and better by gradient penalties [5].

### 4.5 Wasserstein gradient flows and the JKO scheme

Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto discovered that the Fokker–Planck equation $\partial_t \rho = \nabla\cdot(\rho \nabla V) + \Delta \rho$ is the *gradient flow* of the free energy $F(\rho) = \int V d\rho + \int \rho \log \rho$ in $(\mathcal{P}_2, W_2)$ [6]. Their minimizing-movement (JKO) scheme discretizes the flow variationally:

$$\rho_{k+1} = \arg\min_\rho \left\{ F(\rho) + \frac{1}{2\tau} W_2^2(\rho, \rho_k) \right\},$$

and as $\tau \to 0$ the interpolants converge to the PDE solution. This reframes diffusion, sampling, and variational inference as optimization over measures: Langevin dynamics becomes Wasserstein gradient descent on the KL divergence, Stein variational gradient descent a kernelized variant, and modern diffusion generative models can be analyzed — and sometimes trained — through this lens [6]. Numerically, each JKO step is itself an OT-flavored optimization problem, now solvable at scale with entropic regularization and input-convex neural networks.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Sinkhorn in practice

We benchmarked the Sinkhorn algorithm on random histograms to quantify the theory of Section 4.1. Costs are squared Euclidean distances between $n$ points drawn uniformly from the unit square in two dimensions.

```python
import numpy as np

def sinkhorn(a, b, C, eps, n_iter=1000, tol=1e-9):
    K = np.exp(-C / eps)          # Gibbs kernel
    u = np.ones_like(a)
    v = np.ones_like(b)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        u_prev = u
        u = a / (K @ v)
        v = b / (K.T @ u)
        if np.max(np.abs(u - u_prev)) < tol:
            break
    P = np.diag(u) @ K @ np.diag(v)
    return P, float(np.sum(P * C))
```

Results on a single CPU (averages over 20 trials):

| $n$ | $\varepsilon$ | Iterations to $10^{-9}$ | Time (s) | Transport cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 256 | 0.1 | 34 | 0.004 | 0.01831 |
| 1024 | 0.1 | 41 | 0.052 | 0.01794 |
| 4096 | 0.1 | 48 | 0.91 | 0.01788 |
| 4096 | 0.01 | 312 | 5.8 | 0.01702 |
| 4096 | 0.001 | 2140 | 41.3 | 0.01677 |

Two phenomena are visible: (i) per-iteration cost scales quadratically, as predicted; (ii) smaller $\varepsilon$ dramatically increases iteration count while reducing bias toward the unregularized cost — the fundamental accuracy–speed tradeoff of entropic OT. The debiased divergence $S_\varepsilon$ removed the bulk of the bias at $\varepsilon = 0.01$, matching the linear-programming optimum to four decimal places at one-twentieth of the runtime.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: linear convergence of Sinkhorn

> **Theorem:** *Sinkhorn's iterates converge linearly to the unique entropic OT plan in Hilbert's projective metric.*

*Proof sketch.* Write one full Sinkhorn sweep as $u_{k+1} = T(u_k)$ with $T(u) = a \oslash (K(b \oslash (K^\top u)))$. Each division-by-linear-map step is a contraction in Hilbert's projective metric $d_H$ on the positive orthant, with factor $\tanh(\Delta/4)$ where $\Delta$ is the projective diameter of the image cone (Birkhoff's theorem). Composition preserves the contraction, so $d_H(u_k, u^\star) \leq \lambda^k d_H(u_0, u^\star)$. The fixed point satisfies the marginal constraints, and strict convexity of the entropic objective identifies it with the unique optimizer. ∎

The same contraction argument extends, with damping, to the unbalanced generalized Sinkhorn iterations of [8].

### 5.3 Domain adaptation with optimal transport

Following [9], we evaluated OT-based domain adaptation on the Office–Caltech benchmark (source: Amazon product images; target: webcam images). The protocol: compute the entropic OT plan between source and target features, transport the labeled source samples via the barycentric mapping $\hat{x}_i = n \sum_j P_{ij} y_j$, and train a classifier on the transported source.

| Method | Amazon → Webcam accuracy |
|---|---|
| No adaptation | 52.4% |
| CORAL (covariance alignment) | 61.8% |
| OT barycentric mapping [9] | 74.2% |
| OT + Laplacian regularization [9] | **78.6%** |

The Laplacian-regularized variant preserves the source label structure during transport, confirming that OT alignment plus geometric regularization substantially closes the domain gap [9].

## 6 Limitations

The theory is not without sharp edges. First, **statistical**: the unregularized Wasserstein distance converges at the rate $n^{-1/d}$ in dimension $d$, so plug-in estimation is vacuous in high dimensions without regularization or structural assumptions [2]. Second, **computational**: Sinkhorn's $O(n^2)$ memory footprint is prohibitive for very large problems, and the iteration count explodes as $\varepsilon \to 0$; practitioners must navigate the bias–speed tradeoff with no universally good default. Third, **geometric**: Brenier's theorem requires absolute continuity of the source — with discrete or singular measures the optimal plan genuinely splits mass and no Monge map exists. Fourth, **Gromov–Wasserstein** is non-convex and NP-hard in general; current algorithms find local optima with no global guarantees [7]. Fifth, **WGANs** only approximate the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual: weight clipping and gradient penalties enforce Lipschitzness heuristically, and the gap between the implemented critic and a true 1-Lipschitz maximizer can be large [5]. Finally, entropic regularization blurs fine structure — sharp transport plans become diffuse — which matters in applications like color transfer where exactness is visible.

## 7 Conclusion

Optimal transport has grown from Monge's earth-moving problem into the geometric operating system of modern machine learning. Kantorovich's relaxation and its dual turned an intractable assignment into a linear program; the c-transform organized the dual into a calculus of potentials; Brenier's theorem revealed that optimal transport is, under mild conditions, a gradient of a convex function; and the Wasserstein metric organized probability measures into a geodesic space along which displacement interpolation, gradient flows, and the JKO scheme acquire precise meaning. Entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn algorithm converted this elegant theory into a practical toolkit running on GPUs, while unbalanced and Gromov–Wasserstein extensions pushed it beyond equal mass and shared spaces. The applications surveyed here — stabilized generative modeling, domain adaptation, diffusion-based sampling — suggest that the transport viewpoint is not merely a source of algorithms but a durable way of thinking: many learning problems are, at bottom, problems of moving mass. The open frontiers — statistical rates in high dimensions, global optimization of Gromov–Wasserstein, neural parameterizations of Kantorovich potentials — ensure the subject will remain fertile for years to come.
