# The Quantum Hall Effects: Landau Levels, Chern Numbers, Edge-State Transport, and the Fractional Hierarchy

## Abstract

The quantum Hall effects constitute one of the most profound bridges between topology, quantum mechanics, and strongly correlated matter in condensed-matter physics. This thesis develops the subject from first principles: the quantization of cyclotron motion into Landau levels, the emergence of an exact macroscopic quantization of the Hall resistance in integer plateaus, and the topological explanation furnished by the TKNN invariant and its identification with the first Chern number. It then turns to the fractional quantum Hall effect, where electron-electron interactions within a Landau level produce incompressible quantum liquids whose excitations carry fractional charge and obey anyonic statistics, anchored by Laughlin's trial wavefunction and organized by Jain's composite-fermion hierarchy. Edge-state theories, chiral Luttinger liquids, and the Landauer-Buttiker transport formalism are treated alongside the numerical and experimental evidence base, including shot-noise measurements of fractional charge, interferometric probes of statistics, and the recent extension of these ideas to graphene and fractional Chern insulators.

## 1 Intro

In 1980 Klaus von Klitzing and collaborators observed that the Hall resistance of a silicon MOSFET, cooled below one kelvin and subjected to a magnetic field exceeding ten tesla, did not vary continuously with field as classical electrodynamics demands. Instead it locked onto plateaus at values equal to a combination of fundamental constants, h over e squared divided by an integer, with an accuracy better than one part in a million [1]. Within two years, Daniel Tsui, Horst Stormer, and Arthur Gossard found plateaus at *fractional* values of the same unit in higher-mobility gallium arsenide heterostructures [2], an observation that no single-particle theory could explain. The theoretical responses to these two discoveries — Laughlin's gauge argument, the Thouless-Kohmoto-Nightingale-den Nijs (TKNN) topological formula [3], Laughlin's incompressible-liquid wavefunction [4], and Jain's composite-fermion map [5] — transformed the way physicists think about phases of matter: not by broken symmetry, but by topology and entanglement.

The significance of the quantum Hall effects is threefold. *First*, they provide the most precise macroscopic manifestation of quantum mechanics ever measured; the integer plateaus define the metrological resistance standard. *Second*, they exposed topology as a physical principle governing electronic matter, predating the topological insulator revolution and supplying its conceptual vocabulary: Chern numbers, edge-bulk correspondence, and Berry curvature. *Third*, the fractional effect revealed emergent particles with properties forbidden in three dimensions — fractional charge and anyonic braiding statistics — founding the field of topological quantum matter.

This thesis presents a self-contained development of the subject. Section 2 establishes Landau quantization and the integer effect phenomenologically. Section 3 collects the theoretical machinery. Section 4 carries out the deep technical development across four subsections: Landau-level structure and the Chern-number invariant; the Laughlin liquid; composite fermions and the Jain hierarchy; and edge-state theory with anyons and modern frontiers. Section 5 summarizes the decisive empirical evidence and proofs of principle, Section 6 the limitations of our current understanding, and Section 7 the outlook.

## 2 Background

### The classical Hall effect and its limits

The classical Hall effect, discovered by Edwin Hall in 1879, is a consequence of the Lorentz force. A current density J_x flowing in the plane of a two-dimensional conductor, with a perpendicular magnetic field B pointing in the z direction, deflects charge carriers toward one sample edge. The resulting transverse voltage defines the Hall resistivity rho_xy, which classically grows linearly with B. This picture assumes diffusive, semiclassical carrier motion, and it fails catastrophically at low temperatures and high fields, where the transverse resistivity develops the famous plateaus while the longitudinal resistivity rho_xx collapses to zero on the plateaus [1].

### Landau quantization

The quantum-mechanical starting point is a single charged particle in a uniform perpendicular magnetic field. Choosing the Landau gauge A = (0, Bx, 0), the Hamiltonian becomes a shifted harmonic oscillator in the x direction, with plane-wave motion along y:

```
H = p_x^2 / 2m + (p_y + eBx)^2 / 2m
```

The spectrum is the celebrated Landau ladder, E_n = hbar omega_c (n + 1/2), where the cyclotron frequency omega_c = eB / m and n = 0, 1, 2, ... is the Landau-level index [7]. Two quantities organize all subsequent discussion:

- The *magnetic length*, l_B = sqrt(hbar / eB), the quantum-mechanical radius of a cyclotron orbit. At ten tesla this is roughly eight nanometers, a scale that will recur throughout the fractional theory.
- The *filling factor*, nu = n_e / n_phi, the ratio of electron density n_e to the degeneracy density of a Landau level n_phi = eB / h (one state per flux quantum Phi_0 = h / e per unit area). Integer nu means completely filled Landau levels; fractional nu means a partially filled level, where the kinetic energy is quenched and interactions reign.

The single-particle gap between Landau levels, hbar omega_c, explains why the integer plateaus are robust: at integer filling the Fermi energy sits in a gap, and adding a carrier costs a finite energy. But this argument alone cannot explain *exact* quantization in the presence of disorder, nor anything fractional. For those we need topology and correlations.

### The integer effect phenomenology

In von Klitzing's experiment, as the gate voltage (hence carrier density) or magnetic field is swept, rho_xy locks to the values h / (nu e squared) for integer nu = 1, 2, 3, ..., while rho_xx drops to immeasurably small values on the plateaus. Between plateaus, rho_xx spikes as the system passes through extended states at the center of a Landau level. Disorder is not the enemy of this quantization — it is its enabler: localized states in the Landau-level tails absorb density changes without contributing to transport, pinning the chemical potential in the mobility gap while the plateau persists [3][7].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is theoretical and synthetic: it reconstructs the quantum Hall effects from the primary literature using a unified notation, verifying derivations against standard references [7] and numerical sketches of the Hofstadter spectrum and composite-fermion sequences. The methods employed are:

1. **Single-particle quantum mechanics in magnetic fields.** Landau gauge quantization, guiding-center coordinates, and the algebra of magnetic translations, which obey T(a) T(b) = exp(2 pi i times flux enclosed) T(b) T(a), encoding the non-commutativity at the heart of the effect [7].
2. **Linear response and the Kubo formula.** The Hall conductivity is computed from current-current correlators; in a filled band this collapses to a Brillouin-zone integral of Berry curvature — the TKNN formula [3].
3. **Trial wavefunctions and plasma analogy.** Laughlin's wavefunction is analyzed through its mapping onto a classical two-dimensional one-component plasma, which establishes incompressibility and the excitation gap [4].
4. **Chern-Simons flux attachment.** The composite-fermion construction is derived as a singular gauge transformation binding an even number of flux quanta to each electron, transmuting statistics and reducing the fractional problem to an integer one [5].
5. **Edge conformal field theory and Landauer-Buttiker transport.** Chiral edge modes are described as one-dimensional chiral bosons, and multi-terminal conductance is computed from transmission probabilities [6].
6. **Numerical illustration.** A short Python program diagonalizes Harper's equation across rational flux fractions to render the Hofstadter butterfly; a Haskell snippet enumerates the Jain hierarchy of filling factors.

> **Theorem:** (TKNN, 1982) For a two-dimensional electron gas in a periodic potential with the Fermi energy in a spectral gap, the Hall conductivity is quantized as sigma_xy = (e squared / h) times the sum of the first Chern numbers C_alpha of the occupied bands, each C_alpha an integer [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### Landau Levels, Magnetic Translations, and the Chern-Number Invariant

The Landau-gauge wavefunctions factorize into plane waves e^(iky) and displaced oscillator states centered at guiding-center position X = -k l_B squared. The guiding centers obey a non-commutative algebra, the commutator of X and Y being i times l_B squared, so each quantum state occupies an area 2 pi l_B squared — precisely the degeneracy eB / h per unit area derived above. A filled Landau level is therefore a *band insulator* in the language of Bloch theory once a periodic potential is introduced, and band insulators are classified by their Chern numbers. The TKNN breakthrough was to write the Kubo formula for the Hall conductance of a filled band as an integral of the Berry curvature F over the magnetic Brillouin zone:

```
sigma_xy = (e^2 / h) * (1 / 2pi) * integral F d^2k
```

The integral of F over a closed manifold is the first Chern number, an integer, because the Berry connection cannot be defined smoothly over the whole torus: its gauge patches differ by transition functions whose winding number counts the flux of curvature [3]. Thus exact quantization is *topological* — immune to smooth deformations of the Hamiltonian, including weak disorder — provided the Fermi level stays in a gap. This was the first appearance in physics of what is now called a topological invariant of a band structure, and Avron, Seiler, and Simon subsequently identified the TKNN integer with the mathematical Chern number [7].

A vivid corollary is the Hofstadter butterfly: for flux per unit cell equal to a rational p / q, each Landau level splits into q subbands, and every spectral gap carries an integer Hall conductance given by a Diophantine equation in p and q. The following Python sketch computes the butterfly by diagonalizing Harper's equation, the discrete tight-binding equation at rational flux:

```python
import numpy as np

def hofstadter(q_max=60):
    """Eigenvalues of Harper's equation for flux alpha = p/q."""
    points = []
    for q in range(1, q_max + 1):
        for p in range(1, q + 1):
            if np.gcd(p, q) != 1:
                continue
            alpha = p / q
            H = np.zeros((q, q))
            for n in range(q):
                H[n, (n + 1) % q] = H[(n + 1) % q, n] = -1.0
                H[n, n] = -2.0 * np.cos(2.0 * np.pi * alpha * n)
            for e in np.linalg.eigvalsh(H):
                points.append((alpha, e))
    return np.array(points)

spectrum = hofstadter(60)
print("eigenvalues:", len(spectrum))
# Every visible gap in the scatter plot carries a TKNN integer,
# solving r = q*s + p*t with |t| <= q/2 for the Hall conductance t.
```

*Bulk-edge correspondence* completes the picture: a nonzero total Chern number C forces |C| chiral edge modes to cross the gap at any boundary. Each contributes one conductance quantum e squared / h via the Landauer formula, reproducing the bulk quantization from the edge alone — a duality that generalizes to every topological phase discovered since [7].

### The Laughlin Wavefunction and Fractional Plateaus

At fractional filling the single-particle gap argument collapses: a partially filled Landau level is massively degenerate, and the kinetic energy is a constant within it. The physics is then driven entirely by the Coulomb interaction projected into the lowest Landau level. Tsui, Stormer, and Gossard's discovery of a plateau at nu = 1/3 with rho_xx vanishing [2] demanded a new kind of ground state — an *incompressible quantum liquid* with no broken symmetry.

Laughlin's 1983 proposal for the ground state at filling 1/m (m odd) is the wavefunction [4]:

```
Psi_m = product over i<j of (z_i - z_j)^m * exp(- sum |z_i|^2 / 4 l_B^2)
```

where z_i = x_i + i y_i are complex electron coordinates. The Jastrow factor with odd exponent m enforces the Pauli principle while keeping electrons apart: each electron sees every other as an m-th order zero of the wavefunction, suppressing the short-range Coulomb repulsion with exquisite efficiency. Laughlin showed via the *plasma analogy* — interpreting |Psi_m| squared as the Boltzmann weight of a classical two-dimensional plasma with logarithmic interactions — that the state is uniform with density 1 / (m times 2 pi l_B squared), i.e. exactly filling 1/m, and incompressible: density fluctuations cost a finite energy.

The excitations are even more remarkable. Inserting a thin solenoid carrying one flux quantum adiabatically at the origin pumps a quasihole into the liquid, and the plasma analogy shows it carries charge *e / m* — a fraction of the electron charge [4]. Laughlin's gauge argument, adapted from his explanation of the integer effect, proves that transporting a quasiparticle around another accumulates a phase of 2 pi / m rather than pi, the signature of *anyonic statistics*: particles that are neither bosons nor fermions, permitted because exchange in two dimensions is governed by the braid group rather than the permutation group.

The Laughlin sequence nu = 1/3, 1/5, 1/7, ... accounts for the strongest fractions, but experiment quickly revealed a far richer zoo: 2/5, 3/7, 4/9, 2/3, 3/5, 4/7, and, spectacularly, an even-denominator plateau at nu = 5/2. A unifying principle was needed.

### Composite Fermions and the Jain Hierarchy

Jain's composite-fermion theory supplies that principle with a single elegant idea: attach an even number 2m of magnetic flux quanta to each electron via a singular Chern-Simons gauge transformation [5].

```
nu = p / (2*m*p +/- 1)
```

> **Theorem:** (Jain, 1989) The trial states obtained by flux attachment at composite-fermion filling p have overlaps exceeding 0.99 with exact diagonalization ground states for the principal fractions, and their excitation gaps scale as observed experimentally [5].

The formula generates the observed sequences with uncanny precision: for m = 1, p = 1, 2, 3, ...

```haskell
-- Composite-fermion filling factors: nu = p / (2*m*p +/- 1)
jainSequence :: Int -> Int -> [Rational]
jainSequence m n =
    [ p % (2*m*p + 1) | p <- [1..n] ]
    ++ [ p % (2*m*p - 1) | p <- [1..n] ]

main :: IO ()
main = do
    print (jainSequence 1 5)  -- 1/3, 2/5, 3/7, 4/9, 5/11, then 2/3, 3/5, ...
    print (jainSequence 2 3)  -- 1/5, 2/9, 3/13, then 2/7, 3/11, ...
```

| Filling nu | CF Lambda level p | sigma_xy | Experimental status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1/3 | 1 (m = 1) | e squared / 3h | Strongest FQH plateau, GaAs [2] |
| 2/5 | 2 (m = 1) | 2e squared / 5h | Well developed, GaAs |
| 3/7 | 3 (m = 1) | 3e squared / 7h | Observed, high mobility |
| 2/3 | 2 (m = 1, hole branch) | 2e squared / 3h | Particle-hole conjugate of 1/3 |
| 5/2 | paired CFs | 5e squared / 2h | Even denominator; candidate non-Abelian |
| 1/5 | 1 (m = 2) | e squared / 5h | Second Laughlin state |

At half filling, B star vanishes: composite fermions form a Fermi sea, confirmed by surface-acoustic-wave and magnetic-focusing experiments that measure a CF Fermi wavevector directly [5].

### Edge States, Chiral Luttinger Liquids, Anyons, and Modern Frontiers

Bulk topology mandates gapless edges, and in the quantum Hall regime the edges are *chiral*: modes propagate in only one direction, set by the magnetic field orientation.

The fractional edge is subtler.

The braiding statistics themselves have moved from theory to measurement.

The paradigm has meanwhile escaped its semiconductor origins:

- **Graphene** exhibits the integer effect with a relativistic twist: massless Dirac fermions produce Landau levels scaling as the square root of n, and a half-integer-shifted sequence sigma_xy = 4 (n + 1/2) e squared / h, observed at room temperature [8].
- **Fractional Chern insulators** realize fractional Hall physics in lattice bands with nonzero Chern number but *no external magnetic field*, predicted in 2011 [9] and subsequently observed in moire heterostructures and twisted bilayer graphene, where flat bands concentrate Berry curvature.
- **The quantum anomalous Hall effect** achieves integer quantization from intrinsic magnetization, closing the circle from Landau levels to pure band topology.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The evidence base for the quantum Hall effects is among the strongest in physics, combining metrological precision with direct probes of emergent fractionalization:

1. **Exact quantization (integer).** Von Klitzing's plateaus reproduce h / e squared to better than one part in 10^9 in modern metrology laboratories, and the effect now *defines* the ohm in the revised SI [1].
2. **Fractional plateaus.** More than a hundred fractions have been catalogued in GaAs/AlGaAs, following the Jain sequences with gaps that scale as predicted by composite-fermion theory [2][5].
3. **Fractional charge.** Shot-noise measurements at quantum point contacts yield quasiparticle charge e/3 at nu = 1/3 from the Schottky formula S = 2 e star I, a direct observation of charge fractionalization [7].
4. **Chiral edge transport.** Nonlocal and multi-terminal measurements confirm the Landauer-Buttiker predictions, including the equilibration lengths of co-propagating edge modes [7].
5. **Tunneling exponents.** The power-law tunneling density of states at fractional edges matches Wen's chiral Luttinger liquid predictions [6].
6. **Composite-fermion Fermi sea.** Geometric resonances near nu = 1/2 measure the CF Fermi wavevector, confirming the effective-field cancellation [5].
7. **Interferometry.** Fabry-Perot and Mach-Zehnder interferometers report Aharonov-Bohm periodicities consistent with fractional and possibly non-Abelian braiding phases [7].
8. **Graphene and Chern insulators.** Room-temperature quantization in graphene [8] and zero-field fractional Chern insulators [9] prove the phenomenon is topological rather than material-specific.

## 6 Limitations

The theory, for all its triumphs, has sharp boundaries. The Laughlin and Jain wavefunctions are *trial* states: spectacularly accurate for the principal fractions but not derived from the Coulomb Hamiltonian, and their overlaps degrade for higher-order fractions where Landau-level mixing and finite well width matter. The nu = 5/2 state remains unresolved after three decades — the Moore-Read Pfaffian, its particle-hole conjugate the anti-Pfaffian, and newer parton proposals all fit existing data, and thermal Hall conductance measurements intended to discriminate them have given conflicting results.

Disorder, essential to the integer plateaus, is treated only phenomenologically in the fractional regime; a first-principles theory of the plateau transition critical exponent (measured near 2.4, predicted 2.59 from network models) is still debated. Edge reconstruction — the spontaneous rearrangement of the edge density profile — complicates the clean chiral Luttinger liquid predictions in real samples [6]. Finally, topological-quantum-computation proposals face the materials reality that non-Abelian states survive only in the cleanest millikelvin heterostructures.

## 7 Conclusion

From von Klitzing's silicon MOSFET to fractional Chern insulators in moire superlattices, the quantum Hall effects trace a single arc: *geometry and topology, imposed by a magnetic field on quantum motion, generate exact macroscopic laws and entirely new particles*. The integer effect taught us that conductance can be a topological invariant, counted by Chern numbers and protected against disorder [1][3]. The fractional effect taught us that strong interactions in a topologically nontrivial band produce quantum liquids with fractional charge and anyonic statistics, organized by the composite-fermion principle [2][4][5]. Edge-state theory [6] turned the bulk-boundary correspondence into a quantitative transport tool, and the modern extensions to graphene [8] and Chern insulators [9] show that the underlying mathematics — Berry curvature integrated over a closed manifold — is indifferent to the material that hosts it.

Open problems remain formidable: the identity of the 5/2 state, a microscopic theory of the plateau transition, and the demonstration of non-Abelian braiding. But the conceptual legacy is secure: the quantum Hall effects redefined what a phase of matter can be, replacing Landau's symmetry-breaking paradigm with topological order.
