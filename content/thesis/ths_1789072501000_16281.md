# Incorrectness Logic: Under-Approximate Program Logics, Incorrectness Separation Logic, and the Algebra of True Bug-Finding

## Abstract

Hoare logic proves the *absence* of bugs by over-approximating program behaviour; it cannot certify that a bug is truly present. This article develops the theory of **incorrectness logic** (IL), O'Hearn's POPL 2020 dual of Hoare logic [1], in which the incorrectness triple $[p]\\,c\\,[q]$ is valid exactly when every state satisfying the *result* $q$ is reachable by executing $c$ from some state satisfying the *presumption* $p$. We present the reversed consequence rule, the disjunctive proof rules that permit *dropping* of disjuncts, and the exit conditions distinguishing normal ($ok$) from erroneous ($er$) termination. We then treat Raad et al.'s incorrectness separation logic (ISL) [2] — its local-reasoning frame rule, fault axioms for heap errors, and the true-positive theorem grounding begin-anywhere symbolic execution; the Kleene-algebraic unification of correctness and incorrectness reasoning by Möller, O'Hearn, and Hoare [5]; the empirical validation of the theory in Pulse-X, which found fifteen confirmed bugs in OpenSSL [3]; and Zilberstein et al.'s outcome logic as the unifying successor framework [4]. We close with limitations: false negatives, and the original logic's awkward fit for manifest errors and probabilistic programs.

## 1 Introduction

"Program correctness and incorrectness are two sides of the same coin." With this sentence Peter O'Hearn opens the paper that founded incorrectness logic [1], and the metaphor is exact: where Hoare's logic proves that a program *must* satisfy its specification on every execution, incorrectness logic proves that a program *may* violate it on some execution — and that the violation is real.

The asymmetry that motivated the work is old. Dijkstra observed that "program testing can be used to show the presence of bugs, but never to show their absence" [7]. For fifty years the formal-methods community invested overwhelmingly in the *absence* side: Hoare logic, weakest preconditions, model checking, separation logic. Bug-finding — the activity programmers actually spend most of their time on, and the activity industrial static analysers perform — had no principled logical foundation. Analyses such as Infer's were built on bi-abduction [6] and careful engineering, but their reports were heuristic: a "bug" might be unreachable, and developers learned to distrust tools that cried wolf.

Incorrectness logic supplies the missing foundation. Its central move is to replace over-approximation with **under-approximation**: a valid triple guarantees that the described outcome is *reachable*, never that all outcomes are described. Theorems of the logic are therefore **true positives by construction** — a property with immediate practical consequences for any analysis built on it. This article reconstructs the theory from its semantics through its separation-logic extension, its algebraic unification with Hoare logic, its industrial validation, and the outcome-logic framework that now subsumes it.

## 2 Background

A Hoare triple $\{p\}\,c\,\{q\}$ is valid when every state reachable by executing $c$ from a $p$-state satisfies $q$. Writing $\mathrm{post}(c)(p)$ for the set of reachable states, validity is the inclusion

$$\mathrm{post}(c)(p)\ \subseteq\ q,$$

so $q$ is an **over-approximation** of the program's behaviour [1]. The consequence rule of Hoare logic strengthens the precondition and weakens the postcondition:

$$\frac{\{p'\}\,c\,\{q'\}\qquad p \Rightarrow p'\qquad q' \Rightarrow q}{\{p\}\,c\,\{q\}}.$$

Separation logic added spatial locality to this picture: the frame rule lets a proof about a small heap footprint extend to any larger heap, which is what makes verification scale to large codebases. Compositional *shape analysis by bi-abduction* [6] turned these ideas into Infer, Meta's industrial static analyser, and specialised descendants such as RacerD (compositional race detection) and Pulse (memory-safety analysis) followed.

The closest precursor to incorrectness logic is the **reverse Hoare logic** of de Vries and Koutavas, which reversed the consequence rule to reason about reachability — but without any explicit notion of erroneous execution, it could not distinguish a bug from a benign outcome [1][5]. O'Hearn's contribution was to make error a first-class citizen of the semantics: commands may terminate in one of two exit conditions, $ok$ (normal) or $er$ (erroneous, e.g. a crash), and the logic under-approximates *both* channels. The result is a logic whose postconditions describe bugs that must be reachable, rather than invariants that must hold.

The practical stakes are the false-positive/false-negative trade-off. Over-approximate analyses consider a superset of behaviours and therefore report *spurious* bugs; under-approximate analyses consider a subset and miss real ones. Incorrectness logic chooses the second horn deliberately: every alarm is genuine, at the price of silence about bugs the analysis did not find [3].

## 3 Methodology

Our presentation follows the development in the primary literature [1][2][3][4][5]. We fix a simple imperative language of *regular commands* — including `skip`, assignment, the error-raising primitive `error()`, the guard `assume(b)`, and nondeterministic assignment `x := nond()` — with sequential composition, nondeterministic choice, and iteration [1]. O'Hearn calls the precondition the **presumption** and the postcondition the **result**, terminology we adopt throughout.

**Validity.** The incorrectness triple $[p]\,c\,[q]$ is valid, written $\models [p]\,c\,[q]$, iff

$$\mathrm{post}(c)(p)\ \supseteq\ q,$$

i.e. every state in $q$ is reachable by executing $c$ from *some* state in $p$. The full form tags an exit condition: $[p]\,c\,[\varepsilon: q]$ with $\varepsilon \in \{ok, er\}$, valid iff $q \subseteq \mathrm{post}_\varepsilon(c)(p)$ — the reachable *erroneous* states, for $\varepsilon = er$, are exactly what a bug-finder wants to characterise [1][2].

**Method.** We proceed semantically (collecting semantics over sets of states), proof-theoretically (the rules of IL and ISL, their soundness and completeness), algebraically (the Kleene-algebra embedding that unifies IL with HL [5]), and empirically (the Pulse-X analyser and its OpenSSL campaign [3]).

The duality can be stated in one line of code. In a Haskell-like notation over sets of states:

```haskell
-- Collecting semantics: all states reachable by c from states in p
post :: Cmd -> Set State -> Set State

-- Hoare triple: q OVER-approximates the reachable states
validHL :: Set State -> Cmd -> Set State -> Bool
validHL p c q = post c p `isSubsetOf` q

-- Incorrectness triple: q UNDER-approximates the reachable states
validIL :: Set State -> Cmd -> Set State -> Bool
validIL p c q = q `isSubsetOf` post c p
```

The quantifier structure flips accordingly: Hoare validity is $\forall \sigma \in p.\, \forall \delta \in \mathrm{post}(c)(\sigma).\, \delta \in q$ ("every reachable state satisfies $q$"), while incorrectness validity is $\forall \delta \in q.\, \exists \sigma \in p.\, \delta \in \mathrm{post}(c)(\sigma)$ ("every $q$-state is reachable") [1].

| | Hoare logic | Incorrectness logic |
|---|---|---|
| Notation | $\{p\}\,c\,\{q\}$ | $[p]\,c\,[q]$ |
| Reading of $p$ / $q$ | precondition / postcondition | presumption / result |
| Validity | $\mathrm{post}(c)(p) \subseteq q$ | $\mathrm{post}(c)(p) \supseteq q$ |
| Approximation direction | over-approximate | under-approximate |
| Quantifiers | $\forall\sigma\,\forall\delta$ | $\forall\delta\,\exists\sigma$ |
| Proves | absence of bugs | presence of bugs |
| Consequence | strengthen pre, weaken post | weaken pre, strengthen post |
| False alarm possible? | yes (spurious counterexamples) | no — true positives by construction |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Incorrectness Triple and Its Semantics

The semantic definition above is the whole theory in miniature. Consider $[x = 0]\; x := x + 1\; [x = 1]$: valid, because the single result state is genuinely reachable. $[x = 0]\; x := x + 1\; [x = 2]$ is invalid: no presumption state reaches it. Note what the definition does *not* require — it says nothing about states outside $q$. The triple $[true]\; x := x + 1\; [x = 1]$ is valid too (witness $\sigma$ with $x = 0$), even though most executions do not end in $x = 1$. This is the point: the result describes *some* reachable behaviour, and if that behaviour is a bug, the bug is real.

With exit conditions, bug specifications become precise. A null-dereference analysis seeks triples of the form $[p]\,c\,[er: q]$ where $q$ describes crashed states; validity means the crash is reachable from $p$ [2]. The $ok$ channel retains the same under-approximate reading for normal outcomes, which is what lets the logic compose sequentially: $[p]\,c_1\,[ok: r]$ and $[r]\,c_2\,[\varepsilon: q]$ yield $[p]\,c_1;c_2\,[\varepsilon: q]$, because the intermediate result $r$ need only under-approximate the states flowing between the commands.

> **Theorem (Duality of approximation [1]).** For every command $c$ and assertions $p, q$: $\models \{p\}\,c\,\{q\}$ iff $\mathrm{post}(c)(p) \subseteq q$, and $\models [p]\,c\,[q]$ iff $\mathrm{post}(c)(p) \supseteq q$. The two validities coincide exactly when $\mathrm{post}(c)(p) = q$.

### 4.2 Proof Rules: Reversed Consequence, Disjunction, and Dropping

The proof system of IL mirrors Hoare logic rule-for-rule, with every approximation direction flipped [1]. The **consequence rule** is the emblematic case:

$$\frac{[p']\,c\,[q']\qquad p' \Rightarrow p\qquad q \Rightarrow q'}{[p]\,c\,[q]}\tag{Cons-IL}$$

Compare Hoare's rule: there we *strengthen* the precondition ($p \Rightarrow p'$) and *weaken* the postcondition ($q' \Rightarrow q$); here we *weaken* the presumption and *strengthen* the result. Soundness is immediate from the subset reading: $p' \subseteq p$ gives $\mathrm{post}(c)(p') \subseteq \mathrm{post}(c)(p)$, and with $\mathrm{post}(c)(p') \supseteq q' \supseteq q$ the conclusion follows.

The remaining rules, in compact form:

```text
[Empty/False Post]     [p] c [false]                    -- always valid: the empty under-approximation
[Assume]               [p ∧ b] assume(b) [p ∧ b]
[Seq]                  [p] c1 [ok: r]   [r] c2 [ε: q]
                       ───────────────────────────────
                       [p] c1;c2 [ε: q]
[Choice-Left]          [p] c1 [ε: q]
                       ─────────────────
                       [p] c1 + c2 [ε: q]
[Disjunction]          [p1] c [ε: q1]   [p2] c [ε: q2]
                       ─────────────────────────────────
                       [p1 ∨ p2] c [ε: q1 ∨ q2]
[Drop]                 [p] c [ε: q1 ∨ q2]
                       ───────────────────  (admissible)
                       [p] c [ε: q1]
```

Three rules deserve emphasis. **False Post** states that the empty result is always a valid under-approximation — the dual of Hoare's *ex falso*, and the reason IL can never prove absence. **Disjunction** combines evidence from different paths, with an existential rather than universal flavour. And **Drop** — admissible but not primitive — is the workhorse of practical bug-finding: an analysis may *discard* disjuncts, i.e. deliberately stop exploring paths, and the triple stays valid [4]. Hoare logic cannot drop disjuncts; an over-approximate analysis that ignores a path becomes unsound. This single asymmetry explains why IL, and not HL, is the natural foundation of scalable bug-catchers [3].

Loops require infinitary machinery: the iterate rule uses a possibly infinite disjunction over unrollings, where Hoare logic uses loop invariance [5]. Admissible bounded-unrolling rules recover a form of bounded model checking inside the logic [1].

### 4.3 Incorrectness Separation Logic: Local Reasoning About Bugs

Separation logic's great achievement was *local reasoning*: the frame rule lets one prove a program correct by reasoning only about the heap cells it touches. Raad et al. showed that locality survives the passage to under-approximation [2]. **Incorrectness separation logic (ISL)** works with triples $[p]\,c\,[\varepsilon: q]$ where $p, q$ are separation-logic assertions, and retains the frame rule in full:

$$\frac{[p]\,c\,[\varepsilon: q]}{[p * r]\,c\,[\varepsilon: q * r]}\tag{Frame-ISL}$$

The crucial difference from classical separation logic is the abandonment of *fault avoidance*. In Hoare-style separation logic the precondition must supply every resource the command might touch; in ISL the presumption need only describe *some* footprint, and touching an absent cell is not a proof failure but a **theorem**: the fault rules derive $[p]\,c\,[er: q]$ triples for memory errors. ISL's assertion language records deallocated cells, so that use-after-free, double-free, null dereference, and leaks each become provable erroneous outcomes [2].

> **Theorem (True positives, ISL [2]).** Every $[er: q]$ triple derived by the ISL symbolic execution denotes a genuinely reachable memory fault: there exists $\sigma \models p$ such that executing $c$ from $\sigma$ faults in a state satisfying $q$.

From the logic, Raad et al. derive a **begin-anywhere, intraprocedural symbolic execution**: the analysis can start from any program point with a partial heap description, compose summaries via the frame rule, and report faults with no false positives *by construction* — the metatheory, not testing, rules out spurious alarms [2].

### 4.4 The Algebra of Under-Approximation

Möller, O'Hearn, and Hoare embedded both logics in a single algebraic structure: a Kleene algebra with (backwards) diamond operators and countable joins of tests [5]. In this setting an incorrectness triple becomes an inequality between tests, and the two logics appear as two readings of one algebra — unifying correctness and incorrectness reasoning in a single formalism.

The algebra exposes structural facts invisible at the level of proof rules:

- **Isotony in the command.** If $[p]\,a\,[q]$ holds and $a \le a'$ (the command $a'$ has at least the behaviours of $a$), then $[p]\,a'\,[q]$ holds. Hoare triples are *anti*-isotone in the command instead: enlarging behaviour can break a correctness proof, but can only add witnesses to an incorrectness proof [5].
- **Star unfold as admissible rules.** Isotony plus Kleene star-unfold justifies unrolling rules for iteration — all of them *unsound* in Hoare logic — which is the algebraic content of bounded model checking [5].
- **Soundness and completeness.** With star unfold the rules are sound; with star induction they are complete for the image of the embedding, i.e. every valid triple is provable [5].

The algebraic view also clarifies the *falsification* frame: to refute a Hoare triple $\{p\}\,c\,\{q\}$ it suffices to prove an incorrectness triple $[p']\,c\,[\neg q]$ for a reachable $p'$ — incorrectness logic is the logic of counterexamples to correctness claims, and the algebra houses both the claim and its refutation [4][5].

### 4.5 True Positives at Scale: Pulse-X and Manifest Errors

Theory earns its keep in tools. Building on bi-abduction [6] and ISL [2], Le et al. developed **Pulse-X**, a compositional bug-catching analyser for memory errors [3]. Its design exploits exactly the features above: ISL summaries compose across procedure boundaries via the frame rule, and the Drop rule lets the analysis prune the disjunction of symbolic outcomes aggressively, keeping the search space small without endangering the true-positive guarantee [4].

The evaluation is the strongest evidence the programme has produced. Applied to large codebases, Pulse-X found **fifteen new, confirmed bugs in OpenSSL**, which were reported to the maintainers and fixed [3]. The paper introduces a compositional bug-reporting criterion based on the distinction between **latent** and **manifest** errors — roughly, errors that depend on calling context versus errors reachable regardless of it — and studies the resulting fix rates, comparing against Infer, the widely deployed industrial analyser [3].

| Analyser / logic | Foundation | Direction | Guarantee |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hoare logic | over-approximation | correctness | no missed bugs *within the proof* |
| Infer (bi-abduction) [6] | separation logic | shape analysis | compositional, heuristic reports |
| RacerD | separation logic | race detection | compositional, low false positives |
| Pulse | ISL [2] | memory errors | true positives by construction |
| Pulse-X [3] | ISL + latent/manifest criterion | memory errors at scale | 15 confirmed OpenSSL bugs, all fixed |
| Outcome Logic [4] | monadic outcomes | correctness + incorrectness | falsification theorem |

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The metatheoretic record of the programme is unusually complete for a logic motivated by tool-building:

1. **IL is sound and complete.** O'Hearn's proof system derives exactly the valid incorrectness triples (relative to the expressiveness of the assertion language) [1].
2. **ISL is sound, with a true-positive theorem.** Every erroneous triple derived by the ISL symbolic execution corresponds to a reachable fault; the begin-anywhere analysis therefore cannot produce false positives [2].
3. **The algebra is sound and complete** for the image of the embedding, unifying HL and IL derivations in one equational theory [5].
4. **Outcome Logic's falsification theorem.** Zilberstein et al. prove that any OL triple can be *disproven* within the logic: any bug invalidating a correctness specification is expressible, and OL can drop *outcomes* (though not disjuncts), preserving the scalability argument [4].
5. **Pulse-X at scale.** Fifteen new confirmed memory-safety bugs in OpenSSL, reported and fixed; a fix-rate study of the latent/manifest reporting criterion; and a head-to-head comparison with Infer demonstrating the practicality of the under-approximate approach [3].

The through-line: each layer — semantics, proof theory, algebra, tool — preserves the under-approximation invariant, so the true-positive guarantee proved for the logic transfers to the alarms a developer sees.

## 6 Limitations

The theory is honest about what it surrenders, and the surrenders are structural.

1. **False negatives are the price of true positives.** Dropping disjuncts, begin-anywhere search, and heuristic path pruning mean the analysis will miss real bugs; an empty report proves nothing [3]. IL cannot express "this program is correct" — False Post is valid for every command.
2. **Manifest errors and probabilistic programs fit awkwardly.** In the original formulation, errors that occur on *all* paths (manifest errors) and probabilistic outcomes are unnatural to express; this motivated Outcome Logic, whose monadic outcome structure handles both cleanly [4].
3. **Completeness needs infinitary constructs.** The algebraic completeness result relies on countable joins of tests and star induction [5]; automation must approximate with bounded unrolling and heuristics, reintroducing incompleteness in practice.
4. **Triage is not solved.** The latent/manifest distinction improves fix rates, but deciding which latent errors to report remains a heuristic engineering choice, and symbolic execution still faces path explosion on adversarial code [3].

## 7 Conclusion

Incorrectness logic reframes bug-finding as a *deductive* activity. The incorrectness triple $[p]\,c\,[q]$ — valid when every result state is reachable from the presumption — dualizes the Hoare triple by flipping the approximation direction; the reversed consequence rule, disjunctive rules, and sound disjunct-dropping turn that duality into a proof system; incorrectness separation logic [2] shows that spatial local reasoning survives the flip, yielding a true-positive theorem for symbolic execution; the Kleene-algebraic embedding [5] unifies correctness and incorrectness in one equational theory, with isotony-in-command and admissible unrolling as its signature laws; Pulse-X [3] demonstrates that the theory catches real bugs in real systems — fifteen confirmed in OpenSSL; and outcome logic [4] generalises the whole picture with a falsification theorem.

The presumption/falsification frame is the lasting methodological contribution: to falsify $\{p\}\,c\,\{q\}$, prove $[p']\,c\,[\neg q]$. Program correctness and incorrectness are two sides of the same coin [1] — and we finally have a logic for the side programmers actually live on.
