# Approximate Arithmetic over Encrypted Data: The CKKS Scheme, Its RNS Variant, Key Switching, Bootstrapping, and Applications to Encrypted Machine Learning

## Abstract

The Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) scheme redefined the frontier of practical homomorphic encryption by abandoning exactness: it treats the decryption noise inherent to lattice-based cryptography not as a contaminant to be suppressed, but as the least significant digits of an approximate computation. This thesis gives a unified treatment of CKKS for approximate arithmetic over complex vectors. We reconstruct the canonical-embedding encoding that packs N/2 complex slots into a single cyclotomic-ring plaintext with an isometric error bound; the rescaling procedure that tames multiplicative scale growth by descending a modulus chain; the full RNS variant that replaces multi-precision arithmetic with word-sized residue operations; key switching and relinearization with tightened noise bounds; and the bootstrapping pipeline — modulus raising, homomorphic DFT, and polynomial modular reduction — that elevates leveled CKKS to fully homomorphic status. We close with a noise-growth analysis and the scheme's flagship application: privacy-preserving machine learning inference, from CryptoNets to modern RNS-CKKS encrypted neural networks.

## 1 Introduction

Homomorphic encryption has long promised *computation on encrypted data*, yet for decades the promise came with a crippling caveat: the plaintext spaces of lattice-based schemes were discrete rings such as the integers modulo some large prime, while the computations the world actually wants — scientific simulation, statistical analysis, neural network inference — live in the continuum of real and complex numbers [1].

The 2017 ASIACRYPT paper of Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song proposed a conceptual inversion that is as elegant as it is pragmatic [1]. Every Ring-LWE-based encryption scheme already adds small noise to plaintexts for security. Rather than fighting this noise with ever-larger moduli, CKKS *hides the message in the most significant bits* of the plaintext and lets the noise occupy the least significant bits. The decryption of a CKKS ciphertext is therefore not an exact recovery of a message, but an *approximation* of it — and for numerical computation, an approximation with controllable precision is exactly what is needed.

This design decision unlocks three engineering consequences:

1. **Native fixed-point arithmetic.** Messages are vectors of complex numbers scaled by a factor \(\Delta\); addition and multiplication act slot-wise, in SIMD fashion.
2. **Rescaling.** A dedicated modulus-switching operation divides out accumulated scale after each multiplication, keeping the plaintext magnitude — and therefore the ciphertext modulus — growing only *linearly* in multiplicative depth, rather than exponentially [1].
3. **Bootstrappability.** Because the scheme natively tolerates approximation error, the notoriously delicate bootstrapping operation becomes feasible: the homomorphic decryption circuit itself can be evaluated approximately, with polynomial approximations of modular reduction [3].

The remainder of this thesis develops the scheme from first principles (Section 2), its algorithmic machinery (Section 3), the four load-bearing components in depth (Section 4), noise analysis and precision figures (Section 5), limitations (Section 6), and the outlook for encrypted machine learning (Section 7).

> **Thesis claim:** CKKS is best understood not as a "noisy" exact scheme, but as the *exact* realization of an *approximate* arithmetic: every operation's error budget is quantifiable, the rescaling operation makes the error self-limiting, and bootstrapping converts the leveled scheme into a fully homomorphic one at the cost of a few bits of precision.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lattice foundations: RLWE

CKKS inherits its security from the *Ring Learning With Errors* (RLWE) problem over the \(2N\)-th cyclotomic ring \(R = \mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^N + 1)\) with \(N = 2^k\).

The RLWE assumption states that samples of the form \((a,\, a \cdot s + e)\), with \(a\) uniform in \(R_q = R/qR\), \(s\) a small secret, and \(e\) drawn from a narrow discrete Gaussian, are computationally indistinguishable from uniform pairs. All security reductions for CKKS — at the standard 128-bit target — rest on this assumption, with parameters \((N, q)\) chosen so that the best known lattice attacks (primal and dual BKZ) exceed the target cost [1][2].

Typical instantiations use ring dimension \(N\) between \(2^{12}\) and \(2^{16}\) (fixing the slot count at \(N/2\)), a sparse ternary secret of Hamming weight \(h = 64\), discrete Gaussian error with \(\sigma \approx 3.2\), and — in the RNS variant — a ciphertext modulus factored into word-sized primes [1][2].

### 2.2 The canonical embedding and why it matters

The ring \(R\) admits \(N\) distinct embeddings into \(\mathbb{C}\), given by evaluating a polynomial at the primitive \(2N\)-th roots of unity. Pairing complex conjugates yields the **canonical embedding**

$$\sigma : R \longrightarrow \mathbb{C}^{N/2}, \qquad \sigma(m) = (m(\zeta_0), m(\zeta_1), \dots, m(\zeta_{N/2-1})),$$

where \(\zeta_j = \exp(2\pi i (2j+1)/(2N))\). The decisive property, exploited throughout CKKS, is that \(\sigma\) is an *isometric* ring homomorphism: with the appropriate norms on each side,

$$\lVert m \rVert_{\infty}^{\mathrm{can}} = \max_j |m(\zeta_j)|$$

is preserved under the embedding. In plain language: rounding a polynomial's coefficients (as encoding must) perturbs the decoded complex vector by *at most* the rounding error — the embedding does not amplify it [1]. This isometry is the mathematical bedrock on which the entire approximate-arithmetic edifice stands.

### 2.3 Leveled vs. fully homomorphic encryption

A *leveled* scheme evaluates circuits of bounded multiplicative depth \(L\): each multiplication consumes one level of the modulus chain. A *fully* homomorphic scheme removes the depth bound via **bootstrapping** — homomorphically evaluating the scheme's own decryption circuit to refresh a noisy, low-level ciphertext into a fresh, high-level one. CKKS was originally presented as a leveled scheme [1]; practical bootstrapping arrived through refinements in homomorphic modular reduction [3][6].

## 3 Methodology

We present CKKS as five algorithms plus two auxiliary operations. The exposition follows the original construction [1] with the RNS refinements of [2].

### 3.1 Encoding and decoding

Given a message vector \(z \in \mathbb{C}^{N/2}\) and a scaling factor \(\Delta > 0\):

- **Encode:** \(\mathrm{Ecd}(z; \Delta) = \lfloor \Delta \cdot \sigma^{-1}(z) \rceil \in R\). The vector is pulled back through the inverse canonical embedding, scaled up by \(\Delta\), and rounded to the nearest integer polynomial.
- **Decode:** \(\mathrm{Dcd}(m; \Delta) = \sigma(\Delta^{-1} \cdot m)\), undoing the scaling.

Because \(\sigma^{-1}\) is computed via an inverse DFT-like transform, encoding a full slot vector costs \(O(N \log N)\).

### 3.2 Key generation, encryption, decryption

- **KeyGen:** sample ternary secret \(s\) with Hamming weight \(h\); sample uniform \(a_0 \in R_{Q_L}\) and Gaussian \(e\); set the public key \(pk = (-a_0 s + e,\, a_0)\) and secret key \(sk = (1, s)\).
- **Enc:** for plaintext \(m\), sample ephemeral \(v\) and errors \(e_0, e_1\); output \(ct = (b, a) = v \cdot pk + (m + e_0,\, e_1)\).
- **Dec:** compute \(\langle ct, sk \rangle = b + a s = m + e\) in \(R_{Q_\ell}\); decode to obtain \(z + e/\Delta\).

The Haskell sketch below captures the algebraic essence of encoding:

```haskell
-- Canonical-embedding toy model: N = 8, slots = 4
type C = Complex Double

primitiveRoot :: Int -> Int -> C
primitiveRoot n j = cis (2 * pi * fromIntegral (2*j+1) / fromIntegral (2*n))

-- sigma: polynomial coeffs -> slot vector
canonicalEmbed :: [Double] -> [C]
canonicalEmbed coeffs =
  [ sum [ (c :+ 0) * primitiveRoot n j ^ k
         | (k, c) <- zip [0..] coeffs ]
  | j <- [0 .. n `div` 2 - 1] ]
  where n = length coeffs

-- Ecd(z; Delta) = round(Delta * sigma^{-1}(z))
encode :: [C] -> Double -> [Integer]
encode slots delta = map (round . (* delta)) invCoeffs
  where invCoeffs = idft (map realPart slots)  -- inverse DFT analogue
```

### 3.3 Homomorphic addition and multiplication

Addition is component-wise: \(ct_{add} = ct_1 + ct_2\), with noise adding linearly. Multiplication first forms the *tensor product* of \(ct_1 = (b_1, a_1)\) and \(ct_2 = (b_2, a_2)\):

$$(d_0, d_1, d_2) = (b_1 b_2,\; a_1 b_2 + a_2 b_1,\; a_1 a_2),$$

decryptable under \((1, s, s^2)\) at message scale \(\Delta^2\). The **rescale** operation then divides by the last modulus prime to restore scale \(\Delta\) while descending the modulus chain (Section 4.2), and **relinearization** collapses the degree-2 ciphertext back to two components decryptable under \(sk\) (Section 4.3).

### 3.4 Parameter selection discipline

Parameters are chosen *backwards* from the target computation [2]:

1. Fix the required output precision (bits) and multiplicative depth \(L\).
2. Set \(\Delta = 2^p\) for precision \(p\); each level consumes one prime \(q_i \approx \Delta\).
3. Set the top modulus \(Q_L = \prod_{i=0}^{L} q_i\), plus a special modulus \(P\) for key switching.
4. Choose \(N\) so that RLWE at modulus \(P \cdot Q_L\) meets the security target (the standard estimator tables apply).

```python
import math

def ckks_params(depth, precision_bits, security=128):
    """Choose (N, modulus_bits) for a depth-L circuit at p-bit precision."""
    log_delta = precision_bits
    # one special prime P plus (depth+1) chain primes, each ~ log_delta bits
    total_bits = log_delta * (depth + 1) + log_delta  # +P of similar size
    # ring dimension from the standard RLWE security tables (simplified)
    for N in (2**12, 2**13, 2**14, 2**15, 2**16):
        if N / math.log2(N) * 8 > total_bits:  # crude capacity heuristic
            break
    return {"N": N, "slots": N // 2, "log_delta": log_delta,
            "levels": depth + 1, "logQ": total_bits}

print(ckks_params(depth=10, precision_bits=40))
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The canonical embedding as an error isometry

The encoding step is where CKKS most visibly departs from exact schemes like BGV or BFV. In those schemes the message lives in a finite ring and noise must be *removed* by rounding; in CKKS the message lives in \(\mathbb{C}^{N/2}\) and the noise is simply *small*. The isometry property of \(\sigma\) gives a clean, checkable bound:

> **Theorem (Encoding error [1]):** Let \(z \in \mathbb{C}^{N/2}\) with \(\lVert z \rVert_\infty \le B\). Let \(m = \mathrm{Ecd}(z; \Delta)\). Then \(\lVert \mathrm{Dcd}(m; \Delta) - z \rVert_\infty \le \sqrt{N}/(2\Delta)\), provided \(\Delta \cdot B\) stays below the modulus-dependent overflow bound.

With \(N = 2^{15}\) and \(\Delta = 2^{40}\), the encoding error is below \(2^{-33}\) in relative terms — far beneath the noise injected by encryption itself. Two further subtleties deserve emphasis:

- **Conjugate symmetry.** The slot vector must satisfy \(z_{N/2-1-j} = \overline{z_j}\) for the inverse embedding to land in the real-coefficient subring.
- **Overflow discipline.** If \(\Delta \cdot \lVert z \rVert_\infty\) approaches the current modulus, wrap-around destroys the message silently. Parameter selection must therefore bound *intermediate* values, not just inputs.

### 4.2 Rescaling and the full RNS variant

Multiplication of ciphertexts at scale \(\Delta\) yields scale \(\Delta^2\), which would demand exponentially growing moduli without intervention. CKKS's answer is **rescaling** [1]:

$$\mathrm{RS}(ct) = \Big\lfloor q_\ell^{-1} \cdot ct \Big\rceil \pmod{Q_{\ell-1}},$$

where the modulus chain is \(Q_\ell = \prod_{i=0}^{\ell} q_i\). Dividing by the last prime \(q_\ell \approx \Delta\) restores the message scale to \(\Delta\) while the noise is divided by the same factor — the noise-to-signal ratio is essentially unchanged, and the rounding adds at most \(\sqrt{N}/2\) in canonical norm. The ciphertext modulus bit-length therefore grows only *linearly* in depth: this is the single operation that makes deep approximate circuits practical.

The original construction required multi-precision arithmetic over the full \(Q_\ell\), which blocked the two workhorse optimizations of lattice cryptography: the **Residue Number System** decomposition and the **Number Theoretic Transform**. The full RNS variant of Cheon et al. restructures the modulus chain so that each \(q_i\) is a word-sized prime admitting an NTT-friendly primitive root, polynomial multiplication runs limb-by-limb in \(O(N \log N)\) with no big-integer arithmetic, and rescaling becomes an *approximate* modulus-switch operating purely on residues — its tiny rounding error absorbed for free by the approximate-arithmetic model [2]. Every production CKKS deployment today implements the RNS variant.

The rescaling kernel, in the RNS representation, is a limb-wise multiply-and-round:

```rust
/// RNS rescale: divide each limb by q_last, drop the last limb.
/// ct_limbs[l][i]: limb l of component i, values mod q_l.
fn rns_rescale(ct_limbs: &mut Vec<Vec<Vec<u64>>>, q: &[u64], qinv_last: &[u64]) {
    let l = ct_limbs.len() - 1;               // index of the prime being dropped
    let ql = q[l] as u128;
    for limb in ct_limbs.iter_mut().take(l) {
        let qi = q[limb_idx(limb)] as u128;
        for (c0, c1) in limb[0].iter_mut().zip(limb[1].iter_mut()) {
            // (x - x_l * q_l^{-1} mod q_i) / q_l  -- all in 64-bit arithmetic
            let t0 = ((*c0 as u128 + ql - *c1 as u128 % ql) * qinv_last[0] as u128) % qi;
            *c0 = (t0 / ql) as u64;
        }
    }
    ct_limbs.pop(); // discard the top limb: Q_l -> Q_{l-1}
}
```

### 4.3 Key switching and relinearization

After the tensor product, a ciphertext has three components decryptable under \((1, s, s^2)\). **Relinearization** converts it back to a two-component ciphertext under \(sk = (1, s)\), using an *evaluation key* that encrypts \(P \cdot s^2\) under \(s\), where \(P\) is a special auxiliary modulus [1][2]:

$$\mathrm{evk} = (-a' s + e' + P s^2,\; a') \in R_{P Q_\ell}^2.$$

The procedure decomposes \(d_2\) in a gadget base (in RNS form, per-limb), multiplies by the evaluation key, and divides by \(P\). Choosing \(P \approx Q_\ell\) keeps the added key-switching noise comparable to a fresh encryption noise, at the cost of enlarging the maximum modulus (and hence \(N\)) [2]. **Generalized key switching** extends the same mechanism to automorphisms \(X \mapsto X^{5^k}\), producing *rotation keys* that permute SIMD slots — the primitive behind encrypted matrix transposition, convolution packing, and the homomorphic DFT inside bootstrapping.

### 4.4 Bootstrapping: homomorphic decryption

Bootstrapping refreshes a level-0 ciphertext (modulus \(q_0\), noise near the decryption threshold) into a high-level ciphertext with fresh noise budget, without ever decrypting. The CKKS bootstrapping pipeline has four stages [3][6]:

1. **ModRaise.** Lift the ciphertext from modulus \(q_0\) to the top modulus \(Q_L\). The true decryption value is \(m + q_0 \cdot I(X)\) for a small unknown integer polynomial \(I(X)\).
2. **CoeffsToSlots.** Apply a homomorphic discrete Fourier transform (via rotation keys and linear transforms) so that the *coefficients* of the polynomial become the *slots* of the ciphertext.
3. **EvalMod.** Homomorphically evaluate the modular reduction \(t \mapsto t \bmod q_0\) on each slot, approximated by composing a scaled sine approximation with high-degree polynomial corrections, evaluated with baby-step giant-step (BSGS) algorithms. The error-variance-minimizing direct approximation of [3] achieves state-of-the-art accuracy at multiplicative depth 10 for the modular reduction step, and the lazy-BSGS variant halves evaluation complexity.
4. **SlotsToCoeffs.** Apply the inverse homomorphic DFT, restoring a clean encryption of \(m\) at high level.

The price of bootstrapping is precision: the EvalMod approximation error and DFT rounding consume part of the noise budget. Classical CKKS bootstrapping delivered roughly 20 bits of fixed-point precision [6]; the minimax-Remez plus inverse-sine refinement of [6] improves precision by 5.4 to 10.2 bits per parameter setting, and OverModRaise [5] reduces the modulus the procedure consumes, leaving more levels for computation afterward.

The level bookkeeping of the whole pipeline can be stated as a machine-checkable invariant:

```tla+
---- MODULE CkksLevels ----
EXTENDS Naturals
CONSTANTS L, DepthBS          \* max levels, levels consumed by bootstrapping
VARIABLES level

Init == level \in 0..L
Mult == /\ level > 0        \* rescale consumes exactly one level
        /\ level' = level - 1
Bootstrap == /\ level = 0
             /\ level' = L - DepthBS   \* refresh, minus bootstrap's own cost
Next == Mult \/ Bootstrap
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_level
THEOREM Spec => [](level \in 0..L)
====
```

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Noise-growth accounting

The noise analysis of CKKS is unusually pleasant: every operation's error contribution is *additive* in the canonical norm, and rescaling divides both message and noise by the same factor. The standard bounds from [1], with the RNS refinements of [2]:

| Operation | Message scale | Added noise (canonical \(\infty\)-norm) |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh encryption | \(\Delta\) | \(B_{\mathrm{clean}} \approx 8\sqrt{2}\,\sigma N + 6\sigma\sqrt{N} + 16\sigma\sqrt{hN}\) |
| Addition | \(\Delta\) | \(B_1 + B_2\) (linear) |
| Tensor multiply | \(\Delta^2\) | \(\approx \Delta(B_1 + B_2) + B_1 B_2\) |
| Rescale | \(\Delta\) | \(B/ q_\ell + \sqrt{N}/2\) |
| Relinearize | \(\Delta\) | \(B + B_{\mathrm{ks}}/P\)-scale term |
| Bootstrap | \(\Delta\) | fresh-like \(B_{\mathrm{clean}}\) plus EvalMod approximation error |

The crucial row is *rescale*: after the division, the noise is essentially the *relative* noise before multiplication, plus a tiny rounding term. Noise therefore grows *linearly* in depth for leveled circuits, not exponentially — the property that separates CKKS from fixed-point encodings in exact schemes.

### 5.2 Precision figures reported in the literature

| Milestone | Reported result | Source |
|---|---|---|
| CKKS bootstrapping, baseline precision | ~20-bit fixed-point | [6] |
| Direct error-variance-minimizing EvalMod | depth 10 for modular reduction; 93-bit precision at depth 11 | [3] |
| Optimal minimax + inverse-sine bootstrapping | 5.4–10.2 bit precision improvement per parameter set | [6] |
| OverModRaise | reduced modulus consumption of bootstrapping | [5] |
| CryptoNets on MNIST | 99% accuracy; tens of thousands of predictions per hour on a single PC | [7] |
| RNS-CKKS comparison/ReLU | failure rate below \(2^{-15}\) with optimized minimax composite polynomials | [4] |

### 5.3 Correctness theorem

> **Theorem (Leveled correctness [1]):** Let \(C\) be an arithmetic circuit of multiplicative depth at most \(L\), evaluated with rescaling after every multiplication and relinearization after every tensor product. If the parameters satisfy \(\log Q_L \ge (L+1)\log \Delta + \log P + \omega(\log \lambda)\) and the inputs satisfy the overflow bound, then decryption yields \(C(z) + e\) with \(\lVert e \rVert_\infty / \Delta \le 2^{-p}\) for precision \(p\) determined by \(\Delta\) and the accumulated rounding terms.

*Proof sketch.* By induction over the circuit, with the fresh-encryption bound as the base case. The inductive step uses the rescale lemma: if \(\langle ct, sk \rangle = \Delta^2 m + e\), then \(\langle \mathrm{RS}(ct), sk \rangle = \Delta m + e'\) with \(\lVert e' \rVert \le B/q_\ell + \sqrt{N}/2\); since \(q_\ell \ge \Delta\), the relative error after rescaling is bounded by the relative error before multiplication plus \(O(\sqrt{N}/\Delta)\). Summing over \(L\) levels gives the linear-in-depth bound. ∎

## 6 Limitations

CKKS's approximate nature is a feature with sharp edges. The principal limitations, each an active research front, are:

1. **No exact primitives.** Equality tests and comparisons have no native exact form; they must be approximated by polynomials (e.g., the sign function via composite minimax approximations [4]), consuming depth and precision.
2. **Bootstrapping cost and precision loss.** Bootstrapping dominates runtime in deep workloads and each refresh sacrifices precision; precision-hungry applications must budget bootstrap placements carefully [3][6].
3. **Parameter fragility.** Overflow is silent: exceeding the modulus-dependent bound wraps the message without any error signal, so every intermediate value must be bounded *a priori*.
4. **Circuit privacy.** A CKKS ciphertext can leak information about the circuit evaluated to produce it; making approximate HE circuit-private requires injecting exponential-in-\(\lambda\) Gaussian noise, with tight lower bounds showing the parameter is essentially optimal.
5. **Non-linearities in ML.** ReLU, sigmoid, and softmax are not polynomials. Encrypted inference replaces them with low-degree surrogates (square activations in CryptoNets [7], minimax ReLU approximations [4]), trading accuracy for evaluability.
6. **Quantum and side-channel posture.** Security rests on RLWE, conjectured post-quantum but unproven; implementations face the usual lattice side-channel hazards that the theory does not cover.

## 7 Conclusion

CKKS turned a liability of lattice cryptography — its noise — into the central design element of an approximate arithmetic, creating the first practical substrate for encrypted scientific and statistical computing. The arc traced here — *isometric encoding* via the canonical embedding, *rescaling* down a modulus chain, the *full RNS variant*, *key switching* with tight noise control, and *bootstrapping* via homomorphic modular reduction — is a case study in co-designing mathematics with engineering constraints [1][2][3].

The application pull is unmistakable: from the CryptoNets demonstration of 99% MNIST accuracy without ever seeing plaintext [7], to contemporary RNS-CKKS pipelines evaluating encrypted transformers with minimax-approximated non-linearities [4]. The remaining gaps — cheaper and more precise bootstrapping [5][6], exact comparison primitives, and robust parameter automation — define the research frontier. Approximate homomorphic encryption is no longer a curiosity; it is infrastructure in the making.
