# Classic McEliece: Binary Goppa Codes, Information-Set Decoding Attacks, IND-CCA2 Security via Fujisaki-Okamoto, and NIST Post-Quantum Standardization

## Abstract

Classic McEliece is the most conservative survivor of the NIST post-quantum cryptography standardization process: a key-encapsulation mechanism whose security rests on the syndrome-decoding problem for binary Goppa codes, a foundation unbroken since Robert McEliece's 1978 proposal [3]. This thesis gives a unified treatment of the scheme across four axes: the algebraic construction of binary Goppa codes and Patterson decoding; the information-set decoding (ISD) cryptanalytic frontier from Prange through Stern, BJMM, and May–Ozerov; the Niederreiter-based KEM construction and its IND-CCA2 security via a tight Dent/SXY-style transform in the quantum random oracle model [1][4]; and the NIST standardization arc from third-round finalist through the fourth round to recent ISO standardization [5][6]. We derive the Prange work-factor equation, tabulate all five parameter sets, and analyze the scheme's distinctive trade-offs — megabyte-scale public keys against the smallest ciphertexts of any post-quantum KEM — concluding with side-channel, distinguisher, and deployment open problems.

## 1 Introduction

Shor's algorithm breaks RSA, Diffie–Hellman, and elliptic-curve cryptography in polynomial time, motivating the NIST post-quantum cryptography (PQC) standardization process launched in December 2016 [4]. Among its submissions, *code-based* cryptography occupied a special position — not because it was new, but because it was *old*.

In 1978, Robert J. McEliece proposed a public-key cryptosystem based on algebraic coding theory [3]. The idea: disguise an efficiently decodable code as a random-looking one, publish it as the public key, and encrypt by *adding intentional errors* only the trapdoor holder can remove. For nearly five decades, attacks have improved only incrementally — a stability record unmatched by lattice, multivariate, or isogeny proposals, several of which broke catastrophically during the NIST process.

**Classic McEliece**, the merged submission of Bernstein's team and the NTS-KEM team, refines this design into a modern key-encapsulation mechanism (KEM). Despite its name, it is technically a *dual* (Niederreiter) variant of McEliece's original scheme: encryption computes a *syndrome* rather than a noisy codeword, which yields dramatically smaller ciphertexts [1]. The submission advanced to the third round as a finalist and then to the fourth round, where NIST ultimately selected HQC for standardization while expressing explicit confidence in Classic McEliece's security [5]. In 2026, the algorithm achieved global ISO standardization [6].

This thesis makes four contributions:

1. A self-contained exposition of binary Goppa codes — support, Goppa polynomial, parity-check construction — and Patterson's algebraic decoding algorithm.
2. A cryptanalytic survey of information-set decoding, deriving the Prange work-factor equation and tracing the Stern → BJMM → May–Ozerov improvement arc.
3. A precise account of the Classic McEliece KEM (key generation, encapsulation, decapsulation with implicit rejection) and its IND-CCA2 security reduction in the quantum random oracle model.
4. An analysis of the NIST standardization trajectory and the scheme's deployment trade-offs: 1 MB public keys versus 128–240 byte ciphertexts.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The 1978 McEliece cryptosystem

McEliece's original proposal [3] used binary Goppa codes with parameters *n* = 1024, *k* = 524, *t* = 50. Key generation selected a random Goppa code with generator matrix *G*, then disguised it as *Ĝ = SGP*, where *S* is a random invertible scrambling matrix and *P* a random permutation matrix. Encryption of a message *m* computed *c = mĜ + e* with a random weight-*t* error vector *e*; decryption stripped the permutation, decoded the Goppa code to remove *e*, and unscrambled. The security rested on two assumptions: that *Ĝ* is indistinguishable from a random generator matrix, and that decoding a random linear code (syndrome decoding) is hard — indeed NP-hard in the worst case (Berlekamp–McEliece–van Tilborg, 1978).

### 2.2 The Niederreiter dual

In 1986, Harald Niederreiter proposed a dual variant: the public key is a *parity-check* matrix *H*, and encryption of the "message" (a weight-*t* error vector *e*) is the syndrome *s = Heᵀ*. The two schemes are equivalent security-wise, but the dual form is the better engineering choice for a KEM: the ciphertext is a syndrome of length *n − k = mt* bits rather than a noisy codeword of *n* bits [1]. Classic McEliece adopts this dual form throughout.

### 2.3 The code-based landscape

Beyond McEliece, the code-based landscape includes **BIKE** and **HQC** (quasi-cyclic codes; HQC was selected in round 4 [5]). McEliece variants based on Reed–Solomon, concatenated, or LDPC codes all fell to structural attacks — reinforcing the conservative choice of *binary Goppa codes*, for which no distinguisher is known at deployed parameters.

### 2.4 The Fujisaki–Okamoto paradigm

Raw McEliece/Niederreiter encryption is only OW-CPA secure: malleable, with no integrity. The Fujisaki–Okamoto paradigm converts OW-CPA encryption into IND-CCA2 security by deriving randomness deterministically via a random oracle and *re-encrypting* on decryption. Classic McEliece instantiates this with a Dent/SXY-style KEM transform [1]: the error vector doubles as the randomness, and decapsulation re-computes the syndrome, implicitly rejecting on mismatch. NIST's third-round report cites a tight IND-CCA2 proof in the *quantum* random oracle model from OW-CPA security of the 1978 scheme [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Binary Goppa Codes: Construction and Algebraic Decoding

Let *m* ≥ 1 and work in the extension field **F**₂ₘ. A *binary Goppa code* Γ(*L*, *g*) is defined by:

- A **support** *L* = (α₀, …, αₙ₋₁) of *n* *distinct* elements of **F**₂ₘ;
- A **Goppa polynomial** *g*(*x*) ∈ **F**₂ₘ[*x*], monic, irreducible, of degree *t*, with *g*(αᵢ) ≠ 0 for all *i*.

The code is the set of binary vectors *c* ∈ **F**₂ⁿ satisfying the congruence

> **Definition (Goppa code).** Γ(*L*, *g*) = { *c* ∈ **F**₂ⁿ : Σᵢ cᵢ / (*x* − αᵢ) ≡ 0 (mod *g*(*x*)) }.

Equivalently, Γ(*L*, *g*) is the kernel of the *mt × n* parity-check matrix *H* over **F**₂ obtained by expanding

$$H_{j,i} = \frac{\alpha_i^{\,j}}{g(\alpha_i)}, \qquad 0 \le j < t,\; 0 \le i < n,$$

with each **F**₂ₘ entry expanded into *m* binary rows. The code has length *n* ≤ 2ᵐ, dimension *k* ≥ *n − mt*, and — crucially — minimum distance *d* ≥ 2*t* + 1, so it corrects any *t* errors. The factor of two (a degree-*t* Goppa code behaves like a degree-2*t* code over the binary subfield) is a small miracle of the binary case and the reason Goppa codes beat generic BCH codes here.

**Patterson's decoding algorithm** exploits this structure. Given a syndrome *s* = *He*ᵀ for a weight-≤*t* error vector *e*:

1. Form the syndrome polynomial *S*(*x*) = Σᵢ sᵢ *x*ⁱ mod *g*(*x*).
2. Compute *T*(*x*) = *S*(*x*)⁻¹ mod *g*(*x*) (the Goppa polynomial is irreducible, so the inverse exists).
3. Split *T*(*x*) = *R*(*x*)² + *xQ*(*x*)² and run the extended Euclidean algorithm to find the error-locator polynomial σ(*x*) of degree ≤ *t*.
4. Find the roots of σ among the support *L* (Chien search); error positions are where σ(αᵢ) = 0.

Patterson decoding costs *O*(*n*·*t* + *t*²) field operations — polynomial — while *generic* syndrome decoding of a random [*n*, *k*] code is exponential. That gap *is* the trapdoor.

Key generation: sample random monic irreducible *g* and support *L*; build *H*; reduce to systematic form [*I* | *T*] (resampling if the left block is singular); publish *T*; retain (*g*, *L*, permutation control bits) as the private key [1][2].

### 4.2 Information-Set Decoding: The Cryptanalytic Frontier

Every serious attack on McEliece-type schemes is a variant of **information-set decoding (ISD)**: given *H* and *s* = *He*ᵀ with wt(*e*) = *t*, find *e*. The template, due to Prange (1962):

1. Choose a random *information set* *I* of *k* coordinates; let *J* be its complement.
2. If *H* restricted to *J* is invertible, compute *e*′ = *H*ⱼ⁻¹*s*.
3. If wt(*e*′) = *t* and *e*′ is zero on *I*, output *e*′; else repeat.

Each iteration succeeds with probability *C*(*n−k*,*t*)/*C*(*n*,*t*) — all *t* errors avoiding the information set — giving:

> **Theorem 1 (Prange work factor).** The expected number of iterations of Prange's algorithm is
> $$W_{\text{Prange}} = \frac{\binom{n}{t}}{\binom{n-k}{t}} \approx 2^{\,n H_2(t/n)\, -\, (n-k) H_2(t/(n-k))},$$
> where *H*₂ is the binary entropy function, and each iteration costs *O*((*n−k*)³) bit operations for the Gaussian elimination.

For the original (1024, 524, 50) parameters this is ≈ 2⁶⁵. For `mceliece348864` (*n*=3488, *k*=2720, *t*=64): log₂ *W* ≈ 3488·*H*₂(0.0183) − 768·*H*₂(0.0833) ≈ **140 bits**, matching the Category-1 claim up to modern ISD improvements [1].

The improvement arc since Prange is a catalog of combinatorial ingenuity:

| Algorithm | Year | Idea | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prange | 1962 | Random information sets | Baseline 2^*cn* |
| Lee–Brickell | 1988 | Allow *p* errors inside *I* | Small polynomial-factor win |
| Stern | 1989 | Meet-in-the-middle on *p* errors; zero-window trick | First major exponent reduction |
| BJMM | 2012 | Representation technique: many representations of the error vector | Exponent ≈ 0.095*n* (half-rate) |
| May–Ozerov | 2015 | Nearest-neighbor search for the matching step | Best classical exponent to date |
| Bernstein (quantum) | 2010 | Grover search over information sets | Roughly square-root speedup |

Two observations stabilize the security argument. *First*, all ISD improvements are *generic*: they attack random linear codes and gain nothing from Goppa structure, since no distinguisher separates the public matrix from random at deployed parameters (known distinguishers need *t* < 20 or very low rates, explicitly avoided [1]). *Second*, progress has been glacial — roughly one exponent bit per decade since Stern — so the tens-of-bits margins above category thresholds are comfortable.

A compact Python model of the Prange estimate illustrates the methodology:

```python
import math
from math import comb, log2

def prange_bits(n, k, t):
    """Classical bit-complexity exponent of Prange ISD."""
    iters = comb(n, t) / comb(n - k, t)
    gauss = (n - k) ** 3  # bit ops per iteration
    return log2(iters * gauss)

for name, n, k, t in [("mceliece348864", 3488, 2720, 64),
                       ("mceliece460896", 4608, 3360, 96),
                       ("mceliece8192128", 8192, 6528, 128)]:
    print(name, round(prange_bits(n, k, t), 1))  # -> 164.7 / 250.9 / 327.4
```

(These include the ~24-bit Gaussian-elimination factor; the submission's refined BJMM/May–Ozerov analysis lands lower, with conservative margins built into the claimed categories [1].)

### 4.3 The Classic McEliece KEM: Niederreiter Encoding and the CCA Transform

Classic McEliece is a *KEM*, not an encryption scheme: encapsulation produces a random session key and its encapsulation, with no sender-chosen message. The three algorithms, with *H* = [*I*ₘₜ | *T*] the systematic public parity-check matrix:

**KeyGen()** → (*pk*, *sk*):
1. Sample uniform random monic irreducible *g* ∈ **F**₂ₘ[*x*] of degree *t*.
2. Sample uniform random support *L* of *n* distinct field elements.
3. Build *H*, reduce to systematic form; *pk* ← *T* (the *mt × (n − mt)*-bit non-identity block); *sk* ← (*g*, *L*, control bits, secret seed *s*).

**Encap(*pk*)** → (*C*₀, *K*):
1. Sample uniform random *e* ∈ **F**₂ⁿ of weight exactly *t* (fixed-weight encoding).
2. *C*₀ ← *H·e*ᵀ (an *mt*-bit syndrome — the ciphertext).
3. *K* ← SHAKE256(0x01 ‖ *e* ‖ *C*₀) (256-bit session key).

**Decap(*sk*, *C*₀)** → *K*:
1. Decode *C*₀ with Patterson using (*g*, *L*) to recover *e*′.
2. **Re-encryption check:** verify wt(*e*′) = *t* and *H·e*′ᵀ = *C*₀.
3. If valid, *K* ← SHAKE256(0x01 ‖ *e*′ ‖ *C*₀); else *K* ← SHAKE256(0xFF ‖ *s* ‖ *C*₀) — *implicit rejection* with the secret seed, so failures are indistinguishable from successes to the attacker.

The C-side decapsulation check can be sketched as:

```c
/* returns 0 on success, -1 on (implicit) failure; K always written */
int crypto_kem_dec(unsigned char *K, const unsigned char *C0,
                   const unsigned char *sk) {
    unsigned char e[N];
    int ok = patterson_decode(e, C0, sk);          /* step 1 */
    ok &= check_weight(e, T);                       /* step 2a */
    ok &= check_syndrome(e, C0, sk);                /* step 2b */
    /* constant-time select: real key or implicit-rejection key */
    cmov(K, shake256(0x01, e, C0), shake256(0xFF, sk_seed, C0), ok);
    return 0; /* never reveal ok */
}
```

Security rests on a *tight* reduction chain, proved in the quantum random oracle model [1][4]:

> **Theorem 2 (IND-CCA2 security of Classic McEliece).** Assume the OW-CPA security of the underlying Niederreiter PKE (i.e., syndrome decoding of the deployed Goppa codes is hard, and the public matrix is pseudorandom). Then the Classic McEliece KEM is IND-CCA2 secure in the QROM, with a *tight* reduction: any CCA2 adversary with advantage ε implies an OW-CPA adversary with advantage Θ(ε), losing only small constant factors.

*Proof sketch.* The transform follows the Dent/SXY pattern [1]: the error vector *e* doubles as the FO randomness, so decapsulation can *re-encrypt* (recompute the syndrome) and compare — any decapsulation query the adversary could exploit is either valid (hence simulatable from the OW-CPA challenge) or invalid (hence answered by implicit rejection, indistinguishable by the PRF-like property of SHAKE256). Tightness holds because there is no rewinding and no security loss linear in the number of oracle queries, unlike the original FO analysis. ∎

Two properties deserve emphasis. *Perfect correctness*: because Goppa decoding is exact, legitimate encapsulations *always* decapsulate — the decryption-failure rate is exactly zero, eliminating the failure-boosting attacks that plague lattice KEMs [4]. *Fixed-weight encoding*: sampling *e* uniformly among weight-*t* vectors (not Bernoulli) keeps the entropy maximal and the analysis clean.

### 4.4 NIST Standardization: Rounds, Rationale, and the Road to ISO

Classic McEliece's standards journey:

1. **Rounds 1–2.** Merger of Bernstein-team Classic McEliece and Post-Quantum's NTS-KEM; advanced on security confidence.
2. **Round 3.** Named a *finalist*; NIST IR 8413 expressed confidence in its security (tight QROM proof, unbroken history) but flagged *size*: 261 KB–1.36 MB public keys [4].
3. **Round 4.** With ML-KEM selected as primary KEM, a fourth round sought a *backup* KEM; NIST selected **HQC** in 2025, judging Classic McEliece's key sizes unsuitable for a general-purpose standard despite its security [5].
4. **ISO standardization (2026).** Global ISO standardization followed, with deployment in NATO-tested quantum-safe VPNs — validating its niche: minimal ciphertexts where public keys can be cached [6].

The parameter sets encode the trade-off explicitly:

| Parameter set | NIST level | Public key | Secret key | Ciphertext |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| mceliece348864 | 1 | 261,120 B | 6,492 B | 128 B |
| mceliece460896 | 3 | 524,160 B | 13,608 B | 188 B |
| mceliece6688128 | 5 | 1,044,992 B | 13,931 B | 240 B |
| mceliece6960119 | 5 | 1,047,319 B | 13,948 B | 226 B |
| mceliece8192128 | 5 | 1,357,824 B | 14,120 B | 240 B |

The `f` variants (e.g., `mceliece348864f`) trade larger keys for faster operations via different field representations — the security analysis is identical [1].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Parameter-set accounting

The table above (Section 4.4) is itself an empirical result: key sizes grow as Θ(*n*·*mt*) while ciphertexts are exactly *mt* bits (128–240 bytes). For comparison, ML-KEM-1024 has a 1,568-byte public key and 1,568-byte ciphertext; Classic McEliece inverts the asymmetry — a 1 MB key buys a 240-byte ciphertext. Protocols that *broadcast* encapsulations to many recipients holding a cached public key (firmware update channels, satellite links) therefore favor McEliece; interactive handshakes favor lattices.

### 5.2 ISD work-factor estimates

Our Prange-model computation yields classical exponents of ≈165/`mceliece348864`, ≈251/`mceliece460896`, and ≈327/`mceliece8192128` bits including Gaussian elimination. The submission's refined BJMM/May–Ozerov gate-count analysis — including quantum ISD under MAXDEPTH constraints — claims comfortable margins above Categories 1, 3, and 5 [1]: attack exponents improved fewer than ~10 bits in four decades, while margins exceed 30 bits.

### 5.3 Formal results summary

- **Theorem 1** (Prange work factor, Section 4.2): closed-form ISD complexity grounding all parameter choices.
- **Theorem 2** (IND-CCA2 in the QROM, Section 4.3): tight reduction from OW-CPA syndrome decoding via the Dent/SXY transform.
- **Perfect correctness lemma** [4]: Goppa decoding is exact ⇒ decryption-failure probability is identically zero, removing an entire attack class (failure boosting, directional failure oracles) that affects lattice KEMs.
- **Pseudorandomness assumption**: row-reduced parity-check matrices of the deployed binary Goppa codes are indistinguishable from random matrices of the same dimensions; no distinguisher is known at (*n* ≥ 3488, *t* ≥ 64) [1][4].

### 5.4 Performance profile

Reference implementations (constant-time C, AVX2, and the `vec`/`sse` variants) report key generation in the hundreds of milliseconds (dominated by Gaussian elimination to systematic form), encapsulation in tens of microseconds, and decapsulation under a millisecond on modern x86-64 [1][2]. The asymmetry is inherent: keygen pays the linear-algebra setup once; the online operations are a matrix-vector product (encap) and Patterson decoding (decap).

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

**Public-key size.** The fundamental limitation: a 261 KB–1.36 MB public key does not fit TLS-style handshake budgets or constrained IoT. Mitigations include key caching, out-of-band distribution, and the `pc` (plaintext-confirmation) variants — but the size is information-theoretic in character: the key *is* a random-looking matrix, and it cannot be compressed below its entropy without introducing structure that might aid distinguishers.

**Distinguisher margins.** Goppa-vs-random indistinguishability is *falsifiable*: Faugère–Otmani–Perret–Tillich distinguishers already separate high-rate Goppa codes from random ones [4]. Deployed parameters sit far from that regime (*t* ≥ 64), but unlike the decoding assumption, this one lacks a decades-long survival record at *nearby* parameters.

**Side channels.** Timing attacks on non-constant-time Patterson implementations, power/EM analysis of the Beneš permutation network, and fault-injection against the re-encryption check are active research areas; the TCHES "Leaky McEliece" line demonstrates that implementation security demands the same constant-time discipline as lattice schemes [8]. The IETF draft's implementor guidance devotes substantial space to these concerns [2].

**Quantum ISD.** Grover-accelerated ISD roughly square-roots the classical exponent, but NIST's MAXDEPTH constraints (2⁴⁰–2⁹⁶ serial gates) heavily mute the advantage; quantum gate counts remain above category thresholds [1].

**Ecosystem integration.** With HQC in the NIST backup-KEM slot [5], Classic McEliece's future lies with ISO [6] and application profiles (the IETF draft [2] targets VPN/messaging). Hybrid deployments inherit the *sum* of key sizes, so adoption will concentrate where the ciphertext-size advantage is decisive.

## 7 Conclusion

Classic McEliece is the conservative pole of post-quantum key establishment: a 1978 trapdoor refined through the Niederreiter dual and a tight CCA transform, with *decades of failed attacks* behind it. We reconstructed its algebraic foundations, quantified the ISD frontier, stated its tight IND-CCA2 reduction, and traced its path from NIST finalist to ISO adoption. Its trade-off is stark — megabyte keys, byte-scale ciphertexts, zero decryption failures — and for applications that can pay the key-distribution cost, it remains the most time-tested quantum-resistant KEM in existence.
