# Tidal Disruption Events: Loss-Cone Dynamics, Relativistic Pericenter Precession, and the Optical-to-X-Ray Emission Budget of Shredded Stars

## Abstract

A star that wanders within the tidal radius \(r_t\) of a supermassive black hole (SMBH) is torn apart, launching roughly half its mass onto bound orbits that return to the hole at a rate \(\dot{M} \propto t^{-5/3}\). This thesis synthesizes the dynamical pipeline feeding such tidal disruption events (TDEs): the loss-cone refilling of stellar orbits in galactic nuclei, the hydrodynamics of disruption as a function of the penetration factor \(\beta\), and the role of relativistic apsidal precession in forcing debris-stream self-intersection and circularization. We show how these stages imprint on the optical-to-X-ray emission budget: soft X-rays trace prompt accretion near the horizon, while optical/UV flares arise from reprocessing in an optically thick outflow or from self-intersection shocks themselves. Using calibrated analytic scalings, hydrodynamical fallback calculations, and multi-wavelength observations of the archetypal optical/UV event ASASSN-14li and the relativistic jetted event Swift J1644+57, we bound the radiative efficiency, the circularization timescale, and the black-hole mass window \(10^5\)–\(10^8\,M_\odot\) within which TDEs remain observable, and identify precisely where current models fail.

## 1 Introduction

Most supermassive black holes in the local universe are dormant. They emit little light, betraying neither their mass nor their spin, until a star strays too close and is shredded. The resulting **tidal disruption event** (TDE) converts a fraction of a solar mass of stellar debris into a months-to-years-long flare peaking in the extreme ultraviolet and soft X-rays [1][2]. Because the flare luminosity can reach \(\sim 10^{44}\,{\rm erg\,s^{-1}}\) and decay with the theoretically predicted \(t^{-5/3}\) law, TDEs have become the premier probe of otherwise quiescent SMBHs, offering measurements of black hole mass, stellar populations in galactic nuclei, and accretion physics in the super-Eddington regime.

Yet the path from a star on a nearly parabolic orbit to a luminous flare is anything but simple. Three distinct physical regimes must be bridged. *First*, stars must be scattered into disruptive orbits — the **loss-cone problem** — which sets the event rate \(\dot{N} \sim 10^{-4}\)–\(10^{-5}\,{\rm yr^{-1}}\) per galaxy and its dependence on nuclear structure [3]. *Second*, the disruption itself is a hydrodynamical process governed by the penetration factor \(\beta = r_t/r_p\) and the star's internal structure; grazing encounters produce **partial** disruptions with fallback rates steeper than \(t^{-5/3}\), while deep encounters are increasingly relativistic [4]. *Third*, the bound debris does not obligingly circularize: relativistic **apsidal precession** winds the returning stream into self-intersection shocks, and the efficiency with which that shock heat is radiated or advected determines the entire **emission budget** — the partition of \(\sim 10^{51}\)–\(10^{52}\) erg among optical, UV, X-ray, and outflow kinetic channels [5][6].

> **Theorem (Frozen-in fallback):** Under the frozen-in approximation, in which the specific binding energy of each debris element is fixed at the moment of disruption, the late-time mass fallback rate of a complete disruption asymptotes to \(\dot{M} \propto t^{-5/3}\), independent of the stellar structure, with the normalization set by \((dM/dE)|_{E=0}\).

This thesis develops a unified quantitative account of these three stages, derives the observable consequences, and confronts them with the best-studied events in the literature.

## 2 Background

### The tidal radius and the Hills criterion

A star of mass \(M_\star\) and radius \(R_\star\) is disrupted when the tidal acceleration across its diameter exceeds its self-gravity.

$$r_t \simeq R_\star\left(\frac{M_\bullet}{M_\star}\right)^{1/3} \approx 7\times 10^{12}\,{\rm cm}\left(\frac{M_\bullet}{10^6\,M_\odot}\right)^{1/3}\left(\frac{M_\star}{M_\odot}\right)^{-1/3}\left(\frac{R_\star}{R_\odot}\right).$$

Crucially, \(r_t\) scales as \(M_\bullet^{1/3}\) while the Schwarzschild radius \(r_s = 2GM_\bullet/c^2\) scales linearly with \(M_\bullet\).

### The loss cone

Only stars whose angular momentum falls below \(J_{lc}^2 \simeq 2GM_\bullet r_t\) can reach the tidal radius.

$$\dot{N} \sim 10^{-4}\,{\rm yr^{-1}}\left(\frac{M_\bullet}{10^6\,M_\odot}\right)^{-0.25},$$

with order-of-magnitude scatter driven by nuclear star-cluster density, triaxiality (which refills the cone far faster than two-body relaxation), and the presence of a binary SMBH.

### Disruption hydrodynamics: the role of \(\beta\)

The **penetration factor** \(\beta \equiv r_t/r_p\) measures how deeply the star plunges.

- **Complete disruptions** (\(\beta \gtrsim \beta_c\)): \(\sim M_\star/2\) remains bound, and the fallback rate converges to the canonical \(\dot{M} \propto t^{-5/3}\) at late times, with peak timescale \(t_{\rm peak} \sim 30\)–\(80\) days for \(M_\bullet = 10^6\,M_\odot\).
- **Partial disruptions** (\(\beta < \beta_c\)): the surviving core's gravity perturbs the debris energy distribution, steepening the fallback to \(\dot{M} \propto t^{-n}\) with \(n \approx 2.2\) and reducing the total bound mass [4].

Realistic stellar structures from MESA models shift \(t_{\rm peak}\) from \(\sim 30\) days (zero-age main sequence) to \(\sim 54\) days (terminal-age main sequence) for a solar-mass star, and imprint compositional anomalies — nitrogen and helium enrichment — on the debris *before* peak fallback, offering a spectroscopic clock for the disrupted star's evolutionary state [8].

### The \(t^{-5/3}\) fallback law

At disruption, the star's center of mass follows a nearly parabolic orbit while its leading and trailing edges acquire a spread in specific binding energy

$$\Delta E \simeq \frac{GM_\bullet R_\star}{r_t^2}.$$

The most bound debris returns after one orbital period,

$$t_{\rm min} = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\,GM_\bullet\,\Delta E^{-3/2} \approx 41\,{\rm days}\left(\frac{M_\bullet}{10^6\,M_\odot}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{M_\star}{M_\odot}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{R_\star}{R_\odot}\right)^{3/2},$$

and if \(dM/dE\) is approximately constant near \(E=0\), Kepler's third law gives the celebrated result \(\dot{M} = (dM/dE)(dE/dt) \propto t^{-5/3}\) [1][4].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis combines three complementary approaches: analytic loss-cone and orbital theory, calibrated results from published hydrodynamical simulation suites, and a multi-wavelength energy-budget accounting applied to observed events.

### 3.1 Loss-cone rate integrals

We evaluate the steady-state TDE rate by integrating the orbit-averaged Fokker–Planck flux over energy and angular momentum,

$$\dot{N} = \int_{E} \int_{J < J_{lc}(E)} \mathcal{F}(E,J)\,dE\,dJ,$$

distinguishing the *pinhole* regime (where \(\Delta J \gg J_{lc}\) per orbit and the cone is full) from the *diffusive* regime (where it is empty and the rate is logarithmically suppressed).

### 3.2 Fallback-curve synthesis

Rather than re-running hydrodynamics, we build fallback curves from the \(dM/dE\) distributions tabulated by Guillochon & Ramirez-Ruiz across \(\beta\) and polytropic index \(\gamma\), mapped to physical time via

$$\dot{M}(t) = \frac{dM}{dE}\,\frac{2\pi GM_\bullet}{(2t)^{5/3}}\cdot\frac{1}{3t^{2/3}}\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;\dot{M} \propto t^{-5/3}\left.\frac{dM}{dE}\right|_{E(t)}.$$

The following Python reference implementation computes the fallback curve for a complete disruption and identifies the super-Eddington phase:

```python
import numpy as np

G, MSUN, RSUN, DAY = 6.674e-8, 1.989e33, 6.957e10, 86400.0
C = 2.99792458e10

def fallback_rate(t_days, Mbh=1e6, Mstar=1.0, Rstar=1.0, beta=1.0):
    """Canonical t^-5/3 fallback for a complete disruption (frozen-in)."""
    Mb, Ms, Rs = Mbh*MSUN, Mstar*MSUN, Rstar*RSUN
    rt = Rs*(Mb/Ms)**(1/3)                       # tidal radius
    dE = G*Mb*Rs/rt**2                           # energy spread
    t_min = np.pi/np.sqrt(2)*G*Mb*dE**-1.5/DAY   # days, most-bound return
    Mdot_peak = (Ms/3)/ (t_min*DAY)               # ~Mstar/3 / t_min
    t = np.asarray(t_days, dtype=float)
    Mdot = np.where(t >= t_min,
                    Mdot_peak*(t/t_min)**(-5/3), 0.0)
    return Mdot  # g/s

def eddington_ratio(Mdot_gs, Mbh=1e6, eta=0.1):
    Ledd = 1.26e38*Mbh
    return eta*Mdot_gs*C**2/Ledd

t = np.logspace(np.log10(41), 3, 200)
md = fallback_rate(t)
print(f"t_min ~ 41 d; peak Mdot/Mdot_Edd ~ {eddington_ratio(md.max()):.1f}")
# -> super-Eddington by factor ~10^2 for the first months
```

For \(M_\bullet = 10^6\,M_\odot\) the fallback is super-Eddington by two orders of magnitude at peak, remaining so for roughly a year — the engine of the powerful outflows seen in radio observations [9].

### 3.3 Relativistic stream dynamics

Newtonian ellipses are a poor description when the pericenter \(r_p\) approaches a few \(r_s\).

$$\Delta\phi \simeq \frac{6\pi GM_\bullet}{c^2 a(1-e^2)} = \frac{3\pi r_s}{r_p}\quad\text{(weak-field limit)},$$

which reaches \(\sim \pi\) for deeply plunging orbits, forcing the outbound stream to collide with the infalling one.

### 3.4 Emission-budget accounting

The total available energy is bounded by the rest mass of the bound debris, \(E_{\rm tot} \lesssim 0.5\,\eta\,M_\star c^2\) with radiative efficiency \(\eta \sim 0.01\)–\(0.1\).

1. **Soft X-ray** (0.2–2 keV): prompt disk accretion near \(\sim 10\,r_s\), \(T \sim 10^5\)–\(10^6\) K.
2. **Optical/UV blackbody**: \(T \sim 2\)–\(4\times 10^4\) K, radius \(\sim 10^{14}\)–\(10^{15}\) cm — too large and too cool for a bare disk, requiring reprocessing or shock power.
3. **Outflow kinetic**: radio synchrotron from the unbound ejecta or a disk wind shocking the circumnuclear medium, \(E_K \sim 10^{48}\)–\(10^{49}\) erg [9].
4. **Unobserved EUV**: the "missing energy" — most of a \(T \sim 10^5\) K disk spectrum peaks where no telescope observes.

A Rust model of the reprocessing envelope illustrates the radius problem quantitatively:

```rust
// Reprocessing photosphere: L = 4 pi R^2 sigma T^4  =>  R from (L, T)
fn photosphere_radius_cm(luminosity_erg_s: f64, temp_k: f64) -> f64 {
    const SIGMA: f64 = 5.670374419e-5; // erg cm^-2 s^-1 K^-4
    (luminosity_erg_s / (4.0 * std::f64::consts::PI * SIGMA * temp_k.powi(4))).sqrt()
}

fn main() {
    // ASASSN-14li-like: L ~ 1e44 erg/s, T ~ 35,000 K
    let r = photosphere_radius_cm(1e44, 35_000.0);
    println!("R_ph ~ {:.2e} cm (~{:.0} AU)", r, r / 1.496e13);
    // -> ~1e15 cm, ~100x the tidal radius: a bare disk cannot do this.
}
```

The photosphere sits at \(\sim 10^{15}\) cm, two orders of magnitude beyond \(r_t\) — a geometric proof that the optical emission is *not* direct disk light, but reprocessed radiation or shock emission from an extended envelope [6][7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Loss-cone dynamics and the rate discrepancy

The loss cone is refilled by two-body relaxation on the timescale \(t_r\), but the TDE rate is dominated by stars near the critical energy where the cone transitions from full to empty.

- **Dust obscuration** hiding nuclear transients in star-forming hosts;
- **Partial disruptions** producing dimmer, faster flares that fail survey selection cuts;
- **Triaxiality and nuclear star clusters**, which *raise* the theoretical rate and worsen the discrepancy unless selection effects dominate;
- **The Hills cutoff**: above \(10^8\,M_\odot\), disruptions are dark, so the observed sample is biased toward \(10^6\)–\(10^7\,M_\odot\) hosts, consistent with measured host-galaxy velocity dispersions.

| Regime | Condition | Rate scaling | Dominant stars |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pinhole (full cone) | \(\Delta J > J_{lc}\) | \(\dot{N} \propto M_\bullet^{-0.25}\) | Large-\(r\) orbits |
| Diffusive (empty cone) | \(\Delta J < J_{lc}\) | \(\propto 1/\ln(J_c/J_{lc})\) | Near-hole orbits |
| Triaxial/chaotic | Non-axisymmetric | Boost \(\times 2\)–\(5\) | Centrophilic orbits |
| Post-starburst hosts | Dense nuclei | Boost \(\times 10\)–\(100\) | Observed overabundance |

Post-starburst (E+A) galaxies are overrepresented among TDE hosts by an order of magnitude — a clue that nuclear stellar density, and hence loss-cone flux, is the controlling variable [7].

### 4.2 Relativistic pericenter precession and stream self-intersection

For a solar-type star disrupted by a \(10^6\,M_\odot\) black hole at \(\beta = 1\), \(r_p \approx 23\,r_s\) and the weak-field precession is \(\Delta\phi \approx 0.4\) rad per orbit — modest.

$$\dot{E}_{si} \sim \frac{1}{2}\dot{M}v_{\rm rel}^2 \sim 10^{44}\,{\rm erg\,s^{-1}}\left(\frac{\dot{M}}{1\,M_\odot\,{\rm yr^{-1}}}\right)\left(\frac{v_{\rm rel}}{0.1c}\right)^2.$$

This is comparable to observed optical luminosities, which is why self-intersection shocks are a leading candidate for the optical power source [5].

> **Theorem (Precession-driven circularization):** If \(\Delta\phi \gtrsim \pi/2\) at the pericenter of the most-bound debris, stream self-intersection dissipates orbital energy on a timescale shorter than the fallback time, and the debris circularizes near \(r_{si} \sim 2r_p\); if \(\Delta\phi \ll 1\), circularization is delayed by many fallback times and the flare is powered by fallback-modulated shocks instead.

### 4.3 The optical-to-X-ray emission budget

The central observational puzzle is the **dichotomy**: some TDEs are X-ray bright with \(L_X \sim 10^{44}\,{\rm erg\,s^{-1}}\) and weak optical emission; most optically selected events show \(L_{\rm opt} \sim 10^{43}\)–\(10^{44}\,{\rm erg\,s^{-1}}\) with a constant \(T \sim 3\times 10^4\) K blackbody and only faint, slowly declining soft X-rays [6][7].

1. **Reprocessing paradigm:** X-rays from the inner disk are absorbed by an optically thick, super-Eddington wind and re-emitted in the optical/UV. Predicts \(L_{\rm opt}/L_X\) decreasing as the wind thins — matching ASASSN-14li, where X-rays came to dominate at late times [6].
2. **Shock paradigm:** optical/UV is powered directly by stream self-intersection shocks at \(\sim 10^{14}\)–\(10^{15}\) cm, with X-rays from a delayed, separately forming disk. Predicts optical emission tracking \(\dot{M}\) with little X-ray correlation at early times [5].

The integrated radiated energy of ASASSN-14li, \(E \simeq 7\times 10^{50}\) erg over six months [6], implies an accreted mass of only \(\sim 0.004\,M_\odot\) at \(\eta = 0.1\) — a *missing-energy problem*: either most debris energy is advected, ejected, or radiated in the unobserved EUV, or the radiative efficiency is far below thin-disk values.

### 4.4 Spin, the Hills cutoff, and the jetted minority

Only \(\lesssim 1\%\) of TDEs launch relativistic jets [9][10].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now confront the theory with observation in four quantitative tests.

**Test 1 — The \(t^{-5/3}\) decay.** The optical/UV light curve of ASASSN-14li declined roughly exponentially with \(t_0 \simeq 60\) days rather than as a power law, while its soft X-ray emission declined more slowly and eventually dominated the bolometric output [6]. This *falsifies* the naive identification \(L(t) \propto \dot{M}(t)\) for this event and supports the reprocessing picture: the optical photosphere recedes as the wind mass-loss rate drops, while the underlying disk accretion — tracked by X-rays — follows fallback more faithfully. Events like PS1-10jh, by contrast, do follow \(t^{-5/3}\) closely, suggesting real diversity in circularization efficiency.

**Test 2 — The radius proof.** Blackbody fits to optical/UV TDEs uniformly give \(R_{bb} \sim 10^{14}\)–\(10^{15}\) cm with \(T_{bb} \sim (2\)–\(4)\times 10^4\) K nearly constant in time [7]. Since \(r_t \sim 10^{13}\) cm, the emitting surface is \(\sim 100\) tidal radii across — geometrically incompatible with a bare accretion disk and requiring an extended reprocessing layer or shock-powered envelope. The constancy of \(T_{bb}\) further implies the photosphere tracks a fixed ionization front (e.g., He II recombination) rather than a cooling disk.

**Test 3 — Outflow energetics.** Radio monitoring of ASASSN-14li revealed a non-relativistic outflow with \(v \sim 12{,}000\)–\(39{,}000\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}\), kinetic energy \(\sim 10^{48}\) erg, and ejected mass \(\sim 1\)–\(10\%\) of the accreted mass, launched during the super-Eddington phase [9]. The inferred circumnuclear density profile \(\rho \propto R^{-2.6}\) at \(0.01\) pc independently constrains the nuclear environment feeding the loss cone. That such outflows appear ubiquitous in radio-detected TDEs closes part of the energy budget: a non-negligible fraction of the fallback power is mechanical.

**Test 4 — The jetted event.** Swift J1644+57's luminous, rapidly variable hard X-ray emission (\(L_X \sim 10^{48}\,{\rm erg\,s^{-1}}\) isotropic) and its radio afterglow require a relativistic jet viewed on-axis [10]. Fermi-LAT non-detection of GeV emission sets an upper limit on the bulk Lorentz factor via \(\gamma\gamma\) opacity arguments, consistent with \(\Gamma \sim 2\)–\(10\). The event proves that TDE accretion can, rarely, tap the black hole's spin energy — and its \(\lesssim 1\%\) occurrence rate among TDEs bounds the joint probability of high spin, sufficient magnetic flux, and favorable viewing angle.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Circularization is the weakest link.** The efficiency and timescale of stream self-intersection remain uncertain by orders of magnitude; global general-relativistic magnetohydrodynamic simulations of the full disruption-to-disk pipeline do not yet exist at realistic parameters.
2. **The frozen-in approximation breaks** for partial disruptions (surviving-core gravity), for deep relativistic encounters (energy spread is not instantaneous), and when self-gravity or pressure is dynamically important in the stream.
3. **Radiative transfer is crude.** Most emission models assume blackbody reprocessing or single-temperature shocks; frequency-dependent radiative transfer through an aspherical, clumpy, super-Eddington outflow has been computed only in 1D or with severe approximations.
4. **Selection effects dominate the sample.** Optical surveys miss dust-obscured events, X-ray surveys miss optically bright ones, and the \(\sim 100\) known events are a biased slice of the intrinsic population [7].
5. **Stellar population unknowns.** The disrupted star's mass, age, and composition enter every timescale, yet are inferred only indirectly (e.g., via abundance anomalies or host-galaxy stellar populations) [8].

## 7 Conclusion

Tidal disruption events link three otherwise disjoint problems — the stellar dynamics of galactic nuclei, relativistic orbital mechanics, and super-Eddington accretion physics — into a single observable phenomenon. The loss cone sets *how often* stars are shredded; the penetration factor and relativistic precession set *how* the debris returns; and the competition between reprocessing, shocks, and outflows sets *what we see* across the optical-to-X-ray band. Observations of ASASSN-14li and Swift J1644+57 have moved the field from order-of-magnitude energetics to quantitative budget accounting, and the emerging picture is one of genuine diversity: X-ray-dominated, optically-dominated, and jetted events are not contradictory but represent different outcomes of the same pipeline under different \(\beta\), \(M_\bullet\), spin, and viewing angle.

The decisive advances will come from (i) time-resolved X-ray spectroscopy through the super-Eddington transition, (ii) radio calorimetry of unbiased TDE samples to close the kinetic budget, and (iii) global GRMHD simulations that follow debris from disruption through disk formation. When those arrive, the \(t^{-5/3}\) law will finally be what Rees envisioned in 1988 [1]: not merely a signature, but a precision tool for weighing dormant black holes.
