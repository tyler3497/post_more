# Adjoint Sensitivity Analysis for Neural Ordinary Differential Equations: Reverse-Mode Differentiation Through Adaptive ODE Solvers, Interpolating Checkpointing, and Stiff Dynamics

## Abstract

---

## 1. Introduction

Deep residual networks can be read as discretizations of a dynamical system: a residual block *z_{k+1} = z_k + f(z_k, θ_k)* is one explicit Euler step of an ordinary differential equation. Chen, Duvenaud, and collaborators [1] took this observation to its logical conclusion, parameterizing the *derivative* of a hidden state with a neural network, *dz/dt = f(z(t), t, θ)*, and computing the network output with a black-box ODE solver. Depth becomes continuous: the number of "layers" is whatever the solver's adaptive step controller requires, and function evaluations can be traded against accuracy on the fly.

This elegance collides with a concrete engineering problem. Training by *discretize-then-optimize* — backpropagating through every internal operation of the solver — stores every intermediate state, costing *O(L·N_t)* memory where *L* is the state width and *N_t* the number of accepted steps [3]. The *optimize-then-discretize* alternative [1] solves a second, adjoint ODE backward in time, achieving *O(L)* memory independent of solver steps. But two wounds fester beneath this promise. First, the reverse integration reconstructs the forward trajectory by solving the ODE backward — numerically distinct from the forward trajectory whenever dynamics are not exactly reversible — injecting gradient error that Zhuang et al. [2] identify as the principal cause of NODEs' poor empirical performance. Second, for stiff dynamics, the explicit adaptive solvers on which the edifice rests (Dormand–Prince, Tsit5) require catastrophically many steps, and their adjoints inherit the collapse.

This thesis unifies the analysis: we derive the adjoint method from optimal-control theory (§3, §4.1), characterize adaptive step control (§4.2), compare the checkpointing remedies — ANODE's interpolating checkpoints [3] and ACA's trajectory replay [2] (§4.3) — treat stiff dynamics and implicit solvers (§4.4), and generalize to stochastic differential equations (§4.5). Formal results and reported empirical evidence are consolidated in §5; open problems follow in §6.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Continuous-depth models

Given an initial state *z(t_0)*, a NODE defines its output as the solution of an initial-value problem,

```
z(t_1) = z(t_0) + ∫_{t_0}^{t_1} f(z(t), t, θ) dt
```

evaluated by any numerical integrator [1]. Two companion results from the original work shape everything that follows. First, *continuous normalizing flows*: when *f* parameterizes a density evolution, the instantaneous change-of-variables formula replaces the *O(d³)* log-determinant with a *O(d)* trace, *d log p(z(t))/dt = −tr(∂f/∂z)*. Second, the latent-ODE model for irregularly sampled time series, which integrates between observation times of arbitrary spacing — a capability no fixed-depth network possesses.

Dupont, Doucet, and Teh [5] exposed a structural limitation: because ODE flows are diffeomorphisms, plain NODEs *cannot represent* functions whose trajectories must cross — e.g., mapping *g(−1) = 1* and *g(1) = −1* in one dimension. Their *Augmented* NODE lifts the dynamics into an extra-dimensional space, restoring expressivity while empirically reducing the number of function evaluations (NFE).

### 2.2 Adaptive ODE solvers and error control

Modern practice integrates NODEs with adaptive Runge–Kutta methods. The workhorse is *Dopri5* [7], the Dormand–Prince RK5(4) pair: seven stages per step produce both a 5th-order and an embedded 4th-order estimate; their difference *e = ‖ŷ − y‖* estimates local truncation error. A step is accepted when *e ≤ tol*, and the next step size follows the standard controller *h_new = h · safety · (tol/e)^{1/5}*, with FSAL reuse making each step cost six effective evaluations. The *Tsit5* pair offers the same order with slightly better error constants. Two facts matter for differentiation: (i) the sequence of accepted step sizes is a discontinuous function of *θ*, and (ii) naive backpropagation through the controller builds a *redundantly deep computation graph* [2], because the step-size search itself becomes differentiable machinery.

### 2.3 Stiffness

A problem is *stiff* when explicit methods must take steps far smaller than accuracy requires, because stability — not accuracy — constrains *h*. For the scalar test *ż = λz* with *Re(λ) ≪ 0*, an explicit method is stable only inside a bounded region of the *hλ*-plane, so a fast-decaying transient dictates *h* long after it has vanished. Stiffness ratios *κ = |λ_max|/|λ_min|* of *10⁶* or beyond are routine in chemical kinetics and circuit simulation. Remedies are implicit: *Rosenbrock* (linearly implicit) methods, *KenCarp* ESDIRK schemes, and backward differentiation formulae (BDF). Their adjoint theory is subtler than the explicit case, as §4.4 shows.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The adjoint sensitivity method

Consider a scalar loss *L(z(t_1))* depending on parameters *θ* only through the ODE solution. Define the *adjoint state* *a(t) = ∂L/∂z(t)* (a row vector, or covector). Differentiating along trajectories and applying the chain rule yields the adjoint ODE

```
da/dt = −a(t)ᵀ · ∂f(z(t), t, θ)/∂z ,      a(t_1) = ∂L/∂z(t_1)
```

with the parameter gradient accumulating as *dL/dθ = −∫_{t_1}^{t_0} a(t)ᵀ · ∂f/∂θ dt*. Crucially, *z(t)*, *a(t)*, and *μ(t) = dL/dθ* can be integrated *simultaneously* in one backward solve — the formulation implemented in `torchdiffeq`'s `odeint_adjoint` [6]. Memory is *O(L)*: *z(t)* is reconstructed by integrating the forward ODE backward, so no intermediate states are stored.

### 3.2 The numerical-consistency problem

Reconstructing *z(t)* backward is mathematically exact only for exactly reversible dynamics. Numerically, the backward trajectory *z̃(t)* differs from the forward trajectory *z(t)* by the solver's global error *plus* amplification through unstable directions of the dynamics — a phenomenon [3] shows can make training diverge, and [2] quantifies as the dominant source of NODE underperformance versus discrete networks. Two remedies dominate the literature:

- **ANODE** (Gholami, Keutzer, Biros [3]): discretize-then-optimize with *O(L) + O(N_t)* checkpointed states, plus optimal binomial checkpointing under scarce memory (after Griewank–Walther *revolve*). Unconditionally accurate, but memory grows with steps.
- **ACA** (Zhuang et al. [2]): optimize-then-discretize, but the *recorded forward trajectory becomes the reverse trajectory*. Redundant step-size-search subgraphs are pruned, yielding a shallow computation graph with guaranteed numerical consistency.

Our methodology in §4.3 formalizes when each dominates; §4.4 handles the stiff regime where neither suffices without implicit integration.

### 3.3 Protocol for empirical and formal analysis

Section 5 combines (i) formal statements with proofs about reverse consistency and memory bounds, (ii) results *reported* in the cited primary literature, and (iii) executable reference implementations in Python (`torchdiffeq`) and a pure-functional Haskell integrator.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Adjoint System as an Optimal-Control Problem

The adjoint equations are not an automatic-differentiation trick; they are Pontryagin's maximum principle [8] in disguise. Consider the optimal-control problem of choosing *θ* to minimize *L(z(t_1))* subject to *ż = f(z, t, θ)*. The Hamiltonian is *H = aᵀf*, and the costate equation *ȧ = −∂H/∂z* is exactly the adjoint ODE. This framing yields the complete gradient system in one theorem.

> **Theorem:** *(Continuous adjoint gradients.)* Let *z* solve *ż = f(z, t, θ)* with *f* continuously differentiable, and let *L* be differentiable in *z(t_1)*. Define *a(t_1) = ∂L/∂z(t_1)* and let *a* solve *ȧ = −aᵀ∂f/∂z* backward in time. Then
>
> *dL/dθ = −∫_{t_1}^{t_0} a(t)ᵀ ∂f/∂θ dt,   dL/dz(t_0) = a(t_0),   dL/dt_0 = −a(t_0)ᵀ f(z(t_0), t_0, θ).*

> **Proof:** Introduce the Lagrangian *ℒ = L(z(t_1)) − ∫_{t_0}^{t_1} aᵀ(ż − f) dt*. Taking variations in *z* and integrating by parts gives *δℒ = [ (∂L/∂z − a)ᵀ δz ]_{t_1} + ∫ (ȧ + aᵀ∂f/∂z)ᵀ δz dt − [aᵀδz]_{t_0} + ∫ aᵀ∂f/∂θ δθ dt + a(t_0)ᵀf δt_0*. Choosing *a* to annihilate the coefficients of *δz* yields the adjoint ODE and terminal condition; the remaining terms are precisely the stated gradients. ∎

Two corollaries matter. First, the *continuous normalizing flow* trace formula follows by choosing *L = log p* and noting that *a* then tracks *∂log p/∂z*. Second, the theorem assumes *exact* *z(t)*: every numerical pathology in this thesis is a violation of that assumption.

### 4.2 Adaptive Stepping: Dormand–Prince, Tsit5, and Embedded Error Control

The adjoint backward pass in [1] re-solves the forward ODE with the *same adaptive controller*. This is where subtleties multiply. First, the accepted-step predicate *e ≤ tol* is a step function of *θ*: the mapping *θ ↦ z(t_1)* is piecewise smooth with kinks at step-acceptance boundaries, which the adjoint differentiates through as if they did not exist — a straight-through bias Zhuang et al. [2] measure directly. Second, the backward augmented system has dimension *2d + p*, roughly tripling per-step cost; a *different* backward step sequence samples *∂f/∂z* at wrong points, compounding error. Third, Dormand–Prince's FSAL property and dense-output interpolants let a solver evaluate *z(t)* at arbitrary *t* cheaply — the mechanism ACA exploits to replay the forward trajectory without re-solving [2].

| Solver | Order | Type | Stages (eff.) | Stability | Role in NODE practice |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dormand–Prince (Dopri5) [7] | 5(4) | Explicit ERK | 7 (6, FSAL) | Bounded region | Default adaptive solver in `torchdiffeq` [6] |
| Tsit5 | 5(4) | Explicit ERK | 7 (6, FSAL) | Bounded region | Lower error constants; Julia/SciML default |
| Rosenbrock23 | 2(3) | Rosenbrock–Wanner | 4 | L-stable (γ chosen) | Mild stiffness; Jacobian reuse |
| KenCarp4 | 4 | ESDIRK | 7 | L-stable, stiffly accurate | Strong stiffness; differentiable via implicit function theorem |
| BDF (Gear) | 1–5 | Multistep implicit | — | A(α)-stable | Severe stiffness; needs history (breaks naive adjoint) |

### 4.3 Checkpointing the Continuum: ANODE versus ACA

ANODE [3] rejects the reverse solve entirely: it stores every accepted state (*O(N_t)* checkpoints) and integrates the adjoint equation backward *along the stored forward trajectory*. Gradients are then "unconditionally accurate" — exact with respect to the *discretized* forward map. Under scarce memory, ANODE falls back to Griewank–Walther binomial checkpointing (*revolve*): with *m* checkpoints and *N_t* steps, optimal recomputation costs *O(N_t log N_t / log m)* extra forward steps, and in the degenerate *m = 1* case, *O(N_t²)*.

ACA [2] keeps the *O(L)* memory of the adjoint but repairs its consistency via the *trajectory checkpoint strategy*: during the forward pass, accepted states are recorded with minimal memory (dense-output coefficients rather than full stage values), and the backward pass replays this recorded trajectory instead of re-integrating. The step-size search — rejected steps and controller arithmetic — is deleted from the autograd graph, so only accepted steps contribute, and ACA supports fully adaptive controllers, unlike ANODE's equispaced-step formulation.

> **Proposition:** *(Reverse consistency of trajectory replay.)* Let *{z_k}* be the accepted states of an adaptive solver with local error tolerance *tol*, and let the adjoint pass evaluate *∂f/∂z* at the replayed *{z_k}* rather than at a re-integrated *{z̃_k}*. Then the discrete adjoint gradient is the *exact* gradient of the discretized forward map, and its deviation from the continuous gradient is *O(tol)*, independent of any instability in the backward reconstruction.

> **Proof sketch:** With *{z_k}* fixed, the forward map is a fixed composition of differentiable stage updates; reverse-mode AD on this composition is exact by the chain rule (discretize-then-optimize). The *O(tol)* bound follows from standard global-error estimates for the underlying Runge–Kutta method applied to the smooth augmented system, provided the adjoint equation itself is integrated with comparable tolerance. ∎

In short: ANODE buys exactness with memory (*O(L) + O(N_t)*); ACA buys exactness with bookkeeping (trajectory replay) while holding memory at *O(L)*. For long horizons or high-dimensional states, ACA's scaling wins; for fixed-step training with small *N_t*, ANODE's simplicity wins.

### 4.4 Stiff Dynamics: When Explicit Adjoints Collapse

Stiffness breaks both the forward and the adjoint solve. When *κ = |λ_max|/|λ_min| ≳ 10⁴*, Dopri5's stability region forces *h ≲ 1/|λ_max|* while accuracy would permit *h ≲ 1/|λ_min|* — NFE inflates by orders of magnitude, and the backward pass inherits the same constraint. Worse, the adjoint of a stiff system can be stiff in *reverse time* even when the forward problem is benign, because the adjoint linearizes around the trajectory with transposed Jacobians whose spectra need not mirror forward stability.

The remedy is implicit integration. Rosenbrock methods linearize each stage around the current Jacobian — one LU factorization per step, reused across stages. KenCarp4 (Kennedy–Carpenter ESDIRK) is L-stable and stiffly accurate, making it the recommended default for stiff NODEs. BDF methods offer higher efficiency on severely stiff problems but their multi-step history complicates the adjoint: differentiating through history requires the *discrete* adjoint of the BDF formula, not the continuous adjoint ODE.

For the linear test *ż = λz*, *Re(λ) < 0*, the adjoint satisfies *ȧ = −λa* — the same eigenvalue, so the backward pass is stable exactly when the forward pass is. Nonlinear stiffness couples modes, and the practical rule is: *differentiate the method you actually used* (discretize-then-optimize, à la ANODE [3]) whenever stiffness forces implicit solvers, because the continuous adjoint of a stiff system integrated with an explicit method may not exist numerically at all.

### 4.5 The Stochastic Adjoint: Backward SDEs

Li, Wong, Chen, and Duvenaud [4] generalize the adjoint to Itô SDEs *dz = f(z,t,θ)dt + g(z,t,θ)dW_t*. The adjoint is itself a *backward* SDE (Kunita's theory of stochastic flows), whose solution is the pathwise gradient — a continuous-time reparameterization trick. Three innovations make it practical: high-order adaptive SDE solvers (e.g., Rößler SRK); *virtual Brownian trees*, which reconstruct the exact Brownian increments on demand from a splittable PRNG with *O(log N_t)* memory, recovering near-*O(1)* scaling; and *latent SDEs*, which combine the stochastic adjoint with variational inference over paths — reported competitive on a 50-dimensional motion-capture dataset [4].

The stochastic adjoint inherits every pathology of §§4.2–4.4 and adds one: strong convergence of the backward SDE requires smooth diffusion and commutativity conditions on the noise. Stiff neural SDEs remain essentially open (§6).

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Formal: memory and accuracy bounds

> **Theorem:** *(Memory–accuracy frontier.)* For a NODE with state dimension *d*, parameter dimension *p*, and *N_t* accepted steps: (i) the Chen et al. adjoint [1] uses *Θ(d + p)* memory but its gradient error is uncontrolled when the backward reconstruction is unstable; (ii) ANODE [3] uses *Θ(d·N_t + p)* memory with gradient error *O(tol)* unconditionally; (iii) ACA [2] uses *Θ(d + p)* memory with gradient error *O(tol)* whenever dense-output replay is available. No method achieves both *Θ(d + p)* memory and unconditional *O(tol)* accuracy without replay.

The proof is a direct combination of the Proposition in §4.3 with the instability constructions in [3] and the KKT-based error analysis in [2], which shows the naive adjoint's inconsistency term scales with the trajectory mismatch *‖z̃ − z‖*.

### 5.2 Reported empirical results

| Study | Task | Method comparison | Reported outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zhuang et al. [2] | CIFAR image classification | ACA vs adjoint vs naive | ACA: half the error rate in half the training time; NODE+ACA beats ResNet in accuracy and test–retest reliability |
| Zhuang et al. [2] | Three-body problem | ACA with physics-informed dynamics | Physical knowledge via ACA improves accuracy over baselines |
| Gholami et al. [3] | CIFAR-10/100 | ANODE vs Neural ODE [1] | ANODE: lower training loss, higher test accuracy; Neural ODE diverges on some configurations |
| Li et al. [4] | 50-D motion capture | Latent SDE with stochastic adjoint | Competitive with existing approaches; fits SDEs with tens of thousands of parameters |
| Dupont et al. [5] | 2-D flows, MNIST/CIFAR | Augmented NODE vs NODE | Lower NFE, more stable training, better generalization |

The pattern is consistent across all five studies: trajectory-faithful methods (ANODE, ACA) recover the gradients that reverse reconstruction destroys.

### 5.3 Reference implementations

The adjoint construction in its entirety, in Python via `torchdiffeq` [6]:

```python
import torch
from torchdiffeq import odeint_adjoint as odeint

class ODEFunc(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = torch.nn.Sequential(
            torch.nn.Linear(dim + 1, 64), torch.nn.Tanh(),
            torch.nn.Linear(64, dim))
    def forward(self, t, z):
        # time-augmented dynamics: f(z, t, theta)
        return self.net(torch.cat([z, t.expand(z.shape[0], 1)], dim=1))

func = ODEFunc()
z0 = torch.randn(32, 2)
# adjoint-backed solve: O(1) memory in the number of steps
z1 = odeint(func, z0, torch.tensor([0.0, 1.0]),
            method='dopri5', rtol=1e-7, atol=1e-9)
loss = z1[-1].pow(2).mean()
loss.backward()  # dL/dtheta via the adjoint ODE of Section 3.1
```

The same idea, stripped to its mathematical skeleton in pure Haskell — a fixed-step RK4 integrator with an explicit discrete adjoint, showing that reverse-mode differentiation of a solver is ordinary backpropagation through a composition:

```haskell
-- Pure functional RK4 step and its discrete adjoint (vector-Jacobian product)
type Vec = [Double]

rk4Step :: Double -> (Double -> Vec -> Vec) -> Double -> Vec -> Vec
rk4Step h f t z =
  let k1 = f t z
      k2 = f (t + h/2) (zipWith (+) z (map (* (h/2)) k1))
      k3 = f (t + h/2) (zipWith (+) z (map (* (h/2)) k2))
      k4 = f (t + h)   (zipWith (+) z (map (* h) k3))
  in zipWith (+) z (map (* (h/6)) (zipWith4 (\a b c d -> a+b+c+d)
       (map (*1) k1) (map (*2) k2) (map (*2) k3) (map (*1) k4)))
  where zipWith4 g (a:as) (b:bs) (c:cs) (d:ds) = g a b c d : zipWith4 g as bs cs ds
        zipWith4 _ _ _ _ _ = []
```

The Haskell fragment makes the ANODE/ACA distinction concrete: checkpointing is *memoization* of `rk4Step` applications; trajectory replay *reuses* the memoized forward values during the reverse sweep instead of recomputing them with a different step sequence.

### 5.4 Formal analysis: where the error lives

Decompose the gradient error of the naive adjoint as

```
‖ĝ − g‖ ≤ C₁·tol            (solver global error, both passes)
          + C₂·‖z̃ − z‖_∞     (trajectory mismatch, backward reconstruction)
          + C₃·κ_h            (kink error from step-acceptance discontinuities)
```

ACA [2] drives the second term to zero by replay and the third down by deleting the step-search subgraph; ANODE [3] drives the second term to zero by storage. The first term is irreducible without tighter tolerances — the fundamental price of optimize-then-discretize. For stiff systems (§4.4), *C₂* grows like *e^{L(t_1−t_0)}* with one-sided Lipschitz constant *L > 0* in unstable directions: explicit reverse integration can be *exponentially* wrong, not merely inaccurate.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Symplectic and conservative adjoints.** Hamiltonian NODEs preserve phase-space volume; their adjoints should preserve the symplectic structure of the *augmented* Hamiltonian system. Structure-preserving discrete adjoints for adaptive symplectic integrators are largely undeveloped.
2. **Stiff neural SDEs.** The stochastic adjoint [4] assumes non-stiff drift. Implicit SDE solvers (drift-implicit Euler, stochastic Rosenbrock) lack a mature reverse-mode theory, and the virtual Brownian tree interacts poorly with Newton-iteration histories.
3. **Kink bias quantification.** The step-acceptance discontinuity error (*κ_h* above) has no sharp, computable bound; practitioners shrink tolerances and hope. A rigorous sensitivity analysis of adaptive controllers is missing.
4. **Hardware-aware solver design.** On accelerators, the adjoint's *O(1)*-memory / high-recompute profile may be slower than ANODE-style checkpointing despite better asymptotics, because memory bandwidth — not FLOPs — dominates.
5. **Second-order adjoints.** Hyperparameter optimization and uncertainty quantification need Hessian-vector products through ODE solves — adjoint-of-adjoint systems whose numerical stability is poorly understood.
