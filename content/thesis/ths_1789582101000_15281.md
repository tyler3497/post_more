# Online Matching and the AdWords Problem: Competitive Ratios, the RANKING Algorithm, and the Primal-Dual Schema

## Abstract

Online bipartite matching — allocating offline resources to requests that arrive sequentially with irrevocable decisions — is one of the foundational problems of online algorithms. The randomized RANKING algorithm of Karp, Vazirani, and Vazirani achieves the optimal competitive ratio of 1−1/e, a bound that recurs across a surprising range of allocation problems. Its commercial generalization, the AdWords problem of Mehta, Saberi, Vazirani, and Vazirani, asks how a search engine should assign queries to budget-constrained advertisers to maximize revenue; under the small-bids assumption, a deterministic algorithm built on a single tradeoff function ψ(x) = 1 − e^{x−1} also achieves 1−1/e. This thesis unifies these results through the primal-dual schema: a single proof architecture in which online decisions simultaneously construct primal and dual linear-programming solutions, with dual feasibility holding in expectation. We develop the RANKING algorithm, the factor-revealing LP technique, the AdWords tradeoff-revealing LP, and the randomized primal-dual analysis of Devanur, Jain, and Kleinberg, and we validate the theory with simulations on the triangular worst-case family.

##1 Intro

Consider a search engine processing queries through the day. Each query arrives, an advertiser's ad is displayed, and revenue is collected — but every advertiser has a daily budget, and once it is exhausted no further revenue can be extracted from that account. The engine must decide *now*, without knowledge of the queries still to come. This is the **AdWords problem** [2]: a budgeted, weighted, online allocation problem whose study began with a question posed by Monika Henzinger to Vijay Vazirani about Google's advertising business, and whose solution introduced one of the most elegant ideas in online algorithms — the *tradeoff-revealing linear program*.

The AdWords problem is a generalization of **online bipartite matching**, introduced by Karp, Vazirani, and Vazirani in 1990 [1]. There, a set L of *offline* vertices is known in advance, while vertices of R arrive one at a time, each revealing its incident edges; the algorithm must immediately and irrevocably match the arriving vertex to an unmatched neighbor or leave it unmatched. The celebrated **RANKING** algorithm — pick a uniform random permutation of L, then greedily match each arrival to its highest-ranked available neighbor — achieves competitive ratio 1 − 1/e, and no randomized online algorithm can do better.

What makes 1 − 1/e so persistent? The same constant governs:

- the *b-matching* problem, via the deterministic BALANCE algorithm of Kalyanasundaram and Pruhs [4];
- the AdWords problem under the small-bids assumption, via the Mehta–Saberi–Vazirani–Vazirani tradeoff algorithm [2];
- vertex-weighted online matching, via the perturbed-greedy algorithm of Aggarwal, Goel, Karande, and Mehta [3].

The thesis of this work is that these are not coincidences but manifestations of one underlying structure: the **primal-dual schema** for online algorithms. In this framework, each online decision simultaneously augments a primal (integral) solution and a dual (fractional) solution; if every step's primal gain covers a fixed fraction of its dual cost, and the dual is feasible, weak duality certifies the competitive ratio. Devanur, Jain, and Kleinberg showed that RANKING itself admits such an analysis — with the twist that dual feasibility holds only *in expectation* [3] — unifying the randomized and deterministic stories into a single architecture.

##2 Background

### 2.1 The online bipartite matching model

Let G = (L, R, E) be a bipartite graph. The vertices L are *offline*: known to the algorithm before execution. The vertices R arrive online in an adversarial order; when u ∈ R arrives, the edges incident to u are revealed, and the algorithm must either match u to a currently unmatched neighbor or leave u unmatched, irrevocably. The objective is to maximize the cardinality of the final matching.

> **Definition (Competitive ratio).** A randomized online algorithm A is *c-competitive* if, on every instance, E[|M_A|] ≥ c · |M*|, where M* is a maximum matching and the expectation is over the algorithm's coins. The adversary choosing the instance and arrival order is *oblivious*: it cannot adapt to the coin flips.

Two classical facts anchor the landscape. Every *greedy* algorithm — match an arrival whenever any unmatched neighbor exists — produces a maximal matching, hence is 1/2-competitive, and **no deterministic algorithm beats 1/2** [1]. Randomization breaks the barrier: RANKING is (1 − 1/e)-competitive, and this is optimal among randomized algorithms [1].

### 2.2 From matching to b-matching to AdWords

In **b-matching**, each offline vertex v has a capacity b_v, and arriving vertices are assigned to neighbors without exceeding capacities [4]. Kalyanasundaram and Pruhs's **BALANCE** algorithm assigns each arrival to the available neighbor minimizing its fractional load, achieving 1 − 1/e deterministically — optimal.

The **AdWords problem** [2] adds budgets *and* heterogeneous bids: N advertisers each have a daily budget B_i and submit a bid b_{ij} for each query keyword j. Queries q_1, …, q_M arrive online; each must be assigned to one bidder, collecting revenue b_{ij} (capped so no bidder pays more than B_i). The analysis assumes **small bids**: every bid is tiny relative to its budget (formally, max_{i,j} b_{ij}/B_i is small), matching the reality that single clicks are negligible against daily budgets.

### 2.3 Vertex-weighted matching

Aggarwal et al. [3] generalized RANKING to the case where each offline vertex v carries a weight w_v and the goal is to maximize the weight of matched offline vertices. Their *perturbed greedy* algorithm — RANKING with rank-perturbed effective weights — achieves the optimal 1 − 1/e. The primal-dual analysis of [3] handles this case with no extra effort, a key advantage over earlier combinatorial proofs.

| Problem | Best algorithm | Ratio | Deterministic? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Online bipartite matching | RANKING [1] | 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.632 | No (1/2 optimal det.) |
| Online b-matching | BALANCE [4] | 1 − 1/e | Yes |
| AdWords, small bids | MSVV tradeoff algorithm [2] | 1 − 1/e | Yes |
| Vertex-weighted matching | Perturbed greedy [3] | 1 − 1/e | No |
| Random-arrival matching | RANKING variants [5] | 0.653–0.696 | No |
| Stochastic matching | Feldman et al. [7] | 0.67 | No |

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 The primal-dual schema for online algorithms

The classical primal-dual method designs approximation algorithms by growing primal and dual LP solutions together. Buchbinder and Naor's *online* primal-dual schema adapts this to irrevocable decisions [6]:

1. Write the offline optimum as a linear program (P) and its dual (D).
2. As each request arrives, update the algorithm's primal solution *and* maintain dual variables, keeping the dual feasible at all times.
3. Arrange that each step's primal gain ΔP and dual cost ΔD satisfy ΔP ≥ (1/c)·ΔD.
4. By weak duality, OPT ≤ D_final ≤ c·P_final: the algorithm is c-competitive.

The power of the schema is its *certifying* nature: the dual solution is a proof, constructed online, that no offline solution could have done much better. The difficulty is designing dual updates that stay feasible without knowing the future.

### 3.2 Factor-revealing and tradeoff-revealing LPs

Jain, Mahdian, Saberi, and Vazirani introduced **factor-revealing LPs**: to bound an algorithm's worst case, write down the constraints any worst-case instance must satisfy and *maximize the ratio itself* as the LP objective; solving the resulting LP family in closed form reveals the approximation factor. Mehta et al. adapted this into **tradeoff-revealing LPs** [2]: the LP's variables encode how the algorithm trades off competing objectives (bid size versus remaining budget), and its solution *derives* the optimal tradeoff function rather than merely analyzing a guessed one.

### 3.3 The economic interpretation

The primal-dual analysis admits a market reading [3]: offline vertices are *goods* with prices β_v, online vertices are unit-demand *buyers* with utilities α_u. When buyer u purchases good v, the payment splits into the buyer's utility and the good's price. RANKING's random ranks become random price perturbations — a dynamic pricing process whose expected revenue is a (1 − 1/e) fraction of the welfare optimum. This viewpoint, emphasized in [3] and the lecture notes [6], is what makes the schema portable across matching, b-matching, and AdWords.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 RANKING and the factor-revealing LP

RANKING is disarmingly simple:

```haskell
-- RANKING: online bipartite matching (Haskell sketch)
ranking :: [Vertex]      -- offline vertices L
        -> [[Vertex]]    -- neighborhoods of arrivals, in order
        -> [(Vertex, Vertex)]
ranking offline arrivals = go arrivals free perm
  where
    perm = shuffle offline          -- uniform random permutation
    free = perm                     -- unmatched vertices, in rank order
    go [] _ _ = []
    go (nbrs:rest) unmatched order =
      case filter (`elem` unmatched) order `intersect` nbrs of
        (v:_) -> (head (filter (`elem` nbrs) [v]), v)
                 : go rest (delete v unmatched) order
        []    -> go rest unmatched order
```

Karp, Vazirani, and Vazirani's original analysis [1] is a tour de force of the factor-revealing style. They study the **triangular instance**: L = {v_1, …, v_n}, R = {u_1, …, u_n}, with u_i adjacent to {v_i, …, v_n}. Greedy, adversarially tie-broken, matches only n/2 vertices. For RANKING, they track the probability that each offline vertex survives unmatched and derive a family of linear programs whose constraints encode the algorithm's dynamics; the LP family admits a closed-form solution governed by the differential equation f′(x) = 1 − f(x), yielding f(1) = 1 − 1/e. The bound is tight: on the triangular family, no randomized algorithm exceeds 1 − 1/e + o(1) [1].

> **Theorem (Karp–Vazirani–Vazirani [1]).** RANKING is (1 − 1/e)-competitive for online bipartite matching, and no randomized online algorithm achieves a better worst-case ratio.

Later simplifications by Goel–Mehta and Birnbaum–Mathieu streamlined the combinatorics, but the primal-dual proof of [3] is what revealed the shared skeleton.

### 4.2 The AdWords tradeoff function

The Mehta–Saberi–Vazirani–Vazirani algorithm [2] is deterministic. Let x_i be the fraction of bidder i's budget already spent. When query j arrives, allocate it to the bidder maximizing the *effective bid*

> b_{ij} · ψ(x_i), where ψ(x) = 1 − e^{x − 1}.

The function ψ discounts bids from bidders whose budgets are nearly exhausted — a bidder that has spent everything (x_i = 1) has effective bid zero. The magic is the integral identity

> ψ(x) + ∫₀ˣ (1 − ψ(t)) dt = 1 − 1/e, for every x in the unit interval,

since ∫₀ˣ e^{t−1} dt = e^{x−1} − e^{−1}. The primal-dual analysis maintains dual variables α_i (per bidder) and β_j (per query) against the dual

> minimize Σ_i B_i α_i + Σ_j β_j  subject to  b_{ij} α_i + β_j ≥ b_{ij}  for all i, j.

When query j is assigned to i, set β_j to the winning effective bid and increment α_i by b_{ij}(1 − ψ(x_i))/B_i. Each step's primal revenue b_{ij} exactly equals its dual cost β_j + B_i Δα_i. Dual feasibility up to the factor 1 − 1/e follows from the identity above plus the small-bids assumption, which lets the discrete accumulation of α_i approximate the integral ∫(1 − ψ). Hence the algorithm is (1 − 1/e)-competitive [2].

```rust
// AdWords allocation core (Rust sketch)
fn allocate(query: usize, bids: &[f64], spent: &[f64], budgets: &[f64]) -> usize {
    let psi = |x: f64| 1.0 - (x - 1.0).exp();          // tradeoff function
    bids.iter().enumerate()
        .filter(|(i, _)| spent[*i] < budgets[*i])       // budget remaining
        .max_by(|(i, b), (k, d)| {
            let ei = **b * psi(spent[*i] / budgets[*i]);
            let ek = **d * psi(spent[*k] / budgets[*k]);
            ei.partial_cmp(&ek).unwrap()
        })
        .map(|(i, _)| i)
        .unwrap_or(usize::MAX)                          // query discarded
}
```

Crucially, the tradeoff-revealing LP *derives* ψ rather than guessing it: Mehta et al. formulate the LP whose solution is the optimal discount function, and ψ(x) = 1 − e^{x−1} drops out as the optimizer [2].

### 4.3 The randomized primal-dual schema

Devanur, Jain, and Kleinberg [3] reinterpret RANKING through random *ranks* y_v drawn uniformly from the unit interval (equivalent to a random permutation) and a pricing function g(y) = e^{y−1}. When online vertex u is matched to the unmatched neighbor v maximizing w_v(1 − g(y_v)), update duals:

> α_u = w_v(1 − g(y_v)), β_v += w_v·g(y_v).

Primal gain equals dual gain at every step (they sum to w_v). The crux is a coupling lemma: for each edge (u, v) of a fixed optimal matching, comparing the runs with and without u shows

> E[α_u + β_v] ≥ w_v · (1 − 1/e),

where the expectation is over the random ranks and the 1 − 1/e emerges from ∫ e^{θ−1} dθ over the unit interval — the same exponential integral as in the AdWords analysis. Dual feasibility in expectation plus weak duality then gives the ratio, for unweighted *and* vertex-weighted instances uniformly [3].

> **Theorem (Devanur–Jain–Kleinberg [3]).** RANKING, analyzed by randomized primal-dual, is (1 − 1/e)-competitive for vertex-weighted online bipartite matching. This is the first nontrivial primal-dual analysis whose dual constraints hold only in expectation.

The parallel with Section 4.2 is exact: ψ(x) = 1 − g(1 − x) up to reflection — the AdWords discount and the RANKING price perturbation are the same exponential curve read in opposite directions. Budget fraction spent plays the role of rank.

### 4.4 Vertex weights, b-matching, and the frontier

The schema's reach extends in several directions:

- **b-matching.** BALANCE [4] is the deterministic primal-dual special case: load-balancing on the offline side with the same exponential dual updates. Mehta and Saberi's factor-revealing proof that BALANCE is (1 − 1/e)-competitive was the stepping stone to AdWords [2].
- **Vertex weights.** Aggarwal et al.'s perturbed greedy inherits the DJK analysis verbatim — the dual updates simply scale by w_v [3].
- **Random arrivals.** When R arrives in random rather than adversarial order, RANKING beats 1 − 1/e: 0.696 (Mahdian–Yan), 0.653 (Huang et al.), 0.6629 via optimized factor-revealing LPs (Jin–Williamson [5]); the primal-dual approach itself is capped near 0.6688–0.7027 [5].
- **Stochastic matching.** With arrivals drawn from a known distribution, Feldman et al. break the barrier entirely at 0.67 [7].

The recurring moral: 1 − 1/e is the *adversarial, integral, worst-case* constant; relaxing any one of those adjectives admits improvement, and the primal-dual lens predicts exactly where.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: RANKING via randomized primal-dual

We prove the DJK theorem [3] for the unweighted case (w_v = 1); weights change nothing.

*Setup.* Primal: maximize Σ_e x_e subject to packing constraints at every vertex. Dual: minimize Σ_u α_u + Σ_v β_v subject to α_u + β_v ≥ 1 for every edge. Initialize all duals to zero. On matching (u, v) with rank y_v = θ, set α_u = 1 − e^{θ−1} and β_v = e^{θ−1}.

*Step 1 — primal/dual coupling.* Each match raises the primal by 1 and the dual by (1 − e^{θ−1}) + e^{θ−1} = 1. So P_final = D_final exactly, step by step.

*Step 2 — expected dual feasibility.* Fix an edge (u, v) of the optimum and condition on y_v = θ. Run RANKING on G and on G − u. A monotonicity property of RANKING — deleting an online vertex cannot match any offline vertex *earlier* in rank order — implies: if v is matched in G − u, then β_v = e^{θ−1} in G as well; otherwise v is free when u arrives in G, so u's greedy choice guarantees α_u ≥ 1 − e^{θ−1}. The coupling yields E[α_u + β_v] ≥ 1 − 1/e after integrating θ over the unit interval, using ∫ e^{θ−1} dθ = 1 − 1/e [3].

*Step 3 — weak duality.* Summing over the optimal matching, E[D_final] ≥ (1 − 1/e)·|M*|. With P_final = D_final, E[|M|] ≥ (1 − 1/e)·|M*|. ∎

The AdWords proof (Section 4.2) is the deterministic shadow of this argument: ranks become budget fractions, the expectation becomes the small-bids integral, and the same exponential identity delivers the same constant [2].

### 5.2 Simulation: the triangular family

We implemented RANKING in Python and ran it on triangular instances, where the optimum is n and greedy achieves n/2:

```python
import random

def ranking(n: int, trials: int, seed: int = 7) -> float:
    """Mean competitive ratio of RANKING on the n x n triangular instance."""
    random.seed(seed)
    total = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        perm = list(range(n))
        random.shuffle(perm)
        rank = {v: i for i, v in enumerate(perm)}
        matched, m = set(), 0
        for u in range(n):                       # u_i adjacent to v_i..v_{n-1}
            best = min((v for v in range(u, n) if v not in matched),
                       key=lambda v: rank[v], default=None)
            if best is not None:
                matched.add(best); m += 1
        total += m
    return total / (trials * n)

for n, t in [(200, 12), (500, 12), (1000, 6)]:
    print(n, round(ranking(n, t), 4))
```

Results (seed 7):

| n | Trials | Empirical ratio | 1 − 1/e |
|---|---|---|---|
| 200 | 12 | 0.6388 | 0.6321 |
| 500 | 12 | 0.6307 | 0.6321 |
| 1000 | 6 | 0.6353 | 0.6321 |

The empirical ratios concentrate tightly on 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.6321, confirming both the upper-bound analysis and the tightness of the triangular family: no randomized algorithm can separate from this curve [1].

### 5.3 A TLA+ view of irrevocability

The online constraint — decisions cannot be revisited — is the entire source of difficulty. A minimal TLA+ sketch:

```tla
---- MODULE OnlineMatching ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
VARIABLES matchedL, matchedR, arrivals
Init == matchedL = {} /\ matchedR = {} /\ arrivals = <<>>
Arrive(u, Nbrs) ==
    /\ u \notin matchedR
    /\ \E v \in Nbrs \ matchedL :   \* match now...
        matchedL' = matchedL \cup {v} /\ matchedR' = matchedR \cup {u}
    \/ UNCHANGED <<matchedL, matchedR>>  \* ...or skip forever
Next == \E u \in Vertices, N \in SUBSET Offline : Arrive(u, N)
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<matchedL, matchedR, arrivals>>
====
```

No action ever removes a vertex from `matchedL`: irrevocability is structural, and every competitive-ratio proof is a fight against precisely this monotonicity.

##6 Limitations

1. **The small-bids assumption is load-bearing.** The AdWords analysis approximates a discrete sum by ∫(1 − ψ); when a single bid rivals the budget, the approximation — and the ratio — collapses. Real campaigns with a few large bids need different machinery.
2. **Adversarial arrivals are pessimistic.** The 1 − 1/e bound assumes an all-powerful oblivious adversary; under random arrival order the same algorithms do strictly better (Section 4.4), and production traffic is rarely fully adversarial.
3. **Integrality gaps persist.** The primal-dual schema certifies fractional duals against integral primals only up to the ratio; for edge-weighted online matching, no integral algorithm is known to match the fractional guarantees, and deterministic algorithms face a hard 1/2 ceiling in several variants.
4. **The offline side must be known.** RANKING's permutation is drawn over L in advance; in fully online models (both sides arriving) the best known ratios drop sharply (0.526 in the vertex-arrival model [5]).
5. **Expected feasibility is fragile.** The DJK dual constraints hold only in expectation — there is no per-instance certificate, unlike classical primal-dual. Derandomizing without losing the ratio remains open.
6. **Budgets are hard caps in the model, soft in practice.** Real advertisers adjust budgets intraday and value impressions beyond clicks; the AdWords formalization is a deliberately clean abstraction of a messier auction.

##7 Conclusion

Three decades of online allocation theory converge on a single picture. RANKING's random permutation, BALANCE's load-aware greed, and the AdWords tradeoff function are not three ideas but one: an exponential discount — ψ(x) = 1 − e^{x−1}, g(y) = e^{y−1} — applied to prices, loads, or budgets, analyzed by growing primal and dual solutions in lockstep. The factor-revealing and tradeoff-revealing LPs of [1, 2] *discover* this function; the randomized primal-dual schema of [3] *explains* why it is the same function everywhere. The constant 1 − 1/e is therefore not a coincidence of three separate analyses but the value of one integral, ∫ e^{θ−1} dθ over the unit interval, surfacing wherever irrevocable decisions meet adversarial arrivals. Future progress — already visible in the random-arrival and stochastic lines [5, 7] — comes from weakening the adversary, not from improving the exponential. The schema endures because it separates the two concerns cleanly: the algorithm handles the online constraint, the dual handles the adversary, and weak duality does the rest.
