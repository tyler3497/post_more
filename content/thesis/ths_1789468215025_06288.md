# Verifiable Delay Functions: Sequentiality Proofs via Wesolowski and Pietrzak Protocols, Groups of Unknown Order, and Trustless Randomness Beacons

## Abstract

A verifiable delay function (VDF) is a function whose evaluation requires a prescribed number of sequential steps yet whose output is unique and efficiently verifiable, yielding an exponential gap between evaluation time and verification time even against parallel adversaries. This thesis develops the full theory and practice of VDFs: the formal definitional framework of correctness, soundness, and sequentiality; the Rivest–Shamir–Wagner time-lock core of repeated squaring in groups of unknown order; the two dominant proof-of-exponentiation protocols of Wesolowski and Pietrzak, with their contrasting trade-offs in proof size, prover cost, verifier cost, and underlying hardness assumptions; the trusted and trustless setup of RSA groups and class groups of imaginary quadratic fields; and the canonical application of public randomness beacons, together with consensus leader election, proof-of-replication, and energy-efficient consensus. We present theorems with proofs, worked complexity analyses, implementation notes in Rust and TLA+, empirical benchmarking guidance, and a frank assessment of the remaining open problems in tight sequentiality and post-quantum VDFs.

##1 Introduction

Many decentralized systems need *provable elapsed time*. A blockchain wants an unbiased public coin that no miner can grind; a consensus protocol wants a leader election that cannot be manipulated by whoever shouts first; a timestamping service wants to certify that data did not exist before a certain moment. All of these reduce to a primitive whose formalization was long overdue: a function that *requires* a given number of sequential steps to evaluate, produces a *unique* output, and can be verified in time exponentially smaller than the evaluation [1].

Before verifiable delay functions, the closest object was the *time-lock puzzle* of Rivest, Shamir, and Wagner: encrypt a message so that it can be decrypted only after performing *T* sequential squarings modulo a composite *N*, i.e. compute `y = x^(2^T) mod N`. Without the factorization of *N*, the only known algorithm is the naive one, and no parallelism helps because each squaring depends on the previous result. But verification of a time-lock puzzle demands the factorization as a trapdoor, which defeats public verifiability: a VDF must be verifiable by anyone using only public parameters [1][2].

The breakthrough came in 2018 with two independent constructions showing that the Rivest–Shamir–Wagner squaring chain can be *publicly verified* without knowing the group order: Pietrzak's statistically sound public-coin protocol [3], based on a recursive halving argument, and Wesolowski's construction via a short proof element combined with a prime hash challenge [2]. A companion survey [4] compared the two, providing a computational proof of security for one of them and exhibiting an attack on an incorrect implementation of the other, thereby sharpening our understanding of exactly which hardness assumptions each scheme really rests on.


##2 Background

### 2.1 The formal definition

Let `Setup(1^λ, T) → (ek, vk)` generate evaluation and verification keys for delay parameter *T*, `Eval(ek, x) → (y, π)` produce the output and proof, and `Verify(vk, x, y, π) ∈ {accept, reject}` check them. Boneh, Bonneau, Bünz, and Fisch [1] require three properties:

1. **Correctness:** honest evaluation always verifies.
2. **Soundness (uniqueness):** no polynomial-time adversary can produce a proof that verifies for an input *x* and an output `y' ≠ y` (the true output). Equivalently, at most one output admits a convincing proof.
3. **Sequentiality:** no parallel algorithm running in time *σ < T* (measured in sequential rounds) can compute the output, even with polynomially many processors. Formally, for every poly(λ)-processor parallel adversary with depth at most *σ*, the success probability is negligible. The typical normalization is *σ ≈ T·(1 − ε)* for the tight variant [6], while the standard notion asks *σ < T*.


> **Theorem (Sequentiality conjecture):** Let *N* be an RSA modulus of unknown factorization and *T* a delay parameter. No parallel algorithm with depth substantially less than *T* computes `x^(2^T) mod N` with non-negligible advantage, absent factoring *N*.

> **Theorem (Uniqueness via low order):** If the group has no low-order elements other than the identity, then for each *x* there is exactly one *y* for which a valid Wesolowski or Pietrzak proof can exist, reducing uniqueness to the low-order assumption.

### 2.2 Why groups of unknown order?


- **RSA groups** `Z_N^*` with *N = pq* a product of safe primes. Requires a trusted party to generate *N* and discard the factorization — or a trustless multi-party setup — since knowledge of the order is a trapdoor [2][3].
- **Class groups of imaginary quadratic fields** `Cl(Δ)` for a negative fundamental discriminant *Δ*. The group order is unknown, computing it is as hard as factoring *Δ*, and the discriminant can be generated transparently (e.g. `Δ = −pq` hashed deterministically), giving a *trustless setup* [2].

### 2.3 Related objects

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 The proof-of-exponentiation core

Both constructions prove the statement `(G, x, T, y)` satisfies `y = x^(2^T)` in a group of unknown order, where *T* is typically written as `2^t` with *t* the number of sequential squarings. The prover's total work is dominated by solving the underlying time-lock puzzle: it computes the repeated squarings, remembering (or recomputing) intermediate values as needed. The verifier's work must be exponentially cheaper, which is achieved by exploiting the algebraic structure of exponentiation.

### 3.2 Pietrzak's recursive halving protocol

Pietrzak [3] gives a statistically sound public-coin protocol. The key identity is a randomized reduction: to prove `y = x^(2^T)`, pick a random challenge `r`, compute `x1 = x^r · u` and `y1 = u^r · y` where `u = x^(2^(T/2))`, and recurse on proving `y1 = x1^(2^(T/2))`. Each round halves the remaining exponent; after *t* rounds the claim is trivial and checked directly. The proof consists of the *t* intermediate values `u` at each level — about 10 KB for typical parameters.

```haskell
-- Pietrzak recursive proof-of-exponentiation (conceptual)
prove :: Group -> Integer -> Integer -> (Element, [Element])
prove g x t
  | t == 0    = (x, [])
  | otherwise = (y, u : rest)
  where
    u    = pow g x (2 ^ (t `div` 2))     -- T squarings, sequential
    r    = challenge (g, x, t, u)        -- public coin / Fiat-Shamir
    x'   = pow g x r <> u
    y    = pow g x (2 ^ t)
    y'   = pow g u r <> y
    (_, rest) = prove g x' (t - 1)       -- recurse on halved exponent
```


### 3.3 Wesolowski's single-proof protocol

Wesolowski [2] builds a *trapdoor VDF* first, then instantiates it with the trapdoor unknown (not even the setup party learns it). The evaluation is `y = g^(2^T)`. The proof is a *single group element* `π = g^q` where `q = ⌊2^T / l⌋` and `l` is a prime derived by hashing the transcript (Fiat–Shamir) into a large prime set. With remainder `r = 2^T mod l`, verification checks `π^l · g^r = y`. The verifier therefore performs one prime-exponent multiexponentiation — logarithmic in the challenge size — while the prover's extra cost beyond the *T* squarings is the division to compute *q*, done with an asymptotically efficient `O(T / log T)`-style algorithm.

```python
def wesolowski_prove(g, T, l):
    # T sequential squarings; q computed alongside
    y, q = 1, 0
    x = g
    for i in range(T):
        # maintain q such that x = g^(2^i) and q = floor(2^i / l)
        x = x * x
    # in practice q is computed via the division algorithm of Wesolowski
    r = pow(2, T, l)
    pi = pow(g, q)
    return x, pi, r  # y, proof, remainder

def wesolowski_verify(g, y, pi, l, r):
    # single check: pi^l * g^r == y
    return (pow(pi, l) * pow(g, r)) == y
```

### 3.5 Setup and group instantiation

- **RSA groups with trusted setup:** a dealer generates safe primes `p, q`, publishes `N`, and destroys the factors. Alternatively, a multi-party computation generates *N* so that no single party learns the factorization.
- **Class groups (trustless):** pick discriminant `Δ` by hashing public randomness; the class group `Cl(Δ)` has unknown order by the difficulty of computing class numbers, and Wesolowski's standard generator `(2, 1, c)` makes implementations interoperable [2].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The sequentiality model

Sequentiality is modeled in the *parallel random-access machine* (PRAM) framework: an adversary is a family of circuits with *p = poly(λ)* processors and depth *d*; sequentiality demands that any adversary with `d < T` (or `d < T·(1−ε)`) succeeds only negligibly. Two subtleties matter:

1. **Processor scaling.** The definition allows polynomially many parallel processors, capturing ASIC farms and GPU clusters. The repeated-squaring conjecture is *strong* precisely because it asserts that no parallel speedup is possible at all for this specific sequential chain: each squaring's input is the previous squaring's output.
2. **Preprocessing and advice.** The adversary may receive non-uniform advice (e.g. precomputed tables), but standard conjectures hold that preprocessing does not break sequentiality for the RSW function, since the chain is input-dependent and cannot be usefully tabularized for fresh random inputs.


### 4.2 The two proof systems, precisely

**Pietrzak.** For `T = 2^t`, proof size is *t* group elements (the intermediate `u` values); the prover performs `T` squarings plus `O(t)` multiexponentiations of size up to the group order; the verifier performs `O(t)` multiexponentiations. Soundness is *statistical*: a cheating prover succeeds with probability at most roughly `3^t / 2^λ`-scale in the challenge space, independent of computational assumptions beyond the group structure. The low-order assumption is still needed for uniqueness: if the group contained an element of small order *d*, one could shift the output by that element and re-derive a valid-looking proof.


| Aspect | Pietrzak [3] | Wesolowski [2] |
|---|---|---|
| Proof size | *t* group elements (≈10 KB at *T* ≤ 2^40, 2048-bit *N*) | 1 group element |
| Verifier work | ≈ *t* multiexponentiations | 1 multiexponentiation `π^l · g^r` |
| Prover overhead | `O(t)` multiexponentiations | `O(T / log T)` division steps |
| Soundness | Statistical | Computational (adaptive root) |
| Challenge | per-round random *r* | prime *l* via hash-to-prime |
| Uniqueness basis | Low-order assumption | Low-order assumption |
| Best suited group | Class groups (no hash-to-prime) | RSA or class groups |

### 4.3 Instantiation: RSA versus class groups

The RSA group is fast: modular multiplication in `Z_N^*` is heavily optimized, and a 2048-bit squaring takes on the order of a microsecond on modern hardware, so *T = 2^30* squarings is roughly a quarter-hour on a single core. The price is setup trust. The class group is slower per operation (binary quadratic form composition and reduction), but the setup is transparent: the discriminant is derived from public randomness, and nobody learns the group order [2]. The Chia network and several VDF implementations chose class groups for exactly this reason, accepting roughly an order-of-magnitude slowdown in evaluation for a trustless setup [3].

A subtlety in RSA instantiations: the challenge prime `l` in Wesolowski's protocol must be sampled by hashing into primes, and the input must be hashed into the subgroup of quadratic residues to neutralize the sign ambiguity (the element `−1` has order 2, and a sloppy implementation can be attacked via small-order elements — the survey [4] exhibits an attack on an incorrect implementation exploiting precisely this).

```rust
// Sketch: class-group VDF evaluation (conceptual, following w3f/poanetwork style)
// Discriminant D < 0, generator g = (2, 1, c) with c from D.
fn vdf_eval(g: &Form, t: u32, discriminant: &BigInt) -> Form {
    let mut x = g.clone();
    for _ in 0..(1u64 << t) {
        x = x.square().reduce(discriminant); // sequential: no parallelism possible
    }
    x
}
// Proof (Pietrzak halving) and verification recurse on halved exponents;
// each level binds u_i = x_i^(2^(2^(t-1))) via a Fiat-Shamir challenge.
```

### 4.5 Consensus and replication applications

- **Leader election.** In proof-of-stake and BFT protocols, electing a leader from the beacon output removes the grinding attacks that plague naive hash-based election: a stakeholder cannot try many secret keys to become leader because each trial costs *T* sequential steps [1].
- **Proofs of replication.** A storage provider proves it dedicates unique physical storage by encoding data through a slow sequential function bound to its identity; the VDF's sequentiality prevents outsourcing the encoding to a faster party on demand [1][7].
- **Resource-efficient consensus.** Cohen and Pietrzak [5] combine proofs of resources with *incremental* VDFs to build consensus from proofs of space with far lower energy than proof-of-work: the VDF replaces the hash-puzzle grind with a single sequential computation whose verification is cheap.

```tla+
------------------------------- MODULE VDFBeacon -------------------------------
EXTENDS Integers, Sequences
CONSTANTS Epochs, Delay
VARIABLES published, clock
TypeOK == published \in [Epochs -> {"pending", "revealed"}]
         /\ clock \in Nat
Init == published = [e \in Epochs |-> "pending"] /\ clock = 0
Tick == clock' = clock + 1 /\ UNCHANGED published
Reveal(e) == /\ clock >= e * Delay      \* delay elapsed: output now knowable
             /\ published[e] = "pending"
             /\ published' = [published EXCEPT ![e] = "revealed"]
             /\ UNCHANGED clock
Next == Tick \/ \E e \in Epochs : Reveal(e)
Safety == \A e \in Epochs : [](published[e] = "revealed" => clock >= e * Delay)
================================================================================
```

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Principal theorems

> **Theorem (Wesolowski soundness):** Under the adaptive root assumption in a group of unknown order with no low-order elements, the non-interactive Wesolowski protocol is a VDF: evaluation needs *T* sequential squarings, the proof is one group element, and verification is a single multiexponentiation [2].

*Proof sketch.* Completeness follows from the division identity `2^T = q·l + r` with `0 ≤ r < l`: then `g^(2^T) = (g^q)^l · g^r = π^l · g^r = y`. For soundness, suppose an adversary outputs `(y', π')` with `y' ≠ y` passing verification, i.e. `π'^l · g^r = y'`. Dividing by the honest identity yields `(π'/π)^l = y'/y ≠ 1`, exhibiting a nontrivial `l`-th root of `y'/y`, contradicting the adaptive root assumption for the random prime `l` [2][4].

> **Theorem (Pietrzak statistical soundness):** The interactive halving protocol has soundness error at most `O(3^t / |R|)` where *R* is the challenge space, and the Fiat–Shamir-compiled non-interactive protocol is sound in the random oracle model [3][4].

*Proof sketch.* Each round replaces the claim `y = x^(2^T)` by a randomized claim on halved exponent; a standard rewinding argument shows that a prover succeeding with probability `ε` yields either a valid witness or a low-order element, and the challenge space is chosen so the error is negligible. The survey [4] additionally proves computational soundness under the low-order assumption alone, removing reliance on the random oracle for the core reduction.

> **Theorem (Sequentiality of repeated squaring):** In the generic group model for groups of unknown order, computing `x^(2^T)` requires *T* sequential group operations; any generic algorithm with fewer sequential steps succeeds with negligible probability [1].

*Proof sketch.* In the generic group model each new group element is a linear combination (in the exponent) of previously seen elements; producing exponent `2^T` requires doubling the maximum exponent *T* times, and doublings are inherently sequential since each needs the previous result. This is a lower bound in an idealized model — it supports, but does not prove, the concrete conjecture.

### 5.2 Parameter selection and benchmark guidance

| Parameter | Conservative choice | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Group | Class group, 2048-bit discriminant | Trustless setup [2] |
| *T* | 2^30 – 2^40 squarings | Minutes to hours per evaluation |
| Pietrzak proof | *t* = 30–40 elements | ~10 KB at 2048 bits [3] |
| Wesolowski proof | 1 element + 256-bit prime *l* | Minimal bandwidth [2] |
| Hash-to-prime | Rejection sampling on 256-bit candidates | Per [2]; must bind full transcript |
| Challenge space (Pietrzak) | ≥ 2^128 | Statistical soundness [3] |

### 5.3 The beacon security reduction

> **Theorem (Beacon unpredictability):** If the VDF is sequential with delay *T* and the hash *H* is modeled as a random oracle, then no adversary running in parallel time less than *T* predicts the beacon output `H(y_t)` for the current epoch with non-negligible advantage, even after choosing the entropy `e_t*` adaptively subject to the same bound [1].

*Proof sketch.* Predicting `H(y_t)` requires computing `y_t = Eval(e_t)`; sequentiality says this needs parallel time *T*. Grinding over *k* candidate entropies multiplies the cost by *k*, so the adversary's advantage is bounded by the number of sequential evaluations it can afford within its lookahead window. ∎

##6 Limitations

1. **Sequentiality is conjectural.** No reduction to a standard assumption (factoring, discrete log) is known for the full-strength sequentiality of repeated squaring; the best we have are generic-group-model lower bounds [1] and the absence of attacks after years of cryptanalysis. A breakthrough in parallel exponentiation algorithms or in computing class-group orders would collapse the delay.
2. **The trusted-setup problem for RSA groups.** Wesolowski's and Pietrzak's RSA instantiations need a modulus whose factorization is unknown to *everyone*, including the party that generated it. Multi-party generation ceremonies mitigate but do not eliminate this trust assumption; class groups avoid it at a performance cost [2][3].
3. **Wesolowski's adaptive root assumption is non-standard.** It is a strong, relatively new assumption compared with the statistical soundness of Pietrzak's protocol. Conservative deployments prefer Pietrzak when proof size is tolerable [4].
4. **Not post-quantum.** Both constructions live in abelian groups where Shor's algorithm computes the group order (factoring *N*, computing class numbers), destroying sequentiality entirely. Post-quantum VDFs — from isogenies [7], lattices, or hash-based designs — remain far less efficient and their sequentiality conjectures less studied.
5. **Fiat–Shamir fragility.** The non-interactive security proofs are in the random oracle model, and real hash functions with sloppy domain separation (not binding the group parameters, the input, and the delay into the challenge) admit the implementation attacks documented in [4].

##7 Conclusion

Verifiable delay functions turned the humble repeated squaring — the core of the Rivest–Shamir–Wagner time-lock puzzle — into a publicly verifiable primitive with an exponential gap between evaluation and verification. The two 2018 constructions define the design space: Pietrzak's [3] statistically sound recursive halving with *t*-element proofs, and Wesolowski's [2] single-element proof under the adaptive root assumption, both resting on groups of unknown order and both compiled to non-interactivity by Fiat–Shamir. Their comparison [4] clarified the assumption hierarchy and the implementation pitfalls. Built on this foundation, VDF-based randomness beacons give decentralized systems unbiased public coins without committees or trusted dealers, and the same sequentiality powers leader election, proofs of replication, and low-energy consensus [1][5][7]. The central open problems — a standard-model reduction for sequentiality, truly tight VDFs with practical efficiency, and post-quantum instantiations — will determine whether VDFs remain a niche cryptographic jewel or become load-bearing infrastructure for the next generation of decentralized protocols.
