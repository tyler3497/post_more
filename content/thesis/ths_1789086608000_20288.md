# Abstract Machines for the Lambda Calculus: SECD, Krivine, and the Geometry of Interaction

## Abstract

Abstract machines are the mathematically disciplined compromise between the equational purity of the lambda calculus and the brutal operational reality of physical hardware. This article presents a unified treatment of three landmark traditions in the design of such machines: Peter Landin's SECD machine, the first abstract machine conceived explicitly for evaluating lambda calculus expressions; Jean-Louis Krivine's lazy machine, a minimal and elegant device implementing call-by-name weak head reduction; and the token-passing machines born of Girard's Geometry of Interaction, which dispense with environments entirely and compute by routing a token through the graphical structure of a program. We examine the CEK machine and the general theory of environment machines alongside these, introduce linear head reduction and the Linear Substitution Calculus as the micro-step substrate unifying modern machine theory, and survey the correctness and complexity methodology — distillation, decoding, and reasonable cost models — that has transformed abstract machines from ad hoc implementations into objects of rigorous mathematical study.

## 1. Introduction

The lambda calculus is a calculus of substitution: to compute is to substitute arguments for variables, again and again, until no reducible expression remains. Yet substitution is a global, expensive, and logically subtle operation. It traverses terms, renames bound variables to avoid capture, and copies arbitrarily large subterms. No physical computer substitutes in this way. Instead, real implementations *delay* substitution through environments — finite maps from variables to values or thunks — and *localize* control through stacks, dumps, and continuations. The abstract machine is the formal device that captures exactly this engineering: a small-step, deterministic transition system over machine states whose runs correspond, step for step, to the evaluation of the source program.

The history of abstract machines for the lambda calculus is thus the history of how one idea — deferred substitution — has been refined, minimized, and ultimately geometrized. Landin's SECD machine [1] inaugurated the field in 1964 with four registers: a stack, an environment, a control list, and a dump. Two decades later, Felleisen and Friedman's CEK machine distilled the design to three components — control, environment, continuation — crystallizing the connection between machine continuations and continuation-passing style [2]. Krivine's machine [3], conceived in the mid-1980s and published in 2007, demonstrated that call-by-name evaluation requires only *three instructions* — push, access, grab — and no dump at all. And in a seemingly orthogonal direction, Girard's Geometry of Interaction (GoI), reformulated combinatorially by Danos and Regnier, gave rise to token machines that evaluate programs without any environment whatsoever, by sending a token wandering through the proof net of the program [4][5].

This article develops these threads as a single narrative. We begin with the necessary background on reduction strategies, then describe the methodology by which abstract machines are specified and proved correct. The deep dive treats each machine family in turn, culminating in the Geometry of Interaction and in linear head reduction — the micro-step strategy that modern theory uses as the universal yardstick for machine correctness. We present concrete execution traces, state the principal correctness theorems in their standard forms, and discuss what machines still cannot do.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Terms, reduction, and strategies

The untyped lambda calculus is generated by variables $x$, abstractions $\lambda x.M$, and applications $M\,N$. The fundamental computation rule is $\beta$-reduction: $(\lambda x.M)N \rightarrow_\beta M[N/x]$. The calculus is confluent — every reduction order that terminates reaches the same normal form — but *which* redex is reduced *when* determines whether evaluation terminates at all, and at what cost. A *reduction strategy* is a deterministic choice of the next redex. The principal strategies are:

- **Call-by-name (CBN):** reduce the leftmost-outermost redex; arguments are passed unevaluated. Krivine's machine implements its weak form.
- **Call-by-value (CBV):** evaluate arguments before application; Plotkin's $\lambda_v$ formalizes it. The SECD and CEK machines implement weak CBV.
- **Weak vs. strong:** *weak* reduction stops at abstractions; *strong* reduction proceeds under binders to full normal forms, as required by proof assistants.

A key subtlety is that $\beta$-reduction as usually written is *meta-level* substitution — a single step may copy a term of unbounded size. This makes $\beta$-steps a poor unit of cost. Modern theory therefore decomposes substitution into *micro-steps*: the Linear Substitution Calculus (LSC) of Accattoli [6] represents delayed substitution as explicit substitution $M[x := N]$ and performs one occurrence replacement at a time. This is the calculus into which machines are *distilled* for correctness proofs.

### 2.2 Environments and closures

All classical machines share one architectural idea, introduced by Landin [1]: a *closure* pairs a $\lambda$-abstraction with the environment in which it was created, so that free variables are resolved lexically without substitution. An environment is a list (or association structure) of closures; looking up a variable means indexing into it. This is precisely the mechanism that makes lexical scoping implementable in constant-ish time, and it is why every functional language runtime, from Lisp to OCaml's ZINC machine, is an environment machine at heart.

### 2.3 From evaluators to machines

A profound methodological insight, due to Ager, Biernacki, Danvy, and Midtgaard [2], is that abstract machines are not invented but *derived*: starting from a compositional evaluator, one applies closure conversion, transformation into continuation-passing style, and defunctionalization of the continuation, and an abstract machine drops out. Krivine's machine is literally the defunctionalized call-by-name evaluator; the CEK machine is the defunctionalized call-by-value evaluator. This *functional correspondence* explains why machines come in CBN/CBV pairs and provides a uniform recipe for proving them correct: prove the evaluator correct, then show each transformation preserves meaning.

## 3. Methodology

We treat an abstract machine as a *labelled transition system* over configurations. A typical configuration has the form $\langle C, E, K \rangle$ — control (the current code), environment, and continuation (or stack/dump) — and transitions have the form $S \leadsto S'$. The methodology for studying such machines, refined over the last two decades, has three pillars.

**Specification by distillation.** Rather than proving a machine correct directly against $\beta$-reduction, one *distills* the machine into a strategy of the Linear Substitution Calculus [6]. Concretely, one defines a *decoding* function $\underline{\cdot}$ from machine states to LSC terms, and shows a *lock-step simulation*: each machine transition corresponds to either a silent administrative step or exactly one step of the strategy, and conversely. Correctness, completeness, and complexity then transfer from the strategy to the machine. This technique, introduced by Accattoli, Barenbaum, and Mazza, and surveyed by Accattoli [7], is now the standard.

**Quantitative analysis.** A machine is *reasonable* if its overhead over the number of strategy steps is polynomial — ideally linear — in both the number of steps and the size of the initial term. Size explosion phenomena (terms that grow exponentially in the number of $\beta$-steps) mean that naive cost accounting fails; the theory of *reasonable cost models* for the lambda calculus, developed by Accattoli and Dal Lago, identifies leftmost-outermost reduction length as an invariant, machine-independent cost measure. A machine is judged against this yardstick.

**Comparative anatomy.** Machines are compared along orthogonal axes: CBN vs. CBV; weak vs. strong (whether they reduce under binders); environment-based vs. environment-free; and global vs. local environments. The table below summarizes the machines treated in Section 4.

| Machine | Year | Strategy | Components | Environment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SECD | 1964 | weak CBV | Stack, Env, Control, Dump | global |
| CEK | 1986 | weak CBV | Control, Env, Kontinuation | global |
| Krivine (KAM) | 1985/2007 | weak CBN | Code, Env, Stack | global, closures |
| Strong MAM | 2015 | strong CBN | Code, Env, Stack, frame | single global env |
| IAM (GoI) | 1990s | linear head | token + log | none |

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The SECD machine: Landin's four registers

Landin's machine [1] was the first device *designed* to evaluate lambda calculus expressions rather than adapted from arithmetic hardware. Its state is a quadruple $(S, E, C, D)$:

- **S** — a stack of intermediate values,
- **E** — an environment mapping variables to values,
- **C** — a control list of instructions (compiled code),
- **D** — a *dump*, a stack of saved $(S, E, C)$ triples serving as the return continuation.

Compilation translates a term into a linear instruction sequence. Variables compile to lookup instructions, abstractions to closure-creation instructions, and applications to a call sequence that pushes the argument, evaluates the operator to a closure, and transfers control. The characteristic transitions are:

> **Transition (application):** when control reaches an apply instruction with a closure and an argument value on the stack, the machine saves the current $(S, E, C)$ on the dump, installs the closure's environment extended with the argument, and sets control to the closure's code.

> **Transition (return):** when control is exhausted, the top value is returned by restoring $(S, E, C)$ from the dump — the dump is, in modern terminology, a *defunctionalized continuation* [2].

The SECD machine implements weak call-by-value: arguments are evaluated before the call, and reduction never proceeds under a $\lambda$. Its dump discipline is the ancestor of every call-stack mechanism in functional language runtimes. Notably, Landin gave no correctness proof; the first detailed top-down correctness argument for an SECD-like machine is Plotkin's, for his CBV calculus.

The machine's four registers look redundant today — the dump and the control stack can be fused — but that redundancy is historically precious: it exhibits, in unrefactored form, the two layers of continuations (control and meta-continuation) that Danvy's *rational deconstruction* later identified via the CPS hierarchy.

### 4.2 The CEK machine and the environment-machine paradigm

Felleisen and Friedman's CEK machine strips the SECD design to its essence: a triple $\langle C, E, K \rangle$ of *control string*, *environment*, and *continuation*, where the continuation is an explicit datatype with constructors for "evaluate the argument then apply" and "apply the function now". States transition by structural decomposition of the control term: an application $M\,N$ with continuation $K$ steps to $M$ with continuation $\text{arg}(N, E, K)$; a value with continuation $\text{arg}(N, E, K)$ steps to $N$ with continuation $\text{fun}(v, K)$; and so on. There is no dump because the continuation *is* the dump, reified as data.

The CEK machine matters for two reasons. First, it is the canonical *environment machine*: Curien's framework of *categorical* abstract machines showed that CEK-like designs are instances of a general pattern in which environments implement explicit substitution [7]. Second, it is the machine that the functional correspondence derives from an ordinary CBV evaluator [2] — defunctionalize the evaluator's continuation and the CEK transitions appear, with no ingenuity required. This makes CEK the pedagogical bridge between interpreters and machines.

### 4.3 Krivine's machine: call-by-name in three instructions

Krivine's machine (KAM) is a shock of minimalism. A state is a triple $(T, E, \pi)$ — a *code* term $T$, an environment $E$ of closures, and a stack $\pi$ of closures. Terms are compiled to a tiny instruction set, and the machine has exactly three transitions [3]:

```
state (t u,  e, pi)  ~>  (t, e, (u,e) :: pi)      ; push argument closure
state (λx.t, e, c :: pi) ~>  (t, c :: e, pi)      ; grab: bind head closure
state (x,     e, pi)  ~>  (t', e', pi)             ; access: jump to closure
       where (t', e') is the e(x)-th closure in e
```

Reading the rules operationally: to evaluate an application, *push* the unevaluated argument (as a closure, never evaluated) and continue with the operator; to evaluate an abstraction with a pending argument, *grab* the argument closure and extend the environment; to evaluate a variable, *access* its closure and resume evaluation there. That is all. No dump, no continuation datatype, no value stack — the stack holds *thunks*, and evaluation is weak head reduction to head normal form, performed call-by-name.

> **Theorem (Krivine [3]):** For every closed term $T$, the machine started in $(T, \emptyset, \emptyset)$ terminates with stack $\pi$ iff $T$ reduces by weak head reduction to a head normal form $\lambda x_1 \ldots x_n. x_i\, N_1 \ldots N_m$, and the final state decodes to that normal form.

The proof proceeds by a logical relation (a form of realizability) between machine states and terms, an early instance of the now-standard decoding methodology.

### 4.4 The Geometry of Interaction: computing without environments

The Geometry of Interaction began as Girard's operator-algebraic analysis of cut-elimination in linear logic and was recast in elementary combinatorial form by Danos and Regnier: a proof is a graph (a proof net), and cut-elimination is the *execution* of paths through that graph.

The IAM has no environment, no stack of closures, and no substitution.

This is evaluation turned inside out.

The philosophical payoff is a precise sense in which environments are *optional*: the exponentials of linear logic (the $!$ modality governing duplication) can be handled either by copying closures into environments or by the token's log discipline.

### 4.5 Linear head reduction and the Linear Substitution Calculus

*Linear head reduction* ($\multimap_{lh}$) is the strategy that substitutes, at each step, a *single* occurrence of the head variable — the leftmost-outermost occurrence in head position — rather than all occurrences at once. It is the atomic refinement of head reduction, and it is what both the KAM (at the level of closures) and the IAM (at the level of the token) implement under the hood. Its natural habitat is the Linear Substitution Calculus [7][6], where terms carry explicit substitutions $t[x := u]$ and reduction rules separate *multiplicative* steps (creating the substitution, the $\beta$-like part) from *exponential* steps (replacing one variable occurrence, the linear part).

The distillery methodology treats machines as *implementations* of LSC strategies: the KAM distills to linear head reduction; the CEK distills to linear CBV; the Strong Milner Abstract Machine distills to linear leftmost-outermost reduction to full normal form [6].

> **Theorem (reasonability of linear head reduction):** the number of linear head steps to normal form is polynomially related to the number of $\beta$-steps, making it a *reasonable* cost model — the first such model for a non-trivial strong strategy [7].

> **Theorem (distillation correctness):** each known abstract machine simulates its LSC strategy with only linear overhead in the number of steps and the size of the initial term, and the strategy simulates the machine [6].

Together these theorems do for abstract machines what Cook's theorem did for complexity: they anchor a zoo of devices to a single invariant measure of work.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

Execution traces are the empirical content of deterministic machines; simulations are their proofs.

### 5.1 A Krivine trace

Consider the term $(\lambda x. x\,x)(\lambda y. y)$ under call-by-name. Writing closures as $(t, e)$ and environments as lists, the KAM runs:

```
((λx.x x)(λy.y), [], [])            push argument
~> (λx.x x, [], [((λy.y),[])])       grab head closure
~> (x x, [((λy.y),[])], [])          decompose application: push (x, e)
~> (x, [((λy.y),[])], [((x),[((λy.y),[])])])   access x
~> (λy.y, [], [((x),[((λy.y),[])])]) grab
~> (y, [((x),[((λy.y),[])])], [])    access y
~> (x, [((λy.y),[])], [])            access x again
~> (λy.y, [], [])                    halt: head normal form λy.y
```

Six transitions produce the weak head normal form $\lambda y.y$, with the argument closure shared — never copied — in the environment. This sharing is exactly what distinguishes machine evaluation from naive substitution.

### 5.2 An SECD sketch

On the CBV term $(\lambda x.x)(\lambda y.y)$, the SECD machine compiles the application to code that evaluates the operator to a closure $c_1 = \langle \lambda x.x, E \rangle$, evaluates the operand to a closure $c_2 = \langle \lambda y.y, E \rangle$, then applies: it pushes the current $(S,E,C)$ onto the dump, extends $c_1$'s environment with $c_2$, and runs the body. The body returns $c_2$; control empties; the dump restores the caller. The trace is longer than Krivine's — argument evaluation, dump save/restore — reflecting CBV's stricter discipline. The dump discipline is precisely what defunctionalization later revealed as a *meta-continuation* [2].

### 5.3 The principal proof obligations

Every machine in Section 4 carries the same proof burden, discharged by distillation [7][6]:

1. **Simulation:** if $S \leadsto S'$ then $\underline{S} \multimap^* \underline{S'}$ with at most one principal step — the machine never does work the strategy does not do.
2. **Reflection:** every strategy step is matched by a bounded number of machine transitions — the machine never stalls.
3. **Overhead:** the number of administrative (non-principal) transitions between principal steps is bounded linearly in the size of the initial term.

For the IAM, obligation (1) is the hard one: the token's path must be shown to correspond to a linear head derivation, which is why the first proofs detoured through proof nets and why the direct bisimulation proof of [5] is a genuine advance. For strong machines, obligation (3) requires controlling *backtracking phases* — the machine's search for the next redex under binders — which the Strong Milner Abstract Machine bounds using a single global environment [6].

## 6. Limitations

The theory is not complete, and the machines are not universal.

**Strong evaluation remains hard.** Weak machines (SECD, CEK, KAM) are well understood and reasonable, but strong evaluation — reduction under binders, needed by proof assistants — long resisted efficient implementation. The Strong MAM [6] was the first machine with linear overhead for leftmost-outermost reduction to normal form, and it relies on delicate backtracking discipline; extending comparable guarantees to *all* strong strategies is open.

**The IAM's complexity is unresolved.** Token machines use asymptotically little space — no environments — but their *time* behavior is subtle: the token may revisit nodes exponentially many times on certain terms, and no general reasonable-time bound is known. The IAM is a superb semantic device and a poor implementation technique, a tension the field has yet to resolve.

**Effects and control are second-class.** The classical machines model the pure lambda calculus. Control operators, state, and concurrency each require new machine features (control stacks, store-passing, multi-token geometries [5]), and the elegant distillation story fragments: there is no single LSC-like substrate for arbitrary effects.

**Cost models vs. hardware.** Reasonable cost models relate machines to each other, not to silicon. Cache behavior, closure layout, and garbage collection dominate real performance, and the abstraction gap between a transition count and wall-clock time remains unbridged by theory.

---

## 7. Conclusion

From Landin's four registers to Krivine's three instructions to a token wandering a proof net, the sixty-year arc of abstract machines is a story of *progressive dematerialization*: environments shrink from global association lists to shared closures to nothing at all, while control migrates from an explicit dump into defunctionalized continuations and finally into the geometry of the program itself. The modern synthesis — distillation into the Linear Substitution Calculus, linear head reduction as the universal micro-step strategy, and polynomial-overhead reasonability theorems — has turned this history into a science: machines are no longer tricks for implementing languages but mathematical objects with provable, comparable, quantitative behavior.

The open frontier is to extend that science where it is weakest: strong evaluation with tight bounds, the time complexity of token machines, and a uniform account of effects. If the past is any guide, the next machine will be even smaller than a token — and its correctness proof will teach us something new about what computation is.
