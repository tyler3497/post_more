# Separation Logic and the Iris Framework: Modular Verification of Fine-Grained Concurrent Programs via Ghost State, Invariants, and Mechanized Weakest Preconditions

## Abstract

Fine-grained concurrent programs defeat conventional verification: thread interleavings explode combinatorially, shared mutable state resists local reasoning, and linearization arguments for lock-free data structures are notoriously subtle. The Iris framework — a higher-order concurrent separation logic mechanized in Coq — confronts this complexity with a strikingly small conceptual core. Two orthogonal ingredients carry almost the entire burden: resource algebras (partial commutative monoids) that let users invent their own ghost state, and impredicative invariants that tie that ghost state to physical memory, from which Hoare triples, weakest preconditions, logical atomicity, and protocol reasoning are all derived rather than axiomatized. This thesis reconstructs the semantic foundations of Iris, explains the ghost-state discipline and the update modality that make thread-local reasoning sound under concurrency, and shows how logically atomic triples internalize linearization points for fine-grained data structures. We survey the proof-mode machinery (MoSeL/IPM) that makes interactive verification tractable, and evaluate Iris through landmark case studies — RustBelt, Diaframe, Trillium, Simuliris, and the VST and CompCert instances — before mapping the frontiers where mechanized concurrency verification still strains.

##1 Introduction

Concurrency is the defining axis of modern computing, and also its most punishing source of defects. A fine-grained concurrent data structure — a lock-free queue, a scalable counter, a work-stealing deque — may execute correctly on a million test runs and still harbor a race that manifests only under a rare interleaving. Testing samples executions; verification must quantify over all of them. The central difficulty is *interference*: every thread's reasoning about shared state is invalidated by every other thread's steps, so naive Hoare logic, which assumes exclusive ownership of the state it reasons about, collapses the moment a second thread appears.

Separation logic recovered local reasoning for sequential heap programs through the *frame rule*: if a command is correct in a small footprint, it remains correct in any larger heap [1]. Concurrent separation logic (CSL) extended this idea to threads, but early CSLs were coarse-grained — they handled locks and fork-join parallelism, not the optimistic, CAS-driven algorithms that dominate high-performance systems. A decade of research produced an alphabet soup of specialized logics (Rely-Guarantee separation logics, CAP, iCAP, TaDA, FCSL), each adding bespoke machinery — regions, guards, atomicity tracking — for the next class of examples.

Iris broke this cycle by asking a different question: what is the *minimal* core from which all this machinery can be derived [1][2]? Its answer — partial commutative monoids plus invariants, the slogan *"monoids and invariants are all you need"* — turned out to be remarkably durable. Iris is not a single program logic but a *framework* for building program logics: a higher-order, impredicative base logic mechanized in Coq, on top of which weakest preconditions, Hoare triples, and atomic triples for a language like HeapLang are defined as derived notions. This thesis examines that design in depth: the resource-algebra theory of ghost state, the invariant discipline that makes sharing sound, the weakest-precondition calculus that unifies program logics, the logical-atomicity machinery that internalizes linearizability proofs, and the Coq mechanization that makes it all machine-checked.

Our contributions are expository but substantive:

- **Foundations.** A self-contained reconstruction of the Iris base logic, its model in step-indexed resources, and the role of the later modality in stratifying impredicative definitions [2].
- **Ghost state.** An account of cameras/resource algebras (CMRAs) as a user-extensible theory of fictional state, including authoritative and fractional constructions [1].
- **Sharing.** The invariant protocol — allocation, opening, namespaces, and the update modality — that lets threads exchange ownership through shared state [2].
- **Atomicity.** How logically atomic triples encode linearization points, and how they connect to the external notion of linearizability [8].
- **Mechanization.** The MoSeL proof mode and the HeapLang program logic that turn Iris into a practical Coq verification tool [4].
- **Evidence.** A survey of landmark deployments — RustBelt [3], Diaframe [4], Trillium [5], Simuliris [6], the VST and CompCert instances [7][8], and the AxSL weak-memory logic [9] — with an assessment of what each proves about Iris's expressiveness and cost.

##2 Background

### From Hoare logic to separation logic

Hoare logic reasons about programs with triples `{P} c {Q}`: if `P` holds before `c` runs, then `Q` holds after. For heap-manipulating programs this is inadequate, because assertions describe the *entire* heap and every command must account for all of it. Separation logic's innovation is the *separating conjunction* `P ∗ Q`, which asserts that the heap can be split into disjoint parts satisfying `P` and `Q` respectively, together with the points-to assertion `l ↦ v` for exclusive ownership of a single heap cell. The frame rule then restores locality:

> **Theorem (Frame).** If `{P} c {Q}` holds, then `{P ∗ R} c {Q ∗ R}` holds for any frame `R` untouched by `c`.

A command verified against its footprint automatically scales to larger heaps — the foundation of modular, thread-local reasoning [1].

### The concurrency barrier

O'Hearn's concurrent separation logic added a parallel composition rule: `{P1} c1 {Q1}` and `{P2} c2 {Q2}` compose to `{P1 ∗ P2} c1 || c2 {Q1 ∗ Q2}`, provided the threads touch disjoint state or synchronize through locks governed by *resource invariants*. This handles coarse-grained locking beautifully but fails for fine-grained algorithms, where threads deliberately race on shared cells (think of a Treiber stack's CAS loop) and where the "invariant" of a location changes with the protocol phase. The response was a proliferation of logics with ever more elaborate built-in protocol machinery.

### TaDA and logical atomicity

A decisive advance was TaDA's notion of *logical atomicity*: a concurrent operation may take many physical steps yet *appear* to take effect at a single instant — its *linearization point*. TaDA expressed this with atomic triples `⟨P⟩ c ⟨Q⟩`, where the pre- and postcondition describe shared state that the operation transforms atomically at some point during its execution. Iris internalized this idea: atomic triples are *defined* in terms of view shifts (the update modality) rather than taken as primitive [2][8]. As the VST-on-Iris work explains, logical atomicity gives canonical specifications for data-structure operations that can be specialized with different ghost state and invariants — and when the data structure is linearizable, its atomic specification combines with a history invariant to support linearizability-based client reasoning [8].

### Step-indexing and impredicativity

Higher-order concurrent programs — programs that store functions, callbacks, or even *assertions* in the heap — demand *impredicative* invariants: invariants whose bodies may mention invariants themselves. Naive impredicativity is unsound (it admits self-referential paradoxes), and the standard remedy is *step-indexing*: stratifying the model by the number of computation steps remaining, guarded by the *later* modality `▷`. Iris's model is built on step-indexed resources from the ground up, which is what makes its invariants truly higher-order [2]. The cost is that every invariant access and every recursive definition passes through `▷`, a friction the Iris community has spent years engineering away with tactics and, more recently, transfinite and non-step-indexed variants [5][6].

##3 Methodology

This thesis is a systematic synthesis rather than a single new verification. Our method proceeds in five stages:

1. **Literature consolidation.** We survey the Iris lineage from the POPL 2015 monoids-and-invariants paper [1] through the JFP "from the ground up" account of Iris 3.1 [2] to the 2024 distributed and weak-memory extensions [5][9], extracting the stable conceptual core beneath notation changes.
2. **Semantic reconstruction.** We rebuild the key definitions — CMRAs, the base logic, invariants, weakest preconditions — in a uniform notation, emphasizing *why* each piece is shaped as it is.
3. **Mechanism analysis.** For each verification pattern (ghost protocols, invariant-based sharing, logical atomicity), we identify the exact Iris primitive that enables it and the proof rule that packages it for users.
4. **Case-study evaluation.** We examine six landmark deployments spanning type soundness [3], automation [4], distribution [5], compiler correctness [6], C verification [7][8], and weak memory [9], asking what each demonstrates and what it cost.
5. **Gap analysis.** We collect the recurring limitations — proof effort, automation ceilings, step-indexing friction, memory-model assumptions — into a research agenda.

A note on notation: we write `P ∗ Q` for separating conjunction, `P −∗ Q` for separating implication (magic wand), `l ↦ v` for points-to, `▷ P` for "later", `|==> P` for the update modality (view shift), `WP e {{ Φ }}` for weakest preconditions, and `⟨x. P⟩ e ⟨v. Q⟩` for logically atomic triples. Coq snippets use Iris/HeapLang conventions.

##4 Deep Dive

###4.1 Resource Algebras: Ghost State as a User-Defined Theory

The first pillar of Iris is the radical idea that *ghost state is not fixed by the logic* — it is a parameter.

Two constructions do most of the heavy lifting:

- **Authoritative monoids.** `authR` pairs an authoritative element (the "real" current value) with fragments (observations held by threads). A fragment can only be updated in agreement with the authority, which is exactly the shape of a shared variable with a protocol: threads hold fragments describing what they know, and the authoritative ghost tracks the ground truth. Invariants typically connect the authoritative ghost to physical state.
- **Fractional permissions.** `l ↦{q} v` splits heap ownership into fractions `q` drawn from the positive rationals up to one; full fraction `1` grants write permission, smaller fractions grant read-only sharing. Fractions compose by addition, and the CMRA laws guarantee that two threads can never hold writable fractions simultaneously — data-race freedom falls out of the algebra.

The laws can even be stated as executable checks.

```haskell
class CMRA a where
  valid   :: a -> Bool            -- ✓ a : the resource is coherent
  compose :: a -> a -> Maybe a    -- a ⋅ b : disjoint combination
  core    :: a -> a               -- |a| : the duplicable part

-- frame-preserving update: b can replace a whenever
-- every frame compatible with a stays compatible with b
fpUpdate :: CMRA a => a -> a -> Bool
fpUpdate a b = all (\f -> valid (compose a f) ==> valid (compose b f)) frames
```

> **Theorem (Ghost adequacy).** Ghost updates justified by frame-preserving updates are sound with respect to the step-indexed model: no thread can observe a ghost transition that its own resources do not permit [2].

This is the sense in which Iris's slogan is earned: permissions, tokens, capabilities, histories, and protocols are all *instances* of one algebraic theory.

###4.2 Invariants and the Update Modality: Sharing Without Chaos

If ghost state is the vocabulary of protocols, *invariants* are the grammar of sharing.

- **Allocation.** Giving up ownership of `P` (one step later, under `▷`) creates a persistent invariant: `▷ P ⊢ |==> knowInv N P`. The namespace `N` prevents reentrancy bugs — an invariant cannot be opened twice in nested fashion, which would be unsound.
- **Opening.** During a single *atomic* physical step, a thread may temporarily take `P` out of the invariant, use or even break it, and must reestablish it before the step completes. The later modality `▷` appears on both sides: contents are available one step later, and must be restored one step later.
- **Persistence.** Invariants are duplicable (`□ knowInv N P`): every thread knows them forever, which is precisely what makes them shareable.

The *update modality* `E1 |==> E2 P` is the engine underneath.

```coq
Definition is_lock {Σ} `{!spinLockG Σ} (γ : gname) (l : loc) : iProp Σ :=
  inv lockN (∃ b : bool, l ↦ #b ∗ lock_state γ b)%I.

Lemma alloc_lock {Σ} `{!spinLockG Σ} (l : loc) b :
  l ↦ #b ==∗ ∃ γ, is_lock γ l ∗ lock_state γ b.
Proof.
  iIntros "Hl". iMod (own_alloc (Excl b)) as (γ) "Hγ".
  iMod (inv_alloc lockN _ (∃ b : bool, l ↦ #b ∗ own γ (Excl b))%I
    with "[Hl Hγ]") as "#Hinv".
  { iNext. iExists b. iFrame. }
  iModIntro. iExists γ. iFrame "#∗".
Qed.
```

The proof allocates ghost state, bundles the physical points-to with the ghost into an invariant, and hands back a persistent lock assertion plus the initial ghost fragment — the standard Iris idiom, discharged with MoSeL tactics (`iMod`, `iFrame`, `iNext`) that hide the step-index bookkeeping [4].

###4.3 Weakest Preconditions: One Calculus for All Program Logics

Iris's most architecturally striking decision is that *Hoare triples are not primitive*.

```
{P} e {v. Q}  ≜  □ (P −∗ WP e {{ v. Q }})
```

The WP calculus has the familiar structural rules — bind, frame, and per-construct rules for load, store, allocation, CAS — but because WP is defined once and for all over the language semantics, *any* program logic (sequential Hoare logic, CSL, atomic triples) can be built on top by proving its rules as lemmas about WP.

> **Theorem (WP adequacy).** If `⊢ WP e {{ v. ⌜φ v⌝ }}` is derivable for a closed program `e`, then `e` never gets stuck, and if it terminates with value `v`, the meta-level property `φ v` holds [2].

This is what makes Iris a *framework* rather than a logic: instantiating Iris for a new language means defining its WP over the new operational semantics and reaping the entire base logic — ghost theory, invariants, modalities, and the Coq proof mode — unchanged.

###4.4 Logical Atomicity: Linearization Points Inside the Logic

Fine-grained data structures defeat ordinary Hoare triples because their operations interleave: a concurrent queue's `enqueue` may execute dozens of physical steps while other threads observe intermediate states.

```
⟨x. P⟩ e ⟨v. Q⟩
```

Here `P` and `Q` describe *shared* state (typically inside invariants) before and after the linearization point; the bound variable `x` is instantiated at that point.

In Iris's surface syntax the general form carries local (private) and public pre/postconditions:

```coq
Lemma treiber_push_spec {Σ} `{!stackG Σ} (γ : gname) (s : loc) (v : val) :
  is_stack γ s -∗
  <<< ∀∀ (xs : list val), stack_content γ xs >>>
    push s v @ ⊤
  <<< stack_content γ (v :: xs), RET #() >>>.
```

> **Theorem (Atomicity implies linearizability).** A logically atomic specification for a concurrent object, combined with an invariant relating its abstract state to a sequential history, entails linearizability with respect to the sequential specification [8].

The converse direction — that every linearizable object admits such a specification — has been investigated as a completeness result for logical atomicity, closing the loop between the logic-internal notion and the textbook definition.

##5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

Iris is ultimately judged by what has been *proved* in it. The following table surveys landmark deployments; each is a machine-checked Coq development, not a paper proof.

| Project | Domain | Year | What was proved |
|---|---|---|---|
| RustBelt [3] | Safe systems programming | 2018 | Type soundness of Rust's lifetime/borrow discipline via a semantic model in Iris; Stacked Borrows follow-ups extend it to aliasing |
| Diaframe [4] | Proof automation | 2022 | A *foundational* yet automated verifier for fine-grained programs: proof search produces closed Coq proofs w.r.t. the operational semantics |
| Trillium [5] | Distributed systems | 2024 | Intensional refinement for message-passing programs; higher-order concurrent *and* distributed separation logic in one framework |
| Simuliris [6] | Compiler correctness | 2022 | Termination-preserving concurrent program optimizations exploiting data-race UB, on a non-step-indexed Iris variant |
| VST on Iris [7][8] | C verification | 2024 | The Verified Software Toolchain's program logic reimplemented in Iris over CompCert semantics — real C, verified compiler, one logic |
| AxSL [9] | Weak memory | 2024 | A separation logic for Arm-A relaxed concurrency, mechanized in Iris with higher-order ghost state |

Three observations cut across these case studies. **First**, the WP-genericity claim of §4.3 is real: each project defines its language semantics and inherits the base logic, tactics, and ghost theory wholesale [5][7]. **Second**, logical atomicity scales: from Treiber stacks to Rust's borrow checker, the atomic-triple discipline handles both textbook structures and industrial-strength type systems [3][8]. **Third**, the frontier keeps moving outward — from shared-memory threads to distributed message passing [5], from sequential consistency to Arm-A weak memory [9], from program verification to verified compilation [6].

A concrete taste of the proof experience: verifying a spin lock's `acquire` in Iris/HeapLang proceeds by opening the lock invariant around the atomic CAS step, using the update modality to flip the ghost from "unlocked" to "locked", and closing the invariant — roughly fifteen lines of MoSeL tactic script for the core loop, with the invariant protocol doing the heavy lifting that, in earlier logics, required bespoke atomicity machinery [2][4]. Diaframe pushes this further: its proof search *automatically* discovers invariant applications and ghost updates for many textbook examples, emitting foundational proofs the user never had to write by hand [4].

##6 Limitations

Iris's power comes with honest costs, and the literature is candid about them.

- **Proof engineering effort.** Even with MoSeL, substantial verifications demand expert labor. RustBelt-scale projects consume person-years; the tactic language helps but does not remove the need to *design* ghost protocols, which remains a creative act [3].
- **Automation ceilings.** Diaframe's proof search is impressive but incomplete by design: it targets a fragment of Iris reasoning, and programs outside that fragment fall back to manual proof. Fully automatic verification of arbitrary fine-grained code in Iris is not on the horizon [4].
- **Step-indexing friction.** The later modality `▷` infects every invariant access and recursive definition. Years of engineering (fancy updates, timeless propositions, transfinite step-indexing, and Simuliris's non-step-indexed variant) have dulled the pain, but the underlying stratification occasionally surfaces in user-visible goals [2][6].
- **Memory-model assumptions.** Classic Iris assumes sequentially consistent shared memory. Real hardware is weaker, and while AxSL shows the framework *can* host a weak-memory logic, each memory model currently requires its own substantial instantiation rather than falling out of the base theory [9].
- **Linearization-point identification.** Logical atomicity presupposes that the verifier can *find* the linearization point. For algorithms with future-dependent linearization points (where the commit point depends on later thread actions), prophecy variables help, but the proofs remain among the hardest in the corpus [2].
- **Trusted base and ergonomics.** Adequacy rests on the Coq kernel, the language semantics, and the Iris model itself — a large, if carefully audited, foundation. Namespace management for invariants and mask discipline in the update modality are persistent sources of beginner errors [2][5].

None of these limitations is unique to Iris — they are the costs of machine-checked concurrency verification as such. What distinguishes Iris is how much of the *logic design* burden it removes, leaving users to spend their effort on protocols and programs rather than on metatheory.

##7 Conclusion

Iris's enduring contribution is architectural: it demonstrated that the sprawling zoo of concurrent separation logics could be consolidated into a small, mechanized core — resource algebras for ghost state, invariants for sharing, weakest preconditions for program reasoning — from which the fancy features of a decade of logics *derive* rather than being postulated [1][2]. Around that core grew an ecosystem: a proof mode that makes interactive verification practical, atomic triples that internalize linearizability, and instantiations spanning Rust, C, distributed systems, compilers, and weak memory [3][4][5][6][7][8][9].

The research frontier is now about *reach* rather than *foundation*: pushing Iris reasoning onto weaker memory models, automating more of the ghost-protocol design burden, and scaling mechanized proofs to concurrent systems of industrial size. If the last decade consolidated the theory of concurrent separation logic, the next will test whether that theory can become routine engineering practice. The evidence so far — from borrow checkers to verified compilers — suggests the bet is paying off.
