# Halide Perovskite Photovoltaics: Defect Tolerance, Ion Migration, and the Tandem-Cell Frontier

## Abstract

Halide perovskites have progressed from a laboratory curiosity — methylammonium lead iodide sensitizers delivering 3.8% efficiency in 2009 — to the most rapidly improving photovoltaic technology in history, with single-junction efficiencies exceeding 26% and monolithic perovskite–silicon tandems certified above 34%. This thesis develops a unified physical account of three pillars underpinning that trajectory: *defect tolerance*, the anomalous electronic structure that renders most native point defects electronically benign; *ion migration*, the field-driven transport of halide vacancies and interstitials that produces hysteresis and long-term degradation; and *the tandem-cell frontier*, where compositionally engineered wide-bandgap perovskites are stacked atop silicon to breach the single-junction Shockley–Queisser limit. We review formation energetics and charge-transition levels from density-functional theory, derive the Shockley–Read–Hall kinetics distinguishing shallow from deep traps, analyze drift–diffusion models of mobile ionic species, and quantify current-matching constraints in tandem architectures. Drawing on recent milestones — graded-dielectric tandems at 34.18%, co-crystal 2D passivation retaining 95% efficiency after 5,000 h, and all-perovskite tandems with record 2.19 V open-circuit voltage — we conclude with the stability, lead-management, and scale-up barriers separating record cells from terawatt deployment.

## 1. Introduction

The photovoltaic effect in *organometal halide perovskites* burst onto the scientific stage in 2009, when Kojima, Teshima, Shirai, and Miyasaka reported dye-sensitized-style cells using CH₃NH₃PbI₃ and CH₃NH₃PbBr₃ as visible-light absorbers [1]. Within fifteen years, solution-processed halide perovskites had overtaken two decades of organic-photovoltaic research and closed in on the performance of crystalline silicon — a material whose optimization spans seven decades. This ascent is all the more remarkable because perovskite absorbers are grown from low-temperature spin-coating or blade-coating with defect densities of 10¹⁵–10¹⁷ cm⁻³, orders of magnitude above the 10¹⁰–10¹² cm⁻³ typical of zone-refined silicon.

Three intertwined phenomena explain both the promise and the peril of this material class:

1. **Defect tolerance.** The dominant native point defects — lead and halide vacancies, interstitials, and antisites — overwhelmingly produce *shallow* rather than *deep* charge-transition levels. Recent photoluminescence-resolved spectroscopy has shown that recombination in state-of-the-art compositions is governed by shallow defects, contradicting the long-held assumption that deep midgap states dominate [5][6].
2. **Ion migration.** The same soft ionic lattice that enables low-temperature processing also hosts mobile halide ions and vacancies with migration barriers as low as 0.1–0.6 eV, driving current–voltage hysteresis, light-induced phase segregation, and thermally activated degradation [2].
3. **Tandem integration.** Compositional tuning of the A-site cation (Cs⁺, MA⁺, FA⁺) and the halide (I⁻, Br⁻, Cl⁻) allows the bandgap to be continuously adjusted from ≈1.2 eV to ≈2.3 eV, making perovskites the ideal top-cell partner for silicon in monolithic tandems that have now surpassed 34% certified efficiency [4][8].

This thesis treats each pillar in depth, grounding the discussion in defect thermodynamics, non-equilibrium recombination kinetics, ionic transport theory, and device physics, before synthesizing the remaining roadblocks on the path to industrialization.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Crystal Chemistry of ABX₃ Perovskites

Halide perovskites crystallize in the *ABX₃* structure, where the A-site is a large monovalent cation (Cs⁺, CH₃NH₃⁺ = MA⁺, HC(NH₂)₂⁺ = FA⁺), the B-site is typically Pb²⁺ (or Sn²⁺ in lead-free variants), and X is a halide anion. The Goldschmidt tolerance factor,

$$t = \frac{r_A + r_X}{\sqrt{2}\,(r_B + r_X)},$$

together with the octahedral factor μ = r_B/r_X, determines whether the corner-sharing BX₆ octahedra adopt a cubic, tetragonal, or orthorhombic distortion. Compositions near t ≈ 0.9–1.0 exhibit the most symmetric, most electronically desirable phases.

The *electronic structure* is unusual among photovoltaic absorbers. First-principles calculations reveal that:

- The **valence band maximum (VBM)** derives primarily from antibonding hybridization between Pb 6s and I 5p orbitals.
- The **conduction band minimum (CBM)** is dominated by Pb 6p states.
- This *antibonding* character of the VBM is the electronic root of defect tolerance: removing an iodine atom from the lattice perturbs antibonding states that already lie near the band edge, so the associated dangling-bond level tends to fall **inside** or just outside the bands rather than deep in the gap [5].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Computational Framework

Our theoretical analysis rests on three standard computational pillars:

1. **Density-functional theory (DFT).** Defect formation energies are computed in the supercell formalism:

$$\Delta H_f(D^q) = E_{\text{tot}}(D^q) - E_{\text{tot}}(\text{host}) - \sum_i n_i \mu_i + q(E_F + E_{\text{VBM}}) + \Delta E_{\text{corr}},$$

where n_i counts atoms exchanged with chemical reservoirs at potentials μ_i, q is the defect charge state, and ΔE_corr collects finite-size electrostatic corrections (FNV or Kumagai–Oba schemes). Charge-transition levels ε(q/q′) are obtained from the Fermi-level positions at which ΔH_f(D^q) = ΔH_f(D^q′).

2. **Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) kinetics.** The net recombination rate through a trap of density N_t at energy E_t is

$$R_{\text{SRH}} = \frac{np - n_i^2}{\tau_p(n + n_1) + \tau_n(p + p_1)},$$

with n₁ = N_c exp(−(E_c − E_t)/kT) and p₁ = N_v exp(−(E_t − E_v)/kT). A *shallow* trap (E_t near a band edge) yields an exponentially suppressed minority-carrier capture asymmetry; a *deep* midgap trap maximizes R_SRH. This is the quantitative language in which the shallow-defect-dominance result of Kirchartz et al. must be understood [5].

3. **Coupled ionic–electronic drift–diffusion.** Ion migration is modeled via the Nernst–Planck equation for mobile species (iodide vacancies V_I⁺, interstitials I_i⁻, MA⁺ vacancies) coupled to Poisson's equation and electronic continuity equations, solved self-consistently over seconds-to-hours timescales to reproduce hysteresis and slow transient behavior.

### 3.2 Experimental Correlates

The theoretical claims are anchored to measurements: intensity- and time-resolved photoluminescence spanning nine orders of magnitude in excitation density [5], steady-state PL quantum-yield analysis of surface passivation [9], X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy of P–O–Pb bonding in phosphonic-acid-treated films [9], and maximum-power-point tracking under one-sun illumination for 1,000–5,000 h [3][4].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Origins of Defect Tolerance

Conventional photovoltaic wisdom, codified in the 1952 Shockley–Read and Hall analysis, holds that non-radiative recombination is dominated by *deep* defects — states near midgap equally accessible to electrons and holes. Halide perovskites violate this expectation in a way that was, until recently, only theoretically predicted [5][6].

The mechanism has three components:

> **Theorem (Defect-tolerance triad):** *A semiconductor exhibits defect tolerance when (i) its valence band derives from antibonding states, placing vacancy levels near the band edge; (ii) its lattice is soft and polar, so that strong electron–phonon coupling screens charged defects; and (iii) the dominant native defects have low formation energies only in charge states whose transition levels are shallow.*

Concretely, in MAPbI₃ the lowest-energy native defects — the iodine vacancy V_I, the MA vacancy V_MA, the Pb vacancy V_Pb, and their interstitial counterparts — all exhibit ε(q/q′) within ~0.05–0.15 eV of a band edge. The one exception of practical concern, the iodine interstitial I_i, can introduce deeper levels, but its formation energy is high under iodine-poor growth conditions and it is readily passivated by Lewis-base chemistry [3].

Two further effects amplify tolerance:

- **Dielectric screening.** The static dielectric constant ε_r ≈ 25–70 (with large ionic contributions) suppresses the Coulomb capture radius of charged defects, r_c = q²/(4πεkT), slowing trap-assisted capture.
- **Large-polaron formation.** Carriers dressed by the polar lattice couple weakly to localized defect potentials, reducing capture cross-sections by orders of magnitude relative to rigid covalent semiconductors.

The experimental confirmation arrived via a photoluminescence technique capable of resolving decay over timescales from nanoseconds to 170 μs and excitation densities spanning nine orders of magnitude. The FZ Jülich team found that the differential carrier decay time obeys a power law — the signature of *shallow*-defect-dominated recombination — in Cs₀.₀₅FA₀.₇₃MA₀.₂₂PbI₂.₅₆Br₀.₄₄ films, concluding that deep defects are essentially absent and that shallow states, not midgap traps, set the efficiency ceiling [5].

A minimal Python model illustrates the SRH asymmetry between shallow and deep traps:

```python
import numpy as np

kT = 0.02585  # eV at 300 K
Eg = 1.55     # eV, MAPbI3 bandgap

def srh_rate(n, p, Nt, Et, sigma_n=1e-15, sigma_p=1e-15, vth=1e7):
    """Shockley-Read-Hall rate for a trap at energy Et (eV above VBM)."""
    Nc, Nv = 1e19, 1e19
    n1 = Nc * np.exp(-(Eg - Et) / kT)
    p1 = Nv * np.exp(-Et / kT)
    tau_n = 1.0 / (sigma_n * vth * Nt)
    tau_p = 1.0 / (sigma_p * vth * Nt)
    ni2 = Nc * Nv * np.exp(-Eg / kT)
    return (n * p - ni2) / (tau_p * (n + n1) + tau_n * (p + p1))

n = p = 1e15          # 1-sun injection, cm^-3
for label, Et in [("deep midgap", Eg/2), ("shallow", 0.08)]:
    print(label, f"{srh_rate(n, p, 1e15, Et):.2e} cm^-3 s^-1")
```

The shallow trap produces a recombination rate several orders of magnitude below the deep-trap case at equal density — the quantitative essence of defect tolerance.

### 4.2 Passivation Chemistry: From PbI₂ Excess to Co-Crystal 2D Interlayers

Defect tolerance does not imply defect *irrelevance*: surfaces, grain boundaries, and interfaces still host under-coordinated Pb²⁺ and halide vacancies that pin the open-circuit voltage. The field's passivation strategies have evolved through three generations:

1. **PbI₂ excess engineering.** Early devices deliberately incorporated excess PbI₂, which segregates to grain boundaries and passivates under-coordinated sites. However, excess PbI₂ photodecomposes under illumination, degrading stability and introducing hysteresis. A decisive advance converted this liability into an asset: doping with RbCl transforms residual PbI₂ into a new stable phase (PbI₂)₂RbCl, tripling the ion-migration activation energy while retaining the passivation benefit — yielding 25.6% certified efficiency with markedly improved thermal stability at 85 °C [2].
2. **Molecular passivation.** Phosphonic acids (e.g., pentafluorobenzyl phosphonic acid, pFBPA) post-deposited on triple-cation films reduce non-radiative V_oc loss from 107 mV to 85 mV, evidenced by P–O–Pb bond formation at ~145 eV in the Pb 4f XPS spectrum and suppression of metallic Pb⁰ [9].
3. **Co-crystal 2D interlayers.** The most recent advance replaces conventional ionic bulky cations with *neutral* benzoguanamine (BGA) coformers that assemble into a stoichiometric 2D perovskite co-crystal via intermolecular interactions rather than ion exchange. The BGA phase selectively passivates both halide and cationic vacancies through Lewis adducts, blocks ion migration, and suppresses outgassing of volatile MA⁺ — delivering 95% efficiency retention after 5,000 h of one-sun MPP tracking in 48 cm² modules [3].

### 4.3 Ion Migration: The Soft Lattice's Dark Side

Halide perovskites are *mixed ionic–electronic conductors*. The activation energies for vacancy-mediated halide migration computed by DFT are strikingly low — ≈0.08–0.1 eV for V_I along octahedral edges in some pathways, ≈0.3–0.6 eV for MA⁺ vacancy hopping — meaning ionic conductivity is non-negligible even at room temperature. Three consequences follow:

- **J–V hysteresis.** Mobile ions screen the built-in field on second-to-minute timescales, so forward and reverse voltage sweeps sample different internal field profiles. Standardized stabilized-power-output measurements are now mandatory for credible efficiency claims.
- **Light-induced halide segregation.** In mixed I/Br wide-bandgap compositions, illumination drives demixing into iodide-rich and bromide-rich domains, red-shifting the photoluminescence and eroding the tandem top-cell bandgap. Graded-dielectric and compositional strategies that smooth internal field profiles have been shown to inhibit halide accumulation and stabilize 1.68 eV top cells [4].
- **Degradation.** Field-driven ion pile-up at interfaces accelerates chemical reactions with transport layers and electrodes, particularly under reverse bias in partially shaded modules.

A compact TLA⁺ specification captures the essence of the coupled ionic–electronic hysteresis model:

```tla
---------------------------- MODULE IonHysteresis ----------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals

CONSTANTS Ions,       \* mobile ionic species: {VI, Ii, VMA}
          Sites,      \* discrete lattice sites across the absorber
          Vext        \* externally applied bias sequence

VARIABLES rho,        \* ionic charge density per site
          phi,        \* electrostatic potential per site
          swept       \* direction of the J-V sweep: "fwd" | "rev"

IonStep ==
    \* one mobile ion hops to a neighboring site, biased by -grad phi
    \E i \in Ions, s \in Sites :
        /\ rho' = [rho EXCEPT ![s] = rho[s] - 1,
                               ![Nbr(s)] = rho[Nbr(s)] + 1]
        /\ phi' = SolvePoisson(rho')
        /\ UNCHANGED swept

SweepStep ==
    \* advance the external bias; ions lag behind electrons (fast) 
    /\ Vext' \in NextBias(Vext, swept)
    /\ rho' = rho          \* ionic configuration frozen on electronic timescale
    /\ phi' = SolvePoisson(rho')  \* with new boundary conditions
    /\ UNCHANGED swept

Next == IonStep \/ SweepStep
=============================================================================
```

The key modeling insight — *ions frozen on electronic timescales, electrons slaved to the ionic configuration* — is what reproduces the sweep-direction asymmetry observed experimentally.

### 4.4 The Tandem-Cell Frontier

Single-junction perovskites are approaching their practical ceiling near the Shockley–Queisser limit (~31% at 1.55 eV under detailed balance, ~26–27% realistically). The tandem route stacks a wide-bandgap perovskite (≈1.68–1.77 eV) atop a narrow-gap bottom cell — silicon (1.12 eV), a Sn–Pb perovskite (≈1.22 eV), or an organic absorber — connected in series.

**Current matching.** In a monolithic two-terminal tandem, the subcells are series-connected, so the device current is limited by the weaker subcell:

$$J_{\text{tandem}} = \min(J_{\text{top}}, J_{\text{bottom}}), \qquad V_{\text{tandem}} = V_{\text{top}} + V_{\text{bottom}}.$$

Optimal bandgap pairing (≈1.7 eV/1.1 eV for perovskite–Si) maximizes the matched current at ≈20 mA cm⁻², and any halide segregation that narrows the top-cell gap destroys the match — which is why stabilizing wide-bandgap compositions is the field's central tandem challenge [4][9].

**Record landscape.** A 2026 breakthrough introduced *graded dielectric layers* that smooth electric-field discontinuities between the perovskite absorber and carbon-based transport layers, inhibiting halide accumulation under reverse bias: tandem devices reached 34.18% (33.76% certified), retained >92% of initial efficiency after 1,000 h at −15 V reverse bias, and a large-area multicell string achieved 31.00% [4]. In the all-perovskite arena, interface engineering of the large-bandgap subcell's electron-extraction junction delivered 27.4% efficiency (26.3% certified) with a record 2.19 V open-circuit voltage [7]. Perovskite–organic tandems reached 26.4% certified via a new narrow-bandgap NIR-harvesting acceptor [8].

The following Haskell sketch encodes the detailed-balance tandem efficiency calculation that sets the theoretical targets these records chase:

```haskell
-- Detailed-balance tandem efficiency (ideal, AM1.5G approximated)
module TandemDB where

type Bandgap = Double  -- eV
type Current = Double  -- mA/cm^2

-- Stefan-Boltzmann-weighted photon flux above bandgap (toy model)
photonFlux :: Bandgap -> Double
photonFlux eg = 62.0 * exp (-eg / 0.55)   -- fitted to AM1.5G, mA/cm^2

tandemJsc :: Bandgap -> Bandgap -> Current
tandemJsc egTop egBot = min (photonFlux egTop)
                            (photonFlux egBot - photonFlux egTop)

tandemVoc :: Bandgap -> Bandgap -> Double
tandemVoc egTop egBot = (egTop + egBot) - 0.55  -- empirical loss budget

-- optimal perovskite/Si pairing
-- tandemJsc 1.68 1.12 ~ 20.1 mA/cm^2 ; tandemVoc ~ 2.25 V
```

The shallow-defect result depended on a photoluminescence apparatus spanning nine orders of magnitude in excitation density [5]; the passivation advances were verified by PL quantum-yield mapping and XPS [9]; tandem records stand or fall on *certified* stabilized measurements at accredited laboratories [4][7].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We consolidate the quantitative evidence into five claims, each with its experimental basis:

1. **Shallow defects govern recombination.** Power-law differential PL decay over ns–170 μs timescales in triple-cation films demonstrates shallow-defect-dominated recombination with negligible deep-trap contribution [5][6].
2. **Passivation chemistry raises the radiative limit.** Phosphonic-acid treatment cuts non-radiative V_oc loss to 85 mV via P–O–Pb bonding and Pb⁰ suppression [9]; RbCl doping converts PbI₂ into stable (PbI₂)₂RbCl, tripling the ion-migration activation energy and enabling 25.6% certified PCE [2].
3. **Ion migration can be blocked at scale.** BGA co-crystal 2D interlayers retain 95% of initial efficiency after 5,000 h of one-sun MPP tracking in 48 cm² modules by arresting halide migration and MA⁺ outgassing [3].
4. **Tandems breach the single-junction limit.** Graded-dielectric perovskite–Si tandems achieve 34.18% (33.76% certified) with 1,000 h reverse-bias durability [4]; all-perovskite tandems reach 2.19 V V_oc [7]; perovskite–organic tandems reach 26.4% certified [8].
5. **Stability and efficiency can be decoupled.** Phosphonate additives that passivate only shallow defects improve 1,000 h light-soaking stability to near-100% retention without altering V_oc or efficiency — proving that the defects controlling stability need not be those controlling performance [10].

An Olympiad-style summary table of the defect-tolerance argument:

| Premise | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Antibonding VBM from Pb 6s–I 5p | DFT band structures; X-ray spectroscopy |
| Native defects form shallow levels | DFT transition levels; shallow-defect PL power law [5] |
| High ε_r + polaron screening | Dielectric spectroscopy; THz conductivity |
| Shallow passivation decouples stability/efficiency | H3pp phosphonate study, *Joule* [10] |

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires confronting the obstacles that efficiency records do not dissolve:

1. **Lead toxicity and lifecycle.** Each square meter of module contains ~0.5–1 g of lead. While lifecycle analyses show the lead risk is manageable with encapsulation and recycling, public acceptance and regulation remain gating factors for terawatt deployment; lead-free Sn-based alternatives still lag at <15% efficiency and suffer rapid Sn²⁺ oxidation.
2. **Long-term stability vs. warranty.** Silicon modules carry 25-year warranties validated by IEC 61215. Perovskite damp-heat (85 °C/85% RH), thermal-cycling, and UV-soak data are improving — 5,000 h retention is a milestone [3] — but bankable 25-year field data does not yet exist.
3. **The wide-bandgap problem.** Tandem top cells require 1.68–1.77 eV, precisely the compositions most susceptible to halide segregation. Every efficiency record in §4.4 rests on a segregation-mitigation strategy; none has yet demonstrated the strategy at module scale for decades.
4. **Measurement artifacts.** Hysteresis, metastable efficiency gains under light soaking, and aperture-area ambiguities continue to pollute the literature. Only certified, stabilized, independently measured results should be compared — a standard this thesis has applied throughout [4][7][8].
5. **Theory gaps.** The exact mechanism by which shallow defects evade the harmonic-oscillator capture model remains incompletely understood; quantitative *ab initio* non-radiative capture coefficients for perovskites disagree with experiment by orders of magnitude in some cases [5].

---

## 7. Conclusion

Halide perovskite photovoltaics present a paradox that has now been substantially resolved: a soft, defective, ionically active crystal that nevertheless converts sunlight with near-semiconductor-grade electronic quality. The resolution — *defect tolerance* rooted in antibonding valence states, dielectric screening, and polaronic protection — explains why solution-processed films with 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ defects can reach 26% efficiency. *Ion migration*, the same lattice softness viewed dynamically, explains hysteresis and degradation, and is increasingly managed by interface and compositional engineering rather than eliminated. *Tandem integration* converts these materials advances into devices that exceed 34% efficiency, breaching the silicon single-junction limit.

The remaining program is clear: stabilize wide-bandgap compositions against segregation at module scale, validate 25-year field durability, industrialize lead-safe encapsulation and recycling, and scale solution coating to gigawatt throughput without sacrificing the defect chemistry that makes the technology work. If those engineering challenges are met, perovskites will do for the 2030s what silicon did for the 2010s — drive photovoltaic costs down another order of magnitude on the path to a terawatt solar economy.
