# Theoretical Foundations of Diffusion Models: Score Matching, Stochastic Differential Equations, and the Probability Flow ODE

## Abstract

Diffusion-based generative models have emerged as the dominant paradigm for high-fidelity synthesis across image, audio, video, and scientific domains, surpassing adversarial approaches on likelihood-based metrics while offering exact likelihood computation. This thesis develops their theoretical foundations from first principles, unifying three originally disparate threads: denoising diffusion probabilistic models [2], score matching with Langevin dynamics, and continuous-time stochastic differential equations [3]. We derive the forward noising chain and show its equivalence to the variance-preserving SDE, present denoising score matching [1] as the principled training signal, and prove via Anderson's reverse-time diffusion theorem that generation is an exact time-reversal driven by the score function. We analyze the probability flow ODE — a deterministic counterpart sharing all marginals with the reverse SDE — which enables uniquely identifiable encodings and exact log-likelihoods via the instantaneous change-of-variables formula. The design-space elucidation of Karras et al. [6] and the DDIM generalization [4] are examined as the bridge between stochastic and deterministic sampling.

## 1. Introduction

The central problem of generative modeling is density estimation in high dimensions: given samples from an unknown distribution *p_data(x)* over a high-dimensional space such as images, learn a model that both samples faithfully and, ideally, evaluates likelihoods. Classical approaches each sacrificed something. Autoregressive models provide exact likelihoods but impose an artificial ordering and suffer slow sequential sampling. Normalizing flows provide exact likelihoods through invertible transforms but constrain architectural expressiveness. Variational autoencoders optimize a tractable lower bound but leave a gap between the bound and the true likelihood, and their samples historically lagged in fidelity. Generative adversarial networks produce striking samples yet offer no likelihood, unstable training dynamics, and poorly understood mode coverage.

Diffusion models resolve much of this tension through a deceptively simple mechanism: *destroy structure gradually, then learn to restore it*. A forward process perturbs data with Gaussian noise over many small steps until the distribution is nearly isotropic Gaussian; a neural network then learns the reverse process, removing noise step by step. This idea originates in the nonequilibrium thermodynamics framing of Sohl-Dickstein et al. [5], was made practical for high-quality image synthesis by Ho, Jain, and Abbeel [2], was connected to score matching and Langevin sampling in the same work, and was unified under the continuous-time stochastic differential equation framework of Song et al. [3].

> **Theorem (informal):** Under mild regularity conditions on the data distribution, the time-reversal of any Itô diffusion admits a closed-form reverse SDE whose drift depends only on the *score* — the gradient of the log-density — of the perturbed marginals. Learning the score at all noise levels is therefore sufficient for exact sample generation.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the prerequisite machinery: score matching, denoising score matching [1], Langevin dynamics, and the discrete DDPM formulation [2]. Section 3 develops the continuous-time methodology: forward and reverse SDEs, the probability flow ODE, and predictor-corrector samplers. Section 4 dives deeply into five theoretical pillars, including the DDPM–score-matching equivalence, the Karras design space [6], and DDIM [4]. Section 5 presents formal results and empirical benchmarks, Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Score matching and the score function

For a distribution with density *p(x)*, the **score function** is defined as *∇_x log p(x)*. Remarkably, score matching (Hyvärinen, 2005) estimates the score of an unnormalized model *without* computing the partition function: the objective minimizes the expected squared distance between the model score and the data score, and integration by parts removes the unknown data score from the objective, leaving terms computable from samples alone. The fatal limitation of classical score matching was the *manifold hypothesis* — real data concentrate near low-dimensional manifolds, where the score is undefined or ill-behaved — and the fact that the score is only accurate where data density is high, precisely where Langevin sampling needs it least.

### 2.2 Denoising score matching

Vincent [1] introduced **denoising score matching (DSM)**, which sidesteps these difficulties by perturbing data with noise and matching the score of the *noise-perturbed* distribution instead. If *q_σ(x̃ | x)* is a Gaussian perturbation kernel, the DSM objective is:

```python
# Denoising score matching objective (conceptual)
def dsm_loss(score_net, x, sigma):
    x_tilde = x + sigma * torch.randn_like(x)
    target = (x - x_tilde) / sigma**2      # score of q_sigma(x_tilde | x)
    pred = score_net(x_tilde, sigma)
    return ((pred - target) ** 2).sum(dim=-1).mean()
```

Because the perturbed distribution has full support, the score is well-defined everywhere, and a *single* network conditioned on the noise level *σ* learns scores across all perturbation scales [1]. This multiscale score estimator is the engine of modern diffusion models.

### 2.3 The DDPM forward and reverse processes

Ho et al. [2] define a **forward process** as a fixed Markov chain that gradually adds Gaussian noise according to a variance schedule *β_1, …, β_T*:

*q(x_t | x_{t-1}) = N(x_t; √(1 − β_t) x_{t-1}, β_t I)*

A crucial algebraic property is that the marginal at any step is available in closed form: with *ᾱ_t = ∏_{s≤t}(1 − β_s)*, we have *q(x_t | x_0) = N(x_t; √ᾱ_t x_0, (1 − ᾱ_t) I)*, so training can sample arbitrary timesteps without simulating the chain. The **reverse process** is parameterized as *p_θ(x_{t-1} | x_t) = N(x_{t-1}; μ_θ(x_t, t), Σ_θ(x_t, t))*, and training maximizes a variational lower bound (ELBO) on the data log-likelihood. The key simplification of [2] reparameterizes the objective as a *noise prediction* loss:

```python
# DDPM simplified training objective (Ho et al., 2020)
def ddpm_loss(eps_net, x0, t, alpha_bar):
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    xt = sqrt(alpha_bar[t]) * x0 + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t]) * eps
    return ((eps_net(xt, t) - eps) ** 2).mean()
```

Ho et al. [2] further showed this objective is a *weighted variational bound* whose weighting corresponds to denoising score matching with Langevin-like sampling, forging the first link between diffusion probabilistic models and score-based generative modeling. Sampling proceeds by iterating the learned reverse transitions from pure noise, requiring on the order of *T = 1000* network evaluations.

### 2.4 Langevin dynamics

Given an accurate score function, **annealed Langevin dynamics** generates samples by running discretized Langevin updates at decreasing noise levels. Song and Ermon (2019) demonstrated that multiscale DSM scores combined with annealed Langevin sampling produce images competitive with GANs, establishing score-based generation as a viable alternative. The SDE framework [3] later subsumed both Langevin sampling and DDPM ancestral sampling as special cases of numerical SDE solvers.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The continuous-time forward SDE

Song et al. [3] generalize the discrete diffusion chain to a continuous-time stochastic process governed by an Itô SDE:

*dx = f(x, t) dt + g(t) dw*

where *w* is a standard Wiener process, *f* is the drift coefficient, and *g* is the diffusion coefficient. Two canonical instantiations are studied:

| SDE | Drift *f(x,t)* | Diffusion *g(t)* | Perturbation kernel *p_{0t}* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Variance Exploding (VE) | 0 | √(dσ²(t)/dt) | *N(x(t); x(0), (σ²(t) − σ²(0)) I)* |
| Variance Preserving (VP) | −½β(t)x | √β(t) | *N(x(t); x(0)e^{−½∫β}, (1 − e^{−∫β}) I)* |
| sub-VP | −½β(t)x | √[β(t)(1 − e^{−2∫β})] | bounded variance variant of VP |

The VE SDE corresponds to the noise-conditioned score networks with annealed Langevin sampling, while the VP SDE is the continuous limit of DDPM [2]; both are unified as discretizations of the same framework [3].

### 3.2 The reverse-time SDE

The methodological centerpiece is Anderson's (1982) reverse-time diffusion result: the reverse of the forward SDE is itself a diffusion, given by

*dx = [f(x, t) − g(t)² ∇_x log p_t(x)] dt + g(t) dẇ*

where *ẇ* is a reverse-time Wiener process and *p_t* is the marginal density of *x(t)*. Replacing the unknown score *∇_x log p_t(x)* with a learned time-dependent score network *s_θ(x, t)* yields a tractable generative SDE. Training uses a continuous-time DSM objective — a weighted integral over time of per-timestep denoising score matching losses [3].

### 3.3 The probability flow ODE

Every SDE of the above form admits a deterministic counterpart, the **probability flow ODE**:

*dx = [f(x, t) − ½ g(t)² ∇_x log p_t(x)] dt*

whose trajectories share *exactly the same marginal distributions* *p_t(x)* as the SDE at every time *t*. This has profound consequences:

1. **Deterministic sampling:** solving the ODE from noise yields samples with no stochasticity, enabling larger step sizes and fewer function evaluations.
2. **Uniquely identifiable encodings:** the ODE is invertible, so each data point maps to a unique latent code — unlike the stochastic reverse SDE.
3. **Exact likelihood computation:** the instantaneous change-of-variables formula gives *d log p_t(x(t)) / dt = −tr(∇_x [f − ½g²s_θ])*, so integrating the ODE from data to noise and accumulating the trace (estimated efficiently with Hutchinson's estimator) yields exact log-likelihoods under the model [3].

### 3.4 Predictor-corrector sampling

Song et al. [3] propose **predictor-corrector (PC) samplers**: a numerical SDE solver (the predictor, e.g., Euler–Maruyama or reverse diffusion) advances the state, followed by Langevin MCMC steps using the learned score (the corrector) that drive the sample toward the correct marginal. This combination substantially improves sample quality at fixed compute and explains why hybrid stochastic–deterministic schemes dominate practice.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Denoising score matching and its implicit connection to the DDPM objective

The equivalence between the DDPM noise-prediction loss and DSM is not coincidental but algebraic. For the Gaussian perturbation kernel *q(x_t | x_0) = N(√ᾱ_t x_0, (1 − ᾱ_t) I)*, the score of the perturbed conditional is *∇_{x_t} log q(x_t | x_0) = −(x_t − √ᾱ_t x_0)/(1 − ᾱ_t) = −ε/√(1 − ᾱ_t)*. A network predicting the noise *ε* is therefore, up to a known time-dependent scaling, predicting the score of the perturbed data distribution. The DDPM simplified objective is exactly a *weighted* DSM objective with weighting *λ(t) ∝ (1 − ᾱ_t)*, i.e., upweighting high-noise timesteps [2][3]. This reframes DDPM training as multiscale score estimation and explains why the learned *ε_θ* can be plugged directly into Langevin or reverse-SDE samplers: it is a score estimator in disguise.

### 4.2 The reverse-time SDE: Anderson's theorem and exact likelihoods

Anderson's theorem requires that *p_t* be smooth with bounded derivatives and that the forward SDE have well-behaved coefficients — conditions satisfied by Gaussian perturbation kernels. The practical significance is twofold. First, *any* forward diffusion with tractable perturbation kernels yields a generative model whose only learned component is the score; the dynamics themselves are exact. Second, the reverse SDE formulation converts the discrete-time ELBO analysis of [2] into a continuous-time likelihood identity: Song et al. [3] derive that the model log-likelihood equals the DSM training objective plus a nonnegative gap controlled by score-estimation error, making the long-standing goal of *likelihood-based training of score models* rigorous. Reported likelihoods of 2.99 bits per dimension on CIFAR-10 [3] were, at the time, competitive with the best autoregressive models.

### 4.3 The probability flow ODE and deterministic encoding

The probability flow ODE deserves emphasis because it dissolves a false dichotomy: stochasticity is not essential to diffusion generation. Because the ODE and SDE share marginals, any property depending only on marginals — including the learned likelihood — transfers between them. Practically, black-box ODE solvers with adaptive step sizes (e.g., Dormand–Prince) sample in tens of evaluations rather than hundreds, and the invertibility of the ODE enables *latent interpolation* and *temperature-controlled sampling* by manipulating the initial noise. Conceptually, the ODE reveals diffusion models as continuous normalizing flows with a particularly convenient training objective — the score-matching loss replaces the expensive trace computations that made neural ODEs difficult to train at scale.

### 4.4 Schedule design: elucidating the design space

Karras et al. [6] performed a systematic dissection of the diffusion design space, showing that ostensibly different methods (DDPM, DDIM, score-based SDEs) are instantiations of a single modular framework with interchangeable components: noise schedule, network preconditioning, loss weighting, and sampler. Key findings include:

- **Preconditioning:** scaling network inputs, outputs, and skip connections as functions of the noise level keeps activations and gradients at unit scale across the enormous dynamic range of *σ*, dramatically stabilizing training [6].
- **Schedule parameterization:** sampling noise levels from a log-normal distribution during training concentrates capacity where the denoising task is hardest, and the deterministic second-order Heun sampler achieves state-of-the-art FID with as few as 35 network evaluations [6].
- **Separation of concerns:** the *training* objective (which noise levels to emphasize) and the *sampling* dynamics (stochastic vs. deterministic, order of solver) can be chosen independently, explaining why EDM-style models pair DSM training with ODE samplers for speed [6].

This modular view transformed diffusion research from competing monoliths into composable engineering, and underlies the samplers shipped in latent diffusion and Stable Diffusion pipelines.

### 4.5 DDIM: non-Markovian generalization and deterministic sampling

Song et al. [4] observed that the DDPM training objective depends only on the *marginals* *q(x_t | x_0)*, not on the Markovian structure of the forward chain. They constructed a family of **non-Markovian** forward processes with identical marginals but a free variance parameter *σ_t* controlling stochasticity. Setting *σ_t = 0* yields the **denoising diffusion implicit model (DDIM)**: a fully deterministic sampler that inverts the forward process exactly. DDIM enjoys two properties of lasting importance: (i) sampling in 20–50 steps with minimal quality degradation — a 20× to 50× speedup over DDPM [4]; and (ii) a deterministic, invertible mapping between data and latents, enabling semantic interpolation years before the probability flow ODE was widely appreciated. DDIM is recovered in the SDE framework as a particular discretization of the probability flow ODE [3], closing the theoretical loop.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Formal Results

> **Theorem (Likelihood identity, Song et al. [3]):** Let *s_θ(x, t)* be a time-dependent score model and *p_t^θ* the marginals of the probability flow ODE driven by *s_θ*. Then *log p_0^θ(x)* can be computed exactly by integrating the ODE backward from *t = 0* to *t = T* while accumulating *−tr(∇_x f̃_θ)*, where *f̃_θ(x,t) = f(x,t) − ½g(t)²s_θ(x,t)*, with the trace estimated unbiasedly by the Skilling–Hutchinson estimator.

Benchmark results reported in the foundational literature are summarized below. Figures are taken directly from the cited papers and reflect the state of the field at publication.

| Model | Dataset | Metric | Reported value |
|---|---|---|---|
| DDPM [2] | CIFAR-10 (unconditional) | FID | 3.17 |
| DDPM [2] | CIFAR-10 (unconditional) | Inception Score | 9.46 |
| Score SDE, VP deep [3] | CIFAR-10 (unconditional) | FID | 2.20 |
| Score SDE, VP deep [3] | CIFAR-10 (unconditional) | Inception Score | 9.89 |
| Score SDE, VP [3] | CIFAR-10 | NLL (bits/dim) | 2.99 |
| DDIM, 50 steps [4] | CIFAR-10 | FID | 4.67 |
| DDIM, 20 steps [4] | CelebA 64×64 | FID | 3.51 |
| EDM, 35 NFE (Heun) [6] | CIFAR-10 | FID | 1.97 |

Several patterns emerge from these results:

1. **Continuous-time training dominates discrete-time:** the score-SDE models [3] improve on DDPM's FID (2.20 vs. 3.17) while additionally providing likelihoods, confirming that the continuous formulation is not merely elegant but empirically superior.
2. **Deterministic samplers trade little quality for large speedups:** DDIM at 50 steps [4] and the EDM Heun sampler at 35 evaluations [6] demonstrate that most of the generative work is done by the learned score field, not by stochastic exploration.
3. **Likelihood and sample quality can coexist:** unlike GANs, diffusion models report both FID and bits-per-dimension, and improvements in one have historically accompanied improvements in the other [3][6].

A minimal PyTorch sketch of probability-flow-ODE sampling with an Euler solver illustrates the methodology of Section 3.3:

```python
# Probability flow ODE sampling (Euler discretization)
def sample_ode(score_net, shape, sigma_fn, n_steps=50, sigma_max=80.0):
    x = torch.randn(shape) * sigma_max          # prior sample
    sigmas = torch.linspace(sigma_max, 0.0, n_steps + 1)
    for i in range(n_steps):
        s, s_next = sigmas[i], sigmas[i + 1]
        # VE probability flow ODE drift: dx = -1/2 g(t)^2 score dt
        drift = -0.5 * (s**2 - s_next**2) / s * score_net(x, s)
        x = x + drift * (s_next - s) / (s**2 - s_next**2) * (s**2 - s_next**2)
    return x
```

## 6. Limitations

Despite their empirical dominance, diffusion models face significant theoretical and practical limitations.

- **Sampling cost.** Even with DDIM [4] and EDM samplers [6], generation requires dozens of sequential network evaluations — orders of magnitude slower than single-pass GAN generators. Distillation and consistency models address this but introduce their own approximation errors.
- **Score-estimation error and convergence theory.** The reverse SDE is exact only for the *true* score. Finite-sample guarantees bounding the total-variation distance between the generated and data distributions in terms of DSM error, discretization error, and the data distribution's regularity remain an active research area; existing bounds often require strong log-concavity or smoothness assumptions that image data violate.
- **The manifold hypothesis revisited.** DSM [1] perturbs data off the manifold, but at small noise levels the score field must represent extremely sharp transitions near the manifold, demanding high network capacity and careful noise schedules. Mode coverage, while better than GANs empirically, lacks the clean theoretical characterization that likelihood-based training promises.
- **Likelihood–perception gap.** Bits-per-dimension rewards pixel-level fidelity and can prefer blurry, high-entropy predictions over perceptually sharp samples; FID relies on Inception features with known biases. Neither metric fully captures human perceptual quality.
- **Curse of dimensionality in SDE simulation.** The Hutchinson trace estimator used for likelihood computation [3] has variance growing with dimension, making exact likelihoods expensive and noisy for high-resolution images.
- **Conditional generation theory.** Classifier guidance and classifier-free guidance are justified heuristically via Bayes' rule on scores, but their interaction with approximate scores and discretization lacks rigorous analysis.

## 7. Conclusion

Diffusion models admit a remarkably coherent theoretical story. Denoising score matching [1] provides a tractable, well-posed training objective; the DDPM formulation [2] shows that predicting injected noise is score estimation in disguise; Anderson's reverse-time theorem lifts the entire construction to continuous time, where the reverse SDE [3] generates samples and the probability flow ODE computes exact likelihoods and invertible encodings; DDIM [4] reveals deterministic sampling as a special case; and the elucidated design space [6] decomposes the method into interchangeable modules of schedule, preconditioning, weighting, and solver. What began as a thermodynamic curiosity [5] is now understood as a universal framework in which the score function — the gradient of the log-density — is the single learned object from which sampling, likelihoods, encodings, and conditional generation all follow. The outstanding challenges are equally clear: non-asymptotic convergence theory under realistic data assumptions, principled schedule and weighting design, and closing the gap between likelihood-based objectives and perceptual quality. Progress on these fronts will determine whether diffusion models remain an empirical triumph or become a fully understood pillar of probabilistic modeling.
