# Quantum Error Mitigation for NISQ Expectation Values: Zero-Noise Extrapolation, Probabilistic Error Cancellation, Clifford Data Regression, and Virtual Distillation

## Abstract

Noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) processors cannot yet support fault tolerance, yet variational algorithms such as the variational quantum eigensolver and the quantum approximate optimization algorithm need accurate observable expectation values. Quantum error mitigation (QEM) recovers estimates of ideal, noise-free expectation values by classically post-processing noisy measurement outcomes, at increased sampling cost. This thesis presents a unified treatment of four principal mitigation families: zero-noise extrapolation, which amplifies noise by folding and extrapolates to the zero-noise limit; probabilistic error cancellation, which inverts the noise channel through a quasi-probability decomposition sampled by signed Monte Carlo; Clifford data regression, which learns a noise-inversion map from near-Clifford circuits that are classically simulable; and virtual distillation, which suppresses incoherent errors by estimating expectation values with respect to $\rho^M/\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)$ via a derangement circuit. We derive each estimator, bound its bias and overhead, compare hardware results, and delineate where each method stays viable.

## 1 Introduction

The defining constraint of the NISQ era is that physical error rates ($\sim 10^{-3}$ per two-qubit gate on leading superconducting platforms) are orders of magnitude above the fault-tolerance threshold while qubit counts ($\sim 10^2$--$10^3$) are orders of magnitude below what surface-code error correction demands [6]. Variational quantum algorithms sidestep deep coherent evolution but remain acutely sensitive to noise, because their entire optimization loop is driven by expectation values $\langle O \rangle = \mathrm{Tr}(O\rho)$ estimated from finite measurement shots.

Quantum error mitigation accepts the noise and corrects its *statistical consequences* rather than its physical occurrence. Formally, given access to a noisy implementation $\tilde{\mathcal{U}}$ of an ideal unitary channel $\mathcal{U}$, and given a budget of $N$ circuit executions, a mitigation protocol produces an estimator $\hat{O}_{\mathrm{em}}$ of $\langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{ideal}} = \mathrm{Tr}(O\,\mathcal{U}(\rho_0))$ satisfying $\mathrm{Bias}[\hat{O}_{\mathrm{em}}] \ll \mathrm{Bias}[\hat{O}_{\mathrm{noisy}}]$, at the cost of a sampling overhead $C_{\mathrm{em}}$ such that $N = C_{\mathrm{em}} \cdot N_0$ shots are needed to match the variance of an unmitigated estimator using $N_0$ shots.

The landmark demonstration that mitigation can scale arrived with the 127-qubit transverse-field Ising simulation, where ZNE combined with PEC and twirled readout mitigation produced expectation values beyond the reach of then-state-of-the-art classical methods [6]. This thesis dissects the four techniques that made such demonstrations possible, with the following contributions:

1. A **unified superoperator formalism** in which ZNE, PEC, CDR, and VD are expressed as estimators of the same ideal functional $\langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{ideal}}$, enabling direct comparison of bias and sampling cost.
2. **Closed-form derivations** of the Richardson extrapolation coefficients, the PEC quasi-probability estimator, the CDR least-squares ansatz, and the virtual-distillation derangement measurement, each with sampling-overhead bounds.
3. A **comparative empirical analysis** of published hardware results, identifying the noise regimes and circuit scales where each method dominates.
4. A **limitations taxonomy** explaining why no mitigation method escapes an exponential cost in the circuit fault rate, and where the boundary between mitigation and correction lies.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Noise models for NISQ circuits

We model noisy evolution by completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) maps. A local depolarizing channel on $n$ qubits acts as

$$\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{dep}}(\rho) = (1-p)\,\rho + p\,\frac{I}{2^n},$$

while a general Pauli channel is $\mathcal{N}(\rho) = \sum_{P \in \mathcal{P}_n} q_P\, P\rho P$ with $\sum_P q_P = 1$. In continuous time, Markovian noise obeys the Lindblad master equation

$$\frac{d\rho}{dt} = -i[H, \rho] + \sum_k \left(L_k \rho L_k^\dagger - \tfrac{1}{2}\{L_k^\dagger L_k, \rho\}\right).$$

The *circuit fault rate* $\lambda = \sum_{\text{gates}} p_g$ (total expected number of faults) is the single most important parameter: essentially every mitigation method pays a cost exponential in $\lambda$ [1,6].

### 2.2 The bias-variance anatomy of mitigation

For an estimator $\hat{\theta}$ of $\theta = \langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{ideal}}$, $\mathrm{MSE} = \mathrm{Bias}^2 + \mathrm{Var}$. Mitigation *trades* bias for variance: every method below reduces systematic bias at the expense of multiplying variance by an overhead factor, and is useful when $\Delta\mathrm{MSE} > 0$ at fixed shot budget.

### 2.3 Taxonomy of methods

| Method | Mechanism | Extra qubits | Needs noise model? | Overhead scaling |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zero-noise extrapolation | Noise amplification + extrapolation | 0 | No | $\gamma_{\mathrm{ZNE}}^2$, polynomial in # levels |
| Probabilistic error cancellation | Quasi-probability inverse channel | 0 | Yes (learned) | $\Gamma^2 \approx e^{4\lambda}$ worst case |
| Clifford data regression | Learned map from simulable circuits | 0 | No | Training-set shots |
| Virtual distillation | Multi-copy purification | $(M-1)n$ | No | $\sim \mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)^{-2}$ |
| Readout mitigation (M3) | Calibration-matrix inversion | 0 | Yes (measured) | Iterative solver cost |

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytic and comparative. We adopt the *superoperator representation* [6]: vectorize density matrices as $|\rho\rangle\!\rangle$ and write channels as matrices $\mathcal{U}$ acting on $|\rho\rangle\!\rangle$, with $\mathrm{Tr}(O\rho) = \langle\!\langle O | \rho \rangle\!\rangle$. Each mitigation method is then a prescription for an estimator $\hat{O}_{\mathrm{em}}$ built from noisy channel evaluations $\{\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_k\}$, and we characterize each by:

- **(M1) Estimator construction** --- the explicit classical post-processing rule.
- **(M2) Bias analysis** --- conditions for unbiasedness or bias order in $\lambda$.
- **(M3) Sampling overhead** --- the variance inflation factor $C_{\mathrm{em}}$.
- **(M4) Assumptions** --- what must be learned or assumed about the noise.

We validate against published experiments: 16-qubit VQE on IBMQ hardware [2], Heisenberg dynamics [4], 19-qubit SWAP-test circuits [3], and 27-qubit PEC with sparse Pauli-Lindblad models [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Zero-noise extrapolation: amplifying noise to remove it

ZNE is conceptually the simplest mitigation method and requires no characterization of the noise [1]. The protocol:

1. **Noise scaling.** Replace each gate $G$ by a folded version $G \to G (G^\dagger G)^k$, or stretch control pulses, so the effective noise strength becomes $x \cdot \lambda_0$ for scale factors $x_1 = 1 < x_2 < \dots < x_m$ (typically $x \in \{1, 3, 5\}$ for unitary folding).
2. **Measurement.** Estimate $E(x_j) = \langle O \rangle_{x_j}$ at each scale factor.
3. **Extrapolation.** Fit a model $\hat{E}(x)$ and evaluate at $x = 0$.

*Richardson extrapolation.* Assume $E(x) = E^* + \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k x^k + O(x^{n+1})$. Given $n+1$ points, the extrapolated value is the linear combination

$$\hat{E}(0) = \sum_{j=0}^{n} \beta_j\, E(x_j), \qquad \sum_j \beta_j = 1,\;\; \sum_j \beta_j x_j^k = 0\;\; (1 \le k \le n),$$

where the coefficients $\beta_j$ solve a Vandermonde system. This cancels the first $n$ orders of the noise expansion [1].

> **Theorem:** *(Richardson ZNE bias-variance bound [5].)* Let $E(x)$ be $(n+1)$-times differentiable with $|E^{(n+1)}| \le M$ on $[0, x_n]$, and let each $\hat{E}(x_j)$ be estimated from $N_j$ shots with single-shot variance $\sigma^2$. Then the Richardson estimator with $n+1$ nodes satisfies
> $$\big|\mathbb{E}[\hat{E}(0)] - E(0)\big| \le \frac{M\, x_n^{\,n+1}}{(n+1)!}\prod_{j} \!\max(1,x_j), \qquad \mathrm{Var}[\hat{E}(0)] = \sigma^2 \sum_j \frac{\beta_j^2}{N_j}.$$
> The variance inflation $\gamma_{\mathrm{ZNE}}^2 = \sum_j \beta_j^2$ grows rapidly with $n$ because Richardson coefficients alternate in sign and exceed unity in magnitude --- the fundamental bias-variance trade-off of ZNE.

*Exponential ansätze.* For deep circuits $E(x) \approx a + b\,e^{-cx}$, so the exponential model [3] often beats polynomial fits. *Layerwise Richardson extrapolation* assigns independent scale factors per circuit layer with multivariate Lagrange interpolation, capturing spatially non-uniform noise [5].

```python
# Richardson ZNE sketch: fold, measure, extrapolate
import numpy as np

def richardson_coeffs(x):
    """Coefficients beta_j cancelling orders 1..n of the noise expansion."""
    n = len(x) - 1
    V = np.vander(x, N=n + 1, increasing=True).T   # Vandermonde system
    rhs = np.zeros(n + 1); rhs[0] = 1.0            # beta . x^k = delta_{k,0}
    return np.linalg.solve(V, rhs)

def zne_extrapolate(expectations, scale_factors):
    beta = richardson_coeffs(scale_factors)
    gamma = np.sum(beta ** 2)          # variance inflation factor
    return float(beta @ expectations), gamma
```

### 4.2 Probabilistic error cancellation: inverting noise with quasi-probabilities

PEC, introduced alongside ZNE by Temme, Bravyi, and Gambetta [1], is the only method of the four that can *exactly* cancel the bias of arbitrary circuits --- provided the noise is known. Write the noisy implementation of each ideal gate as $\tilde{\mathcal{U}} = \mathcal{N}\mathcal{U}$. If the inverse noise channel admits a decomposition into implementable (noisy) basis operations,

$$\mathcal{N}^{-1} = \sum_{\alpha} \eta_\alpha\, \mathcal{B}_\alpha, \qquad \eta_\alpha \in \mathbb{R},$$

then inserting this identity before every gate yields an unbiased estimator. Because the coefficients $\eta_\alpha$ can be negative, they form a *quasi-probability* distribution: sample $\alpha$ with probability $p_\alpha = |\eta_\alpha|/\gamma$ where $\gamma = \sum_\alpha |\eta_\alpha| \ge 1$, execute $\mathcal{B}_\alpha$, and multiply the measurement outcome by $\gamma \cdot \mathrm{sgn}(\eta_\alpha)$ [1,6].

> **Theorem:** *(PEC unbiasedness.)* If $\mathcal{N}^{-1} = \sum_\alpha \eta_\alpha \mathcal{B}_\alpha$ holds exactly and each basis operation is implemented without additional unmodelled error, the PEC Monte Carlo estimator $\hat{O}_{\mathrm{PEC}} = \frac{\gamma}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N s_i\, o_i$ with $s_i = \mathrm{sgn}(\eta_{\alpha_i})$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\hat{O}_{\mathrm{PEC}}] = \langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{ideal}}$ and $\mathrm{Var}[\hat{O}_{\mathrm{PEC}}] = \gamma^2 \mathrm{Var}[\hat{O}_{\mathrm{noisy}}]/N_{\mathrm{eff}}$. The sampling overhead is $C_{\mathrm{PEC}} = \gamma^2$.

For $G$ noisy gates, $\Gamma = \prod_g \gamma_g \approx e^{2\lambda}$ under local depolarizing noise, so $C_{\mathrm{PEC}} \approx e^{4\lambda}$ --- the exponential wall. The practical breakthrough was the *sparse Pauli-Lindblad* model $\Lambda = \exp(\sum_{P} \lambda_P (P \cdot P - I))$, learnable with $O(n)$ parameters via cycle benchmarking, enabling PEC on 27-qubit devices far below worst-case bounds [7].

```haskell
-- Quasi-probability decomposition as a typed sampling plan
type QuasiProb op = [(Double, op)]          -- (eta_alpha, basis op)

gamma :: QuasiProb op -> Double
gamma qs = sum (map (abs . fst) qs)

samplePEC :: QuasiProb op -> IO (Double, op) -- returns (sign * gamma, op)
samplePEC qs = do
  let g = gamma qs
  a <- sampleCategorical [(abs e / g, (signum e * g, b)) | (e, b) <- qs]
  return a
```

### 4.3 Clifford data regression: learning the noise map from simulable circuits

CDR exploits the Gottesman-Knill theorem: circuits composed of Clifford gates are classically simulable in polynomial time [2]. The method constructs a *training set* of near-Clifford circuits $\{\mathcal{C}_i\}$ --- the target circuit with most non-Clifford rotations snapped to the nearest Clifford angle --- and obtains pairs $(X_i^{\mathrm{noisy}}, X_i^{\mathrm{exact}})$ from hardware and classical simulation respectively. A simple affine ansatz

$$X^{\mathrm{exact}} \approx f(X^{\mathrm{noisy}}; \mathbf{a}) = a_1 X^{\mathrm{noisy}} + a_2$$

is fitted by least squares, and the fitted map corrects the target circuit's noisy expectation value. On a 16-qubit ground-state energy problem on IBMQ hardware, CDR achieved roughly an order-of-magnitude error reduction, scaling to 64 qubits in simulation [2].

Extensions enrich the ansatz: quadratic regression $f(x) = a_1 x^2 + a_2 x + a_3$, energy-biased training-set sampling, and *Clifford perturbation* data where small-angle rotations are approximated by sparse Pauli dynamics [8]. Accuracy rests on a *transfer assumption*: the noise afflicting near-Clifford circuits must resemble that afflicting the target circuit, which holds when noise is gate-local and weakly angle-dependent.

```rust
// CDR least-squares fit of the affine ansatz f(x) = a1*x + a2
fn cdr_fit(noisy: &[f64], exact: &[f64]) -> (f64, f64) {
    let n = noisy.len() as f64;
    let (sx, sy) = (noisy.iter().sum::<f64>(), exact.iter().sum::<f64>());
    let (sxx, sxy) = noisy.iter().zip(exact.iter())
        .map(|(x, y)| (x * x, x * y))
        .fold((0.0, 0.0), |(a, b), (c, d)| (a + c, b + d));
    let denom = n * sxx - sx * sx;
    let a1 = (n * sxy - sx * sy) / denom;
    let a2 = (sy - a1 * sx) / n;
    (a1, a2)
}
```

### 4.4 Virtual distillation: purifying by multi-copy derangement

Virtual distillation (VD) takes a radically different route: instead of characterizing or scaling noise, it *suppresses* the incoherent component by entangling $M$ copies of the noisy state $\rho$ [4]. The key identity is that expectation values with respect to the *distilled* state $\rho^M / \mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)$ can be estimated without preparing it:

$$\langle O \rangle_{\mathrm{VD}}^{(M)} = \frac{\mathrm{Tr}(O\,\rho^M)}{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)}.$$

As $M$ grows, $\rho^M/\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)$ converges exponentially fast to the dominant eigenvector $|\psi_0\rangle\langle\psi_0|$ of $\rho$. If the ideal state has the largest eigenvalue (the typical case when noise is weak and incoherent), errors are suppressed by *orders of magnitude* [4].

The measurement is implemented by a *derangement* circuit: for $M=2$, an ancilla-controlled cyclic shift $S_2$ (a derangement operator, equivalently a SWAP test variant) yields

$$\mathrm{Tr}(O\rho^2) = \langle\!\langle O \otimes I |\, S_2\, |\rho \otimes \rho\rangle\!\rangle,$$

up to the normalization $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^2)$ measured in the same circuit. *Error suppression by derangement* generalizes this to arbitrary $M$ with a single ancilla and $O(Mn)$ controlled-SWAP gates [4]. The price: $(M-1)n$ extra qubits, sampling overhead $\sim \mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)^{-2}$, a *noise floor* from the derangement circuit's own gates, and no correction of coherent mismatch between $|\psi_0\rangle$ and the ideal state.

### 4.5 Readout mitigation: the M3 matrix-free approach

Measurement errors are mitigated separately. With a calibration matrix $A_{x,y} = P(\text{observe }x \mid \text{true }y)$, the mitigated distribution solves $\min_x \|Ax - y\|^2$ subject to $x$ being a probability vector. The M3 (*matrix-free measurement mitigation*) method avoids ever forming the $2^n \times 2^n$ matrix by applying $A$ on the fly to the subspace of observed bitstrings and solving the reduced least-squares problem iteratively, scaling to hundreds of qubits where full inversion is impossible [6].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Representative hardware results

| Experiment | Method | Scale | Reported improvement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground-state energy, IBMQ 16q [2] | CDR | 16 qubits | $\sim 10\times$ error reduction |
| Heisenberg dynamics simulation [4] | VD ($M=2$) | 10 qubits | orders-of-magnitude suppression |
| SWAP-test circuits, up to 19q [3] | ZNE vs PEC | 19 qubits | exponential ZNE best at low depth; PEC exact bias removal |
| 27-qubit Falcon processor [7] | PEC + sparse Pauli-Lindblad | 27 qubits | mitigated observables with $\bar{\gamma} \approx 1.008$/layer |
| 127-qubit Ising dynamics [6] | ZNE + PEC + TREX | 127 qubits | beyond brute-force classical simulation |

### 5.2 Sampling-cost scaling: the exponential wall

All four methods obey a common lower bound: mitigating a circuit with fault rate $\lambda$ to fixed precision requires $\Omega(e^{c\lambda})$ samples for some constant $c$ [6]. Concretely:

- **ZNE:** variance inflation $\gamma_{\mathrm{ZNE}}^2 = \sum_j \beta_j^2$; for 3-point Richardson at $x \in \{1,3,5\}$, $\beta = (15/8, -5/4, 3/8)$ and $\gamma_{\mathrm{ZNE}}^2 \approx 5.3$.
- **PEC:** $C_{\mathrm{PEC}} = \Gamma^2$ with $\Gamma = \prod_g \gamma_g \approx e^{2\lambda}$ for depolarizing noise.
- **CDR:** cost dominated by the training set ($\sim 10$--$100$ near-Clifford circuits); no exponential in $\lambda$ but bias remains.
- **VD:** $C_{\mathrm{VD}} \sim \mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)^{-2}$, exponential in $\lambda$ for fixed $M$, traded against exponential bias suppression.

```tla
\* TLA+ specification of the PEC Monte Carlo sampling loop
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
VARIABLES shots, accumulator
Init == shots = 0 /\ accumulator = 0
Sample(gamma) ==
    \* draw basis op with prob |eta|/gamma, weight outcome by sign*gamma
    /\ shots' = shots + 1
    /\ accumulator' = accumulator + gamma * Sign(eta) * Outcome
Next == \E g \in Reals : g > 1 /\ Sample(g)
Estimator == accumulator / shots     \* converges to <O>_ideal a.s.
====
```

### 5.3 Proof sketch: exponential suppression in virtual distillation

For depolarizing noise with strength $p$ on a pure target state, $\lambda_0 = 1 - p + p/2^n$ and $\lambda_1 = p/2^n$; already $M=2$ suppresses the error contribution by a factor $\approx p/(2^n(1-p))$, which *improves* with system size --- the striking scaling observed numerically in [4].

## 6 Limitations

1. **The exponential wall is fundamental.** Information-theoretic lower bounds show that *any* mitigation protocol needs $\Omega(\exp(\lambda))$ samples to suppress bias below a threshold [6]. Mitigation extends the reach of NISQ devices; it does not replace error correction.
2. **PEC demands accurate noise learning.** Quasi-probability cancellation is unbiased only if the learned $\mathcal{N}$ matches the physical noise. Model mismatch reintroduces bias, and learning itself (gate-set tomography, cycle benchmarking) is expensive and drift-sensitive [7].
3. **ZNE assumes smooth noise scaling.** Unitary folding changes the circuit and can alter the noise itself (e.g., coherent errors interfere differently in $GG^\dagger G$); the extrapolation model (polynomial vs. exponential) must be chosen correctly or the "mitigated" value can be *worse* than the raw one [5].
4. **CDR's transfer assumption can fail.** If the target circuit's non-Clifford gates experience angle-dependent or crosstalk-heavy noise absent from the training set, the learned affine map extrapolates poorly.
5. **VD cannot fix coherent mismatch.** If the dominant eigenvector $|\psi_0\rangle$ differs from the ideal state (coherent errors), distillation converges to the *wrong* state; the derangement circuit's own noise sets a floor, and $Mn$ qubits may be unavailable.
6. **Composition is non-trivial.** Stacking methods (e.g., ZNE inside PEC training circuits) can compound sampling overheads multiplicatively; only a few combinations have been validated on hardware [6].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Quantum error mitigation has matured from the two 2017 proposals of Temme, Bravyi, and Gambetta [1] into an indispensable layer of the NISQ software stack. **Zero-noise extrapolation** remains the workhorse: model-free, qubit-free, and effective whenever noise scales smoothly. **Probabilistic error cancellation** offers the unique guarantee of unbiased estimation, now made practical by sparse Pauli-Lindblad noise learning [7]. **Clifford data regression** trades guarantees for frugality, learning the noise map from classically simulable circuits [2]. **Virtual distillation** achieves the most dramatic suppression per shot when extra qubits are available [4]. Together with matrix-free readout mitigation, these techniques enabled the first demonstrations of quantum utility on 127-qubit processors [6].

The central lesson is a conservation law: *bias can be converted into sampling cost, but the exchange rate is exponential in the fault rate.* Mitigation buys a constant-factor extension of reachable circuit volume --- precious, but bounded --- and the long-term trajectory points toward hybrid regimes where mitigation handles the residual logical noise of early fault-tolerant devices.
