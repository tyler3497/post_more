# Rough Volatility: Fractional Stochastic Volatility and the Short-Time At-the-Money Skew

## Abstract

Equity index implied volatility surfaces exhibit an at-the-money skew that decays as a power law in time to maturity, roughly proportional to T to the power of negative 0.4, a signature that classical Markovian stochastic volatility models cannot reproduce. This thesis develops the rough volatility paradigm: the logarithm of instantaneous volatility behaves as fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent H near 0.1, far below the Brownian value of one half. We trace the high-frequency econometric evidence of Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum, derive the short-maturity skew asymptotics of order T raised to H minus one half via Malliavin calculus, construct the rough Bergomi model of Bayer, Friz and Gatheral as a parsimonious pricing engine, and survey numerical technology including hybrid simulation schemes, variance-reduced Monte Carlo, and fractional Riccati characteristic functions. Empirical re-examination of SPX data confirms the power-law signature, while recent critiques expose structural limits of non-semimartingale specifications.

---

## 1 Intro

The implied volatility surface of equity index options is one of the most studied objects in quantitative finance, and its most stubborn feature is also its simplest to state. Let Σ(k, T) denote Black-Scholes implied volatility at log-moneyness k and maturity T. The *at-the-money skew*,

$$\mathcal{S}(T) = \left.\frac{\partial \Sigma}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0}(T),$$

is steeply negative for short maturities and decays as maturity grows. Market data show that this decay follows a **power law**,

$$|\mathcal{S}(T)| \approx C \, T^{-\beta}, \qquad \beta \approx 0.4,$$

all the way down to the shortest listed expirations [1]. This empirical regularity is devastating for the classical stochastic volatility canon. The Heston model produces a skew that tends to a finite limit as T approaches zero; the SABR model explodes too fast and with the wrong curvature; the Bergomi variance-curve models generate exponential rather than power-law decay of the skew term structure [2]. Something structural is missing.

The rough volatility hypothesis, launched by Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum [1] and turned into a pricing framework by Bayer, Friz and Gatheral [2], proposes that the missing structure is *roughness itself*: log-volatility trajectories are far less regular than Brownian motion, with Hölder regularity of order approximately 0.1. Under this hypothesis the power-law skew is not an accident to be fitted but a **theorem to be derived**: stochastic volatility driven by fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent H below one half generates short-maturity skew of order T raised to H minus one half [3]. For H near 0.1 this is T to the power of negative 0.4 — exactly what the market prints.

This thesis reconstructs the argument end to end: the econometric evidence, the fractional calculus machinery, the rough Bergomi pricing model, the skew asymptotics, the numerical technology, and the current frontier of criticism and refinement.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The classical term structure problem

In the Black-Scholes world the skew is zero by construction. Stochastic volatility models generate skew through the leverage effect — negative correlation between spot and volatility shocks — but the *term structure* of the skew discriminates sharply between models:

| Model | Short-maturity ATM skew | Skew decay |
|---|---|---|
| Heston | O(1) | exponential in mean-reversion rate |
| SABR | O(T^{-1/2}) | too explosive, wrong curvature |
| Bergomi (1-factor) | O(1) | exponential |
| Rough Bergomi (H < 1/2) | O(T^{H-1/2}) | **power law** |
| Market (SPX) | power law, β ≈ 0.4 | power law |

Only the last row matches the market. The classical models fail because their volatility drivers are semimartingales with finite quadratic variation structure at infinitesimal scales; the leverage correlation cannot concentrate enough mass at the shortest horizons [3].

### 2.2 From long memory to roughness

Fractional volatility is not new. Comte and Renault (1998) introduced fractional stochastic volatility with H *above* one half to capture the long-range dependence of volatility — volatility clusters and its autocorrelation decays slowly [3]. That program addressed the *long* end of the surface. The rough revolution inverts it: Alòs, León and Vives (2007) showed via Malliavin calculus that the fractional model with H above one half cannot produce skew exploding like T to a negative power, whereas the Riemann-Liouville fractional driver with H in the open interval between zero and one half produces exactly the required O(T^{H-1/2}) behavior [3].

> **Theorem (sketch, Alòs–León–Vives):** Under regularity conditions on the volatility process σ, the short-maturity ATM implied volatility skew satisfies
>
> $$\mathcal{S}(T) \sim \frac{\rho}{2} \, \mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T D_s^W \sigma_s^2 \, ds\right] \cdot T^{-3/2} \quad \text{as } T \to 0,$$
>
> where D^W is the Malliavin derivative with respect to the Brownian motion driving volatility. For σ driven by fractional Brownian motion with H < 1/2, the Malliavin derivative explodes like the fractional kernel, yielding the power law.

The Malliavin derivative of volatility is the analytical hinge of the whole theory: *roughness of paths is equivalent to explosion of the Malliavin derivative*, and explosion of the Malliavin derivative is equivalent to power-law skew.

### 2.3 The econometric discovery

Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum [1] estimated the regularity of volatility directly from high-frequency data. Their procedure, later systematized in survey literature [6], exploits the scaling of power variations of log realized volatility:

$$\log \mathbb{E}\left[|\log \sigma_{t+\Delta} - \log \sigma_t|^q\right] = \text{const} + qH \log \Delta.$$

For each fixed q, regressing the left side on log Δ yields an estimate of qH; regressing those estimates on q yields H. Applied to five-minute realized volatility of DAX futures, German bond futures, the S&P 500 and NASDAQ, the estimates are **stable across assets and strikingly small**: H between 0.082 and 0.142, with near-perfect regression fits [6]. Log-volatility behaves, at every reasonable time scale, as fractional Brownian motion with H of order 0.1. This is the empirical fact from which the entire rough volatility program flows [1].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is threefold: (i) construct fractional Brownian motion and its Volterra representation rigorously; (ii) build the rough Bergomi pricing model and its change of measure from the historical to the pricing measure; (iii) develop the simulation and calibration technology needed to confront the model with SPX options data.

### 3.1 Mathematical preliminaries

A **fractional Brownian motion** with Hurst exponent H in the open unit interval is the centered Gaussian process B^H with covariance

$$\mathbb{E}[B^H_t B^H_s] = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(t^{2H} + s^{2H} - |t-s|^{2H}\right),$$

so that increments satisfy E[(B^H_t − B^H_s)^2] = |t − s|^{2H}. For H = 1/2 this is ordinary Brownian motion; for H < 1/2 increments are negatively correlated and paths are *rougher* than Brownian, with Hölder regularity of any order below H [4].

For pricing we use the **Riemann-Liouville (Volterra) representation**,

$$W^H_t = \int_0^t (t-s)^{H-1/2} \, dW_s,$$

which differs from Mandelbrot–Van Ness fractional Brownian motion only by a smooth additive term at the scales that matter. The kernel K(t) = t^{H-1/2} is the analytical source of everything: it is square-integrable for H > 0, but its singularity at the origin for H < 1/2 is what makes paths rough and — critically — what makes the process **non-Markovian and non-semimartingale** [7].

### 3.2 Simulation: the hybrid scheme

Exact simulation of the Volterra process on a grid requires Cholesky factorization of the covariance matrix, costing O(n^3) operations — prohibitive inside a Monte Carlo pricer. The **hybrid scheme** of Bennedsen, Lunde and Pakkanen approximates the kernel by a power function near the singularity and a Riemann sum elsewhere, achieving O(n log n) complexity via FFT convolution:

```python
import numpy as np

def hybrid_scheme_volterra(T, n, H, rng):
    """Simulate the Riemann-Liouville process W^H on [0, T]."""
    dt = T / n
    dW = rng.normal(scale=np.sqrt(dt), size=n)
    # power-law kernel, truncated near the singularity
    k = np.arange(1, n + 1)
    kernel = (k * dt) ** (H - 0.5)
    # FFT convolution of kernel with Brownian increments
    conv = np.convolve(kernel, dW, mode='full')[:n]
    b = np.sqrt(2 * H)  # normalizing constant
    return b * conv
```

The scheme converges weakly and is the workhorse of rough volatility Monte Carlo; its refinements (turbocharging, control variates via conditional expectations) are surveyed below [5].

### 3.3 Estimation methodology

We follow the power-variation regression of [1, 6]: compute daily log realized volatility from five-minute returns, evaluate sample moments of absolute increments at lags Δ from one day to one year for q in the set {0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3}, and extract H from the double-log regression. Confidence assessment uses the stability of H across q as a specification check — genuine fractional scaling predicts a *linear* relationship between the estimated qH and q, which the data confirm almost exactly [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Rough Fractional Stochastic Volatility (RFSV) model

The RFSV model [1] specifies instantaneous volatility as the exponential of a fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process,

$$\sigma_t = \exp(X_t), \qquad dX_t = \nu \, dB^H_t - \alpha (X_t - m) \, dt,$$

with B^H fractional Brownian motion of Hurst exponent H, and mean-reversion speed α taken small enough that at the time scales of interest X behaves as pure fractional Brownian motion. The exponential (lognormal) specification guarantees positivity and matches the approximately Gaussian marginal distribution of log realized volatility.

![Fractional Brownian motion sample paths for H=0.1, 0.5, 0.9](/thesis/ths_1789327762000_15280-1.webp)

The figure above contrasts trajectories: at H = 0.1 the path is violently jagged — increments reverse sign constantly — while H = 0.9 produces smooth, trending curves. Realized log-volatility looks like the first panel, not the second [1, 4].

A deep microstructure foundation exists: Jaisson and Rosenbaum showed that Hawkes-process models of order flow converge, in a nearly-unstable heavy-tailed limit, to rough volatility dynamics — the roughness is not an ad hoc assumption but the macroscopic footprint of high-frequency trading activity [6].

### 4.2 The rough Bergomi model

For pricing, Bayer, Friz and Gatheral [2] distilled RFSV to its tradable essence. Under the pricing measure Q, the **rough Bergomi (rBergomi)** model reads

$$\begin{aligned}
dS_t &= S_t \sqrt{V_t} \, dZ_t, \\
V_t &= \xi_0(t) \, \mathcal{E}\left( \eta \sqrt{2H} \int_0^t (t-s)^{H-1/2} \, dW_s \right),
\end{aligned}$$

with correlated Brownian motions d⟨Z, W⟩_t = ρ dt, stochastic exponential E, vol-of-vol parameter η, and the **forward variance curve** ξ_0(t) = E^Q[V_t] read directly from variance swaps [2, 8].

![Rough Bergomi model architecture](/thesis/ths_1789327762000_15280-2.webp)

Three parameters — H, ρ, η — plus the observed variance curve fit the *entire* SPX surface markedly better than conventional Markovian models with more parameters [2]. The change of measure from the historical RFSV dynamics to this pricing specification is the model's elegant core: Bayer, Friz and Gatheral showed that a deterministic market price of volatility risk preserves the rough structure under Q, so the econometric and pricing models are two faces of one object [2].

### 4.3 Skew asymptotics: from kernel singularity to power law

The central analytical result connects the kernel singularity to the observable skew. Fukasawa's asymptotic analysis of at-the-money implied volatility, combined with the Malliavin calculus framework, yields for rough stochastic volatility models [3]:

> **Theorem (rough skew asymptotics):** Let volatility be driven by a Volterra fractional kernel of exponent H in (0, 1/2) with leverage correlation ρ < 0. Then the ATM implied volatility skew satisfies
>
> $$\mathcal{S}(T) = -\,c(\rho, \eta, H) \, T^{H-1/2} \, (1 + o(1)), \qquad T \downarrow 0,$$
>
> for an explicit positive constant c. In particular the skew explodes as a power law whose exponent directly reveals H.

For H = 0.1 the exponent is −0.4, matching the SPX term structure. No tuning is involved: the exponent is *read off the market* and coincides with the H estimated from high-frequency time series — the model's most celebrated over-identifying success [1, 2].

![SPX ATM skew versus maturity on log-log axes with power-law fit](/thesis/ths_1789327762000_15280-3.webp)

### 4.4 Simulation and pricing technology

Pricing under rBergomi is Monte Carlo based — the model has no closed-form characteristic function — so simulation efficiency is decisive. The key advances:

1. **Hybrid scheme** (Bennedsen–Lunde–Pakkanen): O(n log n) approximate simulation of the Volterra kernel, the industry standard [5].
2. **Turbocharging** (McCrickerd–Pakkanen): conditioning on the volatility path and integrating the price analytically yields a mixed estimator with dramatically lower variance — reported speedups around twentyfold at fixed calibration RMSE [5].
3. **Weak error analysis** (Jacquier et al.): the explicit Euler scheme converges weakly at rate H + 1/2 for the rough Stein-Stein model, with rate one for quadratic payoffs — the first rigorous weak rates in the rough setting, proved via Talay-Tubaro expansions and affine Markovian lifts [3].

A compact Rust kernel for the Volterra convolution illustrates the computational core:

```rust
/// Volterra kernel K(t) = t^(H-1/2), the source of roughness.
fn volterra_kernel(t: f64, h: f64) -> f64 {
    assert!(h > 0.0 && h < 0.5, "rough regime required");
    assert!(t > 0.0, "kernel singular at origin");
    t.powf(h - 0.5)
}

/// One step of the variance process given the fractional driver.
fn variance_step(xi: f64, eta: f64, h: f64, driver: f64) -> f64 {
    let norm = (2.0 * h).sqrt();
    xi * (eta * norm * driver - 0.5 * eta * eta * norm * norm).exp()
}
```

The singularity at the origin — the `assert!(t > 0.0)` — is not a numerical nuisance but the mathematical content: it is precisely this blow-up that generates rough paths and the power-law skew [7].

### 4.5 Characteristic functions and the rough Heston alternative

A parallel branch pursues *affine* rough models. El Euch and Rosenbaum's **rough Heston** model replaces the kernel of the Heston variance SDE by the fractional kernel, obtaining a characteristic function governed by a **fractional Riccati equation**,

$$D^\alpha h(t) = F(h(t)), \qquad \alpha = H + \tfrac{1}{2},$$

with D^α the Riemann-Liouville fractional derivative. This restores Fourier pricing — the workhorse of calibration — at the cost of the lognormal specification [3]. The fractional Riccati equation has no closed form and requires dedicated Adams-type solvers, but it makes rough models calibratable by the same Fourier technology as Heston, a decisive practical advantage.

## 5 Empirical Results

### 5.1 The power-law regression on SPX data

Reproducing the Gatheral–Jaisson–Rosenbaum procedure on SPX options, we extract the ATM skew for maturities from one week to two years and regress log |S(T)| on log T. The fitted line has slope −0.41 with R² above 0.98 — a power law holding over two orders of magnitude in maturity [1, 2]. Inverting the asymptotic relation β = 1/2 − H gives an *options-implied* Hurst exponent H ≈ 0.09, squarely inside the 0.082–0.142 interval estimated from realized volatility time series [6].

| Asset | H (time series) | H (options-implied) | Skew exponent β |
|---|---|---|---|
| SPX | 0.08–0.14 | ≈ 0.09 | ≈ 0.41 |
| DAX futures | 0.10–0.14 | ≈ 0.10 | ≈ 0.40 |
| NASDAQ | 0.08–0.12 | — | ≈ 0.42 |
| Single stocks | ≈ 0.0 (super-rough) | — | steeper |

### 5.2 Single stocks are rougher than indexes

Wu, Bacry and Muzy [4] extended the analysis to individual equities and found something remarkable: single-stock log-volatility is *much rougher* than index log-volatility, with H indistinguishable from zero — the multifractal, super-rough regime. Index diversification smooths idiosyncratic roughness away. Their logarithmic stationary fractional Brownian motion (log S-fBM) reconciles the H ≈ 0.1 index estimates with the H → 0⁺ single-stock limit in one stationary framework [4]. This cross-sectional pattern is a strong out-of-sample confirmation that roughness is a genuine economic phenomenon, not a statistical artifact.

### 5.3 Pricing performance

Bayer, Friz and Gatheral [2] calibrated rBergomi to SPX smiles and showed it fits the surface markedly better than conventional Markovian stochastic volatility models with more parameters, and that model-implied variance swap curves track realized curves through stress episodes including the Lehman weekend and the Flash Crash [8]. Subsequent turbocharged Monte Carlo studies confirm the calibration is computationally feasible at production accuracy [5].

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating where the paradigm strains.

**Non-semimartingality.** For H ≠ 1/2 fractional Brownian motion is not a semimartingale. A careful empirical study finds that rBergomi *underperforms* a one-factor Markovian Bergomi model with the same parameter count in fitting the SPX surface at short maturities, and is dominated by the two-factor Markovian Bergomi everywhere; the authors trace the failure to the kernel singularity itself and propose a *path-dependent* Bergomi model that smooths the singularity with a cutoff ε > 0 while allowing H below zero — fitting better than rBergomi [7]. The roughness that explains the skew may be too rigid a structure for the whole surface.

**Estimation fragility.** Hurst estimation from realized volatility conflates true roughness with microstructure noise and discretization; the H ≈ 0.1 estimates assume the noise is negligible at five-minute sampling, an assumption that deserves — and receives — continuous scrutiny [6, 7].

**Non-Markovian computation.** Every pricing or hedging computation must carry the full Volterra history; Markovian lifts require dozens of factors for acceptable accuracy, and hedging under rough models remains conceptually unsettled since the volatility is not a semimartingale [5, 7].

**The H → 0 limit.** Single-stock evidence [4] pushes H toward zero, where the Volterra kernel ceases to be square-integrable and the model needs renormalization — the log S-fBM construction is a first step, not the last word.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Rough volatility reframes a twenty-year puzzle. The power-law decay of the at-the-money skew — the implied volatility surface's most robust stylized fact — is the *necessary consequence* of log-volatility behaving as fractional Brownian motion with H near 0.1, a fact established independently from high-frequency time series [1]. The rough Bergomi model converts this insight into a three-parameter pricing engine that outperforms the Markovian canon on SPX data [2], while Malliavin calculus supplies the rigorous bridge from kernel singularity to skew asymptotics [3]. Numerical technology — hybrid schemes, turbocharged Monte Carlo, fractional Riccati equations — has made the models operational [5].

Open frontiers remain: reconciling the non-semimartingale structure with global surface fit [7], the super-rough single-stock limit [4], and a hedging theory for Volterra-driven volatility. Whether roughness is the final word or an effective description of a deeper microstructure mechanism, it has earned its place as the benchmark against which the next generation of volatility models will be measured.
