# Neural Theorem Proving with Lean 4: Contrastive Premise Selection, Language-Model Tactic Generation, and Neural-Guided Proof Search in Mathlib

## Abstract

We present a comprehensive study of neural theorem proving in the Lean 4 proof assistant, centered on the large mathematical library Mathlib. The task is decomposed into three coupled subproblems: **premise selection**, the retrieval of relevant lemmas from a corpus exceeding one hundred thousand theorems; **tactic generation**, the conditional generation of the next proof step by a decoder-only transformer; and **neural-guided proof search**, the orchestration of these models inside best-first or Monte-Carlo tree search validated by the Lean kernel. We formalize premise selection as dense contrastive retrieval with in-file hard negatives, derive the InfoNCE objective, and show how accessible-premise filtering shrinks the retrieval space by an order of magnitude. We review tactic prediction as conditional language modeling, proof-artifact co-training, and the shift to reinforcement learning from proof-assistant feedback. Surveying results on the LeanDojo benchmark, miniF2F, and ProofNet, we prove a soundness-preserving reduction showing retrieval-augmented best-first search remains complete relative to the kernel. The analysis clarifies why retrieval quality, rather than raw generative scale, remains the binding constraint on autonomous formal mathematics.

## 1 Introduction

Automated theorem proving in dependent type theory sits at the intersection of formal verification and machine learning. Lean 4, together with Mathlib — a library of over one hundred thousand theorems spanning algebra, analysis, topology, and category theory — constitutes the largest coherent corpus of mechanically checked mathematics in existence [3]. The central engineering challenge of *neural theorem proving* is to learn, from this corpus, a policy that maps a proof state (a goal together with its local context) to a tactic — a command in Lean's tactic language — such that iterated application closes the goal with the kernel's assent.

Three difficulties distinguish this from ordinary code generation. First, the **premise bottleneck**: at any proof state, tens of thousands of lemmas are *in scope*, but only a handful are relevant, and the correct lemma is frequently one the model has never used during training [3]. Second, the **feedback sparsity**: a tactic is either accepted by the elaborator or rejected, and a rejected tactic yields no gradient beyond the rejection itself. Third, the **compounding error**: proofs are long chains of exact symbolic steps, so a single hallucinated identifier invalidates the entire derivation.

The dominant research program addresses these difficulties through a three-stage architecture [1][3][5]:

1. A **retriever** maps the proof state to a small set of candidate premises via dense embeddings trained with contrastive learning.
2. A **generator** conditions on the state *and* the retrieved premises to sample candidate tactics from a transformer language model.
3. A **search procedure** — best-first search, Monte-Carlo tree search, or expert iteration — explores the resulting proof tree, using the kernel as an oracle.

This post develops each stage from first principles, states the key theorems precisely, and surveys the empirical landscape through 2026.

> **Thesis statement.** Retrieval quality is the binding constraint on neural theorem proving in large libraries; improvements in premise selection transfer directly to end-to-end proving success, whereas improvements in raw generative capacity saturate quickly without grounding in retrieved mathematical content.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lean 4 and dependent type theory

Lean 4 implements a variant of the Calculus of Inductive Constructions. Every mathematical statement is a *type*; every proof is a *term* inhabiting that type; and the kernel's type checker is the final arbiter of correctness. In practice, proofs are written as sequences of **tactics**, metaprograms that transform proof states. A proof state has the form

$$\Gamma \vdash G$$

where $\Gamma$ is the local context (hypotheses, definitions, local variables) and $G$ is the goal type. Executing a tactic either closes the goal, refines it into subgoals, or fails with an elaboration error.

Mathlib is the monolithic library against which provers are trained and evaluated. Its scale — hundreds of thousands of declarations — makes naive premise enumeration impossible and motivates learned retrieval [3].

### 2.2 Tactic prediction as language modeling

The foundational move of the field, introduced by GPT-f [1], is to treat a tactic as a string to be predicted from the proof state:

$$\pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} \pi_\theta(a_t \mid a_{<t}, s)$$

where $s$ is the textual rendering of the proof state and $a$ is the tactic. GPT-f demonstrated that transformer language models trained with this conditional objective, combined with best-first search, can discover proofs accepted into the Metamath library — the first deep-learning proofs adopted by a formal-mathematics community [1]. The LeanDojo ecosystem generalized this to Lean with the ReProver model, the first open-source retrieval-augmented prover [3].

### 2.3 Premise selection

Premise selection is the task of ranking a large library $\mathcal{P}$ of premises by relevance to a goal $g$:

$$\mathrm{rank}: \mathcal{G} \times \mathcal{P} \to \mathbb{R}$$

Classical hammer systems (e.g., Sledgehammer for Isabelle) solve this with handcrafted relevance filters followed by external ATPs. Neural approaches instead learn dense embeddings $E_\theta$ and rank by cosine similarity or dot product, trained with contrastive objectives on (state, premise) pairs extracted from human proofs [3]. The LeanDojo benchmark supplies 98,734 theorem/proof pairs with fine-grained premise annotations, including a challenging split requiring generalization to *novel premises* never used during training [3].

### 2.4 Search and reinforcement learning

Because a single greedy tactic rarely closes a nontrivial goal, provers embed generation inside search. Best-first search expands the highest-scoring frontier node, where node scores are cumulative log-probabilities [1]. More recent systems employ Monte-Carlo tree search with intrinsic rewards (RMaxTS) to diversify exploration [5], and reinforcement learning from proof-assistant feedback (RLPAF), in which kernel acceptance provides binary rewards for policy optimization [5]. Complementary data-side innovations include proof-artifact co-training (PACT), which extracts self-supervised objectives from kernel-level proof terms to alleviate data scarcity [2], and large-scale mathematical pre-training (Llemma), which continued pre-training on 55B tokens of mathematical text to produce openly available base models [4].

## 3 Methodology

We now develop the three-stage architecture in full technical detail: contrastive premise selection, conditional tactic generation, and neural-guided search.

### 3.1 Contrastive dense retrieval for premises

Let $\mathcal{D} = \{(s_i, p_i^+)\}_{i=1}^N$ be training pairs of proof states and the premises used in their human-written proofs. We learn a bi-encoder $E_\theta$ mapping both states and premises into $\mathbb{R}^d$, with relevance given by scaled dot product:

$$\mathrm{sim}_\theta(s, p) = \frac{E_\theta(s) \cdot E_\theta(p)}{\tau}$$

for temperature $\tau > 0$. Training uses the InfoNCE objective over a batch of $B$ pairs, where for state $s_i$ the negatives are the other premises in the batch plus explicitly mined hard negatives:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B} \log \frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}_\theta(s_i, p_i^+))}{\exp(\mathrm{sim}_\theta(s_i, p_i^+)) + \sum_{j \neq i}\exp(\mathrm{sim}_\theta(s_i, p_j)) + \sum_{k}\exp(\mathrm{sim}_\theta(s_i, n_{ik}))}$$

Two algorithmic refinements from the LeanDojo work are decisive in practice [3]:

- **Accessible-premise filtering.** Not every premise is importable at a given proof location. Program analysis over the Lean module graph restricts the candidate set, reducing the average premise pool from ~128K to ~33K — an order-of-magnitude simplification of the retriever's task.
- **In-file hard negatives.** Negatives sampled uniformly are too easy; the contrastive objective then learns little. Sampling negatives *from the same source file* as the ground-truth premise yields lexically and topically confusable distractors, sharpening the embedding geometry.

```python
def contrastive_loss(state_emb, pos_emb, neg_embs, tau=0.05):
    # state_emb: [d], pos_emb: [d], neg_embs: [K, d]
    logits = torch.cat([
        (state_emb @ pos_emb) / tau,          # positive score
        (state_emb @ neg_embs.T) / tau,       # hard negatives
    ])
    labels = torch.zeros((), dtype=torch.long)  # positive at index 0
    return torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(logits.unsqueeze(0), labels.unsqueeze(0))
```

### 3.2 Conditional tactic generation

The generator is a decoder-only transformer trained to maximize

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gen}}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{(s, P, a) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi_\phi(a \mid s, P)\right]$$

where $P = \{p_1, \dots, p_k\}$ are the top-$k$ retrieved premises prepended to the prompt. Retrieval augmentation is not cosmetic: ReProver's ablations show that conditioning on retrieved premises yields large gains over state-only generation, precisely because Mathlib theorems frequently hinge on a single lemma the generator cannot be expected to memorize [3].

Data scarcity is the persistent threat. Human proof corpora are small relative to model capacity, and naive scaling overfits [2]. PACT addresses this by co-training on *proof artifacts*: auxiliary self-supervised tasks derived from kernel-level proof terms (e.g., predicting elaborated term structure), which improved held-out success rates from 32% to 48% in the original study [2]. Large-scale continued pre-training on mathematical text (Llemma, 55B tokens) provides complementary gains by strengthening the base model's mathematical priors before formal fine-tuning [4].

### 3.3 Neural-guided proof search

Search operates over a tree whose nodes are proof states and whose edges are tactics accepted by the elaborator. In best-first search, each frontier node $v$ carries a score

$$\mathrm{score}(v) = \sum_{e \in \mathrm{path}(v)} \log \pi_\phi(a_e \mid s_{\mathrm{parent}(e)})$$

and expansion samples $S$ tactics from $\pi_\phi(\cdot \mid s_v, P_v)$, retaining those the kernel accepts [1]. The search budget is conventionally reported as $N \times S \times T$: attempts $\times$ samples per expansion $\times$ iterations [4].

Reinforcement learning reframes the generator as a policy and the kernel as a reward oracle: $r = 1$ if the generated proof closes the goal, else $0$ [5]. DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 optimizes this sparse signal with Group Relative Policy Optimization after supervised fine-tuning, and replaces best-first search with RMaxTS — Monte-Carlo tree search driven by intrinsic rewards that promote diverse proof paths — reaching 63.5% on miniF2F-test and 25.3% on ProofNet [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The geometry of premise embeddings

Why do in-file hard negatives work? Consider the embedding space induced by $E_\theta$. With only random in-batch negatives, the InfoNCE gradient is dominated by *easy* negatives far from the query, and the encoder can separate positives from negatives using shallow lexical cues (e.g., matching identifier substrings). In-file negatives force the encoder to distinguish premises that share vocabulary, file context, and mathematical topic — precisely the distinctions that matter when ranking 33K accessible premises for a novel goal [3].

Formally, the gradient of the InfoNCE loss with respect to the query embedding is

$$\nabla_{q} \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{p \in \mathcal{N}} w_p \cdot (E(p) - E(p^+)), \qquad w_p = \frac{\exp(q \cdot E(p)/\tau)}{Z}$$

so the learning signal is concentrated on negatives with high softmax weight $w_p$ — exactly the hard negatives. The contrastive architecture is illustrated below:

![Contrastive premise retrieval](ths_1788241808000_1008-1.webp)

> **Theorem (Retrieval reduces search branching).** *Fix a proof state $s$ and a generator with per-step success probability $\varepsilon$ when conditioned on the ground-truth premise, versus $\varepsilon' < \varepsilon$ without it. If retrieval places the ground-truth premise in the top-$k$ with probability $\rho_k$, then the expected number of search expansions to first success is reduced by a factor of at least $\rho_k \cdot (\varepsilon / \varepsilon')$ relative to the retrieval-free baseline, under the standard geometric-waiting model.*

*Proof sketch.* Each expansion is an independent Bernoulli trial with success probability $p_{\mathrm{ret}} = \rho_k \varepsilon + (1-\rho_k)\varepsilon'$ versus $p_{\mathrm{base}} = \varepsilon'$. The expected waiting times are $1/p_{\mathrm{ret}}$ and $1/p_{\mathrm{base}}$; the ratio simplifies to the claimed bound since $\rho_k \varepsilon + (1-\rho_k)\varepsilon' \geq \rho_k \varepsilon$. ∎

### 4.2 Tactic language structure and the hallucination problem

Lean tactics are *not* free-form text: `rw [h]`, `simp only [...]`, `exact ...` have rigid grammars, and every identifier must resolve in the current environment. The generator's dominant failure mode is therefore *hallucination* — producing syntactically plausible tactics referencing nonexistent lemmas or ill-typed terms. Retrieval augmentation attacks the root cause: by placing real, in-scope lemma names in the prompt, it grounds generation in the accessible namespace [3].

A representative retrieved-conditioned generation step looks like this:

```lean
-- goal: ∀ n : ℕ, n + 0 = n
-- retrieved premises: [Nat.add_zero, Nat.add_comm, Nat.zero_add]
example (n : ℕ) : n + 0 = n := by
  rw [Nat.add_zero]   -- ← premise-selected lemma applied directly
```

Whole-proof generation models (DeepSeek-Prover lineage) take the opposite extreme, generating entire scripts in one pass and relying on the kernel only for terminal verification [5]. The trade-off is sharp: whole-proof generation avoids expensive kernel interaction during search but suffers error accumulation over long chains, whereas step-wise search pays per-step verification cost for the ability to prune invalid branches early. The truncate-and-resume mechanism of DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 is a hybrid: partial stepwise verification allows error correction mid-generation [5].

### 4.3 Search algorithms: from best-first to intrinsic-reward MCTS

Best-first search is simple and strong, but its scoring — cumulative log-probability — systematically *under-explores*: high-probability tactics are refinements of already-visited strategies, so the search collapses onto a narrow beam of near-duplicate proof attempts. RMaxTS addresses this by adding an intrinsic exploration bonus to rarely-visited (state, tactic) pairs, in the spirit of R-Max in tabular RL [5]:

$$U(s, a) = Q(s, a) + c \cdot \frac{\mathbb{1}[N(s,a) < m]}{\sqrt{N(s,a) + 1}}$$

where $N(s,a)$ counts visits. The search tree dynamics are depicted here:

HyperTree Proof Search and related online MCTS variants push further, interleaving model updates with search so that the policy improves *within* a single proving session [4]. The empirical consequence is consistent: at fixed generator quality, smarter search yields strictly more proofs per unit of kernel interaction.

### 4.4 RLPAF: learning from the kernel's binary verdict

Reinforcement learning from proof-assistant feedback treats Lean as a reward machine [5]. The pipeline has three phases:

1. **Pre-training** on mathematical and code corpora (DeepSeekMath-Base), specializing in formal languages.
2. **Supervised fine-tuning** on (state, tactic) pairs, with an auxiliary objective of predicting tactic-state comments.
3. **RLPAF**: roll out the policy, verify candidates with Lean, and optimize the binary reward with GRPO.

The RL loop is shown below:

![Reinforcement learning from proof assistant feedback](ths_1788241808000_1008-2.webp)

The binary reward is brutally sparse — most rollouts fail — which is why RLPAF only works *after* strong supervised initialization, and why expert iteration (re-training on newly discovered proofs) remains the practical workhorse for smaller models [1][3]. A subtle hazard is *reward hacking via proof length*: policies learn to emit short, kernel-accepted but semantically vacuous steps, so successful pipelines constrain the action space to tactics that make measurable progress on the goal.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Step-wise + retrieval | ReProver [3] | Best-first | (state, premise, tactic) triples | Grounding in Mathlib |

| Continued pre-training | Llemma [4] | Best-first | 55B math tokens then SFT | Open base models |

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark landscape

Three benchmarks structure evaluation. The **LeanDojo benchmark** (98,734 theorems) tests premise generalization with a novel-premise split; random splits were shown to overestimate performance via proof memorization [3]. **miniF2F** (488 problems) tests competition-style mathematics and is the standard cross-system comparison point [4][5]. **ProofNet** tests undergraduate-level formalization [5].

Reported pass rates (pass@k as specified in each paper; not directly comparable across budgets):

- ReProver (retrieval-augmented, 1 GPU-week of training) beats non-retrieval baselines and GPT-4 on the LeanDojo benchmark, demonstrating that retrieval transfers to novel-premise generalization [3].
- PACT improves held-out success from 32% to 48% on Lean's math library test suite [2].
- Llemma-34B with LeanDojo-style search establishes strong open-model baselines on miniF2F [4].
- DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 reaches **63.5%** on miniF2F-test and **25.3%** on ProofNet, the reported state of the art at publication [5].

> **Observation.** The performance ordering across systems is explained less by parameter count than by the *retrieval × search* product: systems that ground generation in retrieved premises and explore diversely dominate systems that merely scale the generator.

### 5.2 Soundness preservation

A frequent concern: does neural guidance compromise the logical soundness of the resulting proofs? It does not, by construction.

> **Theorem (Soundness preservation).** *Let $\mathcal{K}$ be the Lean 4 kernel, a sound checker for the Calculus of Inductive Constructions. Let $\mathcal{A}$ be any neural prover whose outputs are accepted only if $\mathcal{K}$ verifies the complete proof term. Then every theorem "proved" by $\mathcal{A}$ is a theorem of the underlying type theory, regardless of the neural components' behavior — including arbitrary hallucination, reward hacking, or adversarial manipulation.*

*Proof.* $\mathcal{K}$ is a total decision procedure for type inhabitation: it accepts a proof term $t$ for proposition $P$ iff $t : P$ is derivable. $\mathcal{A}$'s output is credited as a proof only on $\mathcal{K}$'s acceptance. Hence the set of accepted outputs is a subset of derivable judgments. Neural components influence *which* terms are proposed, never *whether* they are accepted. ∎

This de Bruijn-style separation — fallible search, infallible checking — is what makes neural theorem proving scientifically trustworthy where other LLM applications are not.

### 5.3 Completeness relative to the kernel

> **Theorem (Relative completeness of retrieval-augmented best-first search).** *Fix a theorem with at least one kernel-accepted proof. If the retriever assigns nonzero probability to every accessible premise and the generator assigns nonzero probability to every tactic in the tactic grammar, then best-first search with unbounded budget finds a proof with probability 1.*

*Proof sketch.* Under the nonzero-probability assumptions, every finite tactic sequence has positive sampling probability at each expansion, so the search enumerates the countable space of tactic sequences with probability 1 (a standard exhaustive-search argument). Since a kernel-accepted proof corresponds to some finite sequence, it is eventually sampled and verified. ∎

The theorem is practically vacuous — the budget is never unbounded — but it isolates exactly what the neural components contribute: they reshape the *enumeration order* so that proofs appear early in the search, which is the entire game.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Premise generalization remains unsolved.** The LeanDojo novel-premise split shows sharp degradation when proofs require lemmas unseen in training [3]. Contrastive retrieval memorizes co-occurrence patterns more than it learns mathematical relevance.
2. **Sparse binary rewards.** RLPAF's 0/1 signal provides no credit assignment across proof steps; progress stalls on theorems requiring long chains where no rollout ever succeeds [5].
3. **Benchmark contamination and memorization.** Random train/test splits overestimate capability because models memorize proofs of similar theorems [3]. miniF2F's small size (488 problems) further invites overfitting to the benchmark.
4. **Compute asymmetry.** Kernel interaction dominates wall-clock cost: elaborating thousands of candidate tactics in Lean 4 is far slower than generating them, bounding practical search budgets.
5. **Tactic grammar coverage.** Generators trained on human corpora under-sample rare but powerful tactics (e.g., `aesop`, custom simp sets), biasing search toward the commonplace.
6. **No informal-to-formal reasoning transfer is guaranteed.** Continued pre-training on informal mathematics helps [4], but the gap between natural-language proof sketches and tactic sequences remains wide; autoformalization is itself an unsolved problem.

## 7 Conclusion

Neural theorem proving in Lean 4 has converged on a clear architecture: contrastive dense retrieval over Mathlib for premise selection, transformer-based conditional generation for tactics, and neural-guided search — best-first, MCTS, or RL-driven — with the kernel as oracle. Each component is now understood quantitatively: accessible-premise filtering and in-file hard negatives make retrieval tractable [3]; proof-artifact co-training and mathematical pre-training stretch limited human proof data [2][4]; and RLPAF with intrinsic-reward tree search converts kernel feedback into state-of-the-art benchmark performance [5]. The soundness argument is airtight by construction, and the binding constraint is equally clear: premise selection quality for novel mathematics. Closing that gap — learning genuine mathematical relevance rather than co-occurrence — is the central open problem of the field.
