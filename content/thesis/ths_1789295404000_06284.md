# Analog In-Memory Computing with RRAM Crossbars: Kirchhoff Vector–Matrix Multiplication, Conductance Drift Compensation, Sneak-Path Mitigation, and Precision Scaling for Neural Accelerators

## Abstract

Resistive random-access memory (RRAM) crossbars promise to collapse the von Neumann memory wall by executing vector–matrix multiplication (VMM) directly where synaptic weights reside, exploiting Ohm's law for multiplication and Kirchhoff's current law for summation in O(1) time. This thesis develops a unified treatment of analog in-memory computing on RRAM crossbars: the physics of filamentary switching in metal–oxide–metal cells, the 1T1R and 1S1R cell architectures that tame sneak-path currents, conductance drift and relaxation non-idealities with their compensation strategies (noise-aware training, write–verify programming, global drift scaling, chip-in-the-loop calibration), and precision scaling from bit-sliced multi-crossbar encodings to hybrid analog–digital quantization. We formalize the computation as a noisy linear operator, derive error bounds as functions of array size, wire resistance, and ADC resolution, and survey landmark silicon demonstrations — from the ISAAC architecture to the NeuRRAM chip and PCM-based analog inference engines achieving 93.7% CIFAR-10 accuracy. The thesis closes by quantifying the fundamental precision–density–energy Pareto frontier and identifying open problems in endurance, three-dimensional integration, and on-chip training stability.

## 1 Intro

The energy cost of deep neural network inference is dominated not by arithmetic but by **data movement**: fetching a synaptic weight from DRAM costs two to three orders of magnitude more energy than the multiply–accumulate (MAC) operation that consumes it. As models scale toward tens of billions of parameters, this von Neumann bottleneck hardens into an architectural ceiling that process scaling alone cannot lift. Analog in-memory computing proposes a radical relocation of the computation: store the weight matrix as programmable conductances in a crossbar of non-volatile resistive devices, drive input activations as voltages along the rows, and read the output vector as summed currents along the columns. In one physical step — regardless of array size — the crossbar computes

$$\mathbf{y} = W^\top \mathbf{x}$$

through nothing more than the laws of circuit theory. Multiplication is Ohm's law ($I = GV$); summation is Kirchhoff's current law ($\sum I$); and the weights never move [4].

This thesis focuses on **resistive RAM (RRAM)** — a two-terminal metal–insulator–metal device whose resistance is switched by the growth and rupture of a conductive filament in a transition-metal oxide such as HfO₂. RRAM offers sub-10 nm scalability, CMOS back-end-of-line compatibility, and analog programmability across a continuum of conductance states. Yet the same physics introduces non-idealities: **sneak-path leakage**, **conductance drift and relaxation**, **wire IR drop**, **programming variability**, and **ADC/DAC conversion overhead**. The central question is whether a crossbar can multiply *accurately, reliably, and efficiently enough* to host real neural networks at scale.

Our contributions are organized as follows. Section 2 reviews filamentary switching, 1T1R/1S1R cells, and the Kirchhoff formulation of VMM. Section 3 formalizes the noisy-operator model, bit-sliced precision encoding, and noise-aware training. Section 4 dives into four pillars — the electrodynamics of the compute, sneak-path mitigation, drift compensation, and precision scaling. Section 5 surveys silicon evidence and derives error bounds; Section 6 enumerates limitations; Section 7 concludes.

> **Thesis Statement:** Analog VMM on RRAM crossbars is a physically exact primitive corrupted by quantifiable non-idealities; by modeling these non-idealities as a structured noise channel and co-designing devices, circuits, and training algorithms around that channel, inference accuracies within 1–2% of floating-point baselines are achievable at 10–100× energy efficiency — but only under disciplined constraints on array size, wire parasitics, and programming precision that this thesis makes explicit.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Filamentary Resistive Switching

An RRAM cell is a capacitor-like stack — e.g., TiN/HfOₓ/Pt — in which an applied electric field drives oxygen-vacancy migration, forming a nanoscale conductive filament between the electrodes. The device switches between a high-resistance state (HRS) and a low-resistance state (LRS); intermediate filament radii yield intermediate conductances, enabling **multi-level (analog) storage**. The canonical *I–V* signature is a pinched hysteresis loop: a *forming* step (initial filament creation at elevated voltage), *set* transitions (HRS→LRS, filament growth) and *reset* transitions (LRS→HRS, filament rupture by Joule heating). The switching is intrinsically stochastic — filament nucleation is a thermally activated random process — which manifests as cycle-to-cycle and device-to-device conductance variability, the root cause of write noise [6].

### 2.2 The Kirchhoff Vector–Matrix Multiply

Consider an $M \times N$ crossbar: $M$ wordlines (rows), $N$ bitlines (columns), one RRAM cell of conductance $G_{ij}$ at each intersection. Apply input voltages $V_i$ to the rows and hold each column at virtual ground. By Ohm's law the current through cell $(i,j)$ is $I_{ij} = V_i G_{ij}$; by Kirchhoff's current law the total column current is

$$I_j = \sum_{i=1}^{M} V_i G_{ij}.$$

If we encode synaptic weights $w_{ij}$ as conductances (with a differential pair $G_{ij}^+ - G_{ij}^-$ for signed weights) and activations $x_i$ as voltages, the column currents are exactly the components of $W^\top \mathbf{x}$, computed in parallel in a single read step [2][4]. This is the computational primitive underlying every RRAM accelerator.

```python
# Ideal Kirchhoff VMM: y = W^T x computed via conductances
import numpy as np

def analog_vmm(V: np.ndarray, G: np.ndarray, sigma_relax: float = 0.0,
               adc_bits: int = 8) -> np.ndarray:
    """V: input voltages (M,), G: conductances (M, N).
    Models relaxation noise + ADC quantization."""
    G_noisy = G * (1 + np.random.normal(0, sigma_relax, G.shape))
    I = V @ G_noisy                      # Kirchhoff summation
    levels = 2 ** adc_bits
    I_q = np.round(I / I.max() * (levels - 1)) / (levels - 1) * I.max()
    return I_q
```

### 2.3 From ISAAC to NeuRRAM: A Brief Architectural History

The 2016 **ISAAC** architecture (Shafiee et al., ISCA) was the first full-fledged ReRAM CNN accelerator: tiles of $128 \times 128$ crossbars with shared ADCs, DACs, and shift-and-add units for bit-serial encoding, on a concentrated-mesh network [1][5]. Later, **PUMA/Newton** refined bit-sliced aggregation [5]; **NeuRRAM** (2022, *Nature*) taped out a full RRAM compute-in-memory system reaching CIFAR-10 within 2% of software baselines via noise-resilient training [2][8]; IBM's **PCM analog inference** program hit ResNet-32 93.7% CIFAR-10 / 71.6% ImageNet with device-matched noise injection and global drift scaling [3]; and **Mythic** commercialized analog compute in embedded NOR flash targeting ~4 TOPS/W [10].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Noisy-Operator Model

We model the crossbar as a linear operator corrupted by a structured noise channel:

$$\hat{\mathbf{y}} = Q_{\text{ADC}}\!\left( (W + \Delta W_{\text{prog}} + \Delta W_{\text{drift}}(t) + \Delta W_{\text{read}}) ^\top ( \mathbf{x} + \Delta \mathbf{x}_{\text{DAC}}) + \mathbf{n}_{\text{IR}} \right),$$

where $\Delta W_{\text{prog}}$ is write variability, $\Delta W_{\text{drift}}(t)$ is time-dependent conductance drift, $\Delta W_{\text{read}}$ is read noise (including 1/f noise), $\Delta \mathbf{x}_{\text{DAC}}$ is input quantization noise, $\mathbf{n}_{\text{IR}}$ captures wire-resistance-induced distortion, and $Q_{\text{ADC}}$ is ADC quantization. The methodology of this thesis is to **characterize each term experimentally, inject its statistics during training, and verify the hardened model on hardware** — the hardware–algorithm co-optimization loop pioneered by the NeuRRAM and IBM PCM programs [2][3].

### 3.2 Noise-Aware Training Protocol

The protocol proceeds in four stages:

1. **Characterization.** Measure conductance distributions, drift coefficients, and read-noise spectra on the target RRAM technology (e.g., Gaussian relaxation spread in NeuRRAM [2]).
2. **Noise injection.** During forward passes of software training, perturb weights with noise sampled from the measured distribution — not uniform quantization noise, but the *actual device statistics*. For PCM drift, inject noise proportional to combined read/write conductance noise with a single additional hyperparameter, the weight clip scale $\alpha$ [3].
3. **Write–verify programming.** Program conductances iteratively (program → read → adjust) until each cell lands within tolerance, trading programming time for $\Delta W_{\text{prog}}$ reduction [2].
4. **Chip-in-the-loop calibration.** Fine-tune on measured hardware outputs (progressive model fine-tuning), optimize per-layer input voltage swing to fully utilize ADC dynamic range, and cancel ADC offsets — *model-driven chip calibration* [2].

### 3.3 Precision Encoding

Because a single RRAM cell reliably resolves only ~4–6 bits of conductance, higher-precision weights are encoded across multiple cells or crossbars (**bit slicing**): weight $w$ is decomposed into $k$ slices $w = \sum_{s} 2^{s b} w_s$, each slice stored on a separate crossbar, with partial sums combined by digital shift-and-add [1][5]. Inputs are likewise bit-serialized through DACs or applied as pulse trains. This trades array area and conversion energy for precision, and its cost is analyzed in §4.4.

```haskell
-- Bit-sliced weight encoding: reconstruct w from k slices of b bits
type Slice = [Int]          -- one crossbar's worth of b-bit conductance codes
reconstruct :: Int -> [Slice] -> [Double]
reconstruct b slices =
  [ sum [ fromIntegral (sl !! i) * 2 ^ (s * b)
        | (s, sl) <- zip [0..] slices ]
  | i <- [0 .. length (head slices) - 1] ]
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Electrodynamics of the Crossbar Compute: From Ideal Kirchhoff to IR Drop

The ideal analysis of §2.2 assumes zero wire resistance. In real arrays, wordlines and bitlines have parasitic resistance $r_w, r_b$ per cell pitch. The voltage actually seen by cell $(i,j)$ is not the driven $V_i$ but $V_i - \sum_{k<i} I_{k}^{\text{row}} r_w$, where $I_k^{\text{row}}$ is the current drawn by upstream cells; similarly the column voltage floats above ground by $\sum I^{\text{col}} r_b$. This **IR drop** makes the effective conductance matrix input-dependent and position-dependent — the far corner of a large array systematically under-computes. Analytical studies show the relative MVM current error grows super-linearly with array size once wire resistance becomes comparable to the parallel combination of cell resistances [9].

> **Theorem (IR-Drop Error Scaling):** For an $N \times N$ crossbar with per-segment wire resistance $r$ and mean cell conductance $\bar{G}$, the worst-case relative output-current error satisfies $\varepsilon = \Theta(N^2 r \bar{G})$ to first order; doubling the array dimension quadruples the systematic error.

*Proof sketch.* The row current drawn by $N$ cells is $\sim N\bar{G}V$; the cumulative row drop at the far end is $\sim N \cdot (N\bar{G}V) r = N^2 \bar{G} r V$. The fractional voltage error is therefore $N^2 \bar{G} r$, and column-side drops contribute the same order. ∎

Mitigations: cap array size (128×128 is the community sweet spot [1][2]), engineer lower-$\bar{G}$ states to cut currents at the cost of SNR, and pre-characterize and digitally invert the IR-drop pattern. Note the tension: lower conductance reduces IR drop but also shrinks the signal relative to ADC noise and leakage.

### 4.2 Sneak-Path Mitigation: 1T1R, 1S1R, and Bias Schemes

In a passive 1R crossbar, every read voltage applied to a selected cell also biases a vast network of half-selected and unselected cells, creating **sneak-path currents** that flow through unintended routes (e.g., row $i$ → column $k$ → row $i'$ → column $j$) and corrupt the sensed current. The worst case occurs when the selected cell is at HRS while all others sit at LRS — the sneak leakage can exceed the signal by orders of magnitude in arrays beyond ~1 kbit [6].

Three families of countermeasures exist:

| Approach | Mechanism | Density cost | Compute suitability |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1T1R** | Series transistor per cell; gate selects the cell, all others fully off | 6F² cell, loses crossbar density advantage | Excellent: precise, CMOS-integrated, used by NeuRRAM [2] |
| **1S1R** | Two-terminal nonlinear selector (OTS, MIEC, IMT/VO₂) in series; cuts leakage at low bias | ~4F², near-passive density | Good: selector nonlinearity must survive analog read voltages [7] |
| **Bias schemes** | ½-bias or ⅓-bias on unselected lines; floating-line sensing; sneak current as differential reference [6] | None | Moderate: works for memory reads; complicates parallel analog VMM |

The **1T1R** configuration is the workhorse of analog compute demonstrators: per-cell isolation, programming current compliance, and CMOS integration at the cost of area. **1S1R** preserves density via nonlinear selectors (OTS, VO₂ IMT) whose sub-threshold leakage suppression must be co-designed with analog read levels [7]. Algorithmic biasing — e.g., using sneak current itself as a differential reference, enabling a selector-free 128×128 1R array read at 9.7 μA — pushes passive arrays further than naive analysis suggests [6]; for parallel multi-row *compute*, however, isolation devices remain the robust choice.

```rust
// 1T1R cell access model: transistor gate enables exactly one cell per (row, col)
struct Cell1T1R { g: f64, gate_open: bool, vth: f64 }

fn column_current(cells: &[Cell1T1R], v_row: &[f64]) -> f64 {
    cells.iter().zip(v_row)
        .filter(|(c, _)| c.gate_open)          // selector eliminates sneak paths
        .map(|(c, v)| v * c.g)
        .sum()                                  // Kirchhoff summation, no leakage term
}
```

### 4.3 Conductance Drift Compensation: From Physics to Training

Even a perfectly programmed crossbar decays. In PCM, structural relaxation of the amorphous phase raises resistance as $R(t) = R_0 (t/t_0)^\nu$ with drift exponent $\nu \approx 0.05$–$0.1$; RRAM exhibits analogous **conductance relaxation** — a fast initial decay after programming (seconds to minutes) followed by slow drift — plus **1/f read noise** and random telegraph noise that no static compensation can remove [3][9]. Because drift is multiplicative and state-dependent (high-conductance states drift faster), the entire weight matrix effectively rescales and distorts over time, and a network accurate at $t_0$ can lose several accuracy points within a day.

The compensation stack, from device to algorithm:

1. **Write–verify with relaxation guard-banding.** Program slightly above target so post-relaxation conductance lands on target; iterate until convergence [2].
2. **Global drift compensation.** Periodically measure reference cells and apply a scalar correction $\hat{y} \leftarrow \hat{y}/(1+\hat{\delta}(t))$. IBM's PCM work found this *critical* — hardware ResNet-32 stayed above 92.6% CIFAR-10 over 24 hours [3].
3. **Noise-aware training.** Inject Gaussian noise with the measured relaxation distribution during training. On NeuRRAM this lifted CIFAR-10 from 25.34% (naive mapping) to 85.99% — a 60-point recovery [2]; IBM's variant injects noise proportional to combined read/write conductance noise with a single new hyperparameter, the clip scale $\alpha$ [3].
4. **Batch-norm recalibration + chip-in-the-loop fine-tuning.** Periodically re-estimate batch-norm statistics on hardware activations (92.6% → 93.5% 1-day accuracy [3]); NeuRRAM's progressive fine-tuning trains on the chip's actual noisy outputs [2].

> **Theorem (Drift-Induced Accuracy Floor):** If per-device drift variability has standard deviation $\sigma_\nu$ across the array, no global scalar compensation can reduce the residual weight error below $\approx \sigma_\nu \log(t/t_0) \cdot \bar{w}$; only per-device tracking or noise-hardened training closes this gap.

*Intuition.* Global scaling corrects the mean drift $\bar{\nu}$ exactly, but the spread $\sigma_\nu$ leaves a device-dependent multiplicative error that grows logarithmically with time. Training with injected noise of matching variance makes the network's loss landscape flat along exactly these perturbation directions. ∎

### 4.4 Precision Scaling: Bit Slicing, ADC Economics, and the Energy Wall

Analog precision is bounded by physics — a single RRAM cell's usable conductance resolution is ~4–6 bits given write noise and drift — so system precision is an *architectural* choice. **Bit slicing** distributes an 8-bit weight across two 4-bit crossbars (or four 2-bit), with digital shift-and-add recombining partial sums; inputs are bit-serialized through 1-bit DACs or pulse-width modulation [1][5]. The cost model is unforgiving:

- **ADCs dominate.** In ISAAC-class designs the ADCs account for the majority of tile power and area; every doubling of ADC resolution roughly quadruples conversion energy. Sharing one ADC across 128 columns via sample-and-hold multiplexing amortizes area but serializes readout, trading throughput [1].
- **Slicing multiplies conversions.** $k$ weight slices × $b$ input bits = $k \cdot b$ analog VMM passes per layer, each needing ADC conversion; precision scales the energy bill linearly.
- **The Pareto frontier.** NeuRRAM stored high-precision weights directly as analog conductances on just *two* cells per weight and hardened via noise injection — 4-bit-equivalent accuracy at 2× the density of bit-sliced baselines [2]. Analog programmability, used honestly, beats digital-in-analog emulation.

| Design point | Weight precision | Cells/weight | Relative energy | CIFAR-10 (reported) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ISAAC-CE bit-sliced [1] | 16-bit (sliced) | 8+ | 1.0× (baseline) | simulated |
| 1-cell-per-bit RRAM [2] | 4-bit | 4 | ~0.5× | ~86% |
| NeuRRAM analog pair [2] | ~4-bit effective | 2 | ~0.25× | 85.99% |
| IBM PCM + noise training [3] | analog | 2 (diff. pair) | — | 93.7% (ResNet-32) |

The table's lesson: **precision is a system property, not a device property** — and the cheapest bits are the ones the training algorithm learns to not need.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Silicon Evidence

- **ISAAC (2016)** demonstrated a complete tiled ReRAM CNN accelerator in simulation with detailed circuit models: 12 IMAs per tile, eight 128×128 crossbars per IMA, c-mesh interconnect, reporting order-of-magnitude efficiency gains over digital baselines for CNN inference [1][5].
- **NeuRRAM (2022, *Nature*)** taped out a 48-core RRAM compute-in-memory chip in 130 nm CMOS. With noise-resilient training and write–verify programming it ran CNNs, LSTMs, and RBMs at software-comparable accuracy — the first versatile RRAM CIM chip, not a single-model demo [2][8].
- **IBM PCM inference (2020, *Nature Communications*)** programmed 723,444 PCM devices with ResNet-32 weights and held >92.6% CIFAR-10 for 24 h with drift compensation, reaching 71.6% ImageNet top-1 — the strongest evidence that analog resistive hardware can host modern CNNs [3].
- **Fully hardware memristive MLP (2021, *Science Advances*)** built an end-to-end analog network with hardware neurons, eliminating digital processors from the hidden layers entirely and proving the VMM primitive composes [4].
- **Mythic (commercial)** shipped analog compute-in-memory in 40 nm embedded NOR flash, validating the business case: ~4 TOPS/W targets at INT4/INT8 with weights stationary in flash [10].

### 5.2 Analytical Error Bound

Combining the noise terms of §3.1, the output SNR of an $N$-column analog VMM with $b$-bit ADCs, per-device write noise $\sigma_p$, drift $\sigma_d(t)$, and read noise $\sigma_r$ is approximately

$$\mathrm{SNR}^{-1} \approx \underbrace{\tfrac{2^{-2b}}{12}}_{\text{ADC}} + \underbrace{N(\sigma_p^2 + \sigma_d^2(t) + \sigma_r^2)}_{\text{device, grows with } N} + \underbrace{\Theta(N^4 r^2 \bar{G}^2)}_{\text{IR drop}}.$$

Three design rules follow: (i) keep $N \lesssim 128$; (ii) spend the ADC budget where the signal is — per-layer dynamic-range calibration [2]; (iii) push residual device error into training, where noise injection converts it from accuracy loss into a one-time training cost.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Endurance.** Filamentary RRAM endures $10^6$–$10^{12}$ cycles depending on stack — ample for inference (weights written once) but marginal for on-chip training, where millions of update pulses per device are routine. Update-disturbance studies show resilience to ~100k non-coincident pulses, but full training workloads remain an open stress test [2].
2. **Array-size ceiling.** IR drop and sneak-path economics cap practical analog arrays at ~128×128; larger matrices must be tiled, reintroducing the data-movement costs the architecture was meant to eliminate.
3. **3D integration immaturity.** Stacking crossbars vertically would multiply density, but selector co-integration, thermal crosstalk, and yield in 3D RRAM remain research-grade [6].
4. **Training gap.** All high-accuracy demonstrations train in software and *transfer* to analog; true on-chip gradient training faces asymmetric, nonlinear update behavior that breaks backpropagation's assumptions.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Analog in-memory computing on RRAM crossbars is a maturing paradigm with taped-out silicon, accuracy within striking distance of digital baselines, and a clear foundation in Kirchhoff's laws. This thesis has shown every major obstacle — sneak paths, drift, limited precision, wire parasitics — admits a quantitative model and a co-design layer: 1T1R/1S1R isolation, global and learned drift compensation, noise-injected training, bit-sliced precision scaling, disciplined 128×128 tiling. The deepest lesson is methodological: **treat the crossbar as a noisy channel, characterize it, and code the network against it** — the philosophy that made wireless communication reliable. The open frontier is on-chip training and 3D integration; whoever solves those inherits the post–von Neumann era.
