# Quantum Error Mitigation: Zero-Noise Extrapolation, Probabilistic Error Cancellation, and Virtual Distillation

## Abstract

Noisy intermediate-scale quantum hardware accumulates errors faster than variational workloads can absorb them, while full fault tolerance remains a distant milestone. Quantum error mitigation bridges this gap by extracting improved expectation values from ensembles of noisy circuit executions, without additional qubits or active correction. This thesis develops the theory, practice, and fundamental limits of three flagship techniques: zero-noise extrapolation, which deliberately amplifies noise and extrapolates measured expectation values back to the zero-noise limit; probabilistic error cancellation, which inverts noise channels exactly in expectation through quasiprobability sampling at exponential sampling cost; and virtual distillation, which suppresses errors by entangling multiple copies of the noisy state without ever preparing the purified state. We derive the estimator formalisms, compare their sampling overheads against the fundamental bound that mitigation cost must grow exponentially with the circuit fault rate, and analyze failure modes including non-perturbative noise, coherent mismatch, and benchmarking artefacts. The result is a unified account of where error mitigation genuinely extends near-term hardware, and where it cannot.

## 1 Introduction: Error Mitigation in the NISQ Regime

Quantum computation lives in tension with its substrate. Every gate, idle interval, and measurement exposes the register to decoherence and control imperfections, and the resulting faults accumulate multiplicatively with circuit size. Quantum error correction promises to suppress this accumulation to arbitrarily low levels, but only in exchange for encoding overheads — hundreds to thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit — that place fault tolerance beyond the reach of contemporary devices [1]. Quantum error mitigation (QEM) occupies the intervening territory: a family of protocols that do not remove errors from the hardware but instead combine measurement data from many noisy executions to estimate what the noiseless expectation value would have been [2].

The distinction matters. Error correction acts at the state level, actively restoring the quantum state toward the code space. Mitigation acts at the level of statistics: the estimator's bias is reduced while its variance is inflated, and the price is paid in samples rather than qubits. Because no known mitigation protocol can beat the elementary counting argument that only a fraction P0 = e^{-λ} of circuit runs are fault-free when the circuit fault rate is λ, every mitigation scheme inherits an exponential sampling overhead in λ [2]. The practical question is therefore never whether mitigation is free, but which technique buys the largest bias reduction per unit of sampling cost under realistic, learnable assumptions about the noise.

This thesis develops that accounting for the three most influential mitigation techniques. Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE) [1, 5] deliberately amplifies the noise and extrapolates measured expectation values back to the zero-noise limit, requiring no characterization of the noise model. Probabilistic error cancellation (PEC) [1, 3] learns the noise channels and inverts them exactly in expectation through quasiprobability sampling, trading characterization effort for unbiasedness. Virtual distillation (VD) [4] prepares multiple copies of the noisy state and estimates expectation values with respect to an exponentially purified virtual state, suppressing incoherent errors without ever preparing that state. Together they span the design space of modern mitigation: model-free versus model-based, biased versus unbiased, single-copy versus multi-copy.

## 2 Zero-Noise Extrapolation: Scaling, Fitting, and Inference

The premise of ZNE is disarmingly simple. If the expectation value of an observable depends smoothly on the noise strength, then measuring that dependence at several artificially amplified noise levels and extrapolating to zero noise yields an estimate of the ideal value [1]. Formally, let E(λ) denote the noisy expectation value at noise strength λ. One posits the expansion

E(λ) = E(0) + a_1 λ + a_2 λ^2 + ... + a_m λ^m + R,

and estimates E(0) from measurements at scaled noise strengths c_1 λ through c_K λ with each c_i at least unity. The classical tool is Richardson extrapolation: given K = m + 1 distinct scale factors, there exist weights β_k — the Lagrange basis evaluated at zero — such that the weighted combination of the scaled measurements equals E(0) up to terms of order λ^{m+1}, cancelling the first m powers of the noise [1].

Everything hinges on two operations: amplifying the noise in a controlled way, and choosing an extrapolation model whose residual error and variance amplification are understood. The most hardware-agnostic amplification method is unitary folding, which replaces the circuit U by U followed by n repetitions of U† U, stretching the depth by a factor of roughly 2n + 1 while preserving the logical action [2]. Pulse-level stretching and identity insertion achieve similar effects with finer granularity, and probabilistic error amplification learns a noise model and deliberately injects additional Pauli faults to reach calibrated noise levels [2]. Multivariate generalizations treat the noise of each circuit layer as an independent variable and interpolate in the resulting multi-dimensional space, which can outperform single-variable extrapolation when different layers suffer different error rates [2].

The extrapolation step is where optimism meets statistics. Richardson weights grow rapidly in magnitude with the extrapolation order — alternating signs and large magnitudes that amplify the shot noise of each constituent measurement. The variance of the extrapolated estimator is the weighted sum of the constituent variances with squared weights, and the weights can grow exponentially in m, so each additional cancelled order of bias purchases an exponentially larger variance [5]. Mohammadipour and Li formalize this trade-off with bias and variance bounds for polynomial interpolation and show that polynomial least-squares fitting over more noise nodes than the model degree can suppress the variance amplification and avoid the overfitting to which pure interpolation is prone [5]. They further derive sample-complexity estimates: for any target precision ε, the number of shots required grows with both the approximation error of the non-polynomial noise dependence and the amplified measurement noise. The same analysis extends to joint mitigation of physical and algorithmic error, scaling the Trotter step size together with the noise level to cancel both error sources at once [5].

A subtler danger is conceptual rather than statistical. Because E(λ) is only approximately polynomial, and because noise amplification eventually erodes the signal entirely, extrapolation can manufacture apparent improvements that no longer reflect the underlying physics. Köster and Mauerer demonstrate this failure mode on real hardware: when folding pushes the circuit past the point of usable signal, the Richardson estimate decouples from the truth and overshoots the ideal value by up to 21 percent on an IQM device [6]. They introduce a "garbage-folding" negative control — noise amplification that carries no usable signal — which reports a larger apparent improvement than genuine folding, proving that the magnitude of an improvement is not evidence of its correctness. Their proposed reporting checklist demands that ZNE benchmarks verify the signal survives amplification before extrapolating [6].

```python
# Richardson weights for noise scale factors, Lagrange basis at zero
import numpy as np

def richardson_weights(scales):
    c = np.asarray(scales, dtype=float)
    K = len(c)
    beta = np.zeros(K)
    for k in range(K):
        num, den = 1.0, 1.0
        for j in range(K):
            if j == k:
                continue
            num *= c[j]
            den *= (c[j] - c[k])
        beta[k] = num / den
    return beta

w = richardson_weights([1.0, 3.0, 5.0])
print(w)            # [ 1.875 -1.25   0.375]
print(sum(w))       # 1.0: weights always sum to unity
print(sum(w**2))    # variance amplification for equal shot noise
```

The printout illustrates the central tension: the weights sum to unity, preserving unbiasedness through the modelled order, while the sum of squares — here about 5.2 — multiplies the shot noise. At higher orders and wider node spacing this factor grows exponentially, which is why practical ZNE rarely exceeds second or third order [5].

## 3 Probabilistic Error Cancellation: Unbiased Estimation by Quasiprobability Sampling

Where ZNE is model-free, probabilistic error cancellation is exactingly model-based. The insight of Temme, Bravyi, and Gambetta is that an ideal operation can be written as a real linear combination of noisy operations that the hardware can actually implement [1]. In the superoperator picture, the ideal channel U is decomposed as

U = Σ_α η_α B_α,

where the B_α are implementable noisy basis operations — noisy gates, state preparations, measurements — and the coefficients η_α are real numbers that may be negative. This is a quasiprobability representation: one samples the index α with probability p_α = |η_α| / γ, executes B_α, and multiplies the measurement outcome by γ times the sign of η_α, where γ = Σ_α |η_α| is the one-norm of the representation [1, 2]. The weighted average is exactly the ideal expectation value — the estimator is unbiased — but each sample's variance is inflated by γ^2, so recovering a fixed statistical precision requires γ^2 times as many shots [2].

The method therefore converts the problem of error mitigation into two subproblems: learning the noise well enough to construct the decomposition, and keeping γ small. For a circuit with N noisy gates, the total overhead is the product over gates of the individual γ_i, and the sampling cost scales as the square of that product, which is exponential in the circuit fault rate [2, 3]. This is not a defect of a particular implementation: Takagi et al. prove that PEC is optimal among a wide class of strategies for mitigating local dephasing noise, so the exponential overhead is, in that setting, the best any protocol can do [3]. Their fundamental bound shows more generally that the sampling overhead of any mitigation protocol must grow exponentially with circuit depth under local depolarizing noise [3].

Learning the noise is the practical bottleneck. Full process tomography is infeasible at scale, so modern PEC rests on structured, learnable noise models. The sparse Pauli–Lindblad model assumes the noise is a Pauli channel generated by a sparse set of Pauli error rates, which can be learned efficiently from benchmarking-style experiments and then inverted gate by gate [2]. A crucial refinement is probabilistic error amplification, which uses the same learned representation not to cancel the noise but to amplify it in a calibrated fashion, feeding ZNE with precisely controlled noise levels [2]. This hybrid — learned noise model, extrapolation estimator — powered utility-scale demonstrations on more than a hundred qubits, showing that mitigation can track ideal values in regimes where unmitigated signals have decayed to noise [2].

> **Theorem (informal, after Takagi et al. [3]):** For layered circuits subject to local depolarizing noise, any error-mitigation protocol that reduces the bias below a fixed threshold must incur sampling overhead exponential in the circuit depth. Moreover, probabilistic error cancellation attains the optimal overhead for local dephasing noise.

The theorem sets the terms of the debate: PEC is the gold standard for unbiasedness, but its unbiasedness is purchased at a price that grows exponentially with the very parameter — circuit size — that one wishes to scale.

## 4 Virtual Distillation: Error Suppression by Multi-Copy Purification

### 4.1 The derangement circuit and the virtual state

Virtual distillation takes a strikingly different route: instead of characterizing or scaling the noise, it exploits the spectral structure of the noisy state itself [4]. Given M copies of the noisy state ρ, one measures the ratio Tr(ρ^M O) / Tr(ρ^M) — the expectation value with respect to the "virtual" purified state ρ^M / Tr(ρ^M) — without ever preparing that state. The trick is the cyclic derangement operator S, which permutes the M copies; both numerator and denominator are expectation values of permuted observables on M independent copies, measurable with a shallow network of controlled swaps [4].

As M increases, ρ^M / Tr(ρ^M) converges exponentially fast to the projector onto the dominant eigenvector of ρ — the pure state closest to the noisy state in the appropriate sense [4]. Incoherent errors, which spread the state's weight across many eigenvectors, are therefore suppressed exponentially in M, while the dominant component is coherently reinforced. Huggins et al. demonstrate numerically that virtual distillation can suppress errors by multiple orders of magnitude, with the effect strengthening as system size grows [4].

### 4.2 The dominant eigenvector and coherent mismatch

The method's Achilles' heel is the gap between the dominant eigenvector and the ideal state. Decompose the noisy state into the ideal component plus an orthogonal error component, and let μ denote the coherent mismatch — the weight of the error components aligned against the ideal direction [4]. Virtual distillation drives the incoherent part of the error to zero exponentially in M, but it cannot remove the coherent mismatch: the asymptotic state is the dominant eigenvector, not the ideal state, and the residual bias is set by μ. In the common regime where gate noise is predominantly incoherent, μ is small and the method excels; where coherent over-rotations dominate, the dominant eigenvector is tilted away from the ideal state and distillation stalls at a noise floor [4].

### 4.3 Variance, sampling cost, and the exponential tax

Estimating a ratio of two exponentially small quantities is statistically expensive. Under incoherent noise, Tr(ρ^M) decays exponentially in M, so the denominator must be resolved with a number of shots that grows exponentially in M; the sampling overhead of virtual distillation therefore scales exponentially in the number of copies, mirroring the exponential overheads of ZNE and PEC in their respective control parameters [2, 4]. In practice M = 2 or 3 is the realistic regime, with the M = 2 derangement circuit requiring only one layer of controlled swaps across the copies and therefore modest additional circuit depth [4]. The method also composes naturally with other techniques: because it acts on the output state rather than the circuit, it can be layered atop ZNE or readout-error mitigation [2].

### 4.4 Placement among symmetry-based methods

Virtual distillation belongs to a broader family of state-level techniques that includes symmetry verification, symmetry expansion, and verified phase estimation [2]. What distinguishes it is that it requires no knowledge of the problem's symmetries and no ancilla-assisted verification circuits beyond the swap network; the purification is "virtual" in the precise sense that the estimator targets a state that exists only as a mathematical combination of measurement outcomes [4]. A further intriguing application noted by Huggins et al. is algorithmic rather than physical: virtual distillation can accelerate the convergence of randomized algorithms even in the absence of device noise, by suppressing the variance contributed by subdominant components of the sampled distribution [4].

## 5 A Comparative Accounting: Overheads, Assumptions, and Optimality

The three techniques can be compared along four axes: what must be known about the noise, whether the estimator is biased, how the sampling cost scales, and what additional quantum resources are required.

|  | Zero-noise extrapolation | Probabilistic error cancellation | Virtual distillation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Noise knowledge required | None (model-free) | Full channel characterization | None |
| Bias | Residual higher-order terms plus model error | Zero (exact in expectation) | Noise floor from coherent mismatch |
| Sampling overhead | Exponential in extrapolation order | Exponential in circuit fault rate | Exponential in copy number M |
| Extra quantum resources | Deeper folded circuits | None (randomized circuits) | M copies plus swap network |
| Dominant failure mode | Signal erosion under amplification [6] | Inaccurate noise learning | Coherent mismatch [4] |

Several unifying lessons emerge. First, the exponential sampling overhead is not an artefact of any one method: the fault-free fraction e^{-λ} caps what any protocol can achieve without active correction, and even an idealized post-selection on fault-free runs would cost e^{λ} — a factor of roughly 150 at λ = 5 and ten thousand at λ = 9 [2]. Takagi et al. elevate this intuition to a theorem, proving exponential depth scaling of the overhead for general protocols under local depolarizing noise [3]. Second, the techniques are complementary rather than competitive: calibrated noise amplification feeds ZNE [2], PEC's learned models can supply that amplification [2], and virtual distillation can purify the output of either. Third, the optimality result for PEC [3] provides a rare fixed point — a proof that, for dephasing noise, no clever post-processing can beat quasiprobability sampling — which turns the remaining research question from "can we do better?" to "how close can practical noise learning come to the optimum?"

## 6 Limitations

The limitations of error mitigation are structural, and honesty about them is part of the theory. **Scale.** The exponential sampling overhead confines all three methods to the modest-fault-rate regime, λ of order unity; beyond it, the shot cost exceeds any plausible experimental budget [2, 3]. **Characterization.** PEC's unbiasedness is only as good as its noise model, and real devices drift: a representation learned in the morning may mis-cancel the afternoon's noise, reintroducing bias that the formalism assumes away [2]. **Signal death.** ZNE extrapolates a curve that must be measured, and when amplification destroys the signal, the extrapolation fabricates improvements — a failure mode that is easy to mistake for success without negative controls [6]. **Coherent errors.** Virtual distillation suppresses incoherent noise exponentially but is blind to coherent mismatch, which sets a hard noise floor [4]. **Composability gaps.** The methods combine in principle, but each layer multiplies sampling costs, and the joint optimization of shot allocation across techniques remains poorly understood. Finally, mitigation estimates expectation values; it does not produce corrected quantum states, so it cannot extend the reach of algorithms that require coherent access to the output state, such as amplitude estimation or coherent subroutine calls.

## 7 Conclusion

Quantum error mitigation is best understood not as a stopgap but as a distinct computational paradigm: trading exponential classical sampling cost for improved estimates from noisy quantum hardware, without the qubit overhead of error correction. Zero-noise extrapolation offers the lowest barrier to entry — no noise model, modest circuit modifications — at the price of extrapolation bias and the ever-present risk of signal erosion [1, 5, 6]. Probabilistic error cancellation offers exact unbiasedness and provable optimality for dephasing noise, at the price of demanding noise characterization and sampling costs exponential in the circuit fault rate [1, 3]. Virtual distillation offers exponential suppression of incoherent errors with no noise model at all, at the price of multi-copy overhead and a coherent-mismatch noise floor [4]. The fundamental bounds [2, 3] guarantee that no future technique will escape the exponential tax entirely; progress will come from pushing the constant factors, learning noise more faithfully, and composing methods so that each handles the errors the others cannot. In that light, mitigation is not a detour on the road to fault tolerance — it is the toolkit that determines what near-term devices can usefully compute while the road is still being built.
