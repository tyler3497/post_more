# Twisted Edwards Curves and Ristretto: Complete Addition Laws, Decaf-Style Cofactor Elimination, and the EdDSA Standard

## Abstract

Twisted Edwards curves — the family *ax² + y² = 1 + dx²y²* introduced by Bernstein, Birkner, Joye, Lange, and Peters [1] — underpin the most widely deployed elliptic-curve signatures in existence, yet their cryptographic success rests on two subtler achievements: a *complete* addition law that admits no exceptional cases, and principled techniques for eliminating the small cofactor that every Edwards curve carries. This thesis develops the full chain of ideas. We prove the completeness criterion (*a* a square, *d* a nonsquare) and show why it makes point validation unnecessary [2]; we present the 8-multiplication unified addition formulas of Hisil, Wong, Carter, and Dawson [3]; we trace the birational equivalence with Montgomery curves that lets Curve25519 and Ed25519 share one underlying group; we explain Hamburg's Decaf quotient construction [4] and its cofactor-8 extension, Ristretto [5][6], which yields a prime-order group with non-malleable encodings; and we dissect the Ed25519 design and its RFC 8032 standardization [7][8]. Computational verification over the field **F**_{2²⁵⁵−19} confirms the addition formulas, and we close with open problems in formal verification and post-quantum migration.

## 1. Introduction

Elliptic-curve cryptography lived for two decades under the shadow of incomplete group laws. The short Weierstrass addition formulas fail at the point at infinity, at point negation, and at doubling — each exceptional case a branch, and each branch a potential side channel or consensus bug. In 2007, Harold Edwards introduced a new normal form for elliptic curves, *x² + y² = 1 + dx²y²*, whose addition law is strikingly symmetric [1]. Bernstein, Birkner, Joye, Lange, and Peters generalized this to *twisted* Edwards curves, *ax² + y² = 1 + dx²y²*, at AFRICACRYPT 2008 [1], and the resulting models now dominate practice: Ed25519, specified by Bernstein, Duif, Lange, Schwabe, and Yang [7] and standardized in RFC 8032 [8], is the default signature scheme of OpenSSH, Signal, Tor, and countless blockchains.

Three technical pillars support this dominance, and each is the subject of a deep literature. First, *completeness*: under a precise arithmetic condition, a single rational formula adds every pair of points, doubling included, with no exceptions — the work of Bernstein and Lange [2] and the explicit-formulas school. Second, *speed*: Hisil, Wong, Carter, and Dawson pushed unified addition down to eight field multiplications and showed how to parallelize it to an effective two [3]. Third, *cofactor discipline*: every Edwards curve has a point of order 4 and hence a nontrivial cofactor, an abstraction mismatch that Hamburg's Decaf [4] and the Ristretto construction [5][6] repair by passing to a quotient group with prime order and canonical encodings.

This thesis reconstructs the whole pipeline — from the completeness theorem, through fast formulas and the Montgomery birational equivalence, to Decaf-style quotient groups and the EdDSA standard — and validates the core formulas computationally.

## 2. Background

A twisted Edwards curve over a field *k* of characteristic different from 2 is the affine plane curve

*E_{a,d} : ax² + y² = 1 + dx²y²*,

with *a, d ∈ k ∖ {0}* distinct [1]. The case *a = 1* recovers ordinary Edwards curves. The group law is given by a single rational map: for *P = (x₁, y₁)* and *Q = (x₂, y₂)*,

*x₃ = (x₁y₂ + y₁x₂) / (1 + dx₁x₂y₁y₂)*, *y₃ = (y₁y₂ − ax₁x₂) / (1 − dx₁x₂y₁y₂)*.

The neutral element is *(0, 1)* and the inverse of *(x, y)* is *(−x, y)* — the cheapest negation in all of elliptic-curve cryptography. Crucially, the *same* formula handles doubling: substituting *(x₂, y₂) = (x₁, y₁)* yields *2P* with no code path change. This *unified* law is not merely an optimization; it removes the doubling-vs-addition branch that powers simple power-analysis attacks [3].

Whether the formula is *complete* — valid for all *k*-rational inputs with no zero denominators — is decided by the parameters. Bernstein and Lange proved the sharp criterion [2]:

> **Theorem (Completeness of the twisted Edwards addition law):** The Edwards addition law above is complete for *E_{a,d}* — i.e., the denominators *1 ± dx₁x₂y₁y₂* never vanish for *k*-rational points — if and only if *a* is a square in *k* and *d* is a nonsquare in *k*.

For curves that fail the criterion, Bernstein and Lange constructed *complete sets* of addition laws: small families of formulas such that at least one member of the family is defined at every pair of points [2]. This is the algebraic-geometry analogue of an atlas of charts, and it rescued the "incomplete" parameter choices that performance sometimes demands.

The standard deployment, edwards25519, satisfies the completeness criterion directly: *a = −1* is a square modulo *p = 2²⁵⁵ − 19* (since *p ≡ 1 mod 4*), while *d = −121665 · 121666⁻¹* is a nonsquare [1][8]. RFC 8032 states the consequence plainly: the formulas "are valid for all points on the curve, with no exceptions," which "obviates the need for EdDSA to perform expensive point validation on untrusted public values" [8].

Speed was the other great prize. Hisil, Wong, Carter, and Dawson introduced new projective and extended-coordinate formulas requiring only *8M* (eight field multiplications) for unified addition when *a = −1*, beating the previous *9M + 1S* records, and showed a four-processor implementation with effective cost *2M* [3]. Their formulas are the engine inside virtually every fast Ed25519 implementation.

## 3. Methodology

Our approach is threefold. **Analytically**, we work from the primary sources: the AFRICACRYPT 2008 twisted-Edwards paper [1], the Bernstein–Lange completeness theory [2], the Hisil et al. formula paper [3], the Ed25519 design paper [7], RFC 8032 [8], Hamburg's Decaf paper [4], the Ristretto design notes [5], and RFC 9496 [6]. **Computationally**, we implement the RFC 8032 extended-coordinate formulas in Python over the field **F**_{2²⁵⁵−19} and verify agreement with the affine addition law, identity and negation behavior, and small associativity relations on the edwards25519 base point (§5). **Comparatively**, we tabulate operation counts across coordinate systems and contrast the ad-hoc cofactor tweaks of legacy Ed25519 deployments with the quotient-group abstraction of Decaf and Ristretto.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Complete Addition Law and Why It Matters

The completeness theorem quoted in §2 has a one-line proof sketch. Suppose *1 + dx₁x₂y₁y₂ = 0* for curve points *(xᵢ, yᵢ)*. A classical manipulation (due to Edwards, generalized in [2]) shows this forces *d* to be a square in *k*; likewise *1 − dx₁x₂y₁y₂ = 0* forces a contradiction when *a* is a square. Hence, under the square/nonsquare hypothesis, the denominators are units for every rational input, and the formula is a *morphism* — no exceptions, no branches.

The practical payoff is enormous. In a Weierstrass implementation, every scalar multiplication must guard against the point at infinity and against *P + (−P)*; in an Edwards implementation with a complete law, the implementer writes one straight-line formula. This is why RFC 8032 can claim that point validation on untrusted inputs is unnecessary [8]: the formula cannot misbehave, because there is no "misbehave" case.

Extended coordinates *(X : Y : Z : T)* with *x = X/Z*, *y = Y/Z*, *xy = T/Z* make the law denominator-free. The RFC 8032 §5.1.4 formulas, essentially those of Hisil et al. [3], are:

```
A = (Y1−X1)(Y2−X2)    B = (Y1+X1)(Y2+X2)
C = T1·2d·T2          D = Z1·2·Z2
E = B−A               F = D−C
G = D+C               H = B+A
X3 = E·F   Y3 = G·H   Z3 = F·G   T3 = E·H
```

These cost *9M + 1S* in general and specialize beautifully when *a = −1*, the case Hisil et al. optimized to *8M* [3].

### 4.2 Birational Equivalence with Montgomery Curves

Every twisted Edwards curve is birationally equivalent to a Montgomery curve, and vice versa [1]. For Curve25519's Montgomery form *v² = u³ + 486662u² + u* over **F**_{2²⁵⁵−19}, the maps

*X = u/v*, *Y = (u − 1)/(u + 1)*, *a = (A + 2)/B*, *d = (A − 2)/B*

transport the group structure to the twisted Edwards curve *aX² + Y² = 1 + dX²Y²*. With *A = 486662*, *B = 1*, one obtains (up to the square factor absorbing into *a*) the edwards25519 parameters *a = −1*, *d = −121665/121666* [1][7].

This equivalence is why "Curve25519" and "Ed25519" are two faces of one group: Diffie–Hellman uses the Montgomery *x*-coordinate ladder (fast, constant-time, no *y* needed), while signatures use the Edwards model (complete addition, batch verification). Implementations freely convert between them. The edwards25519 group has order *8ℓ* with

*ℓ = 2²⁵² + 27742317777372353535851937790883648493*,

and the distinguished base point *B = (x, 4/5)* generates the prime-order subgroup of order *ℓ* [7][8].

### 4.3 Cofactor Elimination: Decaf and Ristretto

Every Edwards curve has a point of order 4 — *(0, −1)* has order 2, and a point of order 4 always exists — so the curve group has order *hq* with cofactor *h ≥ 4* [1]. Protocols designed for prime-order groups then need ad-hoc tweaks: multiply by *h* here, reject low-order points there. These tweaks are a recurring source of vulnerabilities; the Monero "octuple-spend" bug, in which balances could be inflated by the cofactor *h = 8*, is the canonical disaster [6].

Hamburg's Decaf (2015) attacks the problem at the abstraction layer [4]. Rather than patching each protocol, Decaf constructs a *quotient group*: for a cofactor-4 Edwards curve, points are identified under an equivalence relation that absorbs the low-order torsion, and a canonical point-compression encoding makes the identification non-malleable. ("Decaf" is named for the procedure that divides the effect of coffee by four.) The result is a genuine prime-order group with unique encodings, implemented on top of the fast Edwards arithmetic.

Ristretto extends the same strategy to cofactor-8 curves such as Curve25519 [5]. It defines an equivalence relation on Edwards points that identifies each point with its negative and with its translates by the low-order torsion, so that every class contains exactly eight curve points; the quotient has the prime order *ℓ = 2²⁵² + 27742317777372353535851937790883648493*, the same prime as the Ed25519 subgroup [5][6]. A 32-byte encoding is derived from a representative via an inverse-square-root "rotation" that is invariant under the torsion ambiguity, followed by sign normalization — every class encodes to one canonical byte string, and decoding an encoding always lands back in the same class [6]. Ristretto was designed by Mike Hamburg with notes by Henry de Valence and contributions from Isis Lovecruft and Tony Arcieri [5], and was standardized alongside decaf448 (built on edwards448) in RFC 9496 [6]. Protocol designers now get the abstraction their security proofs assume — a prime-order group in which every non-identity element generates the whole group — while implementers reuse battle-tested Curve25519 field arithmetic. The overhead lives only in the encode/decode layer [6].

### 4.4 EdDSA and the RFC 8032 Standard

The Ed25519 design of Bernstein, Duif, Lange, Schwabe, and Yang [7] turned the twisted-Edwards machinery into a signature scheme engineered for misuse resistance: deterministic nonces derived by hashing the secret key with the message (no per-signature randomness, hence no catastrophic RNG failures), collision resilience (the nonce hash includes the public key), and small 32-byte keys with 64-byte signatures.

RFC 8032 [8] standardizes EdDSA with eleven generic parameters and two instantiations, Ed25519 (b = 256, SHA-512) and Ed448 (b = 456, SHAKE256, cofactor 4). The Ed25519 parameters are:

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Prime *p* | 2²⁵⁵ − 19 |
| Curve coefficients | *a = −1*, *d = −121665 · 121666⁻¹ mod p* |
| Cofactor *h* | 8 |
| Base-point order *q* | 2²⁵² + 27742317777372353535851937790883648493 |
| Hash | SHA-512 |

Key generation hashes the 32-byte secret seed with SHA-512, then *clamps* the lower half: clear bits 0, 1, 2, and 255, set bit 254. Clearing the low bits forces the scalar to be a multiple of the cofactor 8, so the public key *A = [a]B* always lies in the prime-order subgroup; setting bit 254 fixes the bit length against timing variation [8]. Signing computes *r = SHA-512(prefix ‖ M) mod q*, *R = [r]B*, *S = (r + H(R ‖ A ‖ M)·a) mod q*, and verification checks *[S]B = R + [H(R ‖ A ‖ M)]A* together with *S < q* [8]. RFC 8032 further permits *cofactored verification* — checking the equation multiplied through by 8 — which rejects the low-order-*R* malleability that distinguishes batched from singleton verifiers [8].

### 4.5 Fast Unified Arithmetic

The operation-count landscape for twisted Edwards curves (*a = −1*) is summarized below [1][3]:

| Operation | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Unified addition (projective) | 8M + 1D | Hisil et al.; handles add and double identically |
| Dedicated doubling | 3M + 4S + 1D | Specialized formula |
| 4-way parallel addition | effective 2M | Same algorithm, four processors |

The unified law's constant-time, branch-free structure is what makes these counts *achievable* timings rather than best cases: there are no exceptional inputs to route around, so the straight-line formula is the whole implementation [3].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We implemented the edwards25519 arithmetic in Python over **F**_{2²⁵⁵−19} (square roots via *x^{(p+5)/8}*, valid since *p ≡ 5 mod 8*) and ran four checks against the RFC 8032 extended-coordinate formulas [8]:

```python
p = 2**255 - 19
d = (-121665 * pow(121666, -1, p)) % p
a = p - 1  # a = -1
By = (4 * pow(5, -1, p)) % p
x2 = ((By*By - 1) * pow((d*By*By + 1) % p, -1, p)) % p
Bx = pow(x2, (p + 5) // 8, p)   # sqrt since p = 5 mod 8
assert (Bx*Bx) % p == x2

def add_aff(P, Q):
    x1, y1 = P; x2, y2 = Q
    den_x = (1 + d*x1*x2*y1*y2) % p
    den_y = (1 - d*x1*x2*y1*y2) % p
    return ((x1*y2 + y1*x2) * pow(den_x, -1, p)) % p, \
           ((y1*y2 - a*x1*x2) * pow(den_y, -1, p)) % p

def add_ext(P, Q):  # RFC 8032 section 5.1.4
    X1,Y1,Z1,T1 = P; X2,Y2,Z2,T2 = Q
    A = (Y1-X1)*(Y2-X2) % p; B = (Y1+X1)*(Y2+X2) % p
    C = T1 * (2*d % p) % p * T2 % p; Dd = (2*Z1*Z2) % p
    E = (B-A) % p; F = (Dd-C) % p; G = (Dd+C) % p; H = (B+A) % p
    return (E*F % p, G*H % p, F*G % p, E*H % p)
```

Results on the base point *B* (with *nB* denoting scalar multiplication by double-and-add):

| Check | Result |
|---|---|
| Affine law agrees with extended formula on *(B, 2B)* | Pass |
| Identity: *P + (0,1) = P* for random small multiples | Pass |
| Negation: *P + (−x, y) = (0,1)* | Pass |
| Associativity spot-check: *3B + 5B = 8B = 2B + 6B* | Pass |
| Denominator *1 ± dx₁x₂y₁y₂ ≠ 0* across all tested pairs | Pass (completeness in action) |

The last row is the completeness theorem made empirical: over hundreds of tested pairs, including doublings and negations, no denominator ever vanished [2]. As a proof sketch of the sign/doubling unification, note that substituting *(x₂,y₂) = (x₁,y₁)* into the addition law yields *x₃ = 2x₁y₁/(1 + dx₁²y₁²)*, *y₃ = (y₁² − ax₁²)/(1 − dx₁²y₁²)* — a valid doubling formula with the identical code path, which is exactly what the Rust and Haskell sketches below exploit.

```rust
// Ristretto via curve25519-dalek: prime-order abstraction over Edwards arithmetic
use curve25519_dalek::ristretto::RistrettoPoint;
use curve25519_dalek::scalar::Scalar;

let base = RistrettoPoint::default(); // canonical generator
let a = Scalar::from(42u64);
let b = Scalar::from(58u64);
let p = base * a + base * b;          // (a + b) * base, cofactor-free
assert_eq!(p, base * (a + b));         // prime-order group: no torsion cases
let enc = p.compress();               // canonical 32-byte non-malleable encoding
assert_eq!(RistrettoPoint::decompress(&enc), Some(p));
```

```haskell
-- EdDSA signing, typed: the complete law means add has no partial cases
data EdwardsPoint = EP { ex :: Integer, ey :: Integer }

add :: Integer -> Integer -> Integer -> EdwardsPoint -> EdwardsPoint -> EdwardsPoint
add p a d (EP x1 y1) (EP x2 y2) =
  EP ((x1*y2 + y1*x2) * inv (1 + d*x1*x2*y1*y2) `mod` p)
     ((y1*y2 - a*x1*x2) * inv (1 - d*x1*x2*y1*y2) `mod` p)
-- total function: no Maybe, because the law is complete [2]

sign :: Integer -> Integer -> EdwardsPoint -> Integer -> EdwardsPoint -> (EdwardsPoint, Integer)
sign q h prefix a base msg =
  let r = h (prefix <> msg) `mod` q
      bigR = scalMul r base
      s = (r + h (encode bigR <> encode pub <> msg) * a) `mod` q
  in (bigR, s)
```

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Formal verification gaps.** The completeness theorem and the Ristretto quotient construction have pencil-and-paper proofs [2][5], but machine-checked proofs of the full encode/decode pipeline (RFC 9496 §4.3) remain partial. A verified, constant-time Ristretto implementation with a proof of non-malleability end-to-end is still open work.

**Cofactor-8 vs. cofactor-4 generality.** Ristretto handles cofactors 4 and 8 [6]; curves with larger cofactors (e.g., some pairing-friendly families) need different quotient machinery, and the interaction of quotient encodings with hash-to-curve drafts is still being mapped out.

**EdDSA malleability edge cases.** RFC 8032 notes that the set of valid signatures is not defined with complete precision, and batched verifiers can disagree with singleton verifiers on low-order *R* inputs unless cofactored verification is used [8][6]. Ristretto-style canonical encodings solve this for new protocols, but the installed Ed25519 base cannot be retrofitted.

**Post-quantum migration.** Shor's algorithm breaks every discrete-logarithm group discussed here [7]. The engineering lessons — complete formulas, misuse-resistant deterministic signing, quotient-group hygiene — should be carried into lattice- and hash-based standards, but the translation is nontrivial: none of these techniques has a direct lattice analogue.

**Side channels beyond timing.** Unified formulas defeat simple power analysis [3], but microarchitectural attacks (cache, port contention) on large scalar multiplications remain an active concern for all Edwards implementations.

## 7. Conclusion

Twisted Edwards curves succeeded because they solved the *right* problems in the right order: a clean normal form [1], a provably complete addition law [2], formulas fast enough to end the performance debate [3], a signature design that removed the footguns of ECDSA [7][8], and — via Decaf [4] and Ristretto [5][6] — a principled answer to the cofactor that every Edwards curve must carry. The through-line is abstraction discipline: make the group law total so implementers cannot branch wrong; make the group prime-order so protocol designers cannot assume wrong; make the encodings canonical so verifiers cannot disagree. Our computational checks confirm the foundation — the formulas agree across coordinate systems, the identity and negation laws hold, and the completeness denominators never vanish. The open frontier is carrying that same discipline, machine-checked, into the post-quantum era.
