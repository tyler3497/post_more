# Zeros of the Riemann Zeta Function: Zero-Free Regions and the Prime Number Theorem

## Abstract

This thesis surveys the analytic theory of the zeros of the Riemann zeta function zeta(s) and its consequences for the distribution of primes. From Riemann's 1859 memoir and the Euler product, we develop analytic continuation, the functional equation, the Riemann-von Mangoldt zero-counting formula, and the explicit formula linking the Chebyshev function psi(x) to the nontrivial zeros rho. The technical core is the theory of zero-free regions: the classical de la Vallée Poussin region sigma >= 1 - c/log|t| from the 3-4-1 trigonometric polynomial, the Vinogradov-Korobov region sigma >= 1 - c/(log|t|)^(2/3)(log log|t|)^(1/3) from Vinogradov's mean-value theorem, and the modern explicit constants of Mossinghoff-Trudgian, Ford, Bellotti, and Yang. We show the Prime Number Theorem is equivalent to the nonvanishing of zeta on Re(s) = 1, and derive the error terms psi(x) = x + O(x exp(-c sqrt(log x))) and its Vinogradov-Korobov refinement. We close with the computational verification of the Riemann Hypothesis to height 3·10^12 and the Guth-Maynard zero-density breakthrough.

## 1 Introduction

The distribution of the prime numbers is one of the oldest problems in mathematics, and the Riemann zeta function is its most powerful instrument. For Re(s) > 1, Euler discovered that

$$\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^s} = \prod_{p} \frac{1}{1 - p^{-s}},$$

where the product ranges over all primes p. This identity is a bridge: on the left stands a function of a complex variable, amenable to contour integration and the residue theorem; on the right stands a product over primes, encoding their multiplicative structure. Riemann's insight in his 1859 memoir was that the zeros of the analytically continued zeta function govern the fine structure of the prime-counting function pi(x) [8].

Informally: *every statement about the horizontal distribution of the zeros of zeta is a statement about the distribution of primes, and vice versa.* The Prime Number Theorem, pi(x) ~ x/log x, is equivalent to the assertion that zeta(s) does not vanish on the line Re(s) = 1. Sharper error terms require excluding zeros from regions left of this line — the zero-free regions. The ultimate such statement, that all nontrivial zeros lie on Re(s) = 1/2, is the Riemann Hypothesis, which would give psi(x) = x + O(sqrt(x) log^2 x).

This thesis has three aims. First, a self-contained account of the classical apparatus: the functional equation, the Riemann-von Mangoldt formula for N(T), and the explicit formula connecting psi(x) to the zeros. Second, a detailed treatment of the two great families of zero-free regions — de la Vallée Poussin and Vinogradov-Korobov — with the mechanisms behind each and the explicit constants now available. Third, a survey of the state of the art: computational verification of zeros to height 3·10^12 [4], the explicit regions of Mossinghoff-Trudgian [1], Ford, Bellotti, and Yang, and the 2024 Guth-Maynard breakthrough on zero-density estimates [3].

> **Theorem:** The Prime Number Theorem, pi(x) ~ x/log x as x tends to infinity, is equivalent to the statement that zeta(s) is nonzero for all s with Re(s) = 1.

## 2 Background

For Re(s) > 1, zeta is holomorphic and zero-free, since each Euler factor 1 - p^{-s} is nonzero there. Riemann showed that zeta extends to a meromorphic function on the whole complex plane with a single simple pole at s = 1 of residue 1. The trivial zeros at the negative even integers follow from the functional equation, while all remaining zeros — the *nontrivial* zeros rho = beta + i gamma — lie in the open critical strip, with real part strictly between 0 and 1, symmetric in conjugate pairs and in the reflection s <-> 1 - s.

> **Theorem:** (Functional equation) The completed zeta function xi(s) = s(s-1) pi^{-s/2} Gamma(s/2) zeta(s) is entire of order one and satisfies xi(s) = xi(1-s).

This symmetry is why the critical line Re(s) = 1/2 is distinguished: the Riemann Hypothesis asserts that every nontrivial zero lies on it.

Let N(T) count nontrivial zeros rho with 0 < Im(rho) <= T, with multiplicity. The Riemann-von Mangoldt formula states

$$N(T) = \frac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi e} + O(\log T),$$

with explicit versions sharpening the error term [2]. The main term reflects the functional equation's influence, and its density 1/(2 pi) log(T/2 pi) is the mean spacing law of zeros — the foundation of Montgomery's pair correlation and the GUE random-matrix model [6].

The logarithmic derivative has, for Re(s) > 1, the Dirichlet series

$$-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^s},$$

where Lambda is the von Mangoldt function, equal to log p when n is a prime power and zero otherwise. Summing gives the Chebyshev function psi(x) = sum of Lambda(n) over n <= x. The explicit formula of Riemann and von Mangoldt expresses psi(x) directly through the zeros:

$$\psi(x) = x - \sum_{\rho} \frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho} - \log(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2}\log(1 - x^{-2}),$$

valid for x > 1 not a prime power. Each zero rho = beta + i gamma contributes an oscillation x^beta e^{i gamma log x} / rho, so the error term psi(x) - x is controlled by how far right zeros can reach — exactly the content of a zero-free region [6].

| Zero-free information | Error term in psi(x) | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| zeta nonzero on Re(s) = 1 | psi(x) = x + o(x) | Hadamard, de la Vallée Poussin (1896) |
| sigma >= 1 - c/log|t| | O(x exp(-c1 sqrt(log x))) | de la Vallée Poussin |
| sigma >= 1 - c/((log|t|)^(2/3)(log log|t|)^(1/3)) | O(x exp(-c2 (log x)^(3/5)/(log log x)^(1/5))) | Vinogradov-Korobov |
| All zeros on Re(s) = 1/2 | O(sqrt(x) log^2 x) | Riemann Hypothesis |
| N(sigma,T) << T^{c(1-sigma)} log^B T | Primes in short intervals | Zero-density estimates [3] |

## 3 Methodology

The classical analytic program is a pipeline from complex-analytic estimates to arithmetic conclusions:

1. **Nonvanishing on the one-line.** Lower bounds for |zeta(sigma + it)| and upper bounds for the logarithmic derivative near Re(s) = 1, using the truncated Euler product and elementary trigonometric inequalities.
2. **The 3-4-1 polynomial method.** The inequality 3 + 4 cos theta + cos 2theta >= 0 is the engine of the classical zero-free region. Applied to zeta(sigma)^3 zeta(sigma + it)^4 zeta(sigma + 2it), it contradicts the assumption of a zero beta + i gamma with beta too close to 1, since -zeta'/zeta has a pole-like term 1/(s - rho) at each zero [5].
3. **Exponential sums for Vinogradov-Korobov.** Widening the region beyond 1/log|t| requires subconvex bounds for zeta(sigma + it) with sigma near 1, obtained by approximating zeta with short Dirichlet polynomials and applying Vinogradov's mean-value theorem, then converting growth bounds to nonvanishing via Borel-Carathéodory.
4. **Explicit optimization.** Following Kadiri, Mossinghoff-Trudgian, Ford, and Bellotti, the arguments are made effective: favorable trigonometric polynomials replace 3-4-1, smoothing kernels and verified numerics handle small |t|, and every error term is tracked numerically [1].
5. **Contour integration.** With a zero-free region sigma >= 1 - eta(t) and matching growth bounds, the Perron integral for psi(x) is shifted left to the zero-free boundary; the pole at s = 1 yields the main term x, and eta controls the remainder.

A computational check of the 3-4-1 inequality underlying the classical argument:

```python
import numpy as np

def trig_polynomial(theta):
    """The nonnegative polynomial 3 + 4 cos(theta) + cos(2 theta)."""
    return 3.0 + 4.0 * np.cos(theta) + np.cos(2.0 * theta)

thetas = np.linspace(0.0, 2.0 * np.pi, 1000001)
minimum = trig_polynomial(thetas).min()
print(f"minimum = {minimum:.6f}")  # 0.0, attained at 2*pi/3 and 4*pi/3
assert abs(minimum) < 1e-9
```

/// Laplace-type smoothing used in Kadiri-style arguments.
pub fn smoothing_integral(f: impl Fn(f64) -> f64, d: f64, n: usize) -> f64 {
    let h = d / n as f64;
    let mut acc = 0.0;
    for k in 0..=n {
        let u = k as f64 * h;
        let w = 1.0 - (2.0 * u / d - 1.0).abs(); // triangular kernel
        let coeff = if k == 0 || k == n { 0.5 } else { 1.0 };
        acc += coeff * w * f(u);
    }
    acc * h
}

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The classical de la Vallée Poussin region

Assume rho = beta + i gamma is a zero with beta close to 1 and gamma large. For sigma > 1,

$$-\mathrm{Re}\,\frac{\zeta'(\sigma + it)}{\zeta(\sigma + it)}
  = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\Lambda(n) \cos(t \log n)}{n^{\sigma}}.$$

Multiplying 3 + 4 cos theta + cos 2theta >= 0 with theta = t log n and summing with weights Lambda(n)/n^sigma gives

$$3\left(-\frac{\zeta'(\sigma)}{\zeta(\sigma)}\right)
  + 4\left(-\mathrm{Re}\,\frac{\zeta'(\sigma + it)}{\zeta(\sigma + it)}\right)
  + \left(-\mathrm{Re}\,\frac{\zeta'(\sigma + 2it)}{\zeta(\sigma + 2it)}\right) \ge 0.$$

Since zeta has a pole at s = 1, the first term is ~ 3/(sigma - 1). Near the assumed zero, the second term contributes ~ 4/(sigma - beta) at t = gamma, while the third is O(log|t|). With sigma = 1 + delta this yields

$$\frac{3}{\delta} \ge \frac{4}{\delta + 1 - \beta} - C \log|\gamma|,$$

whence beta <= 1 - c/log|gamma| for a suitable c > 0. This is the de la Vallée Poussin region: zeta(sigma + it) != 0 for sigma >= 1 - c/log|t| at large |t| [5][7].

The constant depends on the auxiliary bounds. Mossinghoff and Trudgian proved the fully explicit nonvanishing sigma >= 1 - 1/(5.573412 log|t|) for |t| >= 2 [1], refined with Yang to the constant 5.558691 [7]. The gains come from higher-degree nonnegative trigonometric polynomials optimized by simulated annealing, plus a careful treatment of the error term in Kadiri's method.

### 4.2 The Vinogradov-Korobov region

In 1958, Vinogradov and Korobov independently obtained a substantially wider region for large |t|:

> **Theorem:** (Vinogradov-Korobov) For every epsilon > 0 there exists c(epsilon) > 0 such that zeta(sigma + it) != 0 for sigma >= 1 - c(epsilon)/(log|t|)^(2/3 + epsilon) at sufficiently large |t|.

The proof replaces the crude bound on -zeta'/zeta with a subconvex estimate. Approximating zeta(sigma + it) by Dirichlet polynomials of length about |t| and applying Vinogradov's mean-value theorem to the resulting exponential sums gives, for sigma near 1,

$$|\zeta(\sigma + it)| \ll |t|^{A(1-\sigma)^{3/2}} \log^{2/3}|t|,$$

and the exponent (1 - sigma)^(3/2) produces the 2/3 in the region. Borel-Carathéodory converts this growth estimate into a lower bound for |zeta|, excluding zeros [7].

Explicit versions were long unavailable because the constants were astronomical. Ford produced the first fully explicit version; Bellotti, building on Ford, proved nonvanishing for sigma >= 1 - 1/(53.989 (log|t|)^(2/3)(log log|t|)^(1/3)) for |t| >= 3, improving to 48.0718 for sufficiently large |t| [7]. Yang obtained the intermediate region sigma >= 1 - (log log|t|)/(21.233 log|t|) for |t| between exp(170.2) and exp(4.8·10^5) [7]. The resulting landscape:

- |t| up to 3·10^12: RH is *verified* computationally — no off-line zeros at all [4].
- 3·10^12 < |t| < exp(64.1): the Mossinghoff-Trudgian-Yang region (R = 5.558691) dominates [7].
- exp(170.2) < |t| < exp(4.8·10^5): Yang's intermediate region is largest [7].
- |t| >= exp(4.8·10^5): Bellotti's explicit Vinogradov-Korobov region takes over [7].
- Asymptotically, the ineffective Vinogradov-Korobov region with exponent 2/3 + epsilon remains widest [7].

### 4.3 The explicit formula as a two-way street

The explicit formula is usually read left-to-right: zeros imply prime asymptotics. It also runs right-to-left. If psi(x) = x + O(x^theta) for theta < 1, then -zeta'/zeta extends holomorphically to Re(s) > theta except for the pole at s = 1, forcing zeta to be zero-free there. In particular, the Prime Number Theorem yields nonvanishing on Re(s) = 1, and conversely nonvanishing on Re(s) = 1 yields the Prime Number Theorem by a Tauberian argument — the equivalence stated in the Introduction.

This duality clarifies zero-density estimates. A bound N(sigma, T) << T^{A(1-sigma)} log^B T lets one truncate the explicit formula's zero sum and bound the tail, yielding primes in short intervals: Huxley's theorem that (x, x + x^theta) contains a prime for theta > 7/12 rests on such an estimate. The Guth-Maynard breakthrough improved the Ingham-Huxley density bounds for the first time since the 1970s [3].

### 4.4 Computational verification

Analytic regions exclude zeros near Re(s) = 1 but say nothing near the critical line. The complementary program is computational: the argument principle on rectangles plus rigorous Turing-method bounds verifies that every zero up to height T lies on the critical line and is simple. Platt and Trudgian verified RH to 3·10^12 [4] — the roughly 10^13 zeros predicted by the Riemann-von Mangoldt formula were all located on the critical line. Explicit zero-counting formulae with sharp error terms [2] power these verifications: they bound the zero count in a rectangle from above, and the located zeros saturate the bound, proving none were missed.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

We assemble the proof of the Prime Number Theorem with the classical error term:

**Step 1 — Nonvanishing on Re(s) = 1.** The 3-4-1 argument with sigma decreasing to 1 shows zeta(1 + it) != 0 for all real t; otherwise the term 4/(sigma - 1) at the zero would dominate the inequality. This already yields the Prime Number Theorem via the Wiener-Ikehara Tauberian theorem.

**Step 2 — Region and growth bounds.** Extend to sigma >= 1 - c/log(|t| + 2) with -zeta'/zeta(s) << log(|t| + 2) there, via Borel-Carathéodory on discs centered at 2 + it.

**Step 3 — Contour shift.** Perron's formula writes psi(x) as an integral of -(x^s/s)(zeta'/zeta)(s) over Re(s) = 1 + 1/log x. Shifting left to sigma = 1 - c/log(|t| + 2), the pole at s = 1 contributes the main term x, and the remaining segments contribute at most x exp(-c' sqrt(log x)) after optimizing the truncation height T = exp(sqrt(log x)).

> **Theorem:** (PNT, de la Vallée Poussin error term) psi(x) = x + O(x exp(-c sqrt(log x))) for an effectively computable c > 0.

Using Vinogradov-Korobov and re-optimizing:

> **Theorem:** (PNT, Vinogradov-Korobov error term) psi(x) = x + O(x exp(-c (log x)^(3/5)/(log log x)^(1/5))).

Under RH, shifting to Re(s) = 1/2 + epsilon gives psi(x) = x + O(sqrt(x) log^2 x), essentially best possible: the explicit formula shows the zero sum has typical size sqrt(x), and Littlewood proved psi(x) - x = Omega(sqrt(x) log log log x / log x), so the square-root barrier is genuine.

The empirical record complements the analysis. The verified zeros to 3·10^12 [4] are consistent with RH but imply nothing about it — the first counterexample could lie at heights far beyond computation. The zero-free regions are theorems at *all* heights, which is why they, not computation, drive the error term.

## 6 Limitations

1. **The 3-4-1 method is exhausted.** The shape sigma >= 1 - c/log|t| is optimal for fixed trigonometric polynomials; only the constant can improve. The Mossinghoff-Trudgian constant R = 5.573412 (5.558691 with Yang) is near the method's theoretical limit [1].
2. **Vinogradov-Korobov is ineffective in practice.** Its explicit versions carry large constants and dominate only at enormous heights (|t| >= exp(4.8·10^5) for Bellotti's). For accessible purposes the classical explicit region is operative.
3. **The 2/3 barrier.** The exponent 2/3 + epsilon has stood since 1958. Improving it needs fundamentally new exponential-sum or zeta estimates; recent decoupling advances have not yet translated into a better zero-free exponent.
4. **Density does not directly improve the PNT error term.** The Guth-Maynard breakthrough [3] transforms primes in short intervals, but the psi(x) error term remains governed by the widest zero-free region. Converting density gains into zero-free gains is a major open problem.
5. **Computation cannot reach the interesting heights.** Verifying RH to 3·10^12 [4] is a triumph of rigorous numerics, but the operative zero-free heights like exp(10^5) are forever beyond computation. The programs are complementary, not competing.
6. **The Riemann Hypothesis.** None of these methods touches it. Random-matrix statistics predict zero spacings with uncanny accuracy [6], but a proof remains as distant as ever.

## 7 Conclusion

The zeros of the Riemann zeta function form a rare program in which one complex-analytic object dictates a concrete arithmetic question. The chain of ideas:

- The **Euler product** ties zeta to the primes.
- The **functional equation** confines nontrivial zeros to the critical strip with the symmetry rho <-> 1 - rho.
- The **Riemann-von Mangoldt formula** counts the zeros and reveals their mean density.
- The **explicit formula** converts zero locations into prime asymptotics, term by term.
- **Zero-free regions** — classical, Vinogradov-Korobov, and explicit refinements — turn nonvanishing into quantitative error terms for the Prime Number Theorem.
- **Zero-density estimates**, revolutionized by Guth and Maynard [3], govern primes in short intervals.
- **Computation** verifies RH to ever-greater heights [4], anchoring the explicit theory at small |t|.

The Prime Number Theorem with the Vinogradov-Korobov error term is the sharpest unconditional result of its kind, while the explicit constants of Mossinghoff-Trudgian, Ford, Bellotti, and Yang make the theory effective across all height ranges [1][7]. The frontier lies in breaking the 2/3 exponent, converting zero-density gains into zero-free gains, and — the oldest dream of all — proving every nontrivial zero lies on the critical line.
