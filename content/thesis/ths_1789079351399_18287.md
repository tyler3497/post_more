# Online Algorithms and Competitive Analysis: Paging, the k-Server Problem, and Randomization against Oblivious Adversaries

## Abstract

Online algorithms must commit to irrevocable decisions as requests arrive, without any knowledge of the future. Competitive analysis, introduced by Sleator and Tarjan in 1985, measures such algorithms against an omniscient offline optimum: an algorithm is *c-competitive* when its cost on every request sequence is at most *c* times the optimal cost, plus an additive constant. This thesis develops the theory from its classical foundations to its modern frontiers. We prove that no deterministic paging algorithm can beat the factor *k* — a bound achieved by LRU, FIFO, and Flush-When-Full — and that randomization breaks the barrier: the marking algorithm is *H_k*-competitive against oblivious adversaries, and this is optimal. We generalize to the *k*-server problem, state the *k*-server conjecture, analyze the work-function algorithm's *2k − 1* bound, and survey the randomized *O(log k)* conjecture and its recent polylogarithmic progress. Along the way we treat metrical task systems, ski rental, list update with Move-To-Front, and Yao's minimax principle for randomized lower bounds. We close with the limitations of worst-case analysis and the beyond-worst-case research program.

## 1 Introduction

A virtual-memory system evicting pages, a fleet dispatching vehicles to new orders, a skier deciding whether to rent or buy — each faces an *online problem*: sequential, irrevocable decisions made without knowledge of future requests. How should such decision-making be measured?

*Competitive analysis*, introduced by Sleator and Tarjan [1], compares the online algorithm against the *optimal offline* algorithm — the hypothetical OPT that sees the entire request sequence in advance. An algorithm staying within a bounded factor of OPT is never catastrophically worse than the best achievable with full information.

> **Definition (Competitive ratio).** A deterministic online algorithm *A* is *c-competitive* if there exists a constant *α ≥ 0*, independent of the request sequence, such that for every request sequence *σ*,
>
> cost<sub>A</sub>(*σ*) ≤ *c* · cost<sub>OPT</sub>(*σ*) + *α*.
>
> The *competitive ratio* of *A* is the infimum of such *c*.

The constant *α* absorbs startup effects; for randomized algorithms the bound holds in expectation for *every* sequence *σ* — against an *oblivious adversary* that knows the algorithm but not its coin flips [2].

Section 2 establishes the model, the adversary hierarchy, and the ski-rental warm-up; Section 3 the toolkit (potential functions, phases, Yao's principle); Section 4 the deep results — the factor-*k* paging barrier, the marking algorithm's *H_k* optimality, the *k*-server conjecture, metrical task systems, list update; Section 5 the central theorems; Section 6 the limitations; Section 7 open problems.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The online model

A *request sequence* is *σ = r₁…rₙ*; on arrival of *r_t* the algorithm chooses *a_t* from the prefix *r₁…r_t* alone (plus random bits). OPT(*σ*) is the minimum cost over all action sequences with full knowledge of *σ*.

Three facts make this model non-trivial:

- **Irrevocability.** Evicting a page, moving a server, or buying skis cannot be undone for free.
- **Adversarial inputs.** The input is chosen adversarially, so the algorithm must hedge against the worst case.
- **Normalization by OPT.** A large absolute cost is acceptable if OPT also pays it; what matters is the *ratio*.

### 2.2 The adversary hierarchy

For randomized algorithms, *who knows what* determines the strength of the adversary [5]:

1. **Oblivious adversary.** Fixes *σ* in advance, knowing the algorithm's code but not its random choices — the standard model for randomized analysis.
2. **Adaptive online adversary.** Chooses each *r_t* from the algorithm's past actions, but must serve the requests itself, paying the cost.
3. **Adaptive offline adversary.** Sees the random choices and builds the worst sequence, served optimally in hindsight — the strongest adversary, against which randomization often offers no advantage.

The marking algorithm's *H_k* bound and its matching lower bound hold against the *oblivious* adversary [2]; matching each bound to the right adversary is a central subtlety of the field.

### 2.3 Ski rental: the canonical example

The simplest non-trivial online problem is *ski rental*: rent at $1/day, buy at *$B*; OPT pays min(*n*, *B*) for a season of *n* unknown days. The *break-even* strategy — rent *B* days, then buy — costs at most 2·OPT, and no deterministic algorithm does better: the adversary ends the season the day after the algorithm buys, or never, if it never buys [5].

Randomization — buying on a random day from a truncated exponential distribution — achieves *e/(e−1) ≈ 1.582*, optimally [5]: the recurring pattern of this thesis is that *determinism hits a combinatorial wall that randomization slips through, but only against an oblivious adversary*.

## 3 Methodology

Four analytical techniques recur throughout:

- **Potential functions.** Bound each request's *amortized cost* (actual cost plus change in a non-negative potential *Φ*) by *c* times OPT's cost; telescoping yields *c*-competitiveness [1].
- **Phase partitioning.** Split the sequence into *phases* where OPT pays ≥ 1 and the algorithm pays ≤ *f(k)* in expectation — reducing global analysis to local [2].
- **Yao's principle.** Lower-bound randomized algorithms by exhibiting a *distribution* over inputs defeating every deterministic algorithm [5].
- **Work functions.** Track *w_t(X)*, the optimal cost of serving *t* requests ending in configuration *X*; moving to maximize its increase inherits OPT's structure [3].

The following table summarizes the notation used throughout.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *σ = r₁…rₙ* | request sequence |
| cost<sub>A</sub>(*σ*) | total cost of algorithm *A* on *σ* |
| OPT(*σ*) | optimal offline cost on *σ* |
| *k* | cache size / number of servers |
| *H_k = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ 1/i* | *k*-th harmonic number (≈ ln *k* + *γ*) |
| *α* | additive constant in the competitive definition |
| *w_t(X)* | work function: optimal cost to serve *r₁…r_t* ending in configuration *X* |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Deterministic Paging: LRU, FIFO, Flush-When-Full, and the Factor-*k* Barrier

The *paging problem* is the hydrogen atom of online algorithms: a cache holds *k* pages from a universe of *n > k*; each *page fault* (demand paging, no prefetching) forces an eviction at unit cost. The offline optimum is *Belady's MIN* — evict the page requested furthest in the future — optimal by an exchange argument.

Three deterministic online rules are *k*-competitive [1]:

- **LRU (Least Recently Used).** Evict the page whose most recent request is earliest.
- **FIFO (First In, First Out).** Evict the page resident longest.
- **FWF (Flush-When-Full).** On a fault with a full cache, evict *all* pages.

LRU's *k*-competitiveness follows from *k*-phases — maximal intervals with at most *k* distinct pages: LRU faults at most *k* times per phase, while OPT faults at least once per phase beyond the first, since two consecutive phases span *k+1* distinct pages [1].

> **Theorem (Sleator–Tarjan, 1985).** No deterministic online paging algorithm has competitive ratio smaller than *k*.

*Proof sketch.* The adversary maintains the set of the *k* pages in the algorithm's cache and always requests the unique page among a fixed set of *k + 1* that the algorithm lacks. The algorithm faults on *every* request. OPT, knowing the sequence, faults at most once per *k* requests (it keeps the *k* pages that will be requested soonest). The ratio tends to *k*. ∎

So *k* is a hard wall for determinism, achieved by LRU, FIFO, and FWF — a triumph (LRU is competitively *optimal*) and an embarrassment (it cannot explain why LRU beats FIFO in practice; see Section 6).

Under *resource augmentation* [1][2] — online with *k* pages versus OPT with *h ≤ k* — LRU<sub>k</sub> is within *k/(k−h+1)* of OPT<sub>h</sub>, optimally; Young showed the marking algorithm is roughly *2 ln(k/(k−h+1))*-competitive here [2].

### 4.2 Randomized Paging: The Marking Algorithm against Oblivious Adversaries

The *marking algorithm* of Fiat et al. [2] breaks the factor-*k* wall: it runs in *k*-phases, *marking* each requested page, and on a fault evicts a uniformly random *unmarked* page.

With *m* *clean* pages per phase (requested now but not in the previous phase), the expected stale-page faults are at most *m(H_k − H_m)*, since eviction victims are uniform among unmarked pages:

> **Theorem (Fiat et al., 1991).** The marking algorithm is *H_k*-competitive against any oblivious adversary.

Moreover *H_k* is optimal: via Yao's principle, no randomized algorithm beats *H_k* against an oblivious adversary — the hard distribution requests uniformly from *k+1* pages, forcing any deterministic algorithm to fault with probability ≥ *1/(k+1)* per request while OPT rarely faults [2]. Randomization buys an exponential improvement, from *k* to ln *k*, but no more.

The marking algorithm is only *weakly* *H_k*-competitive; McGeoch and Sleator's *partitioning algorithm* achieves *strong* *H_k*-competitiveness for all *k*, *n*, at greater complexity [2].

The Python simulation below compares the marking algorithm against Belady's OPT on a synthetic trace.

```python
import random
from collections import OrderedDict

def marking_paging(requests, k, seed=0):
    """Randomized marking algorithm. Returns number of page faults."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    cache, marked = set(), set()
    faults = 0
    for r in requests:
        if r in cache:
            marked.add(r)
            continue
        faults += 1
        if len(cache) < k:
            cache.add(r)
        else:
            # phase boundary: all cached pages marked -> unmark all
            if marked == cache:
                marked = set()
            unmarked = [p for p in cache if p not in marked]
            victim = rng.choice(unmarked)
            cache.discard(victim)
            cache.add(r)
        marked.add(r)
    return faults

def belady_opt(requests, k):
    """Offline optimal (furthest-in-future). Returns fault count."""
    cache, faults = set(), 0
    for t, r in enumerate(requests):
        if r in cache:
            continue
        faults += 1
        if len(cache) < k:
            cache.add(r)
            continue
        future = requests[t+1:]
        victim = max(cache, key=lambda p: future.index(p) if p in future else float('inf'))
        cache.discard(victim)
        cache.add(r)
    return faults

if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = random.Random(42)
    trace = [rng.choice(range(9)) for _ in range(2000)]  # n=9 pages, k=4
    mk = sum(marking_paging(trace, 4, s) for s in range(20)) / 20
    opt = belady_opt(trace, 4)
    print(f"marking avg faults={mk:.1f}  OPT faults={opt}  ratio={mk/opt:.3f}  H_4={sum(1/i for i in range(1,5)):.3f}")
```

### 4.3 The *k*-Server Problem and the *k*-Server Conjecture

In the ***k*-server problem** of Manasse, McGeoch, and Sleator [4], *k* servers occupy points of a metric space *(X, d)*; each request *p_i* must be served by moving some server to it, paying the distance. Paging is *k*-server on the uniform metric; weighted caching is *k*-server on a weighted star.

The foundational results [4]:

- **Lower bound.** No deterministic algorithm beats *k* on any metric with more than *k* points — request the one of *k+1* fixed points lacking a server.
- **Special cases.** The conjecture holds with ratio *k* for *k = 2* servers, on the line, on trees, and on metrics with only *k + 1* (later *k + 2*) points.
- **The conjecture.** *A k-competitive deterministic algorithm exists for every metric space* — open for *k ≥ 3*, even for 3 servers on more than 6 points, after nearly four decades.

After exponential-in-*k* upper bounds, Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou analyzed the **work-function algorithm (WFA)** [3], built on the *work function*:

*w_t(X) = min_Y { w_{t−1}(Y) + d(Y, X) }*, with *w_t(X) = ∞* unless *X* contains the request *p_t*.

On request *p_t*, WFA moves to the configuration *X ∋ p_t* maximizing *w_t(X) − w_{t−1}(X)* — where the optimum's "regret" grew most. The proof rests on *quasiconvexity* of work functions, a *duality lemma* characterizing the maximizers, and a potential function exploiting the duality [3]:

> **Theorem (Koutsoupias–Papadimitriou, 1995).** The work-function algorithm is *(2k − 1)*-competitive on every metric space.

This shattered the exponential barrier and remains the best known deterministic bound; WFA itself is conjectured *k*-competitive [3].

The **randomized *k*-server conjecture** posits *O(log k)*-competitiveness on every metric — known for paging and constant-depth HSTs, and polylogarithmic in *k*, *n* on general metrics since Bansal, Buchbinder, Madry, and Naor [6]; the uniform case is exactly *H_k* [7]. The general conjecture remains open, and weighted *k*-server needs exponential ratios in the worst case.

The TLA+ sketch below formalizes the *k*-server transition system.

```tla
---- MODULE KServer ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Points, K          \* metric space points; number of servers
VARIABLES config             \* current server configuration: a K-subset of Points

TypeOK == config \in {S \in SUBSET Points : Cardinality(S) = K}

Request(p) ==               \* serve request at point p
    /\ p \in Points
    /\ IF p \in config
       THEN UNCHANGED config            \* already covered: free
       ELSE \E s \in config :           \* move some server s to p
                config' = (config \ {s}) \cup {p}

Init == config \in {S \in SUBSET Points : Cardinality(S) = K}
Next == \E p \in Points : Request(p)
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_config
====
```

### 4.4 Metrical Task Systems, Ski Rental Revisited, and List Update

**Metrical task systems (MTS)** of Borodin, Linial, and Saks generalize paging and *k*-server: the system occupies one of *n* metric states; each task assigns a processing cost per state, and the algorithm pays movement plus processing [5][8]. The map is complete: *2n − 1* deterministic (tight), randomized *Θ(H_n)* — the model where randomization's power was first demonstrated [5] — and recently *O(log² n)* randomized with matching existential lower bounds [8].

**List update** maintains an unsorted list under accesses costing *i* (or *i−1* in the partial-cost model), with free moves of the accessed item. **Move-To-Front (MTF)** is *2*-competitive (4 with paid exchanges), while *Transpose* can be made arbitrarily bad and *Frequency Count* fares no better [1]. The proof is a jewel of the potential method: with *Φ* the number of *inversions* between MTF's and OPT's lists, each access costs at most twice OPT's in amortized terms.

```haskell
-- Move-To-Front list update; cost = 1-based position (full cost model)
moveToFront :: Eq a => [a] -> [a] -> (Int, [a])
moveToFront list []     = (0, list)
moveToFront list (x:xs) =
    let (pos, rest) = extract x list          -- pos: 1-based index
        (cost, l')   = moveToFront (x : rest) xs
    in (pos + cost, l')
  where
    extract y (z:zs)
        | y == z    = (1, zs)
        | otherwise = let (p, r) = extract y zs in (p + 1, z : r)
    extract _ [] = error "item not in list"
```

The Rust program below verifies the deterministic 2-competitive ski-rental rule exhaustively.

```rust
/// Ski rental: rent costs 1/day, buying costs `buy`. Returns total cost.
fn ski_rental(season_days: u32, buy: u32) -> u32 {
    let rent_days = season_days.min(buy);   // break-even: rent B days, then buy
    if season_days <= buy { rent_days } else { buy + buy }
}

fn main() {
    let buy = 10;
    for n in [1, 5, 10, 11, 30, 100] {
        let alg = ski_rental(n, buy);
        let opt = n.min(buy);
        println!("n={n:3} alg={alg:3} opt={opt:2} ratio={:.2}", alg as f64 / opt as f64);
    }
}
```

### 4.5 Yao's Principle and the Adversary Hierarchy

All randomized lower bounds in this thesis rest on one tool [5]:

> **Theorem (Yao's minimax principle).** Let *A* range over deterministic online algorithms and *D* over distributions on request sequences. Then
>
> min<sub>A randomized</sub> max<sub>σ</sub> **E**[cost<sub>A</sub>(*σ*)] / OPT(*σ*) ≥ max<sub>D</sub> min<sub>A deterministic</sub> **E**<sub>σ∼D</sub>[cost<sub>A</sub>(*σ*)] / **E**<sub>σ∼D</sub>[OPT(*σ*)].

Hence, to lower-bound randomized algorithms against an oblivious adversary, exhibit a *distribution* over inputs defeating every deterministic algorithm — the route to the *H_k* paging [2], *e/(e−1)* ski-rental, and *H_n* MTS [5] lower bounds.

Yao's principle targets the *oblivious* adversary; against the *adaptive offline* adversary, randomization provably cannot beat the deterministic optimum for paging. Choosing the right adversary is part of choosing the right theorem.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now state the three central theorems with proof sketches and quantitative comparisons.

> **Theorem (Sleator–Tarjan 1985 [1]).** LRU, FIFO, and Flush-When-Full are *k*-competitive for paging, and no deterministic online algorithm achieves a ratio below *k*.

*Proof sketch.* *k*-phases give the upper bound (Section 4.1); the "missing page" adversary on *k+1* pages gives the lower bound. ∎

> **Theorem (Fiat et al. 1991 [2]).** The marking algorithm is *H_k*-competitive against any oblivious adversary, and no randomized algorithm has competitive ratio below *H_k*.

*Proof sketch.* In a *k*-phase with *m* clean pages, expected faults are *m* (clean) plus at most *m·(H_k − H_m)* (stale, by uniformity of eviction among unmarked pages); OPT pays *Ω(m)*, giving *H_k*. The lower bound applies Yao's principle to uniform sequences over *k+1* pages [2]. ∎

> **Theorem (Koutsoupias–Papadimitriou 1995 [3]).** The work-function algorithm is *(2k − 1)*-competitive for the *k*-server problem on every metric space.

*Proof sketch.* WFA's movement is bounded by the *extended cost* max<sub>X∋p_t</sub>(*w_t(X)−w_{t−1}(X)*) plus telescoping terms; the duality lemma and quasiconvexity bound the potential increase, summing to *(2k−1)*·OPT + const [3]. ∎

The harmonic numbers grow slowly:

| *k* | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 64 | 1024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *H_k* | 1.500 | 2.083 | 2.718 | 3.381 | 4.744 | 7.485 |
| deterministic bound | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 64 | 1024 |

| Problem | Deterministic | Randomized (oblivious) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paging (*k* pages) | *k* (LRU/FIFO/FWF) [1] | *H_k* ≈ ln *k* (marking) [2] | both optimal |

| List update | 2 (MTF, partial cost) [1] | 1.6 (COMB, lower bound 1.5) [5] | nearly tight |

## 6 Limitations

Competitive analysis is a theory of *worst cases*, and its limitations are as instructive as its triumphs.

**Pessimism vs. practice.** LRU's ratio of *k* suggests it could be *k* times worse than OPT, yet on real workloads it stays within a small constant — the gap is *locality of reference*. Refined models (access-graph [2], diffuse adversary [5], loosely competitive analysis) try to explain why paging works in practice.

**Intractability of "optimal" algorithms.** WFA minimizes over all *k*-subsets — exponential in general; the partitioning algorithm is too complex to implement. Competitive analysis measures *information-theoretic* performance, ignoring computation.

**The adversary may be too strong.** The adaptive-offline collapse of randomized advantage suggests the pessimism is structural; smoothed analysis and learning-augmented algorithms are active responses.

**Open frontiers.** The deterministic *k*-server conjecture is open for *k ≥ 3* even on 7 points [4][7]; the randomized *O(log k)* conjecture is open on general metrics [6][7]; MTS randomized competitiveness was only recently pinned between *Ω(log n)* and *O(log² n)* [8].

## 7 Conclusion

From Sleator and Tarjan's 1985 analysis of LRU to modern polylogarithmic randomized *k*-server algorithms, competitive analysis is the definitive language for decisions under uncertainty: deterministic paging cannot beat *k*, but randomization against an oblivious adversary reduces it to *H_k ≈ ln k*; the *k*-server problem generalizes paging to arbitrary metrics, where WFA's *2k−1* stands against a conjectured *k*; MTS, ski rental, and list update illuminate generality, the power and limits of randomness, and potential-function proofs; Yao's principle turns lower bounds into a science of distributions.

The field's greatest strength — uncompromising worst-case guarantees — is also its greatest weakness; the frontier lies in relaxing exactly that, via diffuse adversaries, locality models, and learning-augmented algorithms. Whether the *k*-server conjecture falls to a clever potential function or stands for another forty years, the Sleator–Tarjan framework remains the lens for acting without knowing the future.
