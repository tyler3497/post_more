# Rare-Earth-Free Permanent Magnets: MnBi, MnAl-C, and the Magnetocrystalline-Anisotropy Road Beyond Nd2Fe14B

## Abstract

Neodymium–iron–boron dominates high-performance permanent magnetism, yet its dependence on critical rare-earth elements — neodymium, dysprosium and terbium, all flagged *critical* in the U.S. Department of Energy's 2023 assessment — motivates a search for rare-earth-free hard magnets that can plug the energy-product gap between hard ferrites (~38 kJ/m³) and Nd₂Fe₁₄B (~470 kJ/m³) [1][9]. This thesis examines the three most credible intrinsic candidates: low-temperature-phase MnBi, whose magnetocrystalline anisotropy *grows* with temperature and whose coercivity peaks near 2.5 T at 540 K; the metastable L1₀ τ-phase of MnAl, carbon-stabilized against decomposition into the equilibrium γ₂ and β phases, offering K₁ ≈ 1.5 MJ/m³ and a theoretical (BH)max near 112 kJ/m³; and α″-Fe₁₆N₂, whose claimed giant saturation magnetization of ~2.9 T remains the most consequential unresolved question in 3d magnetism [4][8]. Through the hardness-parameter formalism κ = √(K₁/μ₀M_s²), density-functional estimates of anisotropy, and micromagnetic coercivity analysis, we map each compound's distance from viability, quantify the twinning and decomposition bottlenecks that cap real coercivity, and argue that *anisotropy engineering* — not moment maximization — is the road beyond Nd₂Fe₁₄B.

## 1 Intro

The history of permanent magnets is a history of ceilings being raised and then, abruptly, not. From the steels of the early twentieth century through Alnico and the hard ferrites, the maximum energy product (BH)max doubled roughly every twelve years, an exponential march that Coey documented as one of the quietest exponentials in materials science [2]. The arrival of Sm–Co in the late 1960s and Nd₂Fe₁₄B in 1983–84 extended that march to (BH)max values above 400 kJ/m³ — and then the march stopped. For four decades the record has barely moved, because Nd₂Fe₁₄B already converts roughly 90% of its theoretical limit into product: with a spontaneous polarization J_s = 1.61 T, the ceiling μ₀M_s²/4 ≈ 516 kJ/m³ leaves almost no headroom [2].

Two pressures now force the field to look past Nd₂Fe₁₄B. The first is *criticality*. The 2011 rare-earth crisis demonstrated that a supply chain concentrated in a handful of producers can multiply magnet prices several-fold within months, and the U.S. DOE's 2023 Critical Materials Assessment lists neodymium and dysprosium as critical in both the short (2020–2025) and medium (2025–2035) terms, with terbium joining them [1][9]. The pillars of the DOE mitigation strategy — diversifying supply, developing substitutes, and recycling — place rare-earth-free hard magnets squarely in the "developing substitutes" column [9]. The second pressure is *temperature*. Electric-vehicle traction motors and direct-drive wind generators operate at 400–450 K, where Nd₂Fe₁₄B — with a Curie temperature of only 585 K and a strongly negative temperature coefficient of coercivity — requires expensive dysprosium doping to survive [3].

> **Theorem (Coey's hardness criterion):** A uniaxial ferromagnet can function as a permanent magnet — resisting demagnetization by its own stray field — only if the dimensionless hardness parameter κ = √(K₁/μ₀M_s²) exceeds unity. Equivalently, the anisotropy field H_a = 2K₁/μ₀M_s must exceed the saturation magnetization. This single inequality is the sieve through which every rare-earth-free candidate must pass [1][2].

The candidates that survive the sieve are few. This thesis treats the three with the strongest combined intrinsic-and-processing case: **LTP-MnBi**, **τ-MnAl(-C)**, and **α″-Fe₁₆N₂**, with the exchange-spring nanocomposite concept as the architectural overlay that could amplify all three.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Figures of merit

A permanent magnet stores magnetic energy in the external field it sustains against an opposing field. For an ideal rectangular hysteresis loop, the maximum energy product is (BH)max = μ₀M_s²/4 = J_s²/4μ₀, attained at the optimal operating point of the demagnetization curve [2]. Real coercivity H_c never reaches the anisotropy field H_a = 2K₁/J_s — this shortfall, *Brown's paradox*, typically costs a factor of three to five even in well-engineered microstructures, because nucleation at defects and imperfect grain-boundary phases trigger reversal far below the Stoner–Wohlfarth limit [2].

The hardness parameter κ organizes the periodic table of hard magnets. κ > 1 marks materials that can, in principle, be made into magnets of any shape; 0.5 < κ < 1 marks materials usable only in favorable geometries; κ < 0.5 marks soft or semi-hard also-rans. The rare-earth-free challengers cluster near the boundary, which is precisely what makes them interesting: small improvements in anisotropy or microstructure decide their fate.

### 2.2 The gap

Coey framed the strategic problem as *plugging the gap*: between the ~38 kJ/m³ of cheap, abundant hard ferrite and the ~470 kJ/m³ of Nd–Fe–B lies a vast application space — auxiliary motors, sensors, holding devices, mid-power generators — where ferrite is too weak and Nd–Fe–B too expensive or too temperature-fragile [1]. Ferrites sell for a few dollars per kilogram; sintered Nd–Fe–B sells for tens of dollars per kilogram with volatile pricing. A rare-earth-free magnet delivering 80–160 kJ/m³ (10–20 MGOe) at moderate cost would not merely substitute; it would *expand* the market into electrification applications currently priced out of rare-earth magnets [3].

The DOE framing sharpens the economic argument into a security one. Neodymium demand for EV motors and wind turbines is projected to grow along trajectories that strain even expanded mine supply through 2035, while manganese — the backbone element of two of our three candidates — is assessed as *non-critical* [9]. Bismuth, aluminum, carbon, iron and nitrogen are likewise abundant and geographically diversified.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *theoretical and computational synthesis*: we do not report new synthesis, but rather assemble published intrinsic data, first-principles calculations, and micromagnetic analysis into a unified viability map. Our protocol has four layers.

1. **Intrinsic-property compilation.** We extract J_s, K₁, T_C, and crystal-structure data from the primary literature for each candidate, cross-checking magnetometry against neutron-diffraction moments and first-principles values [4][5][6][7][8]. Where experiments and theory disagree — most famously for Fe₁₆N₂ — we preserve the disagreement as data rather than adjudicating it.

2. **First-principles anisotropy.** Magnetocrystalline anisotropy energies are obtained from density-functional theory in the full-potential linearized augmented plane-wave (FLAPW) formalism within the local spin-density approximation, with spin–orbit coupling included self-consistently; the anisotropy constant follows from the torque method, K₁ = −∂E/∂θ evaluated at the hard axis [5]. Curie temperatures are estimated from Heisenberg exchange parameters J_ij via the Liechtenstein formula, fed into mean-field and random-phase-approximation expressions — mean-field being an acknowledged upper bound.

3. **Micromagnetic coercivity modeling.** We solve the Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert equation on realistic grain morphologies to quantify how twin boundaries (in τ-MnAl), peritectic Bi inclusions (in MnBi), and α-Fe precipitates (in Fe₁₆N₂) depress H_c below H_a. The exchange-spring design rule enters here: a soft inclusion is stiffened by the hard matrix only if its dimension stays below roughly twice the hard-phase domain-wall width δ_h = π√(A/K₁) [1].

4. **Figures-of-merit computation.** The following Python routine reproduces every number in Table 1 from the compiled intrinsic data, so that the reader can re-derive the entire viability map:

```python
import math
MU0 = 4 * math.pi * 1e-7          # vacuum permeability, H/m
MGOE = 7.9577                     # kJ/m^3 per MGOe

def figures_of_merit(Js, K1):
    """Js: spontaneous polarization (T); K1: uniaxial anisotropy (J/m^3)."""
    Ms = Js / MU0
    Ha = 2 * K1 / Js                       # anisotropy field, T
    kappa = math.sqrt(K1 / (MU0 * Ms**2))  # hardness parameter
    BHmax = Js**2 / (4 * MU0) / 1000       # kJ/m^3
    return Ha, kappa, BHmax, BHmax / MGOE

candidates = [
    ("Nd2Fe14B", 1.61, 4.9e6),
    ("SmCo5",    1.05, 17.2e6),
    ("LTP-MnBi", 0.91, 1.6e6),
    ("tau-MnAl", 0.75, 1.525e6),
    ("Fe16N2*",  2.90, 1.0e6),   # * claimed values; see Section 4.3
    ("SrFe12O19",0.48, 0.35e6),
]
for name, Js, K1 in candidates:
    Ha, k, bh, mgoe = figures_of_merit(Js, K1)
    print(f"{name:10s} Ha={Ha:5.2f} T  kappa={k:.2f}  "
          f"(BH)max={bh:7.1f} kJ/m^3 ({mgoe:5.1f} MGOe)")
```

A companion screening function in Haskell expresses the κ > 1 design rule as a pure predicate over candidate records, suitable for high-throughput computational searches over hypothetical 3d compounds:

```haskell
-- Hardness-parameter screen for rare-earth-free candidates
data Magnet = Magnet { name :: String, js :: Double
                     , k1 :: Double, tc :: Double }

mu0 :: Double
mu0 = 4 * pi * 1e-7

kappa :: Magnet -> Double
kappa m = sqrt (k1 m / (mu0 * ms * ms))
  where ms = js m / mu0

viable :: Magnet -> Bool
viable m = kappa m > 1.0 && tc m > 400.0   -- hard AND hot enough

-- e.g. filter viable [Magnet "LTP-MnBi" 0.91 1.6e6 628, ...]
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 LTP-MnBi: the compound whose anisotropy improves with heat

Manganese bismuth is the oldest of our candidates and, paradoxically, the one whose physics was understood last. The low-temperature phase (LTP) crystallizes in the hexagonal NiAs-type B8₁ structure: Mn occupies the 2a sites, Bi the 2c sites, with room-temperature lattice parameters a = 0.4285 nm and c = 0.6123 nm [5]. It forms by a peritectic reaction at 628 K and, crucially, transforms to a paramagnetic high-temperature phase at that same temperature — so 628 K is simultaneously the formation temperature and the Curie point [4].

The magnetic behavior is anomalous in the most useful possible way. At 4.2 K the anisotropy is *in-plane* (K ≈ −0.25 MJ/m³); around 100 K a spin reorientation rotates the moments onto the c-axis, and from there K₁ climbs steeply, reaching 1.1–1.6 MJ/m³ at room temperature [5]. Neutron diffraction ties this to the c/a ratio: uniaxial anisotropy switches on when c/a exceeds ~1.425, and the anisotropy field grows with c/a thereafter — thermal expansion of the lattice *is* the anisotropy mechanism [5]. The consequence is the famous positive temperature coefficient of coercivity: H_c rises from 100 K to a maximum of ~2.5 T near 540 K, then declines toward the 628 K transition [4].

For applications, the headline result is the *crossover*: above ~450 K, the (BH)max of MnBi surpasses that of Nd₂Fe₁₄B, whose coercivity collapses with temperature [5]. Bonded LTP-MnBi magnets with >95 wt.% phase purity deliver 8.9 MGOe at room temperature and 5.0 MGOe at 400 K — modest against Nd–Fe–B at 300 K, but already competitive where motors actually run hot [4].

### 4.2 τ-MnAl(-C): winning a kinetic race against equilibrium

The τ-phase of MnAl is a textbook metastable magnet: the ferromagnetic phase that industry wants does not appear on the equilibrium phase diagram at all. The hard phase is tetragonal L1₀ (CuAu-type), a = 0.273–0.277 nm, c = 0.354–0.358 nm, formed not by cooling from the melt but by a shear-plus-ordering transformation from the high-temperature hexagonal ε-phase, typically during annealing near 500 °C [6][7]. Leave it too long, or push the temperature too high, and τ decomposes into the nonmagnetic equilibrium γ₂ and β phases — the TLA⁺ machine of Section 3 states the dilemma exactly.

Intrinsically, τ-MnAl is genuinely promising: 2.3 μ_B per Mn, K₁ = 1.525 MJ/m³, T_C ≈ 650 K, and a theoretical (BH)max near 112 kJ/m³ (14 MGOe), squarely in the gap [6][7]. Carbon doping — typically 1–2 at.% — was the enabling discovery: interstitial carbon stabilizes the τ-phase, widening the annealing window before decomposition and suppressing the competing phases [7].

The experimental record shows both how far the material has come and where it stalls. Powders routinely exhibit coercivities above 4.8 kOe, but consolidation into dense bulk magnets degrades both magnetization and coercivity [7]. Equal-channel angular extrusion of gas-atomized Mn-46 at.% Al retained a nanocrystalline τ-dominated microstructure at >95% theoretical density, yielding H_c ≤ 4.4 kOe with magnetization ≤ 40 emu/g at 10 kOe [6]. Yang's group reported 5 kOe coercivity and 3.1 MGOe energy product in carbon-free τ-MnAl — proof that the intrinsic phase, when pure, performs [4].

The deepest microstructural insight is also the most hopeful: τ-MnAl is plagued by *twinning*, and twin boundaries are potent nucleation sites for reverse domains. Recent work demonstrates a twin-suppression effect when grain sizes fall below a critical value, in both constrained polycrystals and single particles; the resulting roadmap predicts a fully dense magnet at 98.9 kJ/m³ — within striking distance of the 112 kJ/m³ theoretical ceiling [6].

### 4.3 α″-Fe₁₆N₂: the giant moment and the burden of proof

No candidate better illustrates the difference between intrinsic promise and magnetic reality than α″-Fe₁₆N₂. The claim, originating with Jack in 1972, is extraordinary: a body-centered-tetragonal ordered phase, nitrogen occupying octahedral interstices in a 2×2×2 bcc supercell, with saturation magnetization ~2.9 T (≈305 emu/g) — exceeding the Fe₆₅Co₃₅ Slater–Pauling maximum of 2.45 T — and uniaxial anisotropy above 1.0×10⁷ erg/cm³ (1 MJ/m³) [8]. The raw materials are iron and nitrogen: cheap, nontoxic, geopolitically boring. If true, the theoretical (BH)max exceeds 1600 kJ/m³.

The evidentiary history is a fifty-year argument. Conventional magnetometry on films and powders reports a wide scatter of M_s values, contaminated by α-Fe, γ′-Fe₄N, and uncertain phase fractions — the classic pathology of metastable-phase magnetometry [8]. The decisive experiment used polarized neutron reflectometry on epitaxial, surface-plasma-free sputtered films: a *direct*, element- and depth-resolved measurement of the magnetization, yielding M_s up to 2500 emu/cm³, corroborated by polarization-dependent XANES evidence for directional Fe→N charge transfer within the Fe₆N cluster, the microscopic engine of the proposed "cluster + atom" double-exchange picture [8]. First-principles LDA+U calculations show that the giant moment emerges only when the on-site 3d–3d Coulomb repulsion is treated beyond LSDA — partial localization coexisting with itinerant ferromagnetism [8].

But our hardness formalism delivers a cold verdict: with J_s = 2.9 T and K₁ = 1 MJ/m³, κ ≈ 0.39 — far below unity. α″-Fe₁₆N₂, *even if the giant moment is fully vindicated*, cannot be a standalone permanent magnet; it would demagnetize itself. Its destiny, if any, is as the soft high-moment phase in an exchange-spring composite, where a hard shell (MnBi? τ-MnAl?) provides the anisotropy. The processing constraints reinforce this reading: α″ decomposes into α-Fe + γ′-Fe₄N above ~523 K, so consolidation must stay cold — spark-plasma sintering at 373–473 K yields only ~80% density, 1.07 T saturation and 0.20 MA/m coercivity in bulk compacts [8].

### 4.4 The exchange-spring overlay: architecture as anisotropy

Kneller and Hawig's 1991 exchange-spring concept — a hard phase exchange-coupled to a soft high-moment phase on the scale of the domain-wall width — remains the only proposal that could *exceed* the Nd₂Fe₁₄B energy product without rare earths [1]. The physics is unforgiving: the soft regions must be thinner than ~2δ_h (≈ 10–20 nm for our candidates), crystallographically coherent, and exchange-coupled without interdiffusion killing the hard phase's anisotropy. Decades of effort have produced beautiful thin-film demonstrations and no bulk magnet, because self-assembly at the 10-nm scale in three dimensions, at tonnage, is a manufacturing problem of a different order [1].

The strategic reading is therefore layered. MnBi and τ-MnAl are *standalone* candidates: κ > 1, realistic processing routes, and demonstrated bulk coercivity. Fe₁₆N₂ is a *composite-only* candidate: its value is unlocked exclusively inside an exchange-spring architecture.

*\*Claimed intrinsic values; κ < 1 excludes standalone use regardless.*

Three empirical results deserve emphasis as *proofs of mechanism*, not merely of performance:

1. **The MnBi crossover is real.** The positive temperature coefficient of coercivity — H_c peaking at ~2.5 T near 540 K — is observed across synthesis routes (melt-spun ribbons, ball-milled powder, bonded magnets) and tied by neutron diffraction to the c/a-driven spin reorientation [4][5]. The (BH)max-vs-T curves of MnBi and Nd₂Fe₁₄B cross near 450 K [5]. This is the single strongest application argument in the rare-earth-free portfolio: a magnet that gets *better* where motors get *hotter*.

2. **Twin suppression is the τ-MnAl lever.** The prediction that sub-critical grain sizes suppress twinning during the ε→τ transformation — and with it the dominant nucleation pathway — converts Brown's paradox from a lament into an engineering target, with 98.9 kJ/m³ as the quantitative prize [6]. Mechanical milling already reaches 4–4.8 kOe by grain refinement; the remaining task is preserving τ-phase fraction and density simultaneously [7].

3. **The Fe₁₆N₂ moment survives direct measurement.** Polarized neutron reflectometry is insensitive to the phase-fraction ambiguities that plagued fifty years of magnetometry, and it confirms magnetization far above the Slater–Pauling curve in epitaxial films [8].

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires enumerating what the viability map does not show. **First**, κ > 1 is necessary, not sufficient: it says nothing about corrosion (MnBi and MnAl both oxidize), mechanical brittleness, or the cost of the nanostructuring that real coercivity demands. **Second**, every (BH)max in Table 1 assumes a perfectly rectangular loop; real energy products run 30–60% of theory, and the deficit is microstructure, not intrinsic physics — which is good news for research but bad news for timelines. **Third**, the Fe₁₆N₂ literature remains bifurcated: PNR confirms giant moments in epitaxial films, while bulk powders consolidated below the 523 K decomposition limit show ordinary moments and modest coercivity, leaving the scale-up question entirely open [8]. **Fourth**, MnBi's peritectic formation inherently limits phase purity — the >95 wt.% LTP material requires careful melt-spinning and annealing, and Bi is itself a byproduct metal with its own supply quirks [4]. **Fifth**, τ-MnAl's carbon stabilization is a double-edged sword: carbon widens the τ window but occupies interstitial sites that can dilute the moment, and the optimal C content remains composition- and process-dependent [7]. **Finally**, the exchange-spring route that alone could surpass Nd₂Fe₁₄B has no demonstrated bulk manufacturing path after three decades; it should be funded as high-risk research, not booked as capacity [1].

## 7 Conclusion

The road beyond Nd₂Fe₁₄B is paved not with larger moments but with larger anisotropies. The hardness-parameter analysis leaves no ambiguity: LTP-MnBi (κ ≈ 1.56) and τ-MnAl (κ ≈ 1.85) clear the permanent-magnet threshold on intrinsic merit, while α″-Fe₁₆N₂ (κ ≈ 0.39) does not — its giant moment is a composite asset, not a magnet. MnBi brings the extraordinary gift of a positive temperature coefficient of coercivity and a demonstrated high-temperature crossover against Nd–Fe–B; τ-MnAl brings a credible, carbon-stabilized processing window and a twin-suppression roadmap to ~100 kJ/m³; Fe₁₆N₂ brings a vindicated physics anomaly awaiting an architecture. None of them needs to beat Nd₂Fe₁₄B at room temperature to matter. Plugging Coey's gap [1] — delivering 80–160 kJ/m³ from abundant elements, stable at 450 K, free of critical rare earths — is a sufficient victory, and on the evidence assembled here, it is an attainable one. The DOE's substitution pillar [9] has its materials; what remains is the patient, unglamorous work of microstructure.
