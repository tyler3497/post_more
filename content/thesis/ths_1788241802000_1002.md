# Formal Verification of Distributed Consensus: Raft Liveness in TLA+, Paxos Refinement Mapping, and Machine-Checked Safety with the Ivy Protocol Verifier

## Abstract

## 1 Introduction

Distributed consensus — getting unreliable, asynchronously communicating machines to agree on a single sequence of values — is the load-bearing primitive of modern infrastructure. The replicated logs of Raft [1], the ballot protocols of Paxos, and the stores built atop them must satisfy *safety* (nothing bad ever happens: no two leaders in one term, no divergent committed entries) and *liveness* (something good eventually happens: client requests are served). The Fischer–Lynch–Paterson (FLP) impossibility result [2] tells us that no deterministic asynchronous protocol can guarantee both safety and termination under even a single crash failure; practical protocols therefore assume *partial synchrony* — the network eventually behaves well enough, for long enough, for progress to occur. Verification inherits the split: safety is discharged by inductive invariants over reachable states; liveness requires reasoning about infinite executions, fairness, and termination.

> **Theorem (FLP, informal):** In a fully asynchronous message-passing system, there is no deterministic algorithm that solves consensus while tolerating even one unannounced process crash. Every verification effort must therefore make explicit the timing or randomization assumptions under which liveness is claimed.

The field's response is methodological pluralism. TLA+ [3] gives a set-theoretic specification language with explicit temporal logic, a model checker (TLC), and a proof system (TLAPS). Refinement mapping, industrialized by IronFleet [4], structures correctness as a simulation relation between implementation and spec, with liveness decomposed into per-component leads-to arguments. Ivy [5] restricts models to first-order logic compiled into the decidable Effectively Propositional (EPR) fragment, obtaining stable automation with finite counterexamples to induction. Each buys something and pays something: TLA+ buys expressiveness at the cost of unbounded model checking; refinement buys implementation-level guarantees at the cost of tens of thousands of proof lines; Ivy buys automation at the cost of a decidability discipline that shapes modeling.

This thesis argues these are complementary layers of one methodology. Section 2 reviews background; Section 3 states the methodology (TLA+ modeling, TLC checking, refinement, decidable safety discharge); Section 4 is the deep dive — Raft liveness in TLA+ (§4.1), the Paxos refinement mapping (§4.2), machine-checked safety in Ivy (§4.3), synthesis (§4.4); Section 5 compares proof scale and automation; Section 6 states limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Raft Consensus Algorithm

Raft [1] manages a replicated log on servers that are each a *follower*, *candidate*, or *leader*, partitioning time into consecutively numbered *terms* that serve as a logical clock. It decomposes consensus into three subproblems:

1. **Leader election.** Randomized timeouts trigger candidacy; a majority elects the leader per term, and only servers with up-to-date logs can win.
2. **Log replication.** The leader appends client commands and replicates them via `AppendEntries`; an entry is *committed* once stored on a majority.
3. **Safety.** Leader Append-Only plus the Election Restriction (a candidate's log must be at least as up-to-date as a majority's) yield *Election Safety*, *Log Matching*, *Leader Completeness*, and *State Machine Safety*.

On the liveness side, randomized election timeouts make split votes probabilistically rare, and leader completeness plus the commitment rule imply that once the network stabilizes, a stable leader is elected and requests are eventually committed. Formalizing this 'eventually' requires fairness assumptions on delivery and scheduling — the territory of TLA+ temporal operators.

### 2.2 The Paxos Family

Lamport's Paxos [6] solves single-decree consensus via a two-phase ballot protocol — *prepare/promise* then *accept/accepted* — with proposers, acceptors, and learners; Multi-Paxos extends it to a decree sequence by reusing a stable leader's phase-1 work. Where Raft imposes strong-leader discipline, Paxos permits more concurrency and pays in understandability [7]. For verification the crucial artifact is the *refinement mapping*: a function (possibly with history and prophecy variables) from implementation to specification states such that every implementation step simulates a specification step or stutters.

### 2.3 TLA+, TLC, and TLAPS

TLA+ combines set theory with the temporal operators of the Temporal Logic of Actions. A system is `Init ∧ □[Next]_vars ∧ Fairness`; properties are invariants (`□P`) or temporal formulas such as leadsto (`P ~> Q`). The TLC model checker enumerates reachable states of finite instances; TLAPS is a proof system that checks hierarchical, human-structured proofs of TLA+ formulas for unbounded instances, dispatching obligations to SMT solvers and a tableau prover. The Raft paper shipped a TLA+ specification of the static protocol [1]; later work machine-checked Raft reconfiguration safety in ~3,200 lines of TLA+ [8] and moderately complex Paxos variants with proofs an order of magnitude smaller than the Coq equivalents [9].

### 2.4 Decidable Verification and the EPR Fragment

Full first-order logic is undecidable, which makes SMT-based verification of quantified invariants fragile: solvers may diverge or return *unknown*. The Effectively Propositional (EPR) fragment — formulas of the form `∃*∀* φ` with `φ` quantifier-free and no function symbols of positive arity — is decidable: every EPR formula is satisfiable iff it has a finite model of bounded size. Ivy [5] models a protocol and its invariant in first-order logic, then compiles the verification conditions into EPR via modular decomposition and quantifier stratification. The payoff: invariant checking always terminates and, when the invariant is not inductive, produces a *finite counterexample to induction* — a concrete state the engineer uses to strengthen the invariant [10].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology has four stages, each matched to the tool that discharges it best.

**Stage A — TLA+ specification and finite model checking.** We specify Raft in TLA+ and check safety invariants with TLC on small configurations (3–5 servers, bounded terms and logs). Liveness is stated temporally: *eventual election* under weak fairness, *eventual commit* (`RequestIssued(r) ~> Committed(r)`) under strong fairness on delivery and leader actions.

**Stage B — Refinement mapping.** We define an abstract consensus spec — a single monotonically growing sequence of chosen values — and a refinement mapping from a multi-Paxos protocol layer to it, in the IronFleet two-level pattern [4]. Each Paxos action simulates an abstract action or stutters, and liveness decomposes into per-component leads-to proofs.

**Stage C — Decidable safety checking in Ivy style.** The safety core (quorum intersection, ballot monotonicity, vote uniqueness) is recast in first-order logic and stratified into EPR. We check each invariant conjunct's inductiveness, using finite counterexamples to induction to drive strengthening. Because checking is decidable, thousands of iterations can run unattended — enabling automated invariant *inference* for protocols as intricate as Paxos [10].

**Stage D — Cross-validation.** TLC counterexamples, TLAPS's unbounded safety proof, and Ivy's decidable checks run against the same protocol model; a bug caught at one level must be explainable at the others.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Raft Liveness in TLA+

We now turn to what Raft must *eventually* do — and under which assumptions — modeling per-server `currentTerm`, `votedFor`, `log`, `commitIndex`, `state`, and a network multiset `msgs`.

The safety core is by now standard: *Election Safety* (no two leaders share a term). Its proof rests on quorum intersection [1]; the deeper invariant is *Leader Completeness*: a committed entry of term `T` appears in every later leader's log, which with Leader Append-Only yields State Machine Safety.

> **Theorem (State Machine Safety, Raft):** If a server has applied a log entry at a given index to its state machine, no other server will ever apply a different entry for that index. The proof proceeds by induction on terms, using quorum intersection and the Election Restriction that a candidate's log be at least as up-to-date as a majority's [1].

Liveness cannot be stated as an invariant. The properties we want are temporal:

```tla
EventualElection ==
  <>(\E s \in Server : state[s] = "Leader")

RequestCommitted(r) ==
  \E s \in Server : \E i \in 1..Len(log[s]) : log[s][i].request = r

EventualCommit(r) ==
  RequestIssued(r) ~> (\E i : CommittedAt(i, r))
```

Here `~>` is TLA+'s *leads-to* operator; these formulas are *false* without fairness assumptions — the formal shadow of FLP [2]:

1. **Weak fairness on message delivery** (`WF_msgs(Deliver)`).
2. **Randomized election timeouts**, abstracted as strong fairness on elections (Raft's real mechanism is probabilistic [1]).
3. **Stable leader assumption.** Once a leader is elected and the network is synchronous, weak fairness on `AppendEntries` and `AdvanceCommitIndex` yields eventual commitment of every request the leader receives.

The spec conjoins weak fairness on elections and leader replication with strong fairness on message delivery. TLC checks `EventualElection` on small models under these fairness assumptions. The honest limitation: randomized timeouts are *modeled*, not verified — the probabilistic argument that split votes are rare lives outside TLA+'s non-probabilistic semantics.

### 4.2 Paxos Refinement Mapping: From Ballots to a Centralized Log

Where TLA+ specifies the protocol directly, refinement structures the *implementation's* correctness as simulation of an abstract specification. Following IronFleet [4], the abstract machine is a centralized, obviously correct replicated state machine:

The abstract spec is a single monotonically growing command log. The refinement mapping `R` sends each concrete multi-Paxos state — ballot numbers, vote records, promise sets, learner state — to this log: a slot maps to a command iff a quorum voted for it in the highest ballot seen. The proof obligation is the forward-simulation diagram: each concrete transition either stutters or maps to an abstract action:

| Concrete action | Abstract effect under `R` |
|---|---|
| Phase 1a (prepare) | Stutter — no abstract state change |
| Phase 1b (promise) | Stutter — records information only |
| Phase 2a (accept request) | Stutter — proposal not yet chosen |
| Phase 2b (accepted by quorum) | `absPropose` — value becomes chosen |
| Learn / state transfer | Stutter — already-chosen value observed |

IronFleet's key insight is the *two-level* decomposition [4]: an intermediate *protocol layer* with idealized mathematics absorbs the distributed-systems reasoning (quorum intersection, ballot monotonicity, reordering), while the implementation layer absorbs systems reasoning (buffers, overflow, serialization); the refinement `Implementation → Protocol → Spec` composes. Their IronRSL — a Paxos-based replicated state machine with batching, state transfer, and log truncation — carried ~30,000 lines of Dafny proof against ~1,000 lines of spec, proving safety *and* liveness: under eventual synchrony for a live quorum, a repeatedly submitted request eventually receives a reply [4]. The landmark demonstration that machine-checked liveness is achievable, at a proof-to-code ratio near 30:1.

The Paxos mapping is history-sensitive — it must remember each acceptor's highest promised ballot and vote — hence ghost state (auxiliary variables invisible to the implementation) is the standard device. Verdi's Coq proof of Raft used the same device: ghost variables through a refinement layer, 90 invariants discovered iteratively [11].

### 4.3 Machine-Checked Safety with the Ivy Protocol Verifier

Ivy attacks from the opposite direction: instead of scaling up proof engineering, restrict the logic until checking is decidable, then automate completely. An Ivy development has three parts: (i) a protocol model in first-order logic; (ii) an inductive invariant, also first-order; (iii) *isolates* — modular decompositions that stratify quantifier alternation so verification conditions fall in EPR [5][10].

The core Paxos safety invariants — *vote uniqueness per ballot* and *quorum agreement*:

```
# Ivy-style invariant (simplified)
invariant [vote_unique]
  forall N1:node, N2:node, B:ballot, V1:value, V2:value.
    voted(N1,B,V1) & voted(N2,B,V2) -> V1 = V2

invariant [quorum_chosen]
  forall Q:quorum, B:ballot, V:value.
    (forall N:node. member(N,Q) -> voted(N,B,V))
      -> chosen(B,V)
```

The inductiveness check compiles to EPR, so the solver *always terminates* with 'inductive' or a *finite counterexample to induction* (CTI) — a concrete small model of a transition into an invariant-violating state [5]. The engineer inspects the CTI, adds the missing conjunct it exposes (e.g. ballot monotonicity of promises), and repeats. The loop never hangs, never returns 'unknown,' and the CTI is always finite and inspectable.

The price is the *decidability discipline*. Function symbols of positive arity are forbidden — a Raft `log` must be relationalized as `log_entry(server, index, term, value)` with coherence invariants — and quantifier alternation must respect a stratification order on sorts, achieved by modular decomposition into isolates [10]. Transitive closure is inexpressible, so chained-leader arguments become local per-step invariants. The Raft log is a demanding but tractable fit: Log Matching becomes a universal invariant over index pairs, Leader Completeness a statement about the maximum term among quorums — all universal, hence EPR-safe.

Ivy also provides an automatic *liveness-to-safety* reduction via dynamic abstraction [5]: an LTL property is reduced to a safety property of an abstract over-approximating system, discharged in EPR like any other. The reduction is automatic, but the resulting safety proof still needs the engineer's invariant — liveness automation in Ivy is *relative*: the temporal reasoning is mechanized, the inductive insight is not.

### 4.4 Synthesis: One Methodology, Three Instruments

The three traditions compose into one pipeline. **Specify in TLA+**: write the protocol as a temporal formula; use TLC to falsify designs early — the cheapest bugs are the ones TLC finds in seconds; state liveness as leadsto under explicit fairness. **Refine to implementation**: build the refinement mapping from the executable protocol layer to the abstract spec in the IronFleet two-level style, so implementation details are verified against the protocol rather than re-proving consensus. **Discharge safety decidably**: recast the safety core in EPR-stratified first-order logic and let counterexample-driven invariant strengthening run to fixpoint. Each instrument covers the others' blind spots: TLA+ expresses liveness and fairness that EPR cannot; refinement connects the verified artifact to running code; Ivy gives push-button reliability for the safety core. No single tool verifies 'Raft' end to end — each verifies a *model* at a chosen abstraction, and the refinement relations between models are part of the trusted argument.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We compare six artifacts on the two axes practitioners care about: *proof scale* (human effort, roughly proportional to proof lines) and *automation* (how much checking is push-button).

| Artifact | Target | Tool | Proof scale | Safety | Liveness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Raft paper spec [1] | Raft (static) | TLA+/TLC | ~hundreds of lines spec | Model-checked, bounded | Stated informally |
| Verdi Raft [11] | Raft implementation | Coq | ~50,000 lines, 90 invariants, ~18 months | Machine-checked, unbounded | Not proved |
| Raft reconfiguration [8] | Raft + reconfiguration | TLA+/TLAPS | 3,189 lines TLA+ | Machine-checked, unbounded | Not proved |
| Moderately complex Paxos [9] | Multi-Paxos variants | TLAPS | ~10× smaller than Coq equivalents | Machine-checked, unbounded | Not proved |
| IronFleet IronRSL [4] | Paxos-based RSM implementation | Dafny/Z3 | ~30,000 lines proof, ~1,000 lines spec | Machine-checked | Machine-checked (eventual reply) |
| Ivy Paxos [5][10] | Paxos safety core | Ivy/EPR | Invariant + stratification, solver automatic | Decidable, automatic | Via liveness-to-safety reduction [5] |

**First, abstraction level dominates proof scale.** Verdi [11] and the TLA+ reconfiguration proof [8] both establish safety yet differ by more than an order of magnitude: Verdi verifies an OCaml-extracted *implementation*; the TLA+ proof verifies a *protocol model*. Neither subsumes the other, and the refinement mapping between those artifacts is where implementation bugs hide.

**Second, liveness is the expensive property.** Only IronFleet machine-checks liveness of a practical system [4], at roughly 30 proof lines per specification line. The reason is structural: safety is a property of *states*; liveness a property of *infinite behaviors* (fairness, ranking, leads-to composition). Ivy's reduction [5] mechanizes the translation but cannot invent the ranking argument; TLA+ *states* leads-to elegantly but TLC checks it only on finite models.

**Third, decidability changes the development loop qualitatively.** In Ivy, invariant checking is a *decision procedure*: thousands of check–strengthen iterations can run unattended, enabling automated invariant *inference* for protocols as subtle as Paxos [10]. With heuristic SMT, one 'unknown' stalls the loop. The cost is modeling discipline — relationalize functions, stratify quantifiers, avoid transitive closure — natural for the safety core but demanding for richer properties.

A finite executable check corroborates the first stage: a short script asserts that every pair of majorities intersects for cluster sizes 3, 5, and 7 — the finite shadow of the quorum-intersection invariant all three tools prove. Leader Completeness across terms does not reduce to finite combinatorics; that is where machine-checked proof earns its keep.

## 6 Limitations

**Liveness assumptions are modeled, not verified.** Our TLA+ liveness argument assumes eventual synchrony and fair scheduling; Raft's *probabilistic* core — randomized timeouts making split votes rare — is abstracted into fairness. A fully rigorous treatment would need probabilistic model checking, which none of the three traditions provides natively.

**The refinement mapping is manual and trusted.** IronFleet-style refinement [4] connects implementation to spec, but the mapping — including ghost state and the simulation argument — is written and proved by hand. A bug in the mapping voids the guarantee; it is part of the trusted computing base alongside the spec, compiler, and hardware.

**EPR excludes natural formalizations.** Transitive closure, unbounded arithmetic, and function symbols are outside EPR, so protocols depending on them must be re-encoded relationally or verified in a richer, undecidable logic where automation degrades [5][10]. Each encoding step is a new opportunity for modeling error.

**Byzantine faults are out of scope.** All results assume crash-stop (or crash-recovery) failures; Byzantine agreement needs different protocols and harder verification.

**Proof scale remains a barrier.** Even in Ivy/EPR, verifying Paxos demands deep expertise in invariant design and stratification. The 50,000-line Verdi proof [11] is roughly 18 months of expert effort — a cost few industrial teams will pay, which is why decidable, inferable verification [10] is the most promising route to broader adoption.

---

## 7 Conclusion

We have presented a unified methodology for verifying distributed consensus on Raft and Paxos: specify the protocol in TLA+ with liveness as leadsto under explicit fairness; connect implementations to abstract specifications through IronFleet-style two-level refinement; discharge the safety core in the decidable EPR fragment with counterexample-driven invariant strengthening. The three traditions are complementary: TLA+ gives temporal expressiveness and early falsification, refinement gives implementation-level guarantees, Ivy gives reliable automation with finite counterexamples.

Proof scale varies by more than an order of magnitude with abstraction level — ~3,200 lines of TLA+ versus ~50,000 of Coq — and machine-checked liveness remains rare and expensive [4]. The decidable-fragment approach is the most credible path to routine verification: when checking is a decision procedure, the loop becomes automatable, and invariant inference for protocols as intricate as Paxos becomes feasible [10].

*Do not ask which verifier is best; ask which property each verifier discharges cheapest, and compose them.* Safety invariants belong in a decidable fragment; temporal liveness in TLA+ under explicit fairness; implementation correctness under a refinement mapping. FLP [2] taught us consensus has no free lunch; four decades of verification research teach the same about proof.
