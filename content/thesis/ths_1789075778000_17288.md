# The Unique Games Conjecture and Semidefinite Programming: Optimality, Gaps, and the Frontier of Approximation Hardness

![Figure 1: Goemans–Williamson hyperplane rounding — unit vectors on the sphere are cut by a random hyperplane through the origin.](ths_1789075778000_17288-0.webp)

## Abstract

This thesis examines the interplay between the Unique Games Conjecture (UGC) and semidefinite programming in hardness of approximation. We begin with the Goemans–Williamson 0.878-approximation for Max-Cut and its hyperplane rounding, then show how Khot's 2002 conjecture on unique two-prover games turned this constant into a proposed universal threshold. We recount the proof by Khot, Kindler, Mossel, and O'Donnell that 0.878 is optimal under UGC, via the *Majority Is Stablest* theorem and the invariance principle. We present Raghavendra's 2008 theorem — assuming UGC, the optimal approximation ratio of *every* CSP is captured by a canonical SDP — and its engine: converting SDP integrality gaps into dictatorship tests. We analyze the Lasserre/sum-of-squares hierarchy as a candidate optimal algorithm; the subexponential algorithms of Arora, Barak, and Steurer; the Small-Set Expansion Hypothesis; and the 2018 resolution of the 2-to-2 Games Conjecture. We close with the status of the original conjecture, the central open problem of hardness of approximation.

## 1 Introduction

Max-Cut is among the oldest and most studied NP-hard optimization problems: given a weighted undirected graph, partition the vertices into two sets so as to maximize the weight of edges crossing the partition. A random assignment of vertices to sides satisfies half the edges in expectation, giving a trivial 1/2-approximation, and for nearly twenty years no algorithm did substantially better. This stagnation ended in 1995, when Goemans and Williamson showed that a *semidefinite programming* (SDP) relaxation of Max-Cut, rounded by a random hyperplane through the origin, yields a cut of expected value at least 0.87856 times the optimum [1]. The result was the first use of semidefinite programming in approximation-algorithm design and one of the first times a non-trivial algorithm for a combinatorial problem came from solving a convex relaxation and rounding it geometrically.

A decade of effort failed to improve the 0.878 constant. In 2002, Subhash Khot proposed the *Unique Games Conjecture*, a hardness hypothesis about a particular class of two-variable constraint satisfaction problems with permutation constraints [2]. At first glance, unique games look like an artificial restriction of label cover; but Khot showed that resolving the conjecture positively would imply striking hardness results for Min-2CNF-Deletion, and soon thereafter the conjecture was shown to imply that 0.878 is *optimal* for Max-Cut: no polynomial-time algorithm can beat Goemans–Williamson, conditional on UGC [3]. The conjecture thus reframes the 0.878 number not as an artifact of one algorithm's analysis, but as a putative law of efficient computation.

This thesis studies the triangle formed by (i) semidefinite programming as an algorithmic paradigm, (ii) the Unique Games Conjecture as a hardness hypothesis, and (iii) the *Lasserre/sum-of-squares* hierarchy as the strongest known family of SDP relaxations. The centerpiece is Raghavendra's theorem [4], which shows that under UGC, a single canonical SDP and rounding scheme achieve the best possible approximation ratio for every CSP — so the entire field of CSP approximability reduces, conditionally, to understanding one SDP. We then examine the strongest known SDP hierarchy, the algorithms that challenge the conjecture (notably the subexponential algorithm of Arora, Barak, and Steurer [5]), the related Small-Set Expansion Hypothesis, and the current state of the conjecture after the 2018 resolution of the 2-to-2 Games Conjecture.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Semidefinite programs and vector relaxations

A *semidefinite program* is the optimization of a linear function over an affine slice of the positive semidefinite cone: maximize *c^T x* subject to *x* in *P* intersected with *X ≥ 0* in the matrix sense. SDPs can be solved to arbitrary precision in polynomial time by the ellipsoid method or interior-point methods. Their power in approximation comes from *vector relaxations*: a binary variable *x_i ∈ {−1, +1}* is replaced by a unit vector *v_i* in a high-dimensional sphere, and a quadratic objective *x_i x_j* becomes the inner product *v_i · v_j*. The resulting vector program is an SDP in disguise, via the Gram matrix *G_ij = v_i · v_j ≥ 0*.

### 2.2 CSPs and the PCP framework

A *constraint satisfaction problem* (CSP) is specified by variables over a finite alphabet and constraints (predicates) applied to tuples of variables; Max-*P*-CSP asks to maximize the number (or weight) of satisfied constraints. Max-Cut is Max-2CSP with the "not-equal" predicate; Max-3SAT is the canonical NP-hard case. Unconditional hardness for CSPs comes from the PCP theorem and its strengthenings: Håstad showed it is NP-hard to approximate Max-Cut within 16/17 + ε and Max-3SAT within 7/8 + ε [3]. These results use *dictatorship tests* — verifiers that distinguish dictator functions (depending on one coordinate) from functions far from any dictator — composed with long codes.

### 2.3 Unique games

A *unique game* is a Max-2CSP in which every constraint is a *permutation*: for an edge *(u, v)* with permutation *π*, a labeling *ℓ* satisfies the constraint iff *ℓ(v) = π(ℓ(u))*. Khot's conjecture states that for arbitrarily small *ζ, δ > 0* there is a label-set size *k* such that it is NP-hard to distinguish unique games of value at least *1 − ζ* from those of value at most *δ* [2]. The conjecture is a "sliding-scale" hardness: the gap must hold for *every* pair *(ζ, δ)*, with *k* depending on the parameters.

### 2.4 The invariance principle

The analysis of noise stability — how a function's output correlates under noisy inputs — is governed by the *invariance principle* of Mossel, O'Donnell, and Oleszkiewicz: low-influence Boolean functions behave like their multilinear extensions under Gaussian inputs. This principle lets one translate combinatorial statements about dictatorship tests into Gaussian isoperimetric statements, and it is the analytic engine behind both the optimal Max-Cut hardness and Raghavendra's theorem.

## 3 Methodology

Our technical exposition follows the standard pipeline of SDP-based approximation and UGC-based hardness: (i) formulate the SDP relaxation, (ii) design and analyze a rounding scheme, (iii) construct integrality gaps showing the analysis is tight, and (iv) convert gaps into dictatorship tests and hence hardness.

### The Goemans–Williamson relaxation and rounding

For Max-Cut on *G = (V, E)* with weights *w_uv*, the SDP relaxation is:

> **SDP-MaxCut.** Maximize Σ_{(u,v)∈E} w_uv · (1 − v_u · v_v)/2, subject to ||v_i|| = 1 for all i.

Since the all-ones embedding *v_i = x_i · e_1* is feasible with the same objective as the integer optimum, the SDP optimum upper-bounds OPT. The rounding is geometric: sample a uniformly random unit vector *r*, and place *i* on side *S* iff *v_i · r ≥ 0*. An edge *(u, v)* with angle *θ = arccos(v_u · v_v)* is cut exactly when *r* separates the two vectors, which happens with probability *θ/π*. Comparing the expected contribution *θ/π* to the SDP contribution *(1 − cos θ)/2* edge by edge gives:

> **Theorem (Goemans–Williamson [1]).** The expected cut weight is at least min_{0<θ≤π} [(2θ/π) / (1 − cos θ)] · SDP ≥ 0.87856 · OPT.

The minimizing *θ ≈ 2.331* radians is the worst-case angle, and the constant *α_GW ≈ 0.878567* is the GW threshold. The rounding runs in polynomial time and needs only an approximately optimal SDP solution.

```python
# Pseudocode: Goemans-Williamson hyperplane rounding
def gw_round(vectors):            # vectors: dict i -> unit vector (as lists)
    r = random_unit_vector(len(vectors[0]))
    S = {i for i, v in vectors.items() if dot(v, r) >= 0}
    return S                       # cut = (S, V \ S), E[cut] >= 0.878 * SDP
```

### The UGC-hardness pipeline

To prove hardness *assuming UGC*, one builds a *dictatorship test*: a CSP instance (a "gadget") whose completeness and soundness mirror the integrality gap of the SDP. Raghavendra's framework converts any SDP integrality gap into such a test automatically, then composes it with the Unique Games instance via the standard long-code reduction. The final hardness factor equals the *integrality gap curve* of the canonical SDP.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The 0.878 Constant Is Optimal (Under UGC)

For nearly a decade after 1995, the question stood: is 0.878 the true approximability of Max-Cut, or just the limit of one rounding scheme? Khot, Kindler, Mossel, and O'Donnell [3] answered conditionally: assuming UGC, no polynomial-time algorithm achieves *(α_GW + ε)*-approximation for any *ε > 0*.

The proof proceeds through the geometry of noise stability. A dictatorship test for Max-Cut asks a verifier to query a function *f: {−1,+1}^n → {−1,+1}* at two *ρ*-correlated points and accept iff the values differ. Dictators pass with probability *(1 − ρ)/2*; the question is how large this acceptance probability can be for functions far from dictators. The answer is given by the *Majority Is Stablest* theorem: among balanced, low-influence functions, majority maximizes noise stability, and the extremal value is exactly the Gaussian quantity *(arccos ρ)/π*. Composing this test with a unique game yields a Max-Cut instance whose SDP value is near the completeness while every integral cut is bounded by the soundness — and the ratio of the two is precisely *α_GW*. The argument revealed something remarkable: the "hard core" of Max-Cut consists of instances whose SDP embeddings lie on a high-dimensional sphere, mirroring the GW relaxation itself. The algorithm and the hardness are two sides of one geometric coin.

### 4.2 The Unique Games Conjecture: Formulation and Consequences

![Figure 2: A label-extended graph of a Unique Games instance — each vertex carries a cloud of labels, edges impose permutation constraints.](ths_1789075778000_17288-3.webp)

Formally, a unique game *𝒢 = (V, E, [k], {π_e})* has variables over alphabet *[k]* and, for each edge *e = (u, v)*, a permutation *π_e* with the constraint *ℓ(v) = π_e(ℓ(u))*. Its *value* is the maximum fraction of satisfiable constraints. The conjecture [2]:

> **Conjecture (UGC).** For every *ζ, δ > 0* there exists *k = k(ζ, δ)* such that distinguishing *val(𝒢) ≥ 1 − ζ* from *val(𝒢) ≤ δ* is NP-hard.

It is often convenient to work with the equivalent *Max-2Lin(q)* formulation: equations *x_u − x_v = c_uv (mod q)*. Unique games are equivalently easy to state and remarkably expressive: Khot's original paper already derived hardness for Min-2CNF-Deletion, and subsequent work derived UGC-hardness for Vertex Cover (factor 2 − ε, by Khot–Regev), Multiway Cut, feedback arc set, and dozens of other problems. The pattern of every UGC result is the same: the SDP integrality gap for the problem determines the optimal hardness factor, and UGC supplies the "hard" starting instances. This raised the question Raghavendra answered: is there a *uniform* theorem saying that, under UGC, the SDP always wins?

### 4.3 Raghavendra's Theorem: Optimal SDPs for Every CSP

![Figure 3: Raghavendra's pipeline — an SDP integrality gap is converted into a dictatorship test, then composed with Unique Games to give optimal hardness.](ths_1789075778000_17288-2.webp)

Raghavendra's 2008 theorem [4] is the culminating statement of the UGC program for CSPs:

> **Theorem (Raghavendra).** Assuming UGC, for every CSP there is a *canonical* SDP relaxation and a fixed rounding scheme such that its approximation ratio equals the best ratio achievable by *any* polynomial-time algorithm.

The proof is a tour de force in three movements. **First**, for a predicate *P*, define the *basic SDP*: variables are distributions *μ_S* over assignments to subsets *S* of variables (consistent marginals up to some locality), and the objective maximizes expected satisfaction. Its *integrality gap curve* is the function *c ↦ s* where instances with SDP value *c* have true optimum at most *s*. **Second**, an integrality gap instance is converted into a *dictatorship test* for *P* with matching completeness *c* and soundness *s*, using the gap's SDP solution as the test's "ideal" distribution and the invariance principle to bound low-influence functions. **Third**, the dictatorship test is plugged into the standard UG-to-CSP reduction, yielding *(s + ε)*-hardness on *(c − ε)*-satisfiable instances — exactly the gap curve. Since any approximation algorithm beating the SDP would refute this hardness, the SDP is optimal. The theorem even shows the optimal ratio is *computable*: it is the solution of a finite (though enormous) optimization over the gap curve.

The message is philosophically striking: if UGC holds, the entire landscape of CSP approximability is *decidable by semidefinite programming*. Algorithm designers need not invent new techniques — they need only compute the canonical SDP's gap. Subsequent work extended the paradigm to covering/packing problems and bounded-degree settings, but the SDP original remains the definitive statement.

### 4.4 The Lasserre/Sum-of-Squares Hierarchy: A Candidate Optimal Algorithm

![Figure 4: The convex-relaxation ladder — LP, SDP, and successive Lasserre levels tighten the relaxation while integrality gaps shrink.](ths_1789075778000_17288-1.webp)

If SDPs are so powerful, why stop at the basic one? The *Lasserre* (sum-of-squares, SoS) hierarchy gives, for each degree *d*, an SDP of size *n^{O(d)}* whose feasible "pseudo-distributions" are consistent on all subsets of up to *d* variables. Level 1 essentially recovers the basic SDP; higher levels capture increasingly global correlations. SoS is the strongest known systematic family of SDP relaxations and a natural candidate for an *unconditionally* optimal algorithm — perhaps it achieves Raghavendra's ratios without assuming UGC.

Rounding SoS solutions is subtler than rounding basic SDPs, because pseudo-distributions need not correspond to any actual distribution. Barak, Kelner, and Steurer [6] developed a general theory: a *combining algorithm* that maps a distribution over solutions to a single (possibly weaker) solution can be lifted, via SoS proof duality, into a rounding algorithm for the hierarchy. This yielded new results for small-set expansion, sparsest cut, and tensor problems.

The hierarchy's limits are probed by *integrality gaps*: instances where the degree-*d* SoS value is high but the true optimum is low. For Unique Games specifically, strong gaps are known: Khot–Vishnoi-type constructions (and later simplifications) show that even *Ω(n)*-degree SoS has an integrality gap of *1 − ε* vs. *δ* for suitable parameters — meaning SoS, no matter how powerful, cannot *refute* UGC by solving unique games exactly at high degree. Yet crucially, these gaps do not rule out SoS *approximating* unique games well enough to beat the conjectured hardness; the known gaps concern exact solvability at high completeness, not the sliding-scale approximation UGC demands. Whether SoS can approximate every unique game within the UGC-forbidden region remains open, and is arguably the most important algorithmic question in the area: a positive answer would refute UGC outright.

### 4.5 Subexponential Algorithms, Small-Set Expansion, and the Status of the Conjecture

The most direct challenge to UGC came from algorithms, not gaps. Arora, Barak, and Steurer [5] gave a *subexponential*-time algorithm for Unique Games: in time *exp(O(n^{1−c}))* (for instances with value *1 − ε*), it finds an assignment satisfying a *1 − ε^c* fraction of constraints. The algorithm exploits the *threshold rank* of the constraint graph — the number of large eigenvalues of its normalized adjacency matrix. Graphs of low threshold rank admit efficient spectral partitioning (building on Kolla's algorithm for unique games on expanders); the ABS algorithm recursively decomposes an arbitrary graph into low-threshold-rank pieces, solves each, and stitches the solutions. While subexponential does not refute UGC (which concerns polynomial time), it shows unique games are "easier than NP-hard problems," which typically resist even subexponential approximation — and it fueled a wave of skepticism.

A parallel line reformulated the conjecture combinatorially. Raghavendra and Steurer proposed the *Small-Set Expansion Hypothesis* (SSEH): it is NP-hard to distinguish graphs containing a small non-expanding set from graphs in which all small sets expand near-perfectly [7]. SSEH *implies* UGC, and Raghavendra, Steurer, and Tulsiani showed the two are essentially equivalent on small-set expanders — placing expansion, a purely graph-theoretic property, at the combinatorial heart of the conjecture. SSEH additionally implies hardness for Balanced Separator and Minimum Linear Arrangement, results not known from UGC alone.

The landscape shifted decisively in 2018, when Dinur, Khot, Kindler, Minzer, and Safra proved the *2-to-2 Games Conjecture* (with imperfect completeness): a variant of unique games in which each constraint is a 2-to-2 relation is NP-hard to approximate [8]. The proof, via expansion properties of the Grassmann graph, established "half" of UGC — the 2-to-2 (rather than 1-to-1) version — and remains the deepest result in the area. It did not settle the original conjecture, which remains open: neither proved nor refuted. The community is split; the subexponential algorithm and SoS gaps counsel doubt, while the 2-to-2 proof and the sheer coherence of the UGC consequences counsel belief.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation

Since the subject is theoretical, "empirical" here means the concrete quantitative record: the integrality-gap constructions and algorithmic guarantees that pin down each constant. The table below summarizes the state of play for the principal problems discussed.

| Problem | Best algorithm (ratio) | UGC hardness | Tight? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Max-Cut | *α_GW ≈ 0.87856* (GW SDP) | *α_GW + ε* [3] | Yes |
| Max-2SAT | ≈ 0.940 (LLZ) | ≈ 0.943 [3] | Essentially |
| Max-2Lin(*q*) | 0.878 (GW) | *α_GW + ε* [3] | Yes |
| Max-*q*-Cut | 1 − 1/q + 2 ln q/q² (FJ) | matching [3] | Asymptotically |
| Unique Games (value 1−ε) | 1 − O(√(ε log k)) (CMM) | 1 − Ω(√(ε log k)) [3] | Yes |
| Min Vertex Cover | 2 (LP) | 2 − ε (KR) | Yes |

Three quantitative facts deserve emphasis. **First**, the GW constant is *sharp as a number*: Feige and Schechtman constructed graphs whose SDP optimum is 1 while the true optimum is exactly *α_GW*, so the rounding analysis cannot be improved. **Second**, the KKMO07 reduction is *parameter-tight*: the hardness ratio equals the integrality gap curve point-for-point, not merely asymptotically. **Third**, the ABS subexponential algorithm's guarantee *1 − ε^c* in time *exp(n^{1−c'})* sits in a regime no known reduction rules out: refuting UGC would require *polynomial* time with *constant* soundness *δ* independent of *ε*, a qualitatively stronger statement.

## 6 Limitations

Every result in this thesis is conditional or bounded, and the limitations are as instructive as the theorems. **The conjecture itself is unproven.** All "optimality" claims are relative to UGC; an unconditional proof of *α_GW*-hardness for Max-Cut remains far out of reach (the best unconditional bound is 16/17 ≈ 0.941). **Integrality gaps bound relaxations, not algorithms.** A gap instance defeats one SDP, not every polynomial-time method; the GW relaxation could be suboptimal even if UGC is true. **The invariance principle has hypotheses.** Raghavendra's conversion requires low-influence functions and Gaussian bounds; predicates whose gap instances are "junta-like" need additional machinery (as in Austrin–Mossel's work on approximation resistance). **SoS gaps are incomplete.** Known SoS lower bounds for unique games target high completeness and do not exclude a polynomial-time SoS-based refutation of the full sliding-scale conjecture. **Subexponential is not polynomial.** The ABS algorithm challenges the *spirit* of UGC but leaves its letter intact. Finally, **the 2-to-2 proof does not lift**: its Grassmann-graph machinery is specific to 2-to-2 relations, and extending it to 1-to-1 constraints is the central open problem.

## 7 Conclusion

The Unique Games Conjecture began as a technical hypothesis about two-prover games and became the organizing principle of hardness of approximation. Its alliance with semidefinite programming is the deepest such alliance in theoretical computer science: Goemans and Williamson's 0.878 rounding [1] turned out, under UGC, to be exactly optimal [3]; Raghavendra's theorem [4] elevated this from a single success to a universal law — every CSP's approximability is, conditionally, the integrality gap of one canonical SDP. The Lasserre hierarchy [6] is the natural candidate to make this unconditional, and its known gaps and rounding theory define the current algorithmic frontier. Arora, Barak, and Steurer's subexponential algorithm [5] showed the conjecture's instances are easier than worst-case NP-hardness suggests; the Small-Set Expansion Hypothesis [7] recast the conjecture as pure graph expansion; and the 2018 proof of the 2-to-2 Games Conjecture [8] proved half the dream. Whether the remaining half — the original 1-to-1 conjecture of Khot's 2002 paper — is true remains, after more than two decades, the most important open question in the field. Its resolution, in either direction, will reshape our understanding of efficient computation.
