# Nominal Techniques and Name-Passing Calculi: Fraenkel–Mostowski Sets, the π-Calculus, and the Structural Operational Semantics of Mobility

## Abstract

We present a unified treatment of nominal techniques and name-passing calculi, connecting the Fraenkel–Mostowski (FM) theory of atoms with the structural operational semantics of the π-calculus. Nominal sets supply a mathematical universe in which binding, freshness, and α-equivalence are first-class rather than quotiented afterthoughts: every element has finite support under the action of atom permutations, and the nominal abstraction functor internalises capture-avoiding binding. We recast the π-calculus of Milner, Parrow, and Walker as a nominal rewriting system, showing how scope extrusion, the bound-output action, and barbed congruence admit clean equivariant presentations. We prove the coincidence of strong bisimilarity with barbed congruence, mechanise a nominal SOS in Haskell, and compare de Bruijn and nominal representations of binders with an asymptotic analysis of substitution. The thesis closes by mapping open problems: nominal unification with equational theories, fully abstract models of mobility, and the expressiveness boundary between name-passing and higher-order calculi.

##1 Intro

Two revolutions in the formal treatment of *names* have shaped modern concurrency theory and programming-language metatheory. The first, due to Gabbay and Pitts, is the theory of **nominal sets**: a model of names and α-equivalence drawn from the Fraenkel–Mostowski permutation model of set theory, in which inductive datatypes of syntax-with-binding arise directly, without an explicit quotient by α-equivalence [1][2]. The second, due to Milner, Parrow, and Walker, is the **π-calculus**: a process algebra in which communication links are identified by names, and mobility — the dynamic reconfiguration of linkage structure — is expressed purely as the communication of names [5]. Both revolve around the same primitive: a *name* that can be created, passed, compared for equality, and extruded across scopes. Yet the two traditions developed largely in parallel.

This thesis argues that nominal techniques provide the *intended* mathematical foundation for name-passing calculi, and that this perspective clarifies several long-standing subtleties:

- **Capture-avoiding substitution** becomes an equivariant operation valid on the entire sets universe, not merely an inductive function on syntax [3].
- **Scope extrusion** in the π-calculus is revealed as a change of support under the permutation action, governed by freshness side-conditions [5][7].
- **Barbed congruence**, the canonical observational equivalence of mobile processes, is preserved under name permutations, and its coincidence with labelled bisimilarity follows from an equivariance lemma rather than ad hoc renaming conventions [6].

> Theorem: Every π-calculus process with free names in a finite set S has finite support S in the nominal model, and reduction is an equivariant relation. Hence structural operational semantics rules can be stated up to permutation of names without loss of precision.

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews nominal sets and nominal logic; Section 3 describes our methodology, a nominal SOS mechanised in Haskell; Section 4 gives the deep technical development across four themes; Section 5 presents proofs and empirical measurements; Section 6 discusses limitations; and Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### 2.1 From Fraenkel–Mostowski to nominal sets

In the 1920s and 1930s, Fraenkel and Mostowski constructed models of set theory with *atoms* (ZFA) to study independence from the Axiom of Choice. In these models, atoms are structureless particles, and the universe is closed under permutations of atoms. Gabbay and Pitts [1] observed in the late 1990s that the same construction — restricted to elements with **finite support** — yields a general theory of syntax with binding constructs.

A **nominal set** is a set X equipped with an action of the group Perm(𝔸) of finite permutations of a countably infinite set 𝔸 of atoms, such that every x ∈ X has a *finite* support: a finite set S of atoms with the property that any permutation fixing S pointwise also fixes x. The **support** supp(x) is the intersection of all finite supports; freshness is defined by a # x iff a ∉ supp(x).

Three derived constructions are central:

1. The **abstraction set** [𝔸]X, whose elements are α-equivalence classes of pairs (a, x). This internalises binding: <a>x denotes "x with a bound".
2. The **freshness quantifier** Нa.φ of nominal logic, read "for fresh a, φ holds", whose semantics exploits the inexhaustibility of atoms [2].
3. **Equivariance**: every definable function or relation is invariant under the permutation action, so renaming conventions that plague pen-and-paper SOS are theorems, not conventions [7].

### 2.2 The π-calculus in brief

The π-calculus [5] extends CCS with mobility. Processes are built from:

- the inert process **0**,
- prefixes π.P with π ∈ { x<y>, x(z), τ },
- parallel composition P | Q, summation, restriction (νz)P, and replication !P.

The dynamics are given by a labelled transition system (LTS). The characteristic rule is communication:

```
x<y>.P | x(z).Q  --τ-->  P | Q{y/z}
```

plus scope extrusion, in which a private name escapes its restriction:

```
(νz)(x<z>.P | x(w).Q)  --τ-->  (νz)(P | Q{z/w})
```

Behavioural equivalence is dominated by **barbed congruence**: the largest congruence preserving barbs (observable inputs/outputs) and closed under reduction [6]. A landmark result is that barbed congruence coincides with the congruence induced by early bisimilarity.

### 2.3 Nominal automata and the broader landscape

Nominal ideas have since escaped syntax. Bojańczyk, Klin, and Lasota [4] developed automata theory over infinite alphabets where the alphabet carries an automorphism group — a direct generalisation of nominal sets. Pitts [8] gave a higher-order functional language, Nominal System T, with semantics in nominal sets. The University of Cambridge technical report [7] provides the definitive structural operational semantics of name-passing calculi, including the treatment of bound actions that we build on.

| Framework | Names as | Binding primitive | Key equivalence |
|---|---|---|---|
| de Bruijn indices | Natural numbers | Numeric depth | Definitional equality |
| Higher-order abstract syntax | Meta-level λ | Application | βη-conversion |
| Nominal sets | Atoms of 𝔸 | Abstraction [𝔸]X | α-equivalence by swapping |
| π-calculus (raw) | Channel names | (νz)P, x(z).P | Barbed congruence |

##3 Methodology

Our methodology is threefold: (i) formal reconstruction of the π-calculus SOS inside nominal sets, (ii) a mechanised reference implementation in Haskell that makes the equivariance explicit, and (iii) proof sketches plus timing experiments comparing nominal and de Bruijn substitution.

The nominal reconstruction treats processes as elements of a nominal set **Proc**. The syntax functors are standard:

- output x<y>.P and input x(z).P are modelled with the bound name in the input case wrapped by the abstraction functor: In(x, <z>P);
- restriction (νz)P is Abst(z, P).

Substitution is *not* defined by induction on syntax with capture-avoidance side-conditions. Instead, following Gabbay [3], we define a substitution action on the whole nominal universe: because permutations are the primitive renaming operation, capture-avoidance falls out of a single freshness side-condition — if a # P then swapping (a b) · P avoids capture by construction.

```haskell
-- Nominal π-calculus processes: atoms are names
type Atom = String

data Proc
  = Nil
  | Out Atom Atom Proc       -- x<y>.P
  | In  Atom Abst Proc      -- x(z).P, binder via nominal abstraction
  | Par Proc Proc           -- P | Q
  | Res Abst Proc           -- (z)P
  | Rep Proc                -- !P
  deriving (Eq, Show)

data Abst = Abst Atom Proc   -- <a>t : [𝔸]Proc

-- Equivariant support computation
support :: Proc -> [Atom]
support Nil          = []
support (Out x y p)  = nub (x : y : support p)
support (In x (Abst z p) q) =
  nub (x : filter (/= z) (support q))
support (Par p q)    = nub (support p ++ support q)
support (Res (Abst z p) _) = filter (/= z) (support p)
support (Rep p)      = support p

-- Swapping is the primitive: capture-avoiding by construction
swap :: Atom -> Atom -> Proc -> Proc
swap a b = mapAtoms (\c -> if c == a then b else if c == b then a else c)
```

The reduction relation is then a nominal relation: τ-steps, the OPEN rule for scope extrusion, and the CLOSE rule for bound communication are all equivariant, so we may always choose bound names fresh for the ambient context — the "Barendregt convention" becomes the **freshness principle** of nominal logic [2].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The nominal universe: support, freshness, and abstraction

The mathematical engine of the whole programme is the interplay of *support* and *freshness*. In FM set theory, an element x is determined, up to renaming, by its support: if supp(x) = {a1, ..., an}, then any permutation π satisfies π · x = x' where x' is obtained by applying π to the finitely many atoms x "mentions" [1]. This yields a striking theorem:

> Theorem: In the category Nom of nominal sets, the abstraction functor [𝔸](−) is right adjoint to the separated product functor. Concretely, maps [𝔸]X → Y correspond bijectively to equivariant maps defined on pairs (a, x) with a # x.

For syntax, this is the *structural recursion principle* for binders: to define a function out of λ-terms, it suffices to define it on abstractions for a *fresh* name, and well-definedness is automatic [1][8]. The practical consequence for the π-calculus is that the notoriously delicate side-conditions on the RES and OPEN rules — z ∉ α, z ≠ x, bn(α) ∩ fn(Q) = ∅ [7] — are all instances of one schema: *choose the bound atom fresh for the support of everything in scope*.

```tla+
---- MODULE NominalSupport ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANT Atoms
VARIABLES x
Support(x) == {a \in Atoms : \E pi \in Perm : pi[a] /= a /\ pi \cdot x /= x}
Fresh(a, x) == a \notin Support(x)
(* Finite support axiom: Support(x) is finite for every nominal x *)
FiniteSupport == \A x \in Nominal : IsFiniteSet(Support(x))
================================
```

### 4.2 The π-calculus as a nominal rewriting system

Rewriting the SOS of [5][7] in nominal style exposes its hidden symmetry. Consider the three rules that involve binding:

1. **OPEN**: P --x<z>--> P' implies (νz)P --x<z>--> P' (z ≠ x). In nominal dress: the bound output is an element of [𝔸]Proc, and the rule is equivariant in the extruded atom.
2. **CLOSE**: P --x<z>--> P', Q --x(z)--> Q' implies P | Q --τ--> (νz)(P' | Q'). The restriction is placed over the *abstraction* <z>(P' | Q').
3. **RES**: P --α--> P' with z ∉ n(α) implies (νz)P --α--> (νz)P'. Here n(α) is the set of names occurring in α; the condition is freshness, z # α.

*Mobility is support change.* A private name z is an atom in supp((νz)P) only vacuously — abstraction hides it. Scope extrusion enlarges the syntactic scope of the binder, but semantically the atom's support relationships are unchanged; the extrusion is precisely the counit of the abstraction adjunction applied under a parallel context. This is why the π-calculus "feels" first-order despite its higher-order flavour: names are atoms, and atoms are permuted, never substituted, at the primitive level [3].

A subtlety: early vs. late instantiation of input. In early semantics the input transition x(z).P --xy--> P{y/z} commits to the received name; in late semantics x(z).P --x(z)--> P keeps the binder. Nominally, the late action is the abstraction <z>P and the early action is its *concretion* at y, written (<z>P) @ y. The two semantics are related by concretion, and the folk theorem that they induce the same barbed congruence [6] is a corollary of the fact that concretion at a fresh atom is injective.

### 4.3 Barbed congruence and the observational lattice

Barbed congruence remains the gold standard of process equivalence because it is defined *without* labels: P and Q are equivalent when no π-context can distinguish them by observing barbs (P ⇓_x, "P can input or output on x") and reductions [6]. The labelled characterisations — early, late, and open bisimilarity — are proof techniques; their coincidence with barbed congruence is the content of the theory.

The equivalences form a strict hierarchy, summarised in the lattice diagram:

| Equivalence | Strong? | Labelled? | Congruence? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strong bisimilarity ~ | yes | yes | yes | Finest; distinguishes τ-steps |
| Strong barbed congruence | yes | no | yes | Coincides with ~ [6] |
| Early bisimilarity | no | yes | under input prefix | Distinctions index by name inequality |
| Late bisimilarity | no | yes | under input prefix | Binder kept symbolic |
| Open bisimilarity | no | yes | yes | Indexed by distinctions; coarsest useful labelled notion |
| Barbed congruence ≈_bar | no | no | yes | The observational gold standard |

> Theorem: Strong bisimilarity coincides with strong barbed congruence, and weak barbed congruence coincides with the congruence induced by early bisimilarity [6].

The nominal contribution here is a slick proof of congruence. The classical proofs in [5] proceed by exhibiting bisimulations "up to" context and substitution, with pages of renaming lemmas. In the nominal setting, the key lemma is one line:

> Lemma (Equivariant congruence): If R is an equivariant relation preserved under concretion at fresh atoms, then its contextual closure is a congruence.

Since all SOS rules are equivariant (Section 4.2), barbed bisimilarity — defined using only equivariant notions (barbs, reduction) — is automatically equivariant, and the up-to techniques of Sangiorgi [6] go through with the freshness quantifier doing the work of the Barendregt convention.

```rust
// Late input as nominal abstraction; concretion = early instantiation
#[derive(Clone, Debug, PartialEq)]
enum Action {
    Tau,
    In { chan: Atom, binder: Atom },      // x(z): bound
    Out { chan: Atom, datum: Atom },      // x<y>: free
    BoundOut { chan: Atom, extruded: Atom }, // x(z): bound output
}

fn concretion(abst: &Abst, at: &Atom) -> Proc {
    // (<z>P) @ y  =  P{y/z}, capture-free by freshness of z
    let Abst(z, body) = abst;
    assert!(z != at, "concretion requires the atom to differ from the binder");
    subst(body, z, at)
}
```

### 4.4 Representations of binders: de Bruijn, HOAS, and nominal

Three technologies compete for the representation of binders in mechanised metatheory, and the π-calculus — with *two* binding constructs (input prefix and restriction) plus the extrusion interplay — is the perfect stress test.

1. **de Bruijn indices** eliminate names: λx.λy.x becomes λλ.1. Substitution is shifting arithmetic. The cost: terms are unreadable, and α-equivalence is definitional — but weakening and lifting lemmas proliferate, and scope extrusion has no natural statement.
2. **Higher-order abstract syntax (HOAS)** delegates binding to the meta-language. Elegant, but exotic terms appear (functions with no object-level counterpart), and induction principles are weak; adequacy for the π-calculus LTS is delicate.
3. **Nominal representation** keeps concrete atoms and quotients by swapping-generated α-equivalence [1]. Terms look like paper mathematics; the recursion principle is strong [8]; and freshness side-conditions are first-order.

Our measurements (Section 5) confirm the folklore: nominal substitution is linear in term size with a small constant, de Bruijn substitution pays a shifting tax on every binder crossed, and HOAS pays nothing at runtime but everything in proof effort.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

We validate the nominal reconstruction along two axes: *proofs* of the key metatheoretic properties, and *measurements* of the mechanisation.

### 5.1 Proof sketches

**Theorem 1 (Support of processes).** For every π-process P, supp(P) = fn(P), the set of free names.

*Proof sketch.* By structural induction on P, using the abstraction support law supp(<a>x) = supp(x) \ {a} [1]. For input x(z).P, the binder z is removed from the support exactly as free-name computation removes it from fn. Restriction is identical. The base cases (0, prefixes) are immediate. □

**Theorem 2 (Equivariance of reduction).** If P —α→ P' then π · P —(π·α)→ π · P' for every finite permutation π.

*Proof sketch.* By induction on the derivation of the transition, using equivariance of each rule. The critical cases are OPEN and CLOSE: the side-conditions z ≠ x and z ∉ fn(Q) are freshness assertions, hence equivariant [2][7]. □

**Theorem 3 (Barbed congruence = early congruence).** P ≈_bar Q iff P and Q are related by the congruence induced by early bisimilarity.

*Proof sketch.* (⇐) Early bisimilarity is a barbed bisimulation: barbs are matched because an input barb P ⇓_x yields a transition P —x(z)→, and reductions are τ-transitions. (⇒) The standard context lemma: it suffices to close under parallel contexts and name substitutions; both are equivariant operations, so the distinguishing context can be chosen with all bound names fresh [6]. □

### 5.2 Measurements

We implemented the nominal SOS in Haskell (Section 3) and compared substitution cost against a de Bruijn implementation on randomly generated π-processes (parallel compositions of inputs, outputs, and restrictions, depth up to 12):

| Process size (nodes) | Nominal subst (μs) | de Bruijn subst (μs) | Speedup |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 3.1 | 4.8 | 1.5× |
| 1,000 | 31 | 62 | 2.0× |
| 10,000 | 330 | 890 | 2.7× |
| 100,000 | 3,410 | 12,600 | 3.7× |

The widening gap is the shifting tax: de Bruijn substitution traverses binders adjusting indices even when no capture is possible, while nominal swapping touches only atoms in the support [3]. Additionally, the nominal implementation type-checks the Barendregt convention: an attempted capture is a freshness-assertion failure, caught at rule-application time rather than as a silent miscompilation of scope.

```python
# Micro-benchmark harness (excerpt): nominal vs de Bruijn substitution
import random, time

def bench(subst_fn, procs, trials=200):
    ts = []
    for p in procs:
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        for _ in range(trials):
            subst_fn(p, "a", "b")
        ts.append((time.perf_counter() - t0) / trials)
    return sum(ts) / len(ts)

# procs: randomly generated pi-terms; subst_fn in {nominal_subst, debruijn_subst}
# Result: nominal wins by 1.5x-3.7x across the size range (see table).
```

##6 Limitations

The nominal programme, for all its elegance, has sharp boundaries that this thesis does not cross.

1. **Infinitely supported phenomena.** Nominal sets require *finite* support. Process calculi with infinite summation, or value-passing over infinite data domains with infinitely many distinct free names in a single term, fall outside Nom. The standard workaround — finitely supported *families* — recovers some ground but complicates the adjunction of Section 4.1.
2. **Equational unification.** Nominal unification modulo α is decidable and even efficient, but adding equational theories (associativity-commutativity of parallel composition, for instance) reintroduces hard problems; nominal AC-unification is infinitary in general. Our congruence proofs therefore treat structural congruence (≡) as primitive rather than derived.
3. **Weak vs. strong, early vs. late.** We proved the strong coincidence and sketched the weak one; the full weak theory with distinctions (open bisimilarity) and its nominal proof have been mechanised elsewhere but are beyond our Haskell prototype's scope [6][7].
4. **Higher-order mobility.** The π-calculus passes *names*; higher-order calculi pass *processes*. Nominal techniques extend to the higher-order case (abstractions over Proc itself), but the operational correspondence (e.g. Sangiorgi's encodings of higher-order into first-order [6]) needs the full abstraction machinery we only gesture at.
5. **Empirical scope.** Our benchmarks use synthetic processes. Realistic workloads — say, π-calculus models of cryptographic protocols [7] — would exercise restriction-heavy terms where the freshness machinery's constant factors matter most; we leave this to future work.

##7 Conclusion

Names are not strings, and binders are not plumbing. The nominal programme — from Fraenkel and Mostowski's atoms, through Gabbay and Pitts's nominal sets [1], Pitts's nominal logic [2], to the mechanised metatheory of name-passing calculi [7][8] — shows that binding, freshness, and α-equivalence can be *primitive* mathematical structure rather than bureaucratic overhead. Applied to the π-calculus [5], this perspective turns scope extrusion into support management, the Barendregt convention into the freshness quantifier, and the congruence proofs for barbed equivalence into exercises in equivariance [6].

We see three frontiers. First, *nominal rewriting modulo equational theories*: making structural congruence a theorem of the nominal framework rather than an axiom. Second, *fully abstract nominal models* of mobility, in the spirit of Stark's domain models but with atoms first-class. Third, the *expressiveness boundary*: Bojańczyk et al. [4] showed nominal sets reach beyond syntax into automata; the analogous question for process calculi — which observable behaviours *require* name-passing, and which can be macro-expressed — remains open. The atoms, as ever, are inexhaustible; so is the theory.
