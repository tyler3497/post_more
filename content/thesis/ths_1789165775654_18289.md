# Cache Replacement at Scale: Belady's Optimal Algorithm, ARC Adaptivity, and Machine-Learned Policies from Hawkeye to LeCar

## Abstract

Cache replacement is the last scheduling decision in the memory hierarchy: when a miss forces a line into a full cache, the choice of which resident line to evict determines how many future accesses become misses. Since Belady proved in 1966 that the offline-optimal policy evicts the block whose next reference lies farthest in the future, the field has pursued two complementary strategies: *online self-adaptation*, exemplified by the Adaptive Replacement Cache (ARC), which learns the recency–frequency balance of the workload without a priori tuning; and *machine-learned emulation of the optimal*, exemplified by Hawkeye, Glider, and LeCar, which reconstruct Belady's decisions from history and train hardware-feasible predictors to imitate them. This thesis unifies these three eras — optimal theory, adaptive heuristics, and learned policies — tracing why the program counter became the dominant predictive feature and where learned replacement still fails: adversarial drift, phase behavior, and the nanosecond economics of hardware inference. We argue that the frontier is no longer better prediction in isolation, but predictors whose guarantees degrade gracefully when the future stops resembling the past.

## 1. Introduction

Every cache is a bet against the future. An LLC miss can cost two hundred cycles, and the *replacement policy* — the rule selecting a victim on a miss — is one of the highest-leverage microarchitectural decisions in a processor, studied continuously for nearly sixty years [1].

The classical framing is the *paging problem*: a cache holds $k$ pages, a request sequence $\sigma$ arrives online, and each request hits or misses. In 1966, László Belady derived the optimal *offline* rule — MIN, also called OPT — which evicts the page whose next reference lies farthest in the future, and proved no clairvoyant algorithm can do better [1].

Yet worst-case optimality is not practical optimality: LRU fails on *scans* (one-time references evicting the working set) and *thrashing* (a cyclic set slightly larger than the cache yields zero hits). The first response was *adaptivity*: the Adaptive Replacement Cache (ARC) of Megiddo and Modha keeps ghost lists of evicted pages and uses their hit patterns to rebalance recency versus frequency online, matching the best workload-tuned policy with no tuning at all [2].

The second response is *learning*. Researchers found that the load PC correlates strongly with reuse, and that OPT — simulated on past references — provides perfect training labels. Hawkeye [5] reconstructs OPT with OPTgen and trains a PC-indexed classifier; Glider [6] distills an attention-LSTM's insights into an on-chip integer SVM; LeCar [7] treats LRU and LFU as experts and learns via regret minimization which to trust. All three attempt to compute the uncomputable: the future.

Section 2 formalizes the paging model; Section 3 the evaluation methodology; Section 4 the technical core — Belady, ARC, the RRIP–Hawkeye lineage, Glider and LeCar; Section 5 results and proofs; Section 6 limitations; Section 7 conclusions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The paging model

Consider a two-level memory hierarchy with a fast cache of capacity $k$ pages and an unbounded slow memory. The request sequence $\sigma$ is processed under *demand paging*: a page enters the cache only when requested and found absent. A policy is *online* if its eviction decision at time $t$ depends only on $r_1 \dots r_t$; it is *offline* (clairvoyant) if it may consult the entire future.

> **Theorem (Belady, 1966).** Among all demand-paging policies for a fixed sequence $\sigma$, MIN — which on each miss evicts the resident page whose next reference is farthest in the future (or never) — minimizes the total number of misses. [1]

### 2.2 Classical policies and their pathologies

| Policy | Principle | Strength | Fatal weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LRU** | Evict least recently used | Simple, $k$-competitive | Scan pollution, thrashing |
| **LFU** | Evict least frequently used | Captures hot working sets | Stale frequency; slow adaptation |
| **FIFO** | Evict oldest resident | Minimal state | *Belady's anomaly*: larger cache can yield more misses |
| **CLOCK** | One-bit LRU approximation | Cheap hardware | Same qualitative failures as LRU |
| **OPT/MIN** | Evict farthest next use | Optimal (offline only) | Requires perfect future knowledge |

Two access patterns expose the classical policies' weakness:

- **Scans.** A one-time sequential pass touches each block exactly once. LRU inserts every block at the MRU position, flushing the reusable working set — *scan pollution*.
- **Thrashing.** A cyclic pattern over $k+1$ pages in a $k$-way cache yields a hit ratio of exactly zero under LRU, while retaining any $k$ of the $k+1$ pages would yield $k/(k+1)$ hits.

Belady's *anomaly* — FIFO's miss count is not monotone in cache size — shows that "more cache is always better" fails for naive policies; LRU and OPT are *stack algorithms* with monotone miss counts, a property exploited by set-sampling techniques [3].

### 2.3 Competitive analysis

Sleator and Tarjan's *competitive ratio* compares online policies against OPT: an algorithm is $c$-competitive if its cost is at most $c$ times OPT's on every sequence. Their landmarks: LRU and FIFO are $k$-competitive, *no* deterministic online algorithm beats $k$, and randomization buys only $O(\log k)$. This gives LRU a minimax certificate — but one measured against the worst case, not the workload in front of it. The empirical project of the next two decades was to beat LRU's *average* behavior while keeping its worst-case dignity — the path that led to ARC [2].

## 3. Methodology

Modern research evaluates by *trace-driven simulation*: **ChampSim** (the vehicle of both Cache Replacement Championships), **ZSim**, and **gem5** replay recorded address/PC streams under competing policies, using **SPEC CPU2006/2017** and **GAP** workloads, with multi-programmed mixes for shared caches.

Key methodological devices include:

1. **Metrics.** Misses per kilo-instruction (MPKI), hit ratio, and end-to-end speedup (IPC); LLC miss reductions translate nonlinearly to performance because misses overlap and prefetching interferes.
2. **Set sampling and set dueling.** Simulating OPT on all sets is infeasible; sampling 32–64 sets with a long history suffices, because per-set behavior is statistically representative [4][5].
3. **Scale.** LLCs of 1–2 MB per core, 16-way associativity; history windows of $8\times$ cache size for OPT reconstruction [4].
4. **Significance.** Championship scoring uses weighted miss-rate reduction versus LRU across a hidden trace set, guarding against overfitting to public traces.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Belady's MIN: the oracle at the center of everything

Belady's MIN is unimplementable — yet the most *used* algorithm in cache research, because MIN is computable *in retrospect*: simulating it on a recorded trace yields perfect labels, and the gap to MIN measures the irreducible regret of not knowing the future. Every learned policy in this thesis approximates MIN's decisions from observable correlates.

Jain and Lin made this explicit with **OPTgen** [4]: a retrospective OPT simulation using *occupancy vectors* — for each access, the count of distinct blocks touched since its previous access; blocks whose occupancy exceeds the cache capacity would have missed under OPT. Applied to 64 sampled sets with an $8\times$-cache history, OPTgen reproduces OPT's decisions for about 12 KB of state on a 2 MB cache; because repeated accesses collapse the history, 2,400 sampler entries suffice instead of the worst-case 260 K [5].

The philosophical import: *the optimal labels are free*. Unlike domains where supervision is expensive, cache replacement generates its own ground truth from the past. This is why imitation learning — training a predictor to copy MIN — dominates the field, and why the interesting question is never "what is optimal?" but "which cheap features predict optimality?"

```python
def belady_min(cache, request, future):
    """Oracle eviction: evict the resident block with the farthest next use."""
    if request in cache:
        return None  # hit
    if len(cache) < CAPACITY:
        return request  # cold miss, no eviction
    victim = max(cache, key=lambda b: future.next_use(b))  # +inf if never
    return victim
```

### 4.2 ARC: ghost lists and the recency–frequency continuum

ARC [2] starts from a diagnosis: LRU is pure recency, LFU pure frequency, and workloads need a *mixture* that changes over time — learned online from ARC's own mistakes rather than tuned offline.

ARC maintains a cache directory of $2c$ entries for a $c$-page cache: two *resident* LRU lists and two *ghost* (history) lists of metadata only:

- **$T_1$**: pages seen exactly once recently — the *recency* list.
- **$T_2$**: pages seen at least twice — the *frequency* list.
- **$B_1$, $B_2$**: ghost lists of pages recently evicted from $T_1$ and $T_2$.

The adaptive parameter $p \in [0, c]$ is the target size of $T_1$. The learning rule is disarmingly simple:

1. **Hit in $B_1$** (a one-timer we evicted is wanted again): the workload values recency more than assumed → *increase* $p$.
2. **Hit in $B_2$** (a frequent page we evicted is wanted again): the workload values frequency more → *decrease* $p$.
3. **Miss on a new page**: evict from $T_1$ if $|T_1| > p$, else from $T_2$; scan-like streams flush through $T_1$ without disturbing $T_2$'s hot pages — *scan resistance* emerges from the structure, not from a heuristic patch.

Ghost-list hits are "phantom hits" that steer $p$ even though they cost a real miss, so ARC effectively manages a virtual cache of $2c$ while paying for $c$. Megiddo and Modha showed this makes ARC *empirically universal* — matching the best fixed recency/frequency blend chosen with hindsight per workload — at $O(1)$ per request [2]. On 23 real traces the gains are dramatic: on an SPC1-like benchmark at 4 GB cache, LRU achieves 9.19% hit ratio versus ARC's 20% [2]; variants shipped in IBM's DS6000/DS8000 controllers, Solaris ZFS, and PostgreSQL. ARC's residual weakness is its two-list coarseness: multi-modal reuse distances can strand it between its poles — the gap that re-reference interval prediction was designed to fill.

### 4.3 From RRIP to Hawkeye: predicting reuse, then learning the oracle

**RRIP (2010).** Jaleel, Theobald, Steely, and Emer reframed every replacement policy as a *predictor of re-reference interval*: LRU predicts the least-recently-used block is reused farthest in the future; policies differ only in how they form this prediction [3]. RRIP attaches a 2-bit *re-reference prediction value* (RRPV) to each line: insertions are predicted *distant*, hits *promote* the line toward near-term reuse, and victims are chosen among maximal-RRPV lines, aging all counters when none qualifies. SRRIP is scan-resistant but thrashes when the working set exceeds the cache; BRRIP inserts most lines as distant to survive thrashing; **DRRIP** uses *set dueling* — dedicating a few sets to each variant and letting a saturating counter pick the winner — to choose adaptively [3]. RRIP ships in Intel processors from Ivy Bridge onward, making it the most widely deployed advanced policy in history — and the substrate Hawkeye builds on.

**SHiP (2011)** added the crucial feature insight: the *signature* of a memory access — its program counter, memory region, or instruction sequence — predicts its reuse. Wu et al.'s Signature-based Hit Predictor correlated PC-indexed signatures with outcomes, learning which loads stream dead blocks [6].

**Hawkeye (2017)** combined both threads: OPTgen supplies perfect retrospective labels, and a hardware predictor learns to imitate them [5]. Its three components are: **OPTgen** on 64 sampled sets, reconstructing whether OPT would have hit or missed each sampled access; a **sampler** recording (address tag, load PC, timestamp); and the **Hawkeye predictor** — 2,048 entries of 5-bit saturating counters indexed by an 11-bit hashed PC, trained positively when OPTgen reports a hit and negatively otherwise. At replacement time, the predictor classifies lines as *cache-friendly* (insert with RRIP 0) or *cache-averse* (insert with RRIP 7, evict first), for ~12 KB of overhead on a 2 MB LLC [5]. Hawkeye won the 2nd Cache Replacement Championship (2017) [5].

The architectural lesson: *decompose learning into an expensive oracle off the critical path and a cheap classifier on it* — the split Glider pushes to its limit.

### 4.4 Glider and LeCar: two faces of machine-learned replacement

**Glider (MICRO 2019): supervised deep learning, distilled.** Shi, Huang, Jain, and Lin asked whether deep learning could design a better *hardware* predictor [6]. The obstacle is fundamental: neural networks are milliseconds and megabytes; LLC decisions are nanoseconds and kilobytes. Their two-stage pipeline: (1) *offline*, an *attention-based LSTM* is trained on traces to classify lines as cache-friendly or averse, beating all prior hardware predictors and proving the signal is learnable; (2) attention weights reveal the LSTM's key features — PC–reuse correlations and address-region structure — which are distilled into **Glider**'s online predictor, an *integer SVM*: orders of magnitude cheaper, matching the LSTM's accuracy. On 33 memory-intensive programs from SPEC CPU2006, SPEC CPU2017, and GAP, Glider outperformed the CRC2 top finishers [6]. The methodological contribution: *use deep learning as a design tool, not a deployment artifact* — let the LSTM discover the features, then compile the discovery into hardware.

**LeCar (HotStorage 2018): online regret minimization over two experts.** Vietri et al. took the opposite route: no offline training, no PC features, no oracle simulation — just **LRU** and **LFU** as *experts*, with a probability distribution over them updated by *regret minimization* whenever an eviction proves wrong [7]. On each miss an expert is sampled by weight; when its victim is re-referenced, the expert's weight decays exponentially. This two-expert learner *outperforms ARC*, and the gap *widens* as the cache shrinks relative to the working set — the realistic operating point for buffer caches [7].

```rust
// LeCar, simplified: exponential weights over two eviction experts.
struct LeCar { w_lru: f64, w_lfu: f64, lambda: f64 }

impl LeCar {
    fn evict(&mut self, lru_victim: Page, lfu_victim: Page) -> Page {
        let p = self.w_lru / (self.w_lru + self.w_lfu);
        if rand::random::<f64>() < p { lru_victim } else { lfu_victim }
    }
    fn regret(&mut self, expert_was_lru: bool) {
        // The chosen expert evicted a page that was re-referenced:
        if expert_was_lru { self.w_lru *= (-self.lambda).exp(); }
        else { self.w_lfu *= (-self.lambda).exp(); }
    }
}
```

The contrast is instructive. Glider invests in *representation* — rich features, an expensive offline teacher — and deploys a fixed distilled predictor. LeCar invests in *adaptation* — no features, but continuous online reweighting. Hawkeye sits between: oracle labels offline, PC-table learning online. ARC is LeCar's spiritual ancestor: two experts (recency, frequency) reweighted by regret, implemented with ghost lists instead of exponentiated weights.

| Policy | Learning signal | Features | Online cost | Era |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ARC [2] | Ghost-list hits | Recency/frequency only | $O(1)$ | 2003, adaptive |
| RRIP/DRRIP [3] | Set-dueling outcome | 2-bit RRPV | $O(1)$ | 2010, predictive |
| Hawkeye [5] | OPTgen labels | 11-bit hashed PC | Table lookup | 2017, oracle-imitation |
| Glider [6] | LSTM → ISVM distillation | PC + address region | ISVM eval | 2019, deep-learned |
| LeCar [7] | Regret (wrong evictions) | None (LRU/LFU experts) | $O(1)$ | 2018, bandit-style |

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 How close to optimal?

The central question is the *optimality gap* — the fraction of MIN's avoidable misses each policy eliminates (single-core 2 MB LLCs, normalized to LRU):

- **RRIP/DRRIP** closes roughly half the LRU→OPT gap on SPEC CPU2006, with the largest wins on scan-heavy and thrashing workloads [3].
- **Hawkeye** won CRC2 (2017), beating SHiP and the RRIP family; **Glider** then reduced misses beyond the CRC2 top finishers on 33 SPEC/GAP programs — the first proof that deep-learned features transfer to hardware-feasible predictors [5][6].
- An independent comparison reports single-core speedups over LRU of 32.93% (SHiP), 34.56% (Hawkeye), and 34.43% (Glider) on 27 SPEC CPU2006/2017 programs — Glider and Hawkeye statistically tied at the top [8].
- For software caches, **LeCar** beats ARC on file-system and database traces, with the margin growing as the cache-to-working-set ratio shrinks [7].

### 5.2 Proof sketch: optimality of MIN

Fix $\sigma$ and consider any optimal offline schedule $S$. Process misses chronologically; let $t$ be the first miss where $S$ evicts $x$ but MIN evicts $y \neq x$ (so $\text{next}(y) \geq \text{next}(x)$). Construct $S'$ identical to $S$ except evicting $y$ at $t$. Until $\min(\text{next}(x),\text{next}(y))$, both schedules behave identically: requests to other pages hit in both, and a request to $y$ before $\text{next}(x)$ is impossible by definition of next-use. At $\text{next}(x)$, $S'$ still holds $x$ and hits; $S$ must evict *something* at its own next miss, and a symmetric exchange shows $S'$ costs no more than $S$. Iterating the exchange converts $S$ into MIN's schedule without increasing total misses. Hence no policy beats MIN. ∎ [1]

### 5.3 Competitive bounds

Sleator–Tarjan: LRU and FIFO are $k$-competitive, and no deterministic online algorithm beats $k$ [1]. Potential-function analyses extend this to adaptive policies — ARC is $4N$-competitive, CAR $21N$-competitive — proving adaptivity keeps a worst-case certificate [9]. The bounds are loose in practice (real gaps are percents, not factors of $N$), but they rule out pathological sequences that could make adaptive policies collapse — the open question the analysis settled [9].

## 6. Limitations

1. **The oracle is retrospective.** OPTgen labels the *past*; Hawkeye assumes the future resembles it. Under *phase changes* — e.g., switching from graph traversal to streaming — correlations learned in one phase mispredict the next, and retraining lags behind; no published policy bounds the transient regret.
2. **Adversarial and multi-tenant settings.** In shared LLCs, a co-running thread's stream is *adversarial noise* from any single program's perspective. PC-indexed predictors alias across contexts with no isolation guarantees.
3. **Hardware economics of inference.** Glider's LSTM never ships; the ISVM is the product. Every learned policy must fit its predictor in kilobytes and its decision in a few cycles — a constraint that rules out the most accurate models by construction, making "deep learning for caches" really "deep learning for cache-policy *design*" [6].
4. **Feature fragility.** The PC is predictive because compilers and allocators are stable within a binary — but JITs and layout shifts perturb exactly the correlations predictors rely on. LeCar's feature-free design is immune, at the cost of coarser decisions [7].
5. **Uniform-size assumption.** Belady, ARC, and OPTgen all assume uniform line sizes; real hierarchies mix 64 B lines, huge pages, and compressed blocks, and variable-size paging remains unaddressed.
6. **Evaluation blind spots.** Traces assume the memory stream is policy-independent, but prefetchers and out-of-order cores reshape it in response to miss latency — feedback that traces cannot capture.

## 7. Conclusion

Sixty years after Belady, the field has come full circle: the optimal algorithm is unimplementable, so we simulate it on the past and learn to imitate it in the present. ARC learned its recency–frequency balance online with no tuning [2]; RRIP showed two bits per line beat LRU in silicon [3]; Hawkeye learned OPT's decisions from the program counter [5]; Glider distilled deep-learned features into hardware [6]; LeCar beat a tuned adaptive policy with two experts and exponential weights [7].

The pattern across all five is that *the teacher is always Belady* — directly as MIN, retrospectively as OPTgen, or implicitly as the regret signal. The open frontier is not a better oracle but a more honest student: predictors with calibrated uncertainty, phase-aware forgetting, tenant isolation, and graceful degradation when the future diverges from the past. Until then, the humble ghost list remains the most deployed idea in the field — a reminder that in systems, the best learning algorithm is often the one that fits in the hardware you already have.
