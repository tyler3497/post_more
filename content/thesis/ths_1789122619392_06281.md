# Log-Structured Merge Trees: Compaction Strategies, Bloom Filter Optimization, and the Write Amplification Tradeoff

## Abstract

The Log-Structured Merge-tree (LSM-tree) remains the dominant write-optimized index structure for modern key-value storage, yet its fundamental pathology — *write amplification* — dictates device lifetime, tail latency, and operating cost on flash media. This thesis develops a unified treatment of three levers that govern the amplification trilemma: compaction strategies, Bloom filter allocation, and key-value separation. We derive closed-form bounds for leveled, tiered, and lazy-leveled merging, proving why leveled compaction at size ratio T=10 can rewrite each byte up to 50 times before it settles; we formalize the Monkey scheme's exponentially increasing per-level Bloom filter allocation that reduces worst-case zero-result lookup I/O from O(L·e^{-M/N}) to O(e^{-M/N}); and we analyze WiscKey's value-log separation and PebblesDB's fragmented LSM guards as orthogonal attacks on amplification. Synthesizing published microbenchmarks with a Python cost-model simulation, we quantify the read/write/space amplification tradeoffs and expose the residual limitations: GC overhead, range-scan penalties, and workload-adaptive tuning.

## 1 Introduction

When Patrick O'Neil and colleagues introduced the log-structured merge-tree in 1996, the target was a transaction-processing history table whose insert rate had made B-tree maintenance prohibitively expensive [1]. Their insight was disarmingly simple: *defer and batch* index changes, cascading them from a memory-resident component through a hierarchy of increasingly large disk components in a manner reminiscent of merge sort. Three decades later, this out-of-place discipline underlies LevelDB, RocksDB, Cassandra, HBase, and the engines surveyed in [2].

The price of this write efficiency is **write amplification (WA)**: the ratio of bytes physically written to the device to bytes logically written by the application. Every key-value pair flushed from the memtable is re-merged — re-read, re-sorted, re-written — once per level it traverses. On solid-state drives, where each NAND cell tolerates a bounded number of program/erase cycles, WA is not an abstraction; it is the direct multiplier on device wear, dollar cost, and the tail latency spikes that compaction storms inflict on foreground traffic.

This thesis makes four contributions:

1. A **closed-form taxonomy** of compaction strategies — leveled, tiered, lazy-leveled, and fragmented — with their asymptotic write, read, and space amplification bounds.
2. A **formal treatment of optimal Bloom filter allocation** across LSM levels (the Monkey scheme [3]), showing that exponentially increasing bits-per-key per level minimizes worst-case lookup I/O under a fixed memory budget.
3. An analysis of **structural alternatives** — WiscKey's key-value separation [6] and PebblesDB's fragmented LSM [5] — that attack amplification at its root rather than tuning its constants.
4. A **Python cost-model simulation** plus synthesized empirical results from published evaluations, quantifying each design's position on the read/write/space amplification Pareto frontier.

We proceed from the classical design (§2), through methodology (§3) and a deep technical dive (§4), to empirical validation (§5), limitations (§6), and conclusions (§7).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The classical design

An LSM-tree comprises two component classes [1]. The **memory component** (memtable, typically a skip list or B-tree) absorbs inserts, updates, and deletes; deletes materialize as *tombstones* (anti-matter entries). A write-ahead log (WAL) provides durability. When the memtable fills, it is frozen and flushed to disk as an immutable **sorted string table (SSTable)**, a sorted run of key-value pairs. Disk components are organized into **levels** L₀, L₁, …, L_{L−1} whose target capacities grow geometrically by the **size ratio** T (conventionally T = 10):

| Symbol | Meaning | Typical value |
|---|---|---|
| F | Memtable / buffer size | 64 MB |
| T | Size ratio between adjacent levels | 10 |
| L | Number of levels ≈ ⌈log_T(N·e/F)⌉ | 5–7 |
| e | Entry (key+value) size | 1 KB |
| N | Total entries | 10⁸–10⁹ |
| m | Bloom filter bits per key | 10 |
| k | Bloom filter hash functions | 7 |

When level i exceeds its capacity, a **compaction** merges its sorted runs into level i+1. Disk components are immutable, so lookups consult every level newest-to-oldest, reconciling duplicates by recency. Each SSTable carries a **Bloom filter**: a negative answer skips it; a positive answer costs a disk probe [3].

### 2.2 The amplification trilemma

Every LSM design is judged on three coupled metrics [2]:

- **Write amplification (WA):** physical bytes written ÷ logical bytes inserted. Determined by how often each byte is re-merged.
- **Read amplification (RA):** SSTables probed per lookup. Determined by the number of sorted runs and Bloom filter accuracy.
- **Space amplification (SA):** physical bytes stored ÷ live bytes. Determined by obsolete versions awaiting compaction.

> **Theorem: The Amplification Trilemma.** For a fixed size ratio T and memory budget M, no LSM design can simultaneously achieve minimal write amplification, minimal read amplification, and minimal space amplification; improving one beyond the leveling/tiering envelope strictly degrades at least one of the others. This follows from the co-tuning constraint proved in [3]: lookup cost, update cost, and memory footprint are linked through the merge policy, buffer size, and per-level false positive rates, and all classical designs sit strictly inside the optimal frontier.

### 2.3 Merge policies in production

**Leveled compaction** (LevelDB, RocksDB default) keeps each level ≥ L₁ as a single sorted run with non-overlapping SSTables; a compaction merges a handful of files from level i with all overlapping files in level i+1. **Tiered compaction** (Cassandra's STCS, RocksDB universal compaction) allows up to T sorted runs per level and merges all T into one larger run when full. The compaction design space between and beyond these poles has been systematically mapped in [7], including hybrid and partial-compaction variants.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis combines **closed-form asymptotic modeling** with a **discrete cost-model simulation** in Python, cross-checked against published microbenchmarks from the primary sources.

*Analytic model.* We treat the LSM-tree as a parameterized family (F, T, L, merge policy, per-level Bloom filter allocation m_i) and derive expected I/O per operation. Write amplification for leveled compaction follows from counting how many times the average byte is re-merged as it descends: at each level, an entry participates in roughly T/2 merges of average size before reaching the next level, yielding WA ≈ (T/2)·L. For tiered compaction, each level's T runs are merged wholesale once full, yielding WA ≈ T·L in the worst case — but with T times fewer sorted runs to probe.

*Bloom filter model.* With m bits per key and k = m·ln 2 hash functions, the optimal false positive rate is f = (1/2)^k ≈ 0.6185^m. The expected I/O cost of a zero-result point lookup is R = Σᵢ f_i (one probe per false positive across levels). Under a total memory budget Σ n_i·m_i ≤ M, minimizing R is a convex optimization whose solution allocates bits unevenly — the Monkey result [3].

*Simulation.* We implement the Monkey allocation (§4.3) numerically and sweep T ∈ {2, 4, 10, 16}, verifying that the analytic optimum matches the simulated minimum, and we tabulate the published empirical measurements from [3], [5], and [6] for the amplification comparison.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Leveled Compaction: The Write Amplification Floor

Leveled compaction is the read-optimized extreme. Because every level beyond L₀ is a single sorted run, a point lookup probes at most one SSTable per level (after Bloom filter screening), giving the best possible read amplification for a given T. The cost is paid on the write path.

Consider a byte arriving at level i. Level i's capacity is T times that of level i−1. Compaction picks a small key-range from level i and merges it with *all overlapping data* in level i+1. Since level i+1 is roughly T times larger, each compaction at level i rewrites on the order of T times the data that triggered it — but that trigger data is only 1/T of level i's content, so each byte at level i is re-merged approximately T/2 times before descending. Cascading over L levels:

> **Theorem: Leveled Write Amplification.** Under leveled compaction with size ratio T and L levels, the expected write amplification satisfies WA = Θ(T·L). For the canonical RocksDB configuration (T = 10, L = 6 data levels plus L₀), this yields WA ≈ 30–50 in the worst case: every logical byte is physically rewritten dozens of times [2][7].

This bound is tight and workload-independent: even a perfectly sequential insert stream pays it, because the merging is structural. It motivates every alternative in this thesis.

### 4.2 Tiered and Lazy-Leveled Merging

Tiered compaction inverts the tradeoff. Each level accumulates up to T sorted runs; only when all T are present are they merged into a single run at the next level. Write amplification drops: each byte is merged exactly once per level (T runs → 1 run), for WA ≈ L — but the read path now faces up to T sorted runs per level, and space amplification grows because obsolete versions linger across T runs awaiting the wholesale merge.

**Lazy leveling** (Dostoevsky) applies tiering at all levels *except the largest*, which uses leveling. The largest level holds the dominant fraction (1 − 1/T) of all data, so leveling it keeps worst-case lookup cost near the leveled optimum while tiering the smaller levels removes the superfluous merging that dominates WA. The analytic result is striking: lazy leveling achieves WA ≈ (T−1)·L/z + … — asymptotically *less than half* the write amplification of pure leveling — while point-lookup cost degrades by only a small constant factor, and the design exposes a continuous knob (how many of the largest levels are leveled) that traces the Pareto frontier between the two extremes [3][7].

RocksDB's *universal compaction* and Cassandra's strategies are points on this continuum; [7] shows the optimal policy is workload-dependent and no static policy dominates.

### 4.3 Monkey: Optimal Bloom Filter Allocation

Bloom filters are the LSM-tree's primary defense against read amplification, but every production system historically configured them *uniformly*: the same bits-per-key at every level. Monkey [3] proves this is asymptotically suboptimal and derives the optimal allocation.

The key observation: a zero-result lookup's expected I/O is R = Σᵢ f_i, the sum of false positive rates across levels, while the memory budget is Σᵢ n_i·m_i ≤ M. Because level i holds T times more keys than level i−1, a bit of filter memory spent at a *deeper* level protects against probing a much larger SSTable — yet deeper levels are probed only when all shallower filters report (falsely) positive. Minimizing Σ f_i subject to the budget is convex; the optimum sets the FPRs in geometric decay:

> **Theorem (Monkey optimal allocation).** Let f_i be the false positive rate of the Bloom filter at level i and n_i the number of keys at that level. Under Σ n_i·m_i ≤ M with f_i = 0.6185^{m_i}, the allocation minimizing Σᵢ f_i assigns exponentially increasing bits-per-key to deeper levels, with m_{i+1} − m_i = ln(T)/ln(1/0.6185) ≈ 4.79 bits/key for T = 10. The optimal FPRs satisfy f_i ∝ T^{−i}, and the worst-case zero-result lookup cost drops from O(L·e^{−M/N}) under uniform allocation to O(e^{−M/N}) — an exponential improvement in the number of levels.

*Proof sketch.* Form the Lagrangian ℒ = Σᵢ 0.6185^{m_i} + λ(Σᵢ n_i m_i − M). First-order conditions give n_i·0.6185^{m_i}·ln(0.6185) = −λ·n_i, i.e., the marginal FPR reduction per bit is equalized — but the *levels'* contributions enter through the constraint weights n_i ∝ T^i, forcing m_i to grow linearly in i. The sum Σ f_i then becomes a convergent geometric series rather than L equal terms. ∎

Empirically [3] (atop RocksDB), Monkey cuts lookup latency **50–80%** versus uniform 10-bits/key, with the margin growing in N — exactly as predicted. We reproduce the allocation numerically:

```python
import math

PHI = 0.6185  # optimal Bloom filter: f = PHI ** (bits per key)

def monkey_allocation(n_keys, T, budget_bits):
    """Optimal per-level bits/key under a fixed filter memory budget."""
    levels = [n for n in n_keys if n > 0]
    delta = math.log(T) / math.log(1.0 / PHI)  # extra bits/key per deeper level
    lo, hi = 0.0, budget_bits / max(levels)
    for _ in range(80):  # bisection on the base rate m_1
        m1 = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        used = sum(n * (m1 + i * delta) for i, n in enumerate(levels))
        lo, hi = (lo, m1) if used > budget_bits else (m1, hi)
    alloc = [lo + i * delta for i in range(len(levels))]
    fprs = [PHI ** m for m in alloc]
    return alloc, fprs

keys = [10**6 * 10**i for i in range(5)]  # T = 10, five levels
alloc, fprs = monkey_allocation(keys, T=10, budget_bits=2**31)
print("bits/key per level:", [round(m, 2) for m in alloc])
print("FPR per level:      ", [f"{f:.2e}" for f in fprs])
print("worst-case extra I/Os per zero-result lookup:", round(sum(fprs), 3))
```

Bits-per-key rise by ≈4.79 per level while the summed FPR converges to a constant; a uniform allocation instead spreads the budget into L mediocre filters whose FPRs add linearly.

### 4.4 Key-Value Separation and the Value Log

All compaction strategies above share a structural inefficiency: they re-merge *entire key-value pairs*, even though only the keys participate in sorting. WiscKey [6] observes that in real workloads values dwarf keys (e.g., 16-byte keys with 1 KB values), so compaction's I/O is dominated by dead weight. The remedy is **key-value separation**: the LSM-tree stores only keys plus small metadata (a pointer into an append-only **value log**, or vLog); values are written once to the vLog and never re-merged.

Write amplification then scales with the *key* size rather than the pair size — a reduction proportional to the value-to-key size ratio. The published results are dramatic: **2.5×–111× faster database loads** than LevelDB and **1.6×–14× faster random lookups** across microbenchmarks and all six YCSB workloads [6]. The smaller LSM-tree also caches better, compounding the read benefit.

Separation is not free. Three costs appear: (i) **garbage collection** — the vLog accumulates invalid values that must be reclaimed by an online, lightweight GC without stalling foreground I/O; (ii) **range scans** degrade because values lose their sorted order, requiring parallel prefetch designs to exploit SSD internal parallelism; (iii) point lookups pay an extra pointer-chase I/O to fetch the value. RocksDB's BlobDB instead separates at flush time (full pairs stay in the memtable, favoring reads) rather than WiscKey's pre-memtable separation (favoring writes) [2].

### 4.5 Fragmented LSM: Guards Against Rewriting

PebblesDB [5] attacks write amplification from a different angle: *stop rewriting data within the same level*. Its **Fragmented LSM-tree (FLSM)**, inspired by skip lists, introduces **guards** — a sparse set of pivot keys partitioning each level. SSTables (*sables*) are assigned to guards by key range, and compaction merges a sable only with overlapping sables of the *next* level's corresponding guard, never rewriting an entire level. Because guards overlap probabilistically like skip-list towers, the structure avoids the pathological full-level merges of leveled compaction while preserving its read efficiency.

The evaluation shows **2.4–3× lower write amplification than RocksDB** and **6.7× higher write throughput** on write-intensive microbenchmarks; as a drop-in LevelDB replacement inside MongoDB and HyperDex, YCSB throughput rises 18–105% while write I/O falls 35–55% [5]. The cost: small range queries on fully compacted stores pay a penalty, since fragmentation leaves more overlapping sables to reconcile — the read/write tradeoff reasserting itself in a new guise.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The amplification comparison

Synthesizing the analytic bounds (§4) with published measurements yields the following comparison (T = 10, L = 6; WA normalized per logical byte):

| Design | Write amplification | Zero-result point lookup (I/O) | Space amplification | Representative system |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leveled + uniform Bloom filters | Θ(T·L) ≈ 30–50 | O(L·e^{−M/N}) | ≈ 1 + 1/T | RocksDB, LevelDB [2] |
| Tiered + uniform Bloom filters | Θ(T·L)/c ≈ 10–20 | O(T·L·e^{−M/N}) | ≈ T | Cassandra STCS [7] |
| Lazy-leveled + Monkey filters | < ½ leveled WA | O(e^{−M/N}) | ≈ 1 + 1/T | Dostoevsky/Monkey [3] |
| Key-value separation | ∝ key size, not pair size | +1 vLog probe | vLog GC overhead | WiscKey, BlobDB [6] |
| Fragmented LSM (guards) | 2.4–3× below RocksDB | ≈ leveled | ≈ leveled | PebblesDB [5] |

### 5.2 Measured results from the literature

| Study | Workload | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| Monkey on RocksDB [3] | Zero-result lookups, growing N | 50–80% latency reduction vs uniform filters |
| PebblesDB vs RocksDB [5] | Write-intensive microbenchmarks | 2.4–3× lower WA, 6.7× write throughput |
| PebblesDB in MongoDB/HyperDex [5] | YCSB | 18–105% throughput gain, 35–55% less write I/O |
| WiscKey vs LevelDB [6] | DB load | 2.5×–111× faster |
| WiscKey vs LevelDB/RocksDB [6] | YCSB A–F, random lookups | 1.6×–14× faster; wins all six workloads |
| LSM stability study [8] | Compaction-induced variance | Merge policy dominates tail-latency variance |

> **Theorem: Monkey dominance.** For any fixed memory budget M and size ratio T > 1, the Monkey allocation's worst-case zero-result lookup cost is asymptotically strictly less than that of any uniform allocation, and the gap grows without bound as the number of levels L → ∞. *Proof.* Uniform allocation gives R_unif = L·0.6185^{M/N}; Monkey gives R_monkey = 0.6185^{m_1}·T/(T−1) with m_1 chosen so the budget binds. Since R_unif/R_monkey = Θ(L) → ∞, the claim follows [3]. ∎

> **Theorem: FLSM write-amplification bound.** In a fragmented LSM-tree with guard probability p, the expected number of times a key is rewritten within a level is bounded by a constant independent of the level size, versus Θ(T) under leveled compaction; hence total expected WA is reduced by a factor approaching the guard fan-out [5].

## 6 Limitations

**Model assumptions.** The Monkey derivation assumes uniformly random hashing, a known static key distribution, and a fixed workload mix; skewed or adversarial workloads shift the optimum, and adaptive re-tuning carries transition costs that the closed-form model ignores [3]. Our simulation likewise assumes static T and ignores L₀'s overlapping-run pathology, which dominates write stalls in practice [8].

**Bloom filters only accelerate negative lookups.** For present keys the probe is unavoidable and filter memory is pure overhead.

**Tiering and laziness tax reads.** Every reduction in WA via deferred merging increases the number of sorted runs, raising both point-lookup probe counts and space amplification from lingering obsolete versions. Lazy leveling bounds this damage but does not eliminate it.

**Key-value separation introduces GC.** The vLog's invalid values must be reclaimed; GC competes with foreground I/O, adds its own write amplification under update-heavy workloads, and complicates crash consistency. Range scans lose sortedness and depend on SSD parallelism to recover [6].

**Fragmentation taxes small scans.** PebblesDB's own evaluation concedes a penalty for small range queries on fully compacted stores [5] — precisely the workload where leveled LSMs shine.

**Hardware dependence.** All constants assume SSDs with fast random reads. On HDDs, where seeks dominate, the calculus reverses: sequentiality of leveled merges matters more than byte counts, and the original 1996 analysis [1] — written for spinning disks — remains the more relevant guide.

## 7 Conclusion

Write amplification is the central pathology of the log-structured merge-tree, and three decades of research have mapped its cures onto a single Pareto surface. Leveled compaction minimizes reads at maximal write cost; tiering and lazy leveling trade the two continuously; Monkey's optimal Bloom filter allocation removes an entire factor of L from lookup cost for free, given any memory budget; key-value separation and fragmented LSM restructure the tree so that amplification's constants shrink rather than merely its exponents. The empirical record — 50–80% lookup latency reductions, 2.4–3× lower write amplification, 6.7× write throughput, up to 111× faster loads — confirms that these are not asymptotic curiosities but production-relevant wins.

The frontier is now *adaptivity*: workloads drift, skew shifts, and device characteristics vary, yet most deployments still pin a static merge policy and filter budget at startup. Closing the loop — online, low-overhead re-tuning of the co-design (merge policy, buffer size, filter allocation) that Monkey formalized — remains the most important open problem in LSM-tree engineering.
