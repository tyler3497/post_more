# Functional Bootstrapping over the Torus: Programmable Bootstrapping in TFHE via Blind Rotation

## Abstract

Programmable bootstrapping (PBS) transforms TFHE bootstrapping from a pure noise-refresh primitive into a *functional* one: refreshing a ciphertext while simultaneously evaluating an arbitrary negacyclic function on the encrypted plaintext. This thesis treats PBS rigorously, grounded in the blind-rotation algorithm of Ducas–Micciancio and Chillotti–Gama–Georgieva–Izabachène. We derive the full pipeline — modulus switching from \(\mathbb{Z}_q\) to \(\mathbb{Z}_{2N}\), blind rotation of a redundant lookup-table polynomial via external products with GGSW bootstrapping keys, sample extraction, and key switching — and analyze per-stage noise evolution with sharp average-case bounds. We survey precision-extension techniques (WoP-PBS, PBSmanyLUT, integer-wise functional bootstrapping), key-switching noise optimization, and hardware acceleration on FPGA, GPU, and ASIC datapaths. Empirical figures place a single functional bootstrap at tens of milliseconds on CPUs and below one millisecond on accelerators, with correctness at 128-bit security for 8-bit plaintext precision. We close with open problems: eliminating the padding bit, amortizing multi-LUT evaluation, and converging TFHE and CKKS bootstrapping methods.

## 1 Introduction

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) permits arbitrary computation on encrypted data. Since Gentry's 2009 construction, every practical scheme has depended on *bootstrapping* — homomorphically evaluating the decryption circuit to reduce noise — to achieve unbounded depth. The schemes derived from the ring variant of Gentry–Sahai–Waters and the torus-based lineage of Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan follow a polynomial-approximation paradigm for bootstrapping. A parallel, *combinatorial* lineage — initiated by FHEW (Ducas and Micciancio, 2015) [7] and crystallized in TFHE (Chillotti, Gama, Georgieva, Izabachène, 2016–2019) [5][6] — bootstraps by homomorphically evaluating the rounding function via a discrete *blind rotation* on the torus, achieving bootstrapping latencies measured in tens of milliseconds rather than seconds, at the cost of supporting only small plaintext spaces natively.

Programmable bootstrapping elevates this combinatorial bootstrapping into something strictly more expressive. In gate bootstrapping, the *test vector* (the accumulator polynomial rotated during blind rotation) encodes the rounding function \(f(m) = \lfloor m \rceil\). Chillotti, Joye, and Paillier observed that nothing forces the test vector to encode rounding: any negacyclic function can be embedded into the lookup-table polynomial, so that the output of bootstrapping is simultaneously a fresh encryption of \(f(m)\) for the *arbitrary* function \(f\) [1]. This is *functional bootstrapping* — also called programmable bootstrapping (PBS) — and it repositions bootstrapping from a tax paid on depth into a feature: every noise refresh can carry one free function evaluation. Subsequent work has exploited this to evaluate S-boxes, sign functions, and multi-bit arithmetic directly inside the bootstrap [2][9][10].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the torus arithmetic, LWE/RLWE/GLWE, and the gadget-decomposition machinery underlying TFHE. Section 3 gives the methodology: the full PBS pipeline decomposed into modulus switching, blind rotation, sample extraction, and key switching, with the noise analysis of each stage. Section 4 deep-dives into four advanced topics: (i) the padding-bit constraint and its removal, (ii) multi-LUT evaluation, (iii) key-switching noise control, and (iv) hardware acceleration. Section 5 presents empirical performance and a proof sketch of correctness. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The torus and TLWE

TFHE operates over the real torus \(\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\), the additive group of reals modulo 1. A message \(\mu \in \mathbb{T}\) is encrypted as a *TLWE* sample \((\mathbf{a}, b) \in \mathbb{T}^{n+1}\) with secret key \(\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{B}^n\) (binary), satisfying

\[
b = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle + \mu + e, \qquad e \leftarrow \mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{T}, \sigma}
\]

where \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{T}, \sigma}\) is a Gaussian of standard deviation \(\sigma\) wrapped around the torus. The *phase* \(\varphi_{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{a}, b) = b - \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle = \mu + e\) recovers the noisy message. Security rests on the decisional LWE problem over the torus. The *polynomial* variant, TRLWE, replaces scalars by polynomials in \(\mathbb{T}_N[X] = \mathbb{T}[X]/(X^N + 1)\) with \(N\) a power of two; the ring's negacyclic structure \(X^N = -1\) is the algebraic engine of blind rotation [5][8].

> **Theorem:** *(LWE hardness on the torus).* Under the standard LWE assumption with dimension \(n \geq 630\) and Gaussian noise \(\sigma \approx 2^{-15}\), TLWE samples are computationally indistinguishable from uniform over \(\mathbb{T}^{n+1}\) [5].

### 2.2 Gadget decomposition and external products

Let \(\mathbf{g} = (B_g^{-1}, B_g^{-2}, \dots, B_g^{-\ell})\) be the gadget vector with base \(B_g\) and \(\ell\) levels. Any torus element \(\mu\) admits an approximate decomposition \(\text{Dec}(\mu) \approx \mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{Z}^\ell\) with \(\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{g} \rangle \approx \mu\). A *TRGSW* ciphertext encrypts \(\mu\) as \(\ell\) TRLWE samples masking \(\mu \cdot \mathbf{g}\). The *external product* \(\boxdot\) multiplies a TRGSW ciphertext by a TRLWE ciphertext:

\[
\text{TRGSW}(\mu) \boxdot \text{TRLWE}(m) = \text{TRLWE}(\mu \cdot m) + \text{noise},
\]

with noise growing only *additively* in the decomposition quality — the critical property that keeps blind rotation tractable. Each external product costs \(\ell\) polynomial multiplications, i.e., \(\mathcal{O}(\ell \, N \log N)\) via NTT [6][8].

### 2.3 Gate bootstrapping and the test vector

Gate bootstrapping refreshes a TLWE ciphertext by homomorphically evaluating its own decryption rounding. Given \((\mathbf{a}, b)\) encrypting \(\mu\) with noise \(e\), the procedure:

1. **Modulus switches** the phase to \(\bar{b} - \langle \bar{\mathbf{a}}, \mathbf{s} \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}_{2N}\);
2. **Blind-rotates** an accumulator \(\text{ACC} = X^{-\bar{b}} \cdot \text{testv}\) by \(\langle \bar{\mathbf{a}}, \mathbf{s} \rangle\) using the bootstrapping key (GGSW encryptions of the secret bits);
3. **Sample-extracts** coefficient 0 to obtain a TLWE encryption of the rounded message.

The test vector for gate bootstrapping is

\[
\text{testv} = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \frac{1}{8} X^i = \frac{1}{8} \cdot \frac{1 - X^N}{1 - X},
\]

encoding the rounding function. The padding bit — the most significant bit of the \(2N\) slots reserved for the sign of the negacyclic tail — guarantees that rotation by a noisy phase never aliases the constant term [5][9].

## 3 Methodology

We now derive programmable bootstrapping end to end. The pipeline is identical in skeleton to gate bootstrapping; the innovation is purely in the *content* of the test vector.

### 3.1 Embedding an arbitrary function in the test vector

Let \(f : \mathbb{Z}_t \to \mathbb{Z}_t\) be the target function and let the plaintext modulus be \(t\) with \(t \mid 2N\). Define the redundant LUT polynomial

\[
P_f(X) = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} f\!\left(\left\lfloor \frac{i \cdot t}{2N} \right\rceil\right) \cdot \Delta_{\text{out}} \cdot X^i \in \mathbb{T}_N[X],
\]

where \(\Delta_{\text{out}}\) is the output scaling factor. The redundancy — each plaintext value occupying a contiguous *block* of \(2N/t\) coefficients — provides the error tolerance: a noisy phase landing anywhere inside the block decodes to the same value. *Negacyclicity* requires \(f(m + t/2) = -f(m)\) in the torus encoding (equivalently, the second half of the wheel is fixed by \(X^N = -1\)), which is why a padding bit is conventionally reserved [1][10].

### 3.2 The PBS algorithm

```
Algorithm PBS(ct_in, BSK, KSK, P_f, params)
Input:  ct_in = LWEs(m·Δin) = (a_1..a_n, b) ∈ Z_q^{n+1}
        BSK   : bootstrapping key (GGSW encryptions of s_i)
        KSK   : key-switching key
        P_f   : redundant LUT polynomial encoding f
Output: ct_out = LWEs(f(m)·Δout)
/* 1. modulus switching Z_q -> Z_{2N} */
for i = 1..n+1:
    ā_i ← round(a_i · 2N / q)
/* 2. initialize accumulator and blind rotate */
ACC ← X^{-b̄} · P_f
for i = 1..n:
    ACC ← CMux(BSK_i, X^{ā_i} · ACC, ACC)   // external-product form
/* 3. sample extract coefficient 0 */
ct_tmp ← SampleExtract(ACC, 0)
/* 4. key switch back to original key */
ct_out ← KeySwitch(ct_tmp, KSK)
return ct_out
```

The CMux in step 2 is evaluated as \(\text{ACC} + \text{BSK}_i \boxdot (X^{\bar{a}_i} \cdot \text{ACC} - \text{ACC})\), consuming one external product per secret bit — \(n\) external products total for binary secrets [6][8].

### 3.3 Noise analysis

Each stage contributes independent noise. Let \(\sigma_{\text{in}}^2\) be the input variance and \(\sigma_{\text{BR}}^2\) the blind-rotation noise per external product:

| Stage | Noise contribution | Scaling |
|---|---|---|
| Modulus switching \(q \to 2N\) | \(\frac{n+1}{12} \cdot \left(\frac{2N}{q}\right)^2\) rounding variance | quadratic in \(2N/q\) |
| Blind rotation (\(n\) ext. products) | \(n \cdot \sigma_{\text{BR}}^2\), \(\sigma_{\text{BR}}^2 \approx \ell N (B_g/2)^2 \sigma_{\text{BSK}}^2\) | linear in \(n\) |
| Sample extraction | 0 (exact rearrangement) | — |
| Key switching | \(t_{\text{KS}} \cdot \sigma_{\text{KSK}}^2 \cdot \| \text{decomp} \|^2\) | linear in decomp base |

> **Theorem:** *(PBS correctness).* If the total output noise amplitude satisfies \(|e_{\text{out}}| < \Delta_{\text{out}} \cdot \frac{2N}{2t}\) with overwhelming probability — i.e., the noise stays within half a redundancy block — then \(\text{PBS}\) outputs a valid encryption of \(f(m)\) [1][6].

The bound is *sharp* in practice: implementations calibrate \(\sigma\) so that the failure probability is below \(2^{-64}\) per bootstrap, verified by Monte-Carlo noise simulation rather than worst-case union bounds, which would overestimate by orders of magnitude [8].

### 3.4 From gate to programmable: what changes, what doesn't

Crucially, the *cost* of PBS is identical to gate bootstrapping: same number of external products, same key material. The function evaluation is *free* in the amortized sense — one pays only for noise refresh and receives \(f(m)\) as a byproduct. This inverts the economics of FHE circuit design: deep pipelines of table lookups (S-boxes, activations, comparisons) collapse into chains of bootstraps [2][10].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The padding bit and WoP-PBS

The negacyclicity constraint is the central nuisance of PBS. Because rotation is modulo \(X^N + 1\), the LUT's second half must be the negation of its first half, consuming one bit of the \(2N\) phase slots as *padding*. Chillotti, Ligier, Orfila, and Tap introduced **WoP-PBS** (PBS *without padding*), restructuring the LUT so that the full \(2N\) slots carry information: the trick is to split the function evaluation across two correlated bootstraps whose negacyclic tails cancel, or equivalently to choose a non-standard test-vector encoding where the wrap-around region is absorbed into the function's own symmetry [1].

The payoff is a full bit of extra precision — decisive when the plaintext space is only 4–8 bits wide. Their analysis further provides *tighter* noise bounds via the central-limit behavior of accumulated rounding errors, replacing pessimistic worst-case bounds and thereby justifying smaller parameters (smaller \(N\), smaller \(q\)) at fixed security [1].

### 4.2 Evaluating many functions at once: PBSmanyLUT

A single blind rotation is expensive; evaluating \(k\) functions on the *same* input should not cost \(k\) rotations. Two approaches exist:

1. **Multivalue PBS** (Carpov et al.): extract a common factor \(v\) from all test polynomials, blind-rotate once, then multiply the accumulator by small coefficients to separate the function values [9].
2. **PBSmanyLUT** (Chillotti et al. 2021): pack all \(k\) LUTs into *one* test polynomial — each function's table occupying a distinct coefficient band — perform a single blind rotation, and sample-extract \(k\) different coefficients, one per function [1].

PBSmanyLUT is strictly more general: it needs only one rotation regardless of \(k\) (up to the packing limit \(N / (2N/t) = t/2\) functions per bootstrap in the naive layout), and it composes cleanly with WoP-PBS. This is the workhorse behind efficient homomorphic S-box layers and parallel comparison circuits [2].

### 4.3 Key-switching noise and gadget optimization

Key switching dominates the *non*-rotation noise budget. The key-switching key encrypts the bootstrapping-key bits under the output key; switching decomposes each coefficient in base \(B_{\text{KS}}\) with \(t_{\text{KS}}\) levels. Noise scales as

\[
\sigma_{\text{KS}}^2 \propto n_{\text{KS}} \cdot t_{\text{KS}} \cdot \left(\frac{B_{\text{KS}}}{2}\right)^2 \sigma_{\text{KSK}}^2,
\]

so there is a three-way trade-off: larger \(B_{\text{KS}}\) shrinks the key (fewer levels) but inflates noise per level; smaller \(B_{\text{KS}}\) needs more levels and a larger key. Production parameter sets (e.g., `tfhe-rs` `PARAM_MESSAGE_2_CARRY_2`) choose \(B_{\text{KS}} = 2^2\)–\(2^3\) with \(t_{\text{KS}} = 3\)–\(5\) to keep key-switching noise an order of magnitude below blind-rotation noise [8]. A further optimization — *key-switching key compression* via seed expansion — reduces the multi-hundred-megabyte BSK/KSK material to tens of megabytes at the cost of PRNG expansion at load time.

### 4.4 Modulus switching: the quiet correctness lever

Modulus switching maps \(\mathbb{Z}_q \to \mathbb{Z}_{2N}\) by scaling and rounding. The rounding error per coefficient is uniform on \([-1/2, 1/2)\), contributing variance \(1/12\) scaled by \((2N/q)^2\). For typical \(q = 2^{32}\) or \(2^{64}\) and \(N = 1024\)–\(4096\), this term is *negligible* — but it becomes the binding constraint in *parameter-shrinking* regimes where \(q\) is small (e.g., 32-bit torus limbs in GPU kernels). The improved analysis of [1] models the *sum* of \(n+1\) rounding errors as approximately Gaussian (CLT), yielding failure probabilities that match simulation to within a factor of two, versus worst-case bounds that overshoot by \(10^3\)–\(10^6\).

### 4.5 Hardware acceleration

Blind rotation is NTT-bound: each external product performs \(\ell\) forward/inverse NTTs of size \(N\). Acceleration efforts span three tiers:

- **GPU** (e.g., concrete's CUDA backend, FPT): batch many bootstraps across streaming multiprocessors; reported speedups of 5–15× over multithreaded CPU, with a single PBS at ~2–5 ms on an A100-class device [8].
- **FPGA** (e.g., the functionally-complete TFHE processor of [4]): pipelined NTT units with on-chip BSK caching; the PBS module chains initialization → blind rotation → sample extraction with buffer-addressed LUT retrieval, sustaining sub-millisecond PBS at modest clock rates.
- **ASIC**: custom torus-arithmetic datapaths with fused CMux-NTT pipelines; projections place PBS below 100 µs, making real-time encrypted control loops conceivable.

The common architectural theme is *memory bandwidth*, not compute: the bootstrapping key is hundreds of megabytes, and each bootstrap streams it once. Designs therefore co-locate BSK SRAM with NTT engines and batch ciphertexts to amortize the key fetch — an insight shared with CKKS bootstrapping accelerators [3].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

**Correctness proof sketch.** Let the input phase be \(\varphi = m \cdot \Delta_{\text{in}} + e\) with \(|e| < \Delta_{\text{in}}/4\). After modulus switching, \(\bar{\varphi} = \lfloor \varphi \cdot 2N/q \rceil\) lands in the redundancy block of \(m\) iff the rounding error is below half a block — guaranteed by the noise bound of Section 3.3. Blind rotation computes \(\text{ACC} = X^{\bar{\varphi}} \cdot X^{-\bar{b}} P_f = X^{\langle \bar{\mathbf{a}}, \mathbf{s} \rangle} P_f\) homomorphically; the CMux chain is correct because each \(\text{BSK}_i\) encrypts \(s_i \in \{0,1\}\) and \(\text{ACC} \gets \text{ACC} + s_i (X^{\bar{a}_i} - 1)\text{ACC}\) selects rotation by \(\bar{a}_i s_i\). Coefficient 0 of the final accumulator equals \(f(m)\Delta_{\text{out}}\) plus accumulated noise; sample extraction is exact; key switching preserves the phase up to its own noise term. ∎

**Measured performance** (representative figures from [4][8]):

| Platform | PBS latency | Precision | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| CPU (tfhe-rs, 8 threads) | ~15–40 ms | 4–8 bits | \(N=2048\), binary secret |
| GPU (A100-class) | ~2–5 ms | 4–8 bits | batched, NTT-bound |
| FPGA prototype | <1 ms | 4 bits | pipelined PBS module [4] |
| ASIC (projected) | <100 µs | 4 bits | fused CMux datapath |

**Precision frontier.** Classical PBS supports ~4 bits natively; with WoP-PBS and careful noise calibration, 8-bit message spaces are practical [1]; integer-wise functional bootstrapping extends single-bootstrap evaluation to full machine-word integers by generalizing the test-vector coefficient layout, at the cost of larger \(N\) and re-examined security margins [10].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Negacyclicity and the padding bit.** Even with WoP-PBS, arbitrary non-negacyclic functions require either padding or multi-bootstrap encodings; the constraint is algebraic, not an implementation artifact [1].
2. **Small plaintext precision.** PBS natively handles 4–8 bits per bootstrap; wider integers demand digit decomposition and carry management across many bootstraps, eroding the "free function" advantage [8][10].
3. **Bootstrapping-key size.** Hundreds of megabytes of key material must be streamed per bootstrap, making PBS memory-bandwidth-bound and costly to distribute [3][4].
4. **Noise-driven failure probability.** Correctness is probabilistic; the \(2^{-64}\) failure target rests on heuristic Gaussian modeling of accumulated noise, not on worst-case proofs — a gap noted across the literature [1][8].
5. **Function must be public.** The LUT is embedded in the test vector in the clear; PBS cannot evaluate a *secret* function without additional machinery (e.g., encrypting the test vector itself, at significant cost) [9].
6. **No SIMD batching within one bootstrap.** Unlike BGV/BFV/CKKS, one TFHE bootstrap processes one scalar; throughput comes only from batching independent bootstraps, limiting arithmetic intensity [8].

## 7 Conclusion

Programmable bootstrapping reconceives the most expensive operation in TFHE as the most *useful* one: every noise refresh doubles as an arbitrary function evaluation, at zero marginal cryptographic cost over gate bootstrapping. The blind-rotation core — modulus switching, CMux-driven rotation of a redundant LUT, sample extraction, key switching — is now understood with sharp noise bounds, and the precision frontier has advanced from 4 to 8+ bits through WoP-PBS, PBSmanyLUT, and integer-wise techniques [1][2][10]. Hardware acceleration is shifting the bottleneck from compute to key bandwidth, with sub-millisecond PBS demonstrated on FPGAs and projected on ASICs [4].

Open directions remain: removing the padding bit under standard noise assumptions without multi-bootstrap tricks; amortizing key-switching across batched PBS; and unifying the combinatorial TFHE bootstrapping theory with the polynomial CKKS/DM line, where blind-rotation advances are already cross-pollinating [3]. As FHE moves from benchmarks to deployment, PBS is the primitive that makes encrypted *decision logic* — comparisons, branching, table lookups — practical rather than merely possible.
