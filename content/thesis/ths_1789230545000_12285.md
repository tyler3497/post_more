# The Jaynes–Cummings Model and Circuit Quantum Electrodynamics: Dressed States, the Purcell Effect, Dispersive Readout, and the Transmon Qubit

## Abstract

The interaction of a two-level quantum system with a single quantized mode of the electromagnetic field, formalized by Jaynes and Cummings in 1963, is among the most consequential models in modern physics: an exactly solvable light–matter Hamiltonian that predicts vacuum Rabi oscillations, the anharmonic dressed-state ladder, and the collapse and revival of atomic inversion — phenomena that have no classical counterpart and that were only fully realized experimentally once cavity quality factors reached the requisite values. Circuit quantum electrodynamics (circuit QED) transposes this physics from optical cavities and Rydberg atoms to on-chip superconducting circuits, replacing the atom by an engineered nonlinear oscillator — most successfully the transmon qubit — and the cavity by a lithographic microwave resonator, and in doing so achieves dipole couplings two to three orders of magnitude larger than anything possible in atomic cavity QED [1][2]. This thesis develops the full theoretical edifice: the Jaynes–Cummings Hamiltonian and its dressed-state spectrum, the derivation of the transmon as a charge-insensitive artificial atom from the Cooper-pair box [3][4], the Purcell modification of spontaneous emission that governs qubit radiative lifetime, and the dispersive regime of the model that enables quantum-nondemolition readout of the qubit state [5]. It then surveys the experimental confirmations that elevated circuit QED from a proposal to the dominant platform for superconducting quantum computing, analyzes the breakdown mechanisms that bound each regime — the critical photon number, measurement-induced ionization, and ultrastrong-coupling corrections — and closes with the open theoretical questions that remain as processors scale from tens to thousands of qubits.

## 1 Introduction

Quantum electrodynamics describes the interaction of matter with light, but the full theory is analytically impenetrable in almost every case of interest. Progress therefore depends on identifying *minimal models* — Hamiltonians stripped of everything inessential yet rich enough to capture genuine quantum effects. The **Jaynes–Cummings (JC) model**, introduced in 1963 for the study of maser theory, is the archetype: a single two-level atom coupled to a single quantized mode of a resonant cavity, with counter-rotating terms discarded under the rotating-wave approximation (RWA). Despite this austerity, the model is exactly diagonalizable, and its predictions read like a catalogue of everything that distinguishes quantum from semiclassical radiation theory: vacuum Rabi splitting of order $2g$ in the spectrum, collapse and revival of population inversion for coherent-state driving fields, photon blockade, and the generation of atom–field entanglement from initially separable states [1].

For decades these predictions hovered between textbook exercises and unattainable ideals. Atomic cavity QED in the optical domain, pioneered by the groups of Haroche and Kimble, reached the strong-coupling regime only with heroic mirror technology, and even then the atom–cavity coupling $g$ remained a small fraction of the bare frequencies while atoms transited cavities ballistically, limiting interaction times [1]. The decisive turn came in the early 2000s with the proposal and rapid experimental realization of **circuit QED**: implement the JC Hamiltonian with superconducting circuits, where the "atom" is a lithographically defined nonlinear oscillator (a Cooper-pair box, later the transmon) and the "cavity" is a coplanar waveguide resonator whose vacuum field fluctuations are enormous because its mode volume is microscopic [2]. In circuit QED the coupling $g/2\pi$ routinely reaches $\sim 100$ MHz on a $5$ GHz transition — $g/\omega \sim 0.02$ — while cavity and qubit decay rates sit below $0.1$ MHz, a strong-coupling ratio $g/\kappa \sim 10^3$ that atomic physicists could only dream of [1]. This thesis argues that the JC model is not merely a convenient analogy for circuit QED but its exact organizing principle: every operational protocol of a modern superconducting processor is a corollary of the JC Hamiltonian examined in the appropriate limit.

> **Thesis statement:** *The Jaynes–Cummings model provides the complete theoretical skeleton of circuit quantum electrodynamics: its exact solution yields the dressed-state ladder used for spectroscopy, its dispersive limit yields the quantum-nondemolition measurement behind readout, its coupling to a continuum yields the Purcell effect that governs qubit lifetimes, and the transmon is the artificial atom that brings the model into the strongly coupled regime.*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Jaynes–Cummings Hamiltonian

Consider a two-level system with transition frequency $\omega_q$ and a cavity mode of frequency $\omega_r$, coupled via the electric-dipole interaction. In the RWA the Hamiltonian reads

$$\hat{H}_{JC} = \hbar\omega_r \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a} + \frac{\hbar\omega_q}{2}\hat{\sigma}_z + \hbar g\,(\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{\sigma}_- + \hat{a}\hat{\sigma}_+),$$

where $g$ is the vacuum Rabi frequency (half the vacuum Rabi splitting). The discarded counter-rotating terms $\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{\sigma}_+$ and $\hat{a}\hat{\sigma}_-$ oscillate at $\omega_r + \omega_q$ and average away provided $g \ll \omega_r, \omega_q$ and $|\omega_r - \omega_q| \ll \omega_r + \omega_q$ — conditions amply satisfied in the strong-coupling regime of circuit QED but violated in the *ultrastrong* regime where the full quantum Rabi model must be retained [1].

The model's celebrated solvability follows from a conserved quantity: the total excitation number $\hat{N} = \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a} + \hat{\sigma}_+\hat{\sigma}_-$ commutes with $\hat{H}_{JC}$, so the Hilbert space decomposes into two-dimensional manifolds $\{|g,n+1\rangle, |e,n\rangle\}$ (plus the isolated ground state $|g,0\rangle$). Diagonalizing within each manifold yields the **dressed states**

$$|+,n\rangle = \cos\theta_n\,|e,n\rangle + \sin\theta_n\,|g,n+1\rangle,$$
$$|-,n\rangle = -\sin\theta_n\,|e,n\rangle + \cos\theta_n\,|g,n+1\rangle,$$

with mixing angle $\tan 2\theta_n = 2g\sqrt{n+1}/\Delta$, detuning $\Delta = \omega_q - \omega_r$, and eigenenergies

$$E_{\pm,n} = \hbar\omega_r\left(n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right) \pm \frac{\hbar}{2}\sqrt{\Delta^2 + 4g^2(n+1)}.$$

At resonance ($\Delta = 0$) the bare states are maximally mixed and split by $2\hbar g\sqrt{n+1}$ — the vacuum Rabi splitting $2g$ for $n=0$, and an *anharmonic* ladder for $n \geq 1$ since the splitting grows as $\sqrt{n+1}$ [1].

### 2.2 Superconducting qubits: from Cooper-pair box to transmon

The superconducting "atom" begins as the **Cooper-pair box (CPB)**: a small superconducting island connected to a reservoir through a Josephson junction, described by

$$\hat{H}_{CPB} = 4E_C(\hat{n} - n_g)^2 - E_J\cos\hat{\varphi},$$

where $E_C$ is the charging energy, $E_J$ the Josephson energy, $\hat{n}$ counts Cooper pairs, and $n_g$ is a gate-induced offset charge. For $E_J/E_C \sim 1$ the two lowest levels form a charge qubit, but the spectrum is exponentially sensitive to $n_g$, so that $1/f$ charge noise dephases the qubit in nanoseconds [3].

The **transmon**, introduced by Koch *et al.* in 2007, operates the same circuit at $E_J/E_C \sim 20$–$50$ by shunting the junction with a large capacitance [3]. In this regime the charge dispersion $\epsilon_m$ of level $m$ falls *exponentially*, $\epsilon_m \propto \exp(-\sqrt{8E_J/E_C})$, while the anharmonicity $\alpha = E_{12} - E_{01} \approx -E_C$ decreases only as a weak power law. Typical devices have $\omega_{01}/2\pi \sim 5$ GHz and $\alpha/2\pi \sim -200$ to $-300$ MHz, with charge dispersion below $100$ kHz [3][4]. The transmon is thus a weakly anharmonic oscillator whose lowest two levels serve as the qubit, and the full multilevel structure participates in the circuit-QED Hamiltonian as a generalized JC model with multiple transition dipoles [1].

### 2.3 The Purcell effect

In 1946 Purcell noted that spontaneous emission is not an immutable atomic property: it is proportional to the density of electromagnetic modes at the transition frequency, and placing an emitter in a cavity *reshapes* that density [1]. A qubit coupled with strength $g$ to a resonator of linewidth $\kappa$ and detuned by $\Delta$ acquires a radiative decay channel into the resonator's output continuum with rate

$$\Gamma_{\text{Purcell}} = \kappa\,\frac{g^2}{\Delta^2},$$

the **Purcell decay rate**, valid in the dispersive regime $|\Delta| \gg g$. Far from being a curiosity, this formula is the central engineering constraint of circuit QED: the same coupling that enables fast readout also shortens $T_1$, forcing a trade-off between measurement speed and qubit lifetime that is resolved in practice by **Purcell filters** — bandstop structures that present high impedance (low density of states) at the qubit frequency while remaining transparent at the resonator frequency [1].

## 3 Theoretical Framework

The circuit-QED Hamiltonian is not postulated; it is *derived* from circuit quantization, and the derivation determines the precise form of every term including the dispersive shifts [1][2].

### 3.1 Quantizing the resonator and the transmon

A coplanar waveguide resonator is modeled as a distributed $LC$ transmission line; its fundamental mode quantizes to a harmonic oscillator $\hat{H}_r = \hbar\omega_r\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}$ with voltage operator $\hat{V} = V_{\text{rms}}^0(\hat{a} + \hat{a}^\dagger)$. Because the mode volume is of order $(\lambda/100)^3$ rather than $\lambda^3$, the zero-point voltage $V_{\text{rms}}^0$ is millivolts rather than microvolts, and the resulting dipole coupling exceeds atomic cavity QED by orders of magnitude [2].

The transmon, expanded about its potential minimum, is a weakly anharmonic (Duffing) oscillator. Capacitive coupling between the resonator voltage and the transmon charge, followed by the RWA and projection onto the qubit subspace, yields the JC Hamiltonian of §2.1. Keeping the multilevel structure gives the **generalized JC model** with couplings $g_{m,m+1} = g\sqrt{m+1}$ between adjacent transmon levels [1].

### 3.2 The dispersive transformation

For quantum-nondemolition readout we need $|\Delta| \gg g$, where real photon exchange is energetically forbidden. A Schrieffer–Wolff (unitary) transformation $\hat{U} = \exp[\frac{g}{\Delta}(\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{\sigma}_- - \hat{a}\hat{\sigma}_+)]$ eliminates the coupling to second order in $g/\Delta$ and produces the **dispersive Hamiltonian** [1][5]:

$$\hat{H}_{\text{disp}} = \hbar\left(\omega_r + \chi\hat{\sigma}_z\right)\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a} + \frac{\hbar}{2}\left(\omega_q + \chi\right)\hat{\sigma}_z,$$

with dispersive shift $\chi = g^2/\Delta$ (for the two-level truncation; the transmon's higher levels modify this to $\chi = g^2/\Delta - g^2/(\Delta - \alpha)$-type corrections). The crucial property is that $[\hat{H}_{\text{disp}}, \hat{\sigma}_z] = 0$: the interaction shifts the cavity frequency by $\pm\chi$ depending on the qubit state *without* causing transitions — the defining feature of quantum-nondemolition (QND) measurement [5].

> **Theorem (Dispersive QND readout):** *In the dispersive regime $|\Delta| \gg g\sqrt{\bar{n}+1}$ the qubit observable $\hat{\sigma}_z$ is a constant of motion of the effective Hamiltonian, so that repeated interrogation of the cavity transmission yields the qubit state without inducing bit-flips, up to corrections of order $(g/\Delta)^2$ and photon-induced dephasing.*

### 3.3 Open-system extension

Real devices couple to baths. In the Lindblad formalism the JC system evolves under

$$\dot{\hat{\rho}} = -\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{JC}, \hat{\rho}] + \kappa\,\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}]\hat{\rho} + \gamma_1\,\mathcal{D}[\hat{\sigma}_-]\hat{\rho} + \frac{\gamma_\phi}{2}\,\mathcal{D}[\hat{\sigma}_z]\hat{\rho},$$

with $\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}]\hat{\rho} = \hat{L}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}^\dagger - \tfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}^\dagger\hat{L}, \hat{\rho}\}$. In the dispersive limit the cavity dissipator generates **measurement-induced dephasing** $\Gamma_m = 8\chi^2\bar{n}/\kappa$ on the qubit — the backaction price of acquiring information — and the Purcell formula of §2.3 emerges as the qubit's inherited share of $\kappa$ [1][5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The dressed-state ladder and vacuum Rabi splitting

The dressed states of §2.1 are not a mathematical convenience; they are the *physical* eigenstates probed by spectroscopy. Driving the cavity while sweeping a probe tone reveals the vacuum Rabi doublet at $\omega_r \pm g$ — two transmission peaks where the bare cavity had one — and at higher drive powers the $\sqrt{n+1}$ anharmonicity of the ladder appears as a fan of transitions $| \pm, n\rangle \to |\pm, n+1\rangle$ at frequencies $\omega_r \pm g(\sqrt{n+2} \mp \sqrt{n+1})$. This anharmonic ladder is the fingerprint that distinguishes the quantized field from a classical drive: a classical field would produce equally spaced sidebands, whereas the JC ladder compresses as $\sim g/\sqrt{n}$ [1].

With the cavity in a coherent state $|\alpha\rangle$, the inversion becomes a sum over Rabi frequencies $2g\sqrt{n+1}$ weighted by Poissonian photon statistics; the incommensurate frequencies dephase (**collapse**) on a timescale $t_c \sim 1/g$ and rephase (**revival**) at $t_r \sim 2\pi|\alpha|/g$, the celebrated collapse-and-revival phenomenon often cited as the most direct proof of field quantization [1].

```python
import numpy as np

def jc_inversion(t, g, alpha, n_max=60):
    """Collapse & revival of qubit inversion for coherent-state cavity drive."""
    n = np.arange(n_max)
    p_n = np.exp(-abs(alpha)**2) * abs(alpha)**(2*n) / np.array(
        [np.math.factorial(k) for k in n])  # Poisson weights
    omega_n = 2 * g * np.sqrt(n + 1)          # Rabi frequency per manifold
    return np.sum(p_n[:, None] * np.cos(omega_n[:, None] * t), axis=0)
```

### 4.2 The transmon: charge insensitivity from $E_J/E_C \gg 1$

The transmon's design brilliance lies in a single asymptotic trade. Increasing $E_J/E_C$ from $\sim 1$ to $\sim 50$ suppresses charge-noise sensitivity by *five orders of magnitude* while reducing anharmonicity only from $\sim 1$ GHz to $\sim 250$ MHz — still ample for $\sim 10$ ns selective pulses with negligible leakage [3][4].

A subtlety: the transmon's weak anharmonicity means it is not a true two-level system, and the JC model must be generalized. The higher levels $|2\rangle, |3\rangle, \dots$ couple to the resonator with matrix elements $g_{j,j+1} = g\sqrt{j+1}$, and virtual transitions through them renormalize the dispersive shift, which in practice can even change sign with detuning — a measurable consequence of the multilevel JC ladder routinely exploited to engineer fast, high-fidelity readout [1].

| Parameter | Symbol | Typical value | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qubit frequency | $\omega_q/2\pi$ | 4–6 GHz | Computational transition |
| Resonator frequency | $\omega_r/2\pi$ | 6–8 GHz | Readout / bus mode |
| Coupling | $g/2\pi$ | 50–150 MHz | Vacuum Rabi rate |
| Detuning | $\Delta/2\pi$ | 1–2 GHz | Dispersive parameter |
| Dispersive shift | $\chi/2\pi$ | 0.5–2 MHz | Readout signal |
| Resonator linewidth | $\kappa/2\pi$ | 1–10 MHz | Photon leak rate |
| Qubit relaxation | $T_1$ | 50–300 $\mu$s | Energy lifetime |
| Anharmonicity | $\alpha/2\pi$ | $-200$ to $-350$ MHz | Leakage protection |

### 4.3 The Purcell effect: engineering the photonic density of states

Purcell's insight, translated into the JC language, is that the qubit's $T_1$ is not intrinsic: diagonalizing the qubit–resonator–bath system shows the qubit eigenstate acquires a photonic admixture of order $(g/\Delta)^2$, and through that admixture it radiates into the resonator's output line at rate $\kappa(g/\Delta)^2$ [1]. The design tension is sharp: **fast readout** wants large $g$, small $|\Delta|$, and large $\kappa$; **long $T_1$** wants the opposite. The resolution is **Purcell filtering**: insert a microwave bandstop filter between resonator and output line, centered on the qubit frequency but passing the resonator frequency. The qubit then sees a suppressed density of states while readout photons escape freely, decoupling the two requirements and enabling $T_1 > 100\,\mu$s alongside sub-microsecond readout [1]. Modern processors additionally use *intrinsic* Purcell protection (interference-based cancellation of the decay path) and tunable couplers that switch $g$ effectively to zero during idle periods.

### 4.4 Dispersive readout: quantum-nondemolition cavity spectroscopy

Dispersive readout converts the qubit state into a classical microwave signal in three stages [5]:

1. **State-dependent cavity pull.** With the qubit in $|g\rangle$ ($|e\rangle$), the resonator frequency is $\omega_r - \chi$ ($\omega_r + \chi$). Probing at $\omega_r$ with a coherent tone, the transmitted field acquires a qubit-dependent phase shift $\theta = \pm 2\arctan(2\chi/\kappa)$.
2. **Heterodyne detection.** The transmitted microwave field is amplified (by a Josephson parametric amplifier or traveling-wave parametric amplifier at the millikelvin stage, then HEMT amplifiers at 4 K) and digitized; the in-phase and quadrature components $(I, Q)$ form two Gaussian blobs in the IQ plane, one per qubit state.
3. **Thresholding and integration.** Integrating the signal for a time $\tau_m$ separates the blobs; the signal-to-noise ratio grows as $\text{SNR} \propto (2\chi/\kappa)\sqrt{\bar{n}\kappa\tau_m}$, and single-shot fidelities exceeding $99\%$ in under $500$ ns are now routine [5].

The measurement is QND because $[\hat{H}_{\text{disp}}, \hat{\sigma}_z] = 0$: after the probe is switched off, the qubit remains in the measured eigenstate, enabling active reset and mid-circuit measurement for error correction [1]. Careful modeling shows, however, that the dispersive picture is only approximate during resonator ring-up: the joint qubit–resonator state hybridizes into *dressed coherent states* within a single JC ladder, and leakage into neighboring ladders plus transmon-induced self-squeezing of the field must be accounted for at high photon numbers [7].

> **Corollary (Measurement backaction):** *Acquiring which-state information at rate $\Gamma_{\text{meas}} = 8\chi^2\bar{n}/\kappa$ necessarily dephases qubit superpositions at rate $\Gamma_m \geq \Gamma_{\text{meas}}/2$ — information and disturbance are two faces of the same dispersive coupling.*

## 5 Experimental Confirmation and Proofs

The circuit-QED program advanced through a sequence of landmark experiments, each a direct test of the JC framework:

1. **Strong coupling (2004).** Wallraff *et al.* coupled a Cooper-pair box to a coplanar waveguide resonator and observed the vacuum Rabi splitting $2g/2\pi \approx 12$ MHz with $g \gg \kappa, \gamma$ — the first solid-state realization of strong-coupling cavity QED, and the founding experiment of the field [2].
2. **The circuit-QED architecture (2004–2007).** Blais *et al.* showed theoretically that the JC interaction enables qubit readout, qubit–qubit coupling via virtual photons, and gate operations without leaving the charge sweet spot, establishing the blueprint still used today [2][6].
3. **The transmon (2007–2008).** Koch *et al.* derived the charge-insensitive design [3]; Schreier *et al.* demonstrated it experimentally, verifying exponential suppression of charge dispersion and microsecond coherence [4].
4. **Dispersive readout dynamics (2009).** Bianchetti *et al.* measured the transmitted field quadratures versus probe frequency and time, finding quantitative agreement with Bloch equations derived from the dispersive JC Hamiltonian including dissipation — validating the measurement operator formalism used in state tomography [5].
5. **Dressed-state spectroscopy.** Transmission measurements resolving the $\sqrt{n}$ anharmonic ladder, collapse and revival of Rabi oscillations, and photon-number splitting of the qubit line (each Fock state $|n\rangle$ shifting the qubit by $2n\chi$) confirmed the dressed-state picture photon by photon [1].

Collectively these results constitute an unusually complete experimental verification: the JC model in circuit QED has been tested at the level of spectra, dynamics, open-system backaction, and higher-order corrections, with theory and experiment agreeing at the percent level across two decades [1].

## 6 Limitations

No model survives contact with the laboratory unscathed, and the JC description of circuit QED has well-charted boundaries:

- **Critical photon number.** The dispersive expansion breaks down when the cavity population approaches $n_{\text{crit}} = \Delta^2/(4g^2)$. Beyond it, the qubit and resonator hybridize strongly, the measurement ceases to be QND, and the transmon can be *ionized* — driven into unconfined, charge-like states outside the cosine potential — causing readout errors that grow with probe power [1].
- **Multilevel leakage.** The transmon's weak anharmonicity means fast control pulses and strong readout drives populate $|2\rangle$ and above; the two-level JC truncation then fails quantitatively, and full multilevel simulation (e.g., with QuTiP) becomes mandatory [1][7].
- **Ultrastrong coupling.** As $g/\omega$ approaches $0.1$ (achieved with flux qubits and galvanic coupling), the RWA fails: counter-rotating terms produce Bloch–Siegert shifts, the ground state contains virtual photons, and the quantum Rabi model replaces the JC model [1].
- **Material defects.** Real devices suffer dielectric two-level-system (TLS) defects, quasiparticle tunneling, and radiative loss through unintended modes, setting the observed $T_1$ ceilings that no Hamiltonian refinement can remove — only materials science can [1].
- **Measurement-induced dephasing and crosstalk.** In multi-qubit processors, readout resonators couple dispersively to spectator qubits ($\chi_{ij}$ crosstalk), and simultaneous readout induces correlated dephasing; frequency allocation and Purcell filtering become system-level optimization problems [1][6].
- **Scalability of the model itself.** The JC description is intrinsically few-body. A 1000-qubit processor is a many-body driven-dissipative system where the JC picture survives only as a local effective theory, supplemented by full-chip electromagnetic simulation [1].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Sixty years after Jaynes and Cummings wrote down their simple Hamiltonian, it remains the theoretical backbone of the most advanced quantum computing platform in existence. The trajectory traced in this thesis — from the exact dressed-state solution, through the Schrieffer–Wolff dispersive limit, to Purcell-engineered lifetimes and the transmon artificial atom — shows a rare unity: a single model whose distinct parameter regimes correspond one-to-one with the operational primitives of a quantum processor.

The open frontier is quantitative rather than conceptual: pushing $T_1$ toward milliseconds demands understanding TLS defects at the atomic scale; scaling to thousands of qubits demands taming crosstalk in dense JC networks; and faster, higher-fidelity readout demands operating ever closer to the critical photon number without ionizing the transmon [1][5]. Each of these is, at bottom, a question about the Jaynes–Cummings model meeting the real world — which is to say that the model that began as a maser-theory curiosity has become literally the Hamiltonian on which the superconducting quantum computing industry runs.
