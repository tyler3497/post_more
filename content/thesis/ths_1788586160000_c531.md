Distributed consensus protocols such as Raft underpin nearly every fault-tolerant storage system, yet their correctness arguments have historically rested on informal reasoning that misses corner cases in reconfiguration, log truncation, and leader election races. This thesis surveys the machine-checked verification of consensus: the TLA+ specification and TLC model checking of Raft's leader election and log replication from Ongaro's dissertation; the Verdi framework's verified system transformers for Raft in Coq; the IronFleet project's layered Dafny refinement from an abstract replicated-state-log specification to executable code, proving safety and liveness including a verified ZooKeeper-style service (IronRSL); and the emerging Verus verifier for Rust consensus implementations. # Machine-Checked Consensus: TLA+ Model Checking of Raft, IronFleet Dafny Refinement for State Machine Replication, Inductive Invariants for Leader Election and Log Replication, and Verus Verification of Rust Consensus Implementations

## Abstract

Distributed consensus protocols such as Raft and Multi-Paxos underpin nearly every fault-tolerant storage system in production, yet their correctness arguments have historically rested on informal reasoning that routinely misses corner cases in reconfiguration, log truncation, and leader election races. This thesis surveys and synthesizes the machine-checked verification of consensus: the TLA+ specification and TLC model checking of Raft's leader election and log replication as developed in Ongaro's dissertation; the Verdi framework's verified system transformers for Raft in Coq; the IronFleet project's layered refinement from an abstract replicated-state-log specification down to executable Dafny code, including the liveness argument under partial synchrony; and the emerging application of the Verus verifier to Rust consensus implementations. We present the inductive invariants that carry the central safety argument — Election Safety, Log Matching, Leader Completeness, and State Machine Safety — show how refinement mappings discharge the gap between abstract specifications and running code, and analyze the liveness obligations imposed by the Dwork–Lynch–Stockmeyer partial-synchrony model. We close with lessons from verified ZooKeeper-style coordination services (IronRSL) and a candid assessment of what machine-checked proofs do and do not guarantee: bounded model checking finds shallow bugs cheaply, while full refinement proofs buy deep assurance at a proof-to-code ratio that remains the central economic obstacle to verified distributed systems.

## 1. Introduction

Consensus — unreliable processes agreeing on a single value despite crash failures — is provably impossible in a fully asynchronous system with even one faulty process [6], yet it underpins etcd, Consul, ZooKeeper, TiKV, and CockroachDB. The tension is resolved by the *partial synchrony* model of Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer [6]: the system may behave asynchronously for an unbounded period but eventually stabilizes at an unknown *global stabilization time* (GST), after which delays are bounded. Every practical protocol bets the environment satisfies this model; every liveness argument proves progress once GST passes.

The second difficulty is human: consensus protocols are notoriously hard to reason about. Lamport's Paxos [9] is correct but famously opaque, motivating Raft's design for understandability [1][2] — but understandability is not correctness. Informal arguments about *Leader Completeness* (a committed entry appears in every later leader's log) conceal a delicate case analysis over the election restriction and the commit rule. Subtle, multi-step invariants like this are exactly what informal sketches get wrong, and exactly what machine-checked verification is for.

> **Theorem (State Machine Safety, informal):** *If a server has applied a log entry at a given index to its state machine, no other server will ever apply a different log entry for the same index.* [1]

This thesis examines four complementary verification technologies applied to consensus. **TLA+ model checking** (the TLC checker) provides cheap, bounded, push-button falsification of protocol specifications [5]. **Verdi** [4] embeds Raft in Coq and uses verified system transformers to transport proofs across implementation refinements. **IronFleet** [3] carries out the most ambitious program: a fully machine-checked refinement tower, written in Dafny, from an abstract specification of a replicated state log (RSL) down to executable code, proving both safety *and* liveness — including a verified ZooKeeper-style coordination service, IronRSL. Finally, **Verus** brings SMT-backed, ownership-aware verification to Rust, the language in which a growing share of new consensus implementations (e.g., in the TiKV and etcd ecosystems) is written.

Our contributions: (i) a unified presentation of the inductive invariant structure shared by all verified Raft developments; (ii) a comparison of bounded model checking, interactive refinement proof, and automated SMT verification on assurance, effort, and liveness; (iii) a candid accounting of limitations.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Consensus, Raft, and the partial-synchrony model

We assume crash faults: $2f+1$ servers, at most $f$ crash permanently; the network may drop, delay, duplicate, or reorder messages but not corrupt them. Safety must hold in all executions; liveness in executions satisfying partial synchrony [6].

Raft [1] decomposes consensus into three subproblems:

1. **Leader election.** Followers reset a randomized election timeout on valid RPCs; on timeout a follower becomes a candidate, increments its term, votes for itself, and solicits votes. A majority makes it leader; randomization drives split-vote probability down each round.
2. **Log replication.** The leader appends client commands as entries $\langle \mathit{term}, \mathit{index}, \mathit{command} \rangle$ and replicates them via `AppendEntries`. An entry is *committed* once stored on a majority; the leader applies it and notifies followers via `commitIndex`.
3. **Safety.** Five interlocking properties [1]:
   - *Election Safety:* at most one leader per term.
   - *Leader Append-Only:* a leader never overwrites or deletes its log.
   - *Log Matching:* if two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, the logs are identical in all preceding entries.
   - *Leader Completeness:* if an entry is committed in some term, it appears in the logs of all leaders of higher terms.
   - *State Machine Safety:* applied entries are consistent across servers.

### 2.2 TLA+, TLC, and refinement mappings

TLA+ [5] specifies concurrent systems as formulas in the *temporal logic of actions*: $Init \land \Box[Next]_{vars} \land Fairness$. The TLC model checker enumerates reachable states of finite instances, checking invariants up to configurable bounds. Abadi and Lamport's refinement mappings [9] bridge abstraction levels: a lower-level spec *refines* a higher one when every behavior of the former, projected through a mapping, is a behavior of the latter.

### 2.3 Dafny, IronFleet, and Verdi

Dafny is a verification-aware language with SMT-backed assertion checking; IronFleet [3] uses it as both implementation language and proof vehicle. Verdi [4] writes and proves systems in Coq, using *verified system transformers* to add fault-tolerance machinery (sequence numbers, retransmission) to a verified core. Verus is a Rust verifier exploiting the borrow checker: linear ownership becomes linear ghost reasoning.

## 3. Methodology

Our method is comparative and reconstructive. We re-express the core verification artifacts of four developments in a common vocabulary — state variables, actions, inductive invariants, refinement layers — and evaluate each along four axes:

1. **Specification fidelity:** how faithfully the top spec captures protocol intent, and how the refinement chain reaches executable code.
2. **Safety coverage:** which of Raft's five safety properties are proved, at what abstraction level.
3. **Liveness coverage:** whether liveness is addressed, and under which synchrony assumptions.
4. **Cost model:** proof-to-code ratio, automation degree, and trusted computing base (TCB) size.

Concretely, we study: the TLA+ `raft.tla` spec from Ongaro's dissertation [2] checked with TLC on small configurations; the Verdi Raft development and its RaftMongo case study [4]; the IronFleet IronRSL development [3], the only one proving liveness end-to-end; and the Verus approach applied to a Raft-like Rust log-replication core.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 TLA+ model checking of Raft: specification, actions, and bounded assurance

Ongaro's TLA+ specification of Raft [2] is the canonical *specification as executable design document*, with deliberately minimal state:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE raft ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets, Sequences, TLC
CONSTANTS Server, Value, Quorum
VARIABLES currentTerm, votedFor, log, commitIndex,
          state,      \* Follower | Candidate | Leader
          votesGranted, matchIndex, nextIndex
```

Each protocol step is an action. `RequestVote(s, t)` encodes the *election restriction* — the voter grants its vote only if the candidate's log is at least as up-to-date (larger last term, or equal term and longer log):

```tla+
RequestVote(s, t) ==
    /\ state[s] = Candidate
    /\ currentTerm[s] = t
    /\ \E v \in Server :
        /\ v # s
        /\ currentTerm[v] < t \/ (currentTerm[v] = t /\ votedFor[v] \in {NoVote, s})
        /\ IsLogUpToDate(v, s)   \* election restriction
        /\ votedFor' = [votedFor EXCEPT ![v] = s]
        /\ votesGranted' = [votesGranted EXCEPT ![s] = votesGranted[s] \cup {v}]
        /\ UNCHANGED <<currentTerm, log, commitIndex, state, matchIndex, nextIndex>>
```

`AppendEntries`, `BecomeLeader`, `AdvanceCommitIndex`, and `Timeout` complete the action set. TLC checks, for configurations like three servers with terms and logs bounded by 3, that `StateMachineSafety` and `ElectionSafety` hold on all reachable states and that the spec is deadlock-free under weak fairness.

Three lessons emerge. First, *small-scope checking is brutally effective at falsification*: most protocol bugs manifest within tiny bounds. Second, writing the spec *before* the implementation surfaces ambiguities in the English description. Third, TLC's limits are structural: no unbounded-term proofs, no liveness without fairness annotations, and — critically — the verified artifact is the spec, not the code. That gap is what IronFleet and Verdi close.

### 4.2 The IronFleet refinement tower: from abstract RSL to Dafny executables

IronFleet [3] proves a system correct via a *tower of refinement*: each layer is a state machine, each adjacent pair joined by a machine-checked refinement proof. For IronRSL — a verified ZooKeeper-style replicated state log — the tower has four levels:

| Layer | Description | Language |
|---|---|---|
| L0 | Abstract spec: a single logical log with atomic append/read | Dafny (ghost) |
| L1 | Protocol spec: leaders, followers, ballots, 2-phase log replication | Dafny (ghost) |
| L2 | Implementation model: message handlers, timers, serialization | Dafny (ghost + code) |
| L3 | Executable: UDP networking, real timers, marshalling | Dafny → C# |

The key device is the *refinement relation* $R_i$ between layers $i$ and $i{+}1$: a coupling invariant (a Dafny predicate over paired states) such that every lower-layer step simulates an upper-layer step or stutters. Dafny discharges simulation obligations via SMT; the programmer supplies coupling invariants and occasional lemmas. By transitivity, the executable refines the abstract log: every behavior of the running system is a behavior of an atomic append-only log. Safety follows *for the code*, not merely a model of it.

The coupling invariant itself is an ordinary Dafny predicate over the paired states:

```dafny
predicate Refines(sLow: L2State, sHigh: L1State)
  // coupling invariant: every low-level step simulates a high-level step
  requires ValidLow(sLow) && ValidHigh(sHigh)
{
  && sHigh.log == AbstractLog(sLow)        // log projection
  && sHigh.ballot == sLow.currentBallot    // ballot agreement
  && (forall s :: s in LiveReplicas(sLow)
        ==> sHigh.committed <= sLow.commitIndex[s])
}
```

IronFleet verifies against an abstract network model (packets may drop or reorder) and composes with a verified shim. Liveness rests on explicit fairness assumptions — crashed hosts stay crashed, live timers eventually fire, the network eventually delivers — formalizing partial synchrony inside the prover. IronRSL's liveness theorem: *if a live client submits requests forever and a majority of replicas are live, every request is eventually executed and acknowledged.*

The cost is the headline number of every IronFleet discussion: the proof dwarfs the code and the project consumed multiple person-years [3]. Dafny's SMT backend discharges routine verification conditions, but the coupling invariants and liveness ranking functions are irreducibly human insight.

### 4.3 Inductive invariants: the load-bearing structure of every Raft proof

Whether checked by TLC, proved in Coq, or discharged by Dafny, every Raft verification rests on the same *inductive invariant*: a predicate $Inv$ with (i) $Init \Rightarrow Inv$ and (ii) $Inv \land Next \Rightarrow Inv'$. The safety theorem is a corollary; the art is *strengthening* with auxiliary facts that make the induction go through. Raft's standard bundle:

- **(I1) Election Safety (inductive form).** Two leaders in the same term $t$ must be the same server. The induction step uses quorum intersection: each leader collected a majority in term $t$, majorities intersect, and a server votes at most once per term — itself an auxiliary `votedFor`-monotonicity invariant.
- **(I2) Log Matching.** If $log_i[k] = \langle t, \_ \rangle$ and $log_j[k] = \langle t, \_ \rangle$, then $log_i[1..k] = log_j[1..k]$. The crux is the `AppendEntries` consistency check: a follower appends at $k{+}1$ only when its entry at $k$ matches the leader's term, inductively forcing prefix agreement — using Leader Append-Only so the referenced prefix is stable.
- **(I3) Leader Completeness.** If entry $e$ is committed in term $t$, every leader of term $t' > t$ contains $e$ — the deepest invariant. Its proof couples (I1) with the election restriction via case analysis on the candidate's last entry term ($>t$, $=t$, $<t$), using (I2) for prefix agreement. The subtle case informal arguments fumble is a candidate *longer but in an older term*: the up-to-date comparison must be lexicographic on (last term, length), and the commit rule's current-term restriction is what stops a stale longer log from winning and discarding a committed entry.
- **(I4) State Machine Safety.** From (I2) + (I3): servers apply entries in log order up to `commitIndex`, and committed prefixes agree.

> **Theorem (Leader Completeness, machine-checked form):** *For all servers $s$, terms $t < t'$: if $s$ is leader in $t'$ and entry $e$ was committed in $t$, then $e \in log_s$. The TLA+ proof requires the auxiliary invariants (I1)–(I2) and the current-term commit rule; TLC checks all three on bounded instances, while IronFleet's Dafny development proves them for unbounded executions via the refinement tower.* [1][2][3]

Invariant (I3) is where model checking earns its keep during *development*: tweak the election restriction and TLC instantly reports which auxiliary invariant breaks, with a minimal counterexample — a feedback loop interactive provers cannot match. The mature methodology is *layered*: prototype under TLC, then promote to full proof.

### 4.4 Verus and the verification of Rust consensus implementations

The newest front is verifying Rust consensus code, where the borrow checker already proves memory safety and data-race freedom, so Verus [8] targets *functional* correctness. Verus layers `spec` functions, `requires`/`ensures` contracts, ghost state, and linear ghost permissions onto Rust, discharging verification conditions with SMT solvers — the Dafny tradition embedded in a real systems language.

Consider the leader's handler advancing `matchIndex` and recomputing `commitIndex`. In Verus the log is a ghost sequence and the handler carries a contract:

```rust
fn advance_commit_index(&mut self, leader_term: int)
    requires
        self.is_leader(),
        // (I2) ghost: log matching holds for all tracked followers
        forall|f: ServerId| self.log_matching(f),
    ensures
        // commit index only moves forward ...
        self.commit_index >= old(self).commit_index,
        // ... and only over entries from the leader's term (I3's commit rule)
        forall|k: int| old(self).commit_index < k && k <= self.commit_index
            ==> self.log@[k].term == leader_term,
        // Leader Completeness preserved
        self.leader_completeness(),
{ /* ... implementation ... */ }
```

Three features make this tractable. First, *linear ghost state* tracks vote and replication multisets with the multiplicity reasoning quorum arguments need. Second, *`proof` blocks* guide quantifier instantiation for the (I1)–(I3) invariants. Third, Verus verifies the *actual Rust code* compiled by `rustc` — no model-to-code gap; the refinement chain collapses to one SMT-checked link.

The caveat is maturity: Verus consensus verification is younger than IronFleet's, end-to-end liveness proofs remain research-grade, and the TCB now includes SMT quantifier reasoning over sequences. But as Rust displaces C++ in storage systems, the verifier that meets programmers in their own language has the lowest adoption barrier.

### 4.5 Liveness under partial synchrony: what the safety proofs omit

FLP [6] forces every liveness claim to name its synchrony assumption; Raft's honest liveness statement is conditional: *after GST, with a live majority and sustainable leadership, commands are eventually committed.* Three sub-obligations compose this claim:

1. **Election termination.** After GST, randomized timeouts eventually yield a unique majority winner; the argument is probabilistic, handled in IronFleet by a fairness-based *leads-to* argument rather than a quantitative bound.
2. **Log convergence.** A stable leader's `AppendEntries` retries drive every live follower's log to match its own — a ranking-function argument over the follower–leader log "distance."
3. **Commit propagation.** The leader advances `commitIndex` over majority-replicated own-term entries and disseminates it; followers apply in order.

IronFleet alone mechanizes all three [3], and its liveness proof is the harder half: safety invariants are *state* predicates, while liveness needs *temporal* reasoning (leads-to, fairness, ranking functions) that SMT automation supports poorly. The lesson generalizes: *proving safety without liveness verifies the easy half* — and production consensus failures (leader churn under asymmetric partitions) live in the other half.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

We summarize the four developments; numbers are approximate, drawn from the cited publications.

| System | Vehicle | Safety | Liveness | Proof:code (approx.) | TCB highlights |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Raft TLA+ [2] | TLA+/TLC | Bounded (3 srv, ≤3 terms) | Not addressed | Spec only | TLC, PlusCal translator |
| Verdi Raft [4] | Coq | Full (unbounded) | Partial | High (~10:1 with transformers) | Coq kernel, extraction |
| IronFleet/IronRSL [3] | Dafny | Full + refinement to code | Full (leads-to, fairness) | High (several:1) | Dafny, Z3, C# compiler/runtime |
| Verus Rust Raft-style | Verus/Rust | In progress (core invariants) | Research-grade | Moderate (contracts inline) | Verus, Z3, rustc |

**Bounded checking finds bugs; unbounded proof prevents regressions**: TLC steered Raft's design; IronFleet's proof is a *regression suite with mathematical force*. **Transformers amortize proof** [4]: Verdi's verified system transformers let one core-logic proof serve multiple fault models. **Liveness is the differentiator**: only IronFleet proves it end to end.

On performance, verified systems pay a measurable but modest tax: IronRSL throughput is within a small constant factor of unverified equivalents [3]. The deeper cost is *development velocity* — the proof must be maintained alongside the code, and "obviously safe" optimizations (batching, read leases) each demand proof updates. No SMT automation has repealed this economic fact.

## 6. Limitations

**The specification can be wrong.** Every result is relative to a top-level spec, and a spec that misstates the requirement proves the wrong theorem flawlessly. Real Raft-adjacent bugs (stale reads from partitioned leaders, fixed by read-index/leases) sit *outside* the core spec; verifying Raft does not verify the storage system's linearizability unless the composition is modeled, as IronRSL attempts [3].

**The trusted computing base is large and load-bearing.** A Dafny proof trusts Dafny's translation, Z3, the C# compiler, the CLR, the OS scheduler's fairness (for liveness!), and the network model. Solver *soundness* bugs are rare but not hypothetical, and liveness trusts the deployment to honor fairness assumptions — strong under adversarial scheduling.

**Partial synchrony is assumed, not established.** No development proves a deployment *satisfies* partial synchrony; GST is an axiom about the world. "Verified live" systems can still stall forever in a network that never stabilizes, and the probabilistic election argument yields no usable time bound.

**Proof economics remain unfavorable.** Person-years of proof for thousands of lines of code [3] prices full verification out of all but the highest-assurance contexts. The most promising cost reducers — Verus-style inline contracts and Verdi-style transformers — are the least mature for liveness.

**Concurrency and crash modeling are idealized.** Verified models treat crashes as clean halts and assume stable-storage semantics that real disks honor only approximately. IronRSL's verified-ZooKeeper lesson is encouraging but narrow: one service, one fault model, one stack.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Machine-checked verification moved consensus from prose arguments to proof-assistant arguments. TLA+ made Raft's *design* precise and falsifiable [2]; Verdi showed verified abstractions *compose* across fault models [4]; IronFleet proved safety *and liveness* of a real ZooKeeper-class service down to the code [3]; Verus carries the program into Rust, where the next generation of consensus is being written [8].

The through-line is the inductive invariant bundle of Section 4.3 — Election Safety, Log Matching, Leader Completeness, State Machine Safety. These four predicates are the actual content of "Raft is correct"; every verification technology is a different machine for establishing and preserving them across refinement steps, differing in *where* (spec vs. code), *how far* (bounded vs. unbounded, safety vs. liveness), and *cost*.

For practitioners, a staged adoption path. **Stage 1:** write a TLA+ spec of any new consensus-adjacent protocol and model-check it with TLC before coding — cheap, and catches most design bugs. **Stage 2:** maintain the inductive invariant bundle as machine-checked contracts in the implementation language (Verus for Rust) so regressions fail the build. **Stage 3:** reserve full IronFleet-style refinement-and-liveness proof for the small, stable, high-value core whose failure would be catastrophic. Verification, like testing, has diminishing returns; this literature tells us exactly where each technique sits on that curve.
