# Gradual Typing: the Dynamic Type, Blame Calculus, and the Performance of Sound Interoperation

## Abstract

Gradual typing unites static and dynamic disciplines in a single language with sound interoperation across the typed–untyped boundary. This thesis develops the theory from foundations to performance frontier. We begin with Siek and Taha's dynamic type `?` and the precision order that turns migration from scripts to programs into a theorem. We then reconstruct Wadler and Findler's blame calculus — the cast-insertion intermediate language in which every runtime cast carries a blame label, and whose Blame Theorem proves well-typed regions can never be blamed for cast failures. We contrast the three dominant enforcement strategies — guarded contracts, transient tag checks, and monotonic type state — and explain why full soundness can cost orders of magnitude in runtime overhead, drawing on the Typed Racket lattice studies. Finally, we show how space-efficient coercions, threesome factorization, and monotonic references recover constant-space tail calls without sacrificing blame: a unified account of how far soundness stretches before performance demands principled compromise.

---

## 1. Introduction

Static and dynamic type systems embody a genuine engineering tradeoff. Static typing delivers *early error detection*, *machine-checked documentation*, and *optimized execution*; dynamic typing delivers *rapid prototyping* and *graceful evolution of data representations*. Large systems are heterogeneous: a prototype migrates toward production, a typed core calls into untyped plugins, a scripting layer orchestrates compiled libraries. What was missing was a *principled* account of the boundary.

Gradual typing supplies that account. Introduced by Siek and Taha, it adds a single new type — the **dynamic (or unknown) type**, written `?` — to an otherwise static discipline. A program in which every annotation is `?` behaves like its dynamic counterpart; one with no `?` behaves like its static counterpart; everything in between is checked by a type system conservative over both extremes. The crucial innovation is not the permissive checker but the **refined criteria**: type safety, the *gradual guarantee* (adding precision never introduces new behavior), and blame safety [5][8].

This thesis argues three claims. **First**, the dynamic type `?` is best understood not as "the absence of types" but as the *top element of a precision lattice*, with consistency — not subtyping — as its characteristic relation. **Second**, soundness across the boundary is inseparable from *blame*: Wadler and Findler's blame calculus turns the static/dynamic boundary into explicit, labeled casts, and the resulting Blame Theorem is the formal content of the intuition that "the untyped code is at fault" [1]. **Third**, soundness is not free: naive higher-order casts degrade tail calls from constant to linear space and can slow programs by orders of magnitude [2][7], but transient checking, space-efficient coercion normalization, and monotonic references recover much of the lost ground.

The remainder of this thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the background: the gradually typed lambda calculus, precision, consistency, and the cast-calculus pipeline. Section 3 describes our methodology. Section 4 dives deep into the four pillars — the dynamic type, the blame calculus, the transient/guarded/monotonic spectrum, and space efficiency. Section 5 presents the empirical performance results and the proof-theoretic results on blame and space. Section 6 confronts the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The gradually typed lambda calculus



```haskell
-- Gradual types: base types, functions, references, and the dynamic type
data GType = GInt | GBool | GFun GType GType | GRef GType | Dyn
```

Two relations organize the system. **Precision**, written `T₁ ⊑ T₂` ("`T₁` is less precise than `T₂`"), is the partial order in which static types are minimal and `?` is the maximum: `Int ⊑ ?`, `? → ? ⊑ Int → Bool`, with structural rules for function and reference constructors. Precision makes *type migration* meaningful: replacing `?` by a more precise type is movement down the lattice, and the gradual guarantee demands such movement be behavior-preserving. **Consistency**, written `T₁ ~ T₂`, is the symmetric relation replacing subtyping in the typing rules: `Int ~ Int`, `? ~ T` and `T ~ ?` for all `T`, and `T₁→T₂ ~ T₁'→T₂'` when `T₁ ~ T₁'` and `T₂ ~ T₂'`. Where subtyping says "this value can always be used here," consistency says "this value *might* be usable here; check at runtime."

Whenever the rule's premise uses consistency rather than subtyping, the compiler must insert a runtime cast — the seed of everything that follows.

### 2.2 From surface types to cast calculi

A gradual surface language is elaborated into a **cast calculus**: an explicitly typed intermediate language in which every use of consistency becomes a cast term `⟨T₁ ⇒ᵖ T₂⟩ e`, carrying a *blame label* `p` identifying the source location. The cast calculus leaves open *how* casts are enforced — eagerly or lazily, with wrappers or with checks — a design space spanning Typed Racket's guarded contracts, Reticulated Python's transient tag checks, and TypeScript's deliberate erasure. We survey these strategies in §4.3 after establishing the blame calculus that makes their guarantees comparable.

This elaboration strategy, shared by gradual types, hybrid types, and multi-language interoperation, reduces the metatheory of gradual typing to the metatheory of one intermediate language [5][8].

### 2.3 The cost of soundness: space and time

Two performance pathologies haunted early gradual implementations. The **space pathology**, identified by Herman, Tomb, and Flanagan, is that naive higher-order casts accumulate: two mutually recursive functions crossing the typed/untyped boundary in tail position wrap each other in one more proxy per call, degrading a constant-space loop into linear-space allocation and breaking proper tail calls [2]. The **time pathology**, quantified by Takikawa, Feltey, Greenman, New, Vitek, and Felleisen, is that sound enforcement at fine-grained boundaries can slow Typed Racket programs by one to two orders of magnitude relative to their untyped versions — enough to provoke the paper's titular question, *"Is sound gradual typing dead?"* [7].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is expository-formal: we reconstruct the central definitions, state the key theorems with proof sketches, and synthesize the empirical record from the published benchmark literature.

1. **Formal reconstruction.** We present `λ_?→`, its precision order and consistency relation, the elaboration to the blame calculus `λ_B` with labeled casts `⟨T₁ ⇒ᵖ T₂⟩`, and the operational semantics of casts — the now-standard surface-to-cast-calculus pipeline [1][5][8].
2. **Proof sketches.** We sketch the Blame Theorem via Wadler and Findler's decomposition of subtyping into positive and negative subtyping, and the space-efficiency result via threesome normalization [1][2][3].
3. **Empirical synthesis.** We report the measured overheads from the Typed Racket performance lattice [7] and the transient-vs-guarded comparison in Reticulated.

We take as our correctness standard the **refined criteria** of Siek, Vitousek, Cimini, and Boyland: type safety, conservative extension of the static and dynamic languages, the gradual guarantees, and blame safety [5]. Siek and Chen's parameterized cast calculi later mechanized these criteria in Agda for eight cast-calculus instantiations, giving a reusable yardstick for each design [6].

> **Theorem: (Static gradual guarantee, informal).** If `e` is well-typed and `e ⊑ e'` (i.e., `e'` is obtained by making some annotations more precise), then `e'` is well-typed. Dually, removing precision preserves typability. The *dynamic* gradual guarantee states that making annotations more precise can only make a program *fail more* (blame the new precision) — never change a successful result into a different successful result.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The dynamic type `?` and the precision lattice

The single most misunderstood idea in gradual typing is the dynamic type itself. It is tempting to read `?` as "no type information," but the precision lattice gives it exact content: `?` is the *greatest element*, the type consistent with everything. In Figure 1 (`ths_1789144151011_13282-0.webp`), static types sit at the bottom while `?` crowns the lattice, with `? → ?` and `Int → ?` as waypoints on the migration path from script to program.

This lattice structure is what makes the gradual guarantee *stateable*. Migration is lattice descent: replacing `?` with a more precise type. The static gradual guarantee says descent preserves typability; the dynamic one says it preserves observable behavior up to blame. Without precision as a partial order, "adding types" would be a refactoring heuristic; with it, it is a theorem [8].

Consistency `~` is the working relation of the type checker, and it is deliberately *not* transitive — `Int ~ ?` and `? ~ Bool` do not imply `Int ~ Bool`. This non-transitivity is the price of admitting a top element: the checker is optimistic, and the runtime casts inserted at each consistency use are where optimism is audited.

### 4.2 Cast insertion and the blame calculus

Elaboration translates each consistency use into an explicit cast. The application rule becomes `e₁ (⟨T₁' ⇒ᵖ T₁⟩ e₂)` with a fresh blame label `p` — the optimism of consistency, audited at runtime.

The target language is Wadler and Findler's **blame calculus** `λ_B` [1]. A cast `⟨T₁ ⇒ᵖ T₂⟩ v` checks a value against the target type; on failure it raises `blame p` (or the *complement* label `blame p̄`, depending on polarity). For higher-order casts the checking is lazy and directional: casting `⟨T₁→T₂ ⇒ᵖ T₁'→T₂'⟩ f` wraps `f` so that *arguments* are cast from `T₁'` to `T₁` (blaming the *context* on failure — negative blame `p̄`) and *results* are cast from `T₂` to `T₂'` (blaming the *function* — positive blame `p`). Figure 2 (`ths_1789144151011_13282-1.webp`) depicts this flow: a value crossing the boundary is wrapped, and each subsequent elimination consults the labels to attribute violations to the correct side.

The masterstroke of [1] is the analysis of *where* each polarity of blame can arise, via the decomposition of subtyping into positive (`<:+`, codomain direction safe) and negative (`<:-`, domain direction safe) subtyping. Ordinary subtyping is their conjunction; **naive subtyping** (`<:n`) factors as positive in one direction and negative in the other. The Blame Theorem then follows from standard progress-and-preservation, avoiding the heavy operational-equivalence machinery of earlier contract-blame proofs:

> **Theorem: (Blame safety — "well-typed programs can't be blamed").** Let `t` be a closed, well-typed term containing a cast `⟨D ⇐ C⟩ᵖ` as its only occurrence of label `p`. If `C <: D` (the cast goes from a subtype to a supertype) then `t` never reduces to `blame p` or `blame p̄`. If `C <:n D` (naive subtyping, i.e., `C` more precise), then `t` never reduces to `blame p` — positive blame always lies with the *less precisely typed* side.

In plain terms: blame for a cast failure is always allocated to the less-precisely-typed party. The typed side of the boundary is exonerated *by construction*, not by testing — the formal content of the slogan that contracts between typed and untyped code protect the typed code [1][4].

The framework generalizes. Wadler's "blame for all" results extend the theorem to arbitrary coercions and the full coercion calculus `λ_C`, with the *Fundamental Property of Casts* established via full abstraction rather than bespoke bisimulations [4]. Siek and Chen's parameterized calculi then showed that blame safety, the gradual guarantees, and space bounds can be proved *once*, generically, and instantiated across eight cast calculi [6].

### 4.3 Transient vs. guarded vs. monotonic semantics

The blame calculus leaves open *how* casts are enforced. Three answers dominate.

**Guarded semantics** (Typed Racket). A cast on a higher-order value installs a *proxy* — a wrapper that interposes checks on every future use. Calling a wrapped function checks the argument against the domain type and the result against the codomain type, recursively wrapping again at higher order. Guarded semantics gives the *strongest* guarantees: full blame precision and *deep* invariants — a value of type `(Int → Int) → Int` is monitored at every level. The cost is the two pathologies of §2.3: proxies accumulate in tail position (space), and every boundary crossing pays for wrapper allocation and indirection (time). Moreover, proxies break *object identity*, which matters for languages where identity is observable.

**Transient semantics** (Reticulated Python). The transient strategy refuses to wrap. Instead, the compiler inserts *shallow checks* — tag tests like "is this value's runtime tag consistent with `Int`?" — at every *use site* within typed code. No wrappers means no space leak, no identity crisis, and far lower constant factors. The price is *weaker* guarantees: only first-order, shallow properties are enforced, and blame is coarser — a failure is blamed on the nearest typed use site rather than the exact boundary crossing. Transient semantics satisfies a *weaker* variant of the gradual guarantee, trading precision of diagnosis for performance [5].

**Monotonic semantics** (references and objects). Mutable state poses a special problem: a reference cell `Ref ?` later refined to `Ref Int` cannot be handled by wrapping (which breaks aliasing — two aliases must see the same cell) or by shallow checks (which cannot prevent a later `Bool` write through an old alias). The **monotonic** strategy gives each reference a *type state* that only ever becomes *more precise*: casting a cell from `Ref ?` to `Ref Int` *refines the cell itself*, and all subsequent reads and writes are checked against the most precise state so far, with violations blamed on the writer. Because precision only increases, a value that passed yesterday's check cannot fail tomorrow's — hence "monotonic." This recovers soundness for mutable references without proxies, and it is the strategy Reticulated adopted for Python objects [5][6].

| Strategy | Enforcement | Proxies? | Blame quality | Identity | Overhead |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Guarded | deep contracts at boundaries | yes | exact boundary | broken | 10×–100× |
| Transient | shallow tag checks at use sites | no | nearest use site | preserved | low single-digit |
| Monotonic | refining type state on cells | no | exact for refs | preserved | moderate |
| Erasure (TypeScript) | none | no | none | preserved | ~1× (unsound) |

The table's last row is the cautionary tale: TypeScript's `any` and bivariant methods buy zero-cost interoperation by abandoning soundness — pragmatic for an ecosystem, but not gradual typing in the technical sense [5].

### 4.4 Space efficiency: threesomes and coercions

Even with guarded semantics, the space pathology is fixable without weakening guarantees. The key insight of Herman, Tomb, and Flanagan is that a *sequence* of casts can be *normalized* into a single cast of bounded size [2]. A cast is a "twosome" `T₁ ⇒ T₂`; any twosome factors through an intermediate type into a **threesome** `(T₁ ⇒ T₂ ⇒ T₃)` — a downcast to the intermediate type followed by an upcast — and any *chain* of threesomes collapses into one by taking greatest lower bounds of the intermediate types. Tail calls wrapped in casts therefore consume constant space: the even/odd example that naively accumulates `⟨Bool ⇔ ?⟩⟨? ⇔ Bool⟩…` instead normalizes at each step.

Henglein's coercion calculus gives this idea a runtime representation: casts compile to *coercions*, and coercion composition `c₁ ; c₂` is normalized eagerly [2]. Wadler and Findler's "Threesomes, With and Without Blame" showed the factorization works *with* blame labels intact — the collapsed threesome remembers which original twosome to blame, so space efficiency costs no diagnostic precision [3]. And "Blame and Coercion: Together Again for the First Time" unified the two calculi: `λ_B` (blame) and `λ_C` (coercions) are related by full abstraction, and the Fundamental Property of Casts transfers across [4].

Modern implementations internalize these lessons. Siek and Chen's parameterized framework includes space-efficient instantiations *by construction* — the "space-efficient" parameter is exactly coercion normalization with a size bound [6]. The lesson generalizes: any system layering runtime checks on higher-order values must normalize check sequences or accept space leaks.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The blame theorem, precisely

We restate the central proof-theoretic result with its full generality [1]:

> **Theorem: (Blame safety).** Let `t` be closed and well-typed in `λ_B`, with `⟨D ⇐ C⟩ᵖ s` the only occurrence of blame label `p` in `t`. (i) If `C <: D` then `t` never reduces to `blame p` nor `blame p̄`. (ii) If `C <:n D` then `t` never reduces to `blame p`. (iii) If `D <:n C` then `t` never reduces to `blame p̄`.

The proof proceeds by progress and preservation over a type system tracking, for each cast, whether positive or negative blame is *possible*. The crucial lemma is the factoring of subtyping: `C <: D` iff `C <:+ D` and `C <:- D`, and naive subtyping `C <:n D` iff `C <:+ D` and `D <:- C` (note the reversal). Since gradual precision coincides with naive subtyping, clauses (ii) and (iii) say exactly that blame follows imprecision — no operational equivalence, no logical relations, just Wright–Felleisen soundness with blame labels threaded through.

### 5.2 The performance lattice: "Is sound gradual typing dead?"

Takikawa et al. subjected Typed Racket to the most thorough performance study of sound gradual typing to date [7]. Rather than benchmarking hand-picked configurations, they measured *every* configuration of the typed/untyped module lattice — all 2ⁿ assignments of modules to typed or untyped. The results were sobering: fully-typed configurations of some benchmarks ran *slower* than fully-untyped ones, and mixed configurations exhibited worst-case slowdowns of one to two orders of magnitude, concentrated at boundaries crossed in hot loops. The overhead is a *boundary-crossing* tax: each crossing allocates wrappers and performs deep contract checks, so performance is a function of boundary *traffic*, not program size.

The study's conclusion was nuanced rather than fatal.

### 5.3 Space-efficiency, formally

> **Theorem: (Space efficiency, Herman–Tomb–Flanagan).** In the coercion-based cast calculus, every sequence of casts normalizes to a coercion of bounded size, and evaluation of a well-typed program consumes space proportional to the underlying untyped program — in particular, tail calls execute in constant space regardless of how many casts intervene [2][3].

## 6. Limitations

**The dynamic gradual guarantee is fragile.** It demands that adding precision never alter successful behavior, but extensions enforcing *hyperproperties* (e.g., information-flow disciplines) admit counterexamples, and parametric polymorphism required the "Jack-of-All-Trades" conjecture plus a static/gradual type-variable distinction before its metatheory stabilized [5].

**Transient blame is coarse.** By refusing to track values across boundaries, transient semantics can only blame the nearest typed use site — a genuine ergonomic cost when debugging large migrations, since *no* transient check catches a higher-order violation until the misbehaving value is observed at first order.

**Migration itself is hard.** Checking whether a program admits *some* well-typed migration is tractable, but finding a *maximal* migration is NP-hard via reduction from 3SAT [8]; automated tools settle for heuristic, non-maximal migrations.

**The object-oriented frontier remains open.** Gradual objects must reconcile width/depth subtyping with precision, mutable state with aliasing, and blame with method dispatch — a combination for which no single semantics is fully satisfactory, and where space-efficient blame tracking for effectful programs is still active research [2][5].

**Empirical gaps.** The performance lattice exists for Typed Racket's guarded semantics; comparable exhaustive studies for transient and monotonic implementations are thinner. The overhead table in §4.3 should be read as order-of-magnitude guidance, not a controlled experiment.

## 7. Conclusion

Gradual typing began as a modest proposal — add `?` to the type system and check the rest at runtime — and matured into one of the most technically demanding areas of programming-language research. Its arc runs from Siek and Taha's precision lattice, through Wadler and Findler's blame calculus and its theorem that well-typed programs can't be blamed, through the sobering performance measurements that asked whether soundness was affordable, to the engineering response: transient checking, coercion normalization, threesome factorization, and monotonic references. Each step preserved the core promise — *sound, blamed, space-efficient interoperation between typed and untyped code* — while renegotiating its price.

The enduring lesson is methodological. The cast-calculus pipeline — elaborate the surface language into an explicit intermediate language, prove the metatheory once, instantiate many semantics — turned a sprawling design space into a disciplined science [6][8]. Blame transformed an informal debugging intuition ("the dynamic code must be wrong") into a theorem with a proof. As gradual typing moves into production systems, the questions this thesis examined will recur in new guises: what is the precision order, where are the casts inserted, who gets blamed, and what does it cost? The answers will differ; the questions are now permanent.
