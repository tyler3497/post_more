# Matching Markets: Gale–Shapley Deferred Acceptance, Strategy-Proofness, and the Design of Two-Sided Markets

## Abstract

Two-sided matching markets — from medical residency placement to public school assignment and kidney exchange — allocate indivisible goods where prices cannot clear the market: a student cannot buy a seat at a school that will not admit her. This thesis develops the modern theory and engineering of such markets, beginning with the Gale–Shapley deferred acceptance algorithm of 1962 [1], which guarantees a *stable* matching for any profile of strict preferences. We characterize the distributive lattice structure of the set of stable matchings, prove that deferred acceptance is strategy-proof for the proposing side [3] yet that no stable mechanism can be strategy-proof for both sides [2], and derive the rural hospitals theorem constraining which positions can ever be filled [9]. We then trace the mechanism-design pipeline from theory to practice: the redesign of the National Resident Matching Program [8], the replacement of the manipulable Boston mechanism by deferred acceptance in school choice [6][10], and the top-trading-cycles engine behind large-scale kidney exchange [7]. Empirical evidence, complexity results, and the boundary case of couples with complementary preferences complete the picture.

## 1. Introduction

Most of economic theory presumes that prices mediate exchange. Yet some of the most consequential allocations in modern life — which resident trains at which hospital, which child attends which public school, whose kidney saves whose life — are **two-sided matching markets** in which money is absent, forbidden, or ethically repugnant. Here an allocation is not a price vector but a *matching*: a pairing of agents on two sides of a market, each side holding preferences over the other. The central question is what properties such an allocation should satisfy, and which procedures can achieve them.

The modern answer begins with David Gale and Lloyd Shapley [1]. In 1962 they asked whether a set of colleges and applicants, or men and women, could always be paired so that no unmatched pair would both prefer each other to their assigned partners — a condition they called *stability*. Their deferred acceptance algorithm answered affirmatively and constructively, and its intellectual lineage runs directly to the 2012 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences, awarded to Lloyd Shapley and Alvin Roth "for the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design."

This thesis reconstructs that lineage in full. Section 2 formalizes the model. Section 3 presents the algorithm and its optimality properties. Section 4 dives into five themes: the lattice structure of stable matchings, the strategy-proofness frontier, school choice, kidney exchange, and the NRMP with couples. Section 5 marshals empirical results and proof sketches; Section 6 confronts the theory's limits; Section 7 concludes with open frontiers.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The stable marriage model

A **two-sided one-to-one matching market** consists of two disjoint finite sets, conventionally *men* $M$ and *women* $W$ (in applications: students and schools, residents and hospitals, workers and firms), with $|M| = |W| = n$. Each agent has a **strict** preference ordering over the agents on the other side, possibly declaring some partners *unacceptable* (preferring to remain unmatched). A **matching** $\mu$ is a bijection pairing each man with at most one woman and vice versa. A pair $(m, w)$ **blocks** $\mu$ if $m$ prefers $w$ to $\mu(m)$ and $w$ prefers $m$ to $\mu(w)$. A matching is **stable** if it is *individually rational* (no one is matched to an unacceptable partner) and admits no blocking pair.

> **Theorem (Gale–Shapley, 1962):** For every profile of strict preferences, a stable matching exists [1].

The college-admissions generalization allows each college $c$ a **quota** $q_c$ and preferences over *groups* of students. When college preferences are **responsive** — meaning the college's ranking of individual students extends consistently to sets — the one-to-one results carry over to the many-to-one case [5].

### 2.2 A market that failed, then a clearinghouse

The motivating real market was the American market for medical interns. In the 1940s, hospitals competed by making exploding offers earlier and earlier — a pathology economists call **unraveling** — until appointments were settled two years before graduation on almost no information [4]. The National Resident Matching Program (NRMP), founded in 1952, centralized the market: both sides submitted rank-order lists, and a clearinghouse computed the match. Roth showed that the procedure the NRMP had stumbled upon was essentially the hospital-proposing deferred acceptance algorithm, and that its stability explained the clearinghouse's survival where unstable predecessors collapsed [4].

The pattern — *a decentralized market fails; a clearinghouse running deferred acceptance succeeds* — would repeat in school choice and kidney exchange, and it is the founding narrative of market design.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The deferred acceptance algorithm

With men proposing, the algorithm proceeds in rounds [1]:

1. Each unmatched man proposes to the most-preferred woman who has not yet rejected him.
2. Each woman tentatively *holds* her most-preferred proposal received so far (including any held from a previous round) and rejects the rest.
3. Repeat until no rejections occur; tentative holds become final.

Termination is immediate: no man proposes to the same woman twice, so at most $n^2$ proposals occur. Stability follows from a simple invariant — a woman's held partner only improves over time — so any man she rejects ends matched to someone she likes better, and any man who never proposed to her likes his own partner better. Hence no blocking pair can form.

> **Theorem (Optimality):** The man-proposing deferred acceptance algorithm produces the **man-optimal stable matching**: every man is matched to his most-preferred achievable partner among all stable matchings, and every woman to her least-preferred [1][5].

The following reference implementation makes the procedure executable:

```python
def deferred_acceptance(proposers, prefs_p, prefs_r):
    """Men-proposing DA. prefs_p[m]: list of receivers, best first.
       prefs_r[w]: ranking dict receiver -> rank (lower is better)."""
    free = list(proposers)
    next_idx = {m: 0 for m in proposers}
    hold = {}          # receiver -> current proposer
    while free:
        m = free.pop(0)
        w = prefs_p[m][next_idx[m]]
        next_idx[m] += 1
        if w not in hold:
            hold[w] = m
        elif prefs_r[w][m] < prefs_r[w][hold[w]]:
            free.append(hold[w])   # jilted proposer returns to the pool
            hold[w] = m
        else:
            free.append(m)          # rejected; proposes again next round
    return {m: w for w, m in hold.items()}
```

A priority-queue refinement by McVitie and Wilson achieves the same output with each agent processed once, a staple of algorithms courses [11].

### 3.2 The core and the mechanism-design stance

In cooperative game theory, the **core** of a matching market is the set of matchings no coalition can improve upon by rematching among themselves. For strict preferences, the core of the one-to-one market coincides exactly with the set of stable matchings [5]. A **mechanism** is a game form mapping reported preference profiles to matchings; it is **strategy-proof** for an agent if truthful reporting is a dominant strategy. The mechanism-design question is which desiderata — stability, strategy-proofness, efficiency — can coexist.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The lattice structure of stable matchings

The set of stable matchings is not a shapeless cloud; it carries rich algebraic structure. Say $\mu$ **dominates** $\nu$ from the men's perspective, written $\mu \succeq_M \nu$, if every man weakly prefers his partner in $\mu$. Conway observed, and Knuth formalized, that under this order the stable matchings form a **finite distributive lattice** [5]:

- The **join** $\mu \vee_M \nu$ assigns each man his preferred partner of the two; the **meet** $\mu \wedge_M \nu$ assigns each man his less-preferred partner. Both are stable.
- The lattice's top element is the man-optimal stable matching $\mu^M$; its bottom is the woman-optimal $\mu^W$.
- The man-optimal and woman-optimal matchings are *polar opposites*: what is best achievable for one side is worst achievable for the other.

This structure is algorithmically potent. *Rotations* — minimal cyclic differences between stable matchings — generate the entire lattice, enabling enumeration, counting, and optimization (for example, finding the stable matching minimizing the egalitarian cost) in polynomial time [11]. The lattice viewpoint also yields a compact Haskell-flavored encoding of the order operations:

```haskell
-- Join and meet in the lattice of stable matchings (men's order)
joinM :: Matching -> Matching -> Prefs -> Matching
joinM mu nu prefs = Map.fromList
  [ (m, preferred m (partner mu m) (partner nu m) prefs) | m <- men ]

meetM :: Matching -> Matching -> Prefs -> Matching
meetM mu nu prefs = Map.fromList
  [ (m, dispreferred m (partner mu m) (partner nu m) prefs) | m <- men ]
```

Counting stable matchings is **#P-complete** in general, yet the average instance has only on the order of $n \log n$ of them — the lattice is typically shallow even when it can be exponentially deep in pathological instances [5].

### 4.2 Strategy-proofness and Roth's impossibility

Deferred acceptance is not merely stable; it is *safe to use* for one side of the market.

> **Theorem (Dubins–Freedman, 1981; Roth, 1982):** In the man-proposing deferred acceptance mechanism, truthful preference revelation is a dominant strategy for every man [2][3].

*Proof sketch.* Suppose man $m$ deviates and the mechanism returns a matching $\mu'$ in which $m$ does better than under truthful reporting ($\mu(m)$). Consider the first round at which the deviation changes the algorithm's trajectory relative to truth-telling; the standard "rejection chains" argument shows that $m$'s deviation can only cause women he truly prefers to $\mu'(m)$ to reject him earlier, never improving his outcome. Hence no profitable deviation exists. ∎

But the proposing side's privilege is the receiving side's vulnerability: women *can* profitably misreport under man-proposing DA [2]. Worse, the asymmetry is unavoidable:

> **Theorem (Roth, 1982 — Impossibility):** There exists **no** mechanism that always produces a stable matching with respect to true preferences and is strategy-proof for *all* agents on both sides of the market [2].

Market design therefore faces a genuine trilemma among stability, two-sided incentive compatibility, and efficiency. The practical resolution — used by every real clearinghouse — is to run deferred acceptance with the *more vulnerable or more important* side proposing, accepting manipulability by the receiving side as the price of stability.

One more structural result disciplines expectations about *who* gets matched. In the many-to-one college-admissions model:

> **Theorem (Rural Hospitals, Roth 1986):** The set of agents matched, and the number of positions filled at each college, is **identical across all stable matchings**. A hospital that fails to fill a slot in one stable matching fails in every stable matching [9].

A rural hospital cannot escape its unpopularity by switching mechanisms among stable ones; only an unstable (or preference-changing) intervention can help.

### 4.3 School choice: Boston versus deferred acceptance

School choice repurposes matching theory for public education: students rank schools, schools hold priorities (sibling status, walk zones, lotteries), and a mechanism assigns seats. For decades Boston used the **immediate acceptance (Boston) mechanism**: first choices are processed first, then second choices among remaining seats, and so on. The Boston mechanism is *transparently manipulable* — ranking a popular school second is nearly equivalent to discarding the choice, since its seats fill in round one — forcing families into strategic guessing games that reward sophistication over need [6].

Abdulkadiroğlu and Sönmez proposed replacing it with student-proposing deferred acceptance, which is strategy-proof for students and eliminates the penalty for truthful ranking [6]. The reform was adopted in Boston (2005) and New York City, whose high-school match — over 90,000 students annually — became the largest live deployment of matching theory [10]. The table below summarizes the design contrast:

| Property | Boston (immediate acceptance) | Student-proposing DA |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy-proof for students | No — sophisticated ranking required | Yes [6] |
| Stability w.r.t. priorities | Not guaranteed | Guaranteed |
| Student-optimality | No | Yes, among stable matchings |
| Fairness concern | Rewards strategic families | Truth-telling is safe |

Empirically, the switch measurably reduced the "strategy tax" on unsophisticated families, though it also surfaced a philosophical tension: DA's stability can assign a student her tenth choice while a Pareto improvement for students alone exists — strategy-proofness and stability jointly preclude student-side Pareto efficiency [6].

### 4.4 Kidney exchange and market design without money

Kidney transplantation faces a compatibility barrier: a willing donor may be blood-type or immunologically incompatible with her intended recipient. **Kidney exchange** pools such incompatible patient–donor pairs so that donor $A$ gives to patient $B$ while donor $B$ gives to patient $A$ — a two-cycle — or longer cycles and chains initiated by altruistic non-directed donors [7].

Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver showed that the **top trading cycles (TTC)** mechanism, adapted from Shapley and Scarf's housing market, yields efficient, individually rational, and strategy-proof allocations for paired donation [7]. Real exchanges add logistical constraints: cycles longer than three or four transplants are hard to schedule simultaneously, and the optimization — packing a compatibility graph with vertex-disjoint cycles of bounded length — is NP-hard, solved in practice with integer programming over formulations with one variable per feasible cycle. Chains, which need no simultaneity, can be arbitrarily long and now produce the majority of transplants in organized exchange programs [7].

### 4.5 The NRMP, couples, and the limits of stability

The NRMP's 1998 redesign (Roth–Peranson) reversed the proposing side so that *residents* propose, made the algorithm handle supplemental rank lists and reversions, and — most delicately — accommodated **couples** who seek two positions in the same geography [8]. Couples introduce *complementarities*: a couple's preference over pairs of positions cannot be decomposed into independent responsive preferences. With couples, stable matchings may not exist at all, and deciding existence is NP-complete [5]. The redesigned algorithm therefore settles for an "almost stable" outcome, verified empirically: in two decades of operation, only a tiny fraction of matches admit blocking pairs, and the mechanism remains strategy-proof for the vast majority of single residents [8].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Scale.** The NRMP today matches roughly 50,000 applicants to 40,000 residency positions each year; its algorithm terminates in seconds, with the number of proposals bounded by the product of applicants and list lengths [8]. New York City's high-school match processes on the order of 90,000 rank-order lists annually [10]. Organized kidney exchange in the United States has facilitated tens of thousands of transplants through national registries.

**Manipulability in the wild.** Laboratory and field evidence confirms the theory's incentive predictions: under the Boston mechanism, a large share of families misreported strategically, and the families most harmed were those least equipped to strategize [6]. Under DA, truth-telling rates are high, and the mechanism's strategy-proofness demonstrably compresses the sophistication gap.

**Complexity landscape.** The following table collects the central computational results:

| Problem | Complexity |
|---|---|
| Find one stable matching (strict preferences) | $O(n^2)$, Gale–Shapley [1] |
| Enumerate all stable matchings via rotations | Polynomial per matching [11] |
| Count stable matchings | #P-complete [5] |
| Stable matching with couples (existence) | NP-complete [5] |
| Max-weight cycle packing (kidney exchange) | NP-hard (bounded cycles) [7] |

**Proof sketch: Roth's impossibility [2].** Assume a stable, two-sided strategy-proof mechanism. In a three-agent market one can construct preferences where stability forces the mechanism to select a particular matching, yet a receiving-side agent can profitably deviate to a truncated list, inducing instability under true preferences that the mechanism must then resolve in her favor — contradicting stability. Hence no mechanism satisfies both requirements universally. ∎

**Proof sketch: rural hospitals [9].** Suppose hospital $h$ fills $k$ of $q_h$ slots in stable matching $\mu$ but $k' > k$ in stable $\nu$. The lattice join $\mu \vee \nu$ (hospitals' order) would fill $\max(k,k')$ positions at $h$ while remaining stable; iterating the argument against the extremal elements of the lattice forces the fill count constant across the lattice. ∎

## 6. Limitations

The classical theory's elegance rests on assumptions that real markets strain:

1. **Strict, complete, known preferences.** Real rank-order lists are short (NRMP applicants rank a dozen programs out of thousands), and preference *formation* — interviews, school visits — is costly and endogenous to the mechanism.
2. **One-shot static clearing.** Labor markets are dynamic; exploding offers reappear in clerkships and fellowships despite clearinghouses, because timing itself is a strategic variable the static model omits.
3. **No complementarities.** Couples, and more generally firms hiring *teams*, break existence guarantees; the theory's core results assume substitutable, responsive preferences.
4. **Indifferences and ties.** School priorities contain massive ties (lottery numbers aside), and tie-breaking choices materially affect welfare and strategy-proofness; the strict-preference model is an idealization.
5. **Equity versus efficiency.** DA's stability can block Pareto improvements among students, and the "best stable" outcome for one side is the worst for the other — the lattice's polarity is a distributive fact, not a design choice.
6. **Verification and trust.** Participants must trust the clearinghouse's code and the secrecy of others' lists; a compromised or non-transparent implementation reintroduces manipulation through the back door.

## 7. Conclusion

From Gale and Shapley's nine-page note [1] to the software that assigns tens of thousands of residents and schoolchildren each year, two-sided matching theory is the rare body of mathematics that became infrastructure. Its three pillars — the *existence* of stable matchings via deferred acceptance, the *lattice* ordering those matchings from one side's heaven to the other's, and the *impossibility* of reconciling stability with two-sided strategy-proofness — jointly dictate the shape of every real clearinghouse. The engineering record is unambiguous: where the Boston mechanism punished honesty, deferred acceptance rewards it [6][10]; where decentralized markets unraveled, the NRMP's stable clearinghouse endured [4][8]; where money could not be used, cycle-and-chain exchange manufactured liquidity from incompatibility itself [7].

Open frontiers remain: dynamic matching with arrivals and timing games, approximately stable mechanisms for couples and team formation, and the integration of machine-learned preference estimates with strategy-proof design. But the core lesson is settled — *institutions are algorithms*, and their incentive properties must be proved, not assumed.
