# Neural Operators and Operator Learning: DeepONet, Fourier Neural Operators, and Universal Approximation of Solution Operators

## Abstract

Operator learning lifts deep learning from finite-dimensional regression to the approximation of nonlinear maps between infinite-dimensional function spaces. Instead of solving one partial differential equation instance at a time, a neural operator learns the entire solution operator: the map from coefficient fields, initial conditions, or boundary data to the corresponding solution fields. This article develops the mathematical foundations and practical machinery of the field. We state the universal approximation theorem for operators of Chen and Chen, explain how DeepONet realizes it through a branch-trunk factorization, and derive the Fourier neural operator layer that parameterizes global convolution in spectral space with discretization-invariant weights. We compare alternative architectures — graph neural operators, low-rank parameterizations, and nonlinear manifold decoders — and survey the approximation theory that quantifies error rates and shows how concrete PDE operators break the curse of dimensionality. Empirical landmarks are reviewed, from sub-percent errors on turbulent Navier-Stokes to FourCastNet's global weather forecasts, alongside the limitations that keep operator learning an active research frontier.

## 1 Introduction

Most of scientific machine learning still treats each physical system as an isolated regression problem. A physics-informed neural network is trained to satisfy one partial differential equation with one set of coefficients, one initial condition, and one domain [4]. Change the viscosity, the forcing, or the geometry, and the network must be retrained from scratch. Classical numerical analysis never had this weakness: a finite-element solver is a *method*, not a single solution, and it applies to every instance of the PDE family it discretizes.

Operator learning closes that gap. The object of interest is a **solution operator** *G* mapping an input function space into an output function space — for instance, the map from a permeability field *a(x)* to the pressure field *u(x)* solving the Darcy flow equation, or the map from an initial vorticity field to the flow state one time unit later. Once *G* is learned, evaluating it on a new input is a single forward pass, amortizing the cost of thousands of traditional solves over the lifetime of the model [3].

This article surveys the theory and practice of learning such operators. Section 2 fixes the function-space setting and states the universal approximation theorem that makes the enterprise possible. Section 3 describes how training data is generated and how the two dominant architectures — **DeepONet** [2] and the **Fourier neural operator (FNO)** [3] — encode the required invariances. Section 4 goes deeper into architectures, spectral convolution, alternative parameterizations, and approximation theory. Section 5 collects empirical evidence, Section 6 the limitations, and Section 7 the outlook.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The operator learning problem

Let *D* be a bounded domain in *R^d* and let *V* be a compact subset of a Banach space of functions on *D*, for example the continuous functions *C(D)* or a Sobolev space. A **nonlinear continuous operator** *G : V → C(K2)* maps each admissible input function *u* to an output function *G(u)* defined on a compact set *K2*. Canonical examples include:

- **Darcy flow.** The coefficient-to-solution map *a ↦ u* for the elliptic equation *−∇·(a∇u) = f* with fixed forcing and boundary conditions [3].
- **Burgers' equation.** The initial-condition-to-solution map *u0 ↦ u(·, T)* for the viscous Burgers PDE [4].
- **Navier-Stokes.** The map from initial vorticity to the vorticity field at a later time in the turbulent regime [3].
- **Antiderivative.** The map *u ↦ s* with *s(x) = ∫u(y)dy* over the unit interval [7].

Learning *G* means producing a parametric model *Gθ* such that the supremum norm *sup_{u∈V, y∈K2} |G(u)(y) − Gθ(u)(y)|* is small, using only finitely many input-output pairs *(u_j, G(u_j))*. The functions are observed through point evaluations, so the model must be **discretization-invariant**: the same parameters must work for any grid, and ideally the trained model can be evaluated on finer grids than it saw during training — *zero-shot super-resolution* [3].

### 2.2 The universal approximation theorem for operators

The theoretical bedrock of the field predates deep learning's modern era by decades. Chen and Chen proved that a single-hidden-layer network can approximate any nonlinear continuous operator, not merely any continuous function [1]:

> Theorem: (Universal Approximation Theorem for Operators, Chen & Chen [1]). Suppose *σ* is a continuous non-polynomial activation, *X* is a Banach space, *K1 ⊂ X* and *K2 ⊂ R^d* are compact, *V* is a compact set in *C(K1)*, and *G : V → C(K2)* is a nonlinear continuous operator. Then for any *ε > 0* there exist positive integers *n, p, m* and real parameters *c_i^k, ξ_{ij}^k, θ_i^k, ζ_k, w_k, x_j* such that the estimate *|G(u)(y) − Σ_{k=1}^{p} Σ_{i=1}^{n} c_i^k σ(Σ_{j=1}^{m} ξ_{ij}^k u(x_j) + θ_i^k) σ(w_k · y + ζ_k)| < ε* holds for all *u ∈ V* and *y ∈ K2*.

The structure of the approximant is revealing: the input function *u* enters only through its values *u(x_j)* at finitely many **sensor locations**, while the output coordinate *y* enters through a separate set of basis-like terms *σ(w_k · y + ζ_k)*. This factorization of the *function dependence* from the *coordinate dependence* is exactly what DeepONet turns into an architecture [2]. A later generalization by Lu et al. shows that two deep networks combined by a dot product suffice: there exist *g : R^m → R^p* and *f : R^d → R^p* with *‖G(u)(y) − ⟨g(u(x_1), …, u(x_m)), f(y)⟩‖ < ε*, freeing both sides to be deep rather than single-hidden-layer [2].

| Architecture | Operator form | Discretization invariance | Key strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| DeepONet [2] | *⟨branch(u), trunk(y)⟩ + b0* | Query points arbitrary | Arbitrary geometries, scattered sensors |
| Fourier neural operator [3] | Spectral convolution + pointwise nonlinearity | Yes, shared spectral weights | Zero-shot super-resolution, speed |
| Graph neural operator [4] | Message passing on graphs | Yes, via kernel integration | Irregular meshes, non-uniform grids |
| NOMAD [7] | Nonlinear decoder *f(β, y)* | Yes | Low-dimensional nonlinear solution manifolds |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Generating training data in function space

Operator learning is supervised learning, and its supervision is expensive: each training pair requires solving a PDE. The standard pipeline samples input functions from a probability measure *μ* over *V*, typically a **Gaussian random field** with a radial-basis or Matérn covariance, which produces smooth, physically plausible coefficient and initial fields. For each sampled input, a high-fidelity classical solver — spectral, finite-element, or finite-difference — computes the output. The pair is stored as point evaluations on a grid, and training minimizes the expected relative *L2* loss *E_{u∼μ}[‖Gθ(u) − G(u)‖_2 / ‖G(u)‖_2]*.

Because data generation dominates cost, sample efficiency is paramount. Several refinements are standard practice:

1. **Active sensor placement.** The input function is encoded by its values at sensors; more sensors reduce encoding error but grow the branch network [5].
2. **Multi-fidelity data.** Cheap coarse-grid solves supplement a few expensive fine-grid solves.
3. **Physics-informed losses.** PDE residuals evaluated on the predicted field are added to the data loss, constraining the model between supervised samples [4].
4. **Randomized trunk sampling.** DeepONet can be trained with different random query points *y* per batch, since the trunk accepts arbitrary coordinates [2].

### 3.2 DeepONet: the branch-trunk realization

DeepONet directly implements the factorized form suggested by the universal approximation theorem. A **branch network** encodes the discretized input function *[u(x_1), …, u(x_m)]* into a coefficient vector *b ∈ R^p*; a **trunk network** encodes the query coordinate *y* into a basis vector *t ∈ R^p*; the prediction is the dot product

*Gθ(u)(y) ≈ Σ_{k=1}^{p} b_k t_k + b_0.*

In the *stacked* variant there are *p* parallel branch networks, mirroring the theorem's double sum; the *unstacked* variant uses one branch network emitting all *p* coefficients and is the practical default [2]. Both sub-networks are typically plain feedforward networks, though convolutional or attention-based encoders are possible. Crucially, the trunk can be queried at any *y*, so the model is a genuine function-to-function map rather than a fixed-grid image translator.

### 3.3 The Fourier neural operator layer

The FNO takes a different route, generalizing the convolutional neural network to function space. The input *a* is lifted to a higher-dimensional representation *v_0(x) = P(a(x))* by a pointwise network *P*, then updated through *T* layers before a pointwise projection *Q* produces the output [3]:

> Definition: (Fourier layer [3]). The update *v_t ↦ v_{t+1}* is *v_{t+1}(x) := σ( W v_t(x) + (K(a; φ) v_t)(x) )*, where *W* is a pointwise linear map and *K* is the spectral convolution operator *(K(φ) v_t)(x) = F^{−1}( R_φ · (F v_t) )(x)*. Here *F* is the Fourier transform and *R_φ* is a learnable complex-valued weight tensor acting on a truncated set of low Fourier modes.

The Fourier transform diagonalizes translation-invariant kernels, so *R_φ* parameterizes a global integral operator with *O(k_max · d_v^2)* parameters independent of grid resolution. Because the weights live in frequency space, the same trained layer applies to any discretization — the source of discretization invariance and zero-shot super-resolution [3]. Truncation to low modes acts as a learned low-pass filter; high-frequency detail is recovered through composition with the nonlinearity *σ*, which is why FNOs can represent functions with slow Fourier decay [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Branch–Trunk Factorization and Its Consequences

DeepONet's separation of concerns is its defining feature. The branch network never sees coordinates and the trunk network never sees the input function; all interaction happens in the final dot product. This makes the architecture a learned **separable expansion**: the trunk learns a data-adapted basis *{t_k(y)}* for the output function space while the branch learns the coefficient functionals *b_k(u)*. Unlike a proper orthogonal decomposition basis, both sides are nonlinear and trained end to end.

The factorization has practical consequences. Because the trunk is a continuous function of *y*, predictions can be queried on arbitrary point clouds, which matters for applications with moving sensors or adaptive meshes [2]. The branch side, however, requires the input function at a *fixed* set of sensor locations during both training and inference; permuting or changing sensors invalidates the encoding. Variants with attention-based or set-based encoders relax this restriction, but the vanilla form assumes aligned sensor grids. Another subtlety is the **alignment of capacity**: the rank *p* of the expansion must be large enough to capture the solution manifold, and in practice *p* of order tens to hundreds is typical [2].

### 4.2 Spectral Convolution: Why the Fourier Domain

The FNO's power comes from an old observation in numerical analysis: integral operators with translation-invariant kernels become pointwise multiplication under the Fourier transform. A layer of the form *F^{−1}(R_φ · F(v_t))* is therefore a *global* convolution with a learned kernel, computed in *O(n log n)* time via the FFT, while the weight tensor *R_φ* itself has no notion of grid spacing. This is the precise mechanism behind discretization invariance: the parameters are functions of *frequency*, not of grid indices [3].

Two design choices deserve emphasis. First, **mode truncation**: only the lowest *k_max* Fourier modes carry learnable weights (commonly 12 to 32 per dimension), and higher modes are discarded before the inverse transform. This is both a computational necessity and an inductive bias — PDE solution operators are typically smoothing, so low modes carry the signal. Second, the residual pointwise map *W* and the activation *σ* reintroduce locality and high frequencies, so the network is not merely a linear low-pass filter [3]. For non-periodic problems the inverse FFT imposes an artificial periodicity; the FNO paper shows that local layers (the pointwise *P*, *Q*, and *W* maps) can still approximate non-periodic boundary behavior, as demonstrated on Darcy flow with non-periodic boundaries and Navier-Stokes with non-periodic time dependence [3].

### 4.3 Graphs, Low-Rank Maps, and Nonlinear Manifold Decoders

The neural operator framework of Kovachki et al. unifies these designs as compositions of linear integral operators and nonlinear activations, and introduces four efficient parameterizations: graph neural operators, multipole graph neural operators, low-rank neural operators, and Fourier neural operators [4]. **Graph neural operators** replace the Fourier kernel with message passing on a graph built from the discretization, which handles irregular geometries where the FFT is inapplicable. **Multipole variants** use a hierarchical decomposition reminiscent of the fast multipole method to achieve near-linear complexity on large graphs [4].

**NOMAD** attacks a different bottleneck: the *decoder*. DeepONet and PCA-based methods reconstruct outputs as linear combinations of learned basis functions, *Σ_k β_k τ_k(y)*. Seidman et al. show this is fundamentally limited when the solution set forms a curved low-dimensional manifold: for the antiderivative operator acting on sinusoids of varying frequency, the outputs trace a one-dimensional curve in *L2*, yet a linear decoder needs at least twenty modes to represent it accurately [7]. NOMAD replaces the linear reconstruction with a fully nonlinear decoder *D̃(β, y) = f(β, y)*, a network taking the latent code and the query point jointly, and captures the same manifold with a single latent dimension — at significantly smaller model size and training cost on a fluid dynamics benchmark [7]. The lesson is that universality theorems guarantee *existence* of approximants but say nothing about the *size* required, and decoder nonlinearity can dramatically change the scaling.

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proof Sketches

### 5.1 Benchmark results

The FNO paper reports error reductions of **30% on Burgers' equation, 60% on Darcy flow, and 30% on turbulent Navier-Stokes** relative to the best prior deep learning methods at fixed 64×64 resolution. For full time-series prediction it reaches below 1% relative error at Reynolds number 1000 and 8% at Reynolds number 10000 [3]. The speed advantage is the headline: on a 256×256 grid, FNO inference takes **0.005 seconds versus 2.2 seconds** for the pseudo-spectral solver used to generate the data — a factor of over four hundred — with no accuracy degradation when the surrogate is used downstream in a Bayesian inverse problem [3]. Zero-shot super-resolution is demonstrated by training at low resolution and evaluating at higher resolution with flat error curves [3].

DeepONet's empirical signature is convergence *rate*: Lu et al. observe polynomial error decay from half order to fourth order with dataset size, and even exponential convergence on some operators, with test errors routinely reaching the 10^{−3} to 10^{−5} range on ODE and PDE benchmarks [2]. NOMAD matches state-of-the-art accuracy on a complex fluid dynamics benchmark with a markedly smaller model [7].

### 5.2 Reference implementations

The two core layers are compact to implement. First, a Fourier spectral-convolution layer in PyTorch:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class SpectralConv2d(nn.Module):
    """One Fourier layer: v -> sigma(W v + F^{-1}(R * F(v)))."""
    def __init__(self, in_channels, out_channels, modes1, modes2):
        super().__init__()
        self.modes1, self.modes2 = modes1, modes2
        self.scale = 1.0 / (in_channels * out_channels)
        # Learnable complex weights R_phi, truncated to low modes
        self.weights = nn.Parameter(
            self.scale * torch.rand(in_channels, out_channels,
                                   modes1, modes2, dtype=torch.cfloat))
        self.w = nn.Conv2d(in_channels, out_channels, 1)

    def compl_mul2d(self, x, weights):
        return torch.einsum("bixy,ioxy->boxy", x, weights)

    def forward(self, x):
        # x: (batch, channels, height, width)
        x_ft = torch.fft.rfft2(x, norm="ortho")
        out_ft = torch.zeros_like(x_ft)
        out_ft[:, :, :self.modes1, :self.modes2] = self.compl_mul2d(
            x_ft[:, :, :self.modes1, :self.modes2], self.weights)
        x_spectral = torch.fft.irfft2(out_ft, s=(x.size(-2), x.size(-1)),
                                      norm="ortho")
        return torch.relu(x_spectral + self.w(x))
```

Second, the unstacked DeepONet branch-trunk skeleton:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class DeepONet(nn.Module):
    """Unstacked DeepONet: G(u)(y) ~ <branch(u), trunk(y)> + b0."""
    def __init__(self, n_sensors, p, width=128):
        super().__init__()
        self.branch = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(n_sensors, width), nn.Tanh(),
            nn.Linear(width, width), nn.Tanh(),
            nn.Linear(width, p))          # b = [b_1 .. b_p]
        self.trunk = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(2, width), nn.Tanh(),
            nn.Linear(width, width), nn.Tanh(),
            nn.Linear(width, p))          # t = [t_1 .. t_p]
        self.b0 = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1))

    def forward(self, u, y):
        # u: (batch, n_sensors); y: (batch, n_points, 2) query coords
        b = self.branch(u)                # (batch, p)
        t = self.trunk(y)                 # (batch, n_points, p)
        return torch.einsum("bp,bnp->bn", b, t) + self.b0
```

### 5.3 FourCastNet: operators at planetary scale

The most striking deployment of neural operators is **FourCastNet**, a global data-driven weather model built on Adaptive Fourier Neural Operators. Trained on a subset of the ERA5 reanalysis dataset at 0.25-degree resolution (a 720×1440 global grid), it forecasts 20 atmospheric variables — surface winds, temperature, pressure, humidity, geopotential, and integrated water vapor — and matches the ECMWF Integrated Forecasting System on large-scale variables at short lead times while *outperforming* it on fine-scale structure such as precipitation [8]. A week-long forecast is generated in **under two seconds**, orders of magnitude faster than numerical weather prediction, enabling thousand-member ensembles for probabilistic forecasting and extreme-event tracking, including tropical cyclones [8]. It is the clearest proof that operator learning has crossed from benchmark PDEs to operational-scale science.

## 6 Limitations

Operator learning is powerful but not a universal solvent. Its weaknesses are worth stating plainly:

- **Data hunger.** Every training pair costs a high-fidelity PDE solve; turbulent or 3D problems make dataset generation the dominant expense, and the model is only as good as the solver that produced its labels [4].
- **Distribution shift.** The theory assumes inputs drawn from a fixed measure *μ* on a compact set *V*. Querying far outside the training distribution — rougher coefficients, unseen geometries — degrades accuracy with no warning and no certified bound [5].
- **Sensor rigidity.** Vanilla DeepONet needs identical sensor locations at train and test time; FNO needs a regular grid (or a graph variant) and implicitly assumes periodicity in its spectral core [2][3].
- **No free lunch on physics.** The model learns the operator it was shown. Conservation laws, symmetries, and boundary conditions are approximated, not enforced, unless built into the architecture or loss [4].
- **Training cost vs. inference speed.** The headline speedups amortize only when many queries follow training; for a single PDE instance, a classical solver remains cheaper and more accurate [3].

| Criterion | Classical solvers (FEM/FDM) | DeepONet | FNO |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost per new instance | Full solve | One forward pass | One forward pass |
| Training cost | None | Dataset of solves + training | Dataset of solves + training |
| Resolution transfer | Remesh and re-solve | Arbitrary query points | Zero-shot super-resolution |
| Error certification | A posteriori estimators | None in practice | None in practice |
| Irregular geometry | Native | Native (sensors anywhere) | Needs graph variant |

## 7 Conclusion

Operator learning reframes scientific machine learning: the goal is not one solution but the *solution map itself*. The Chen–Chen universal approximation theorem [1] guarantees that such maps are learnable in principle; DeepONet [2] and the Fourier neural operator [3] provide two complementary realizations — separable branch-trunk expansions and discretization-invariant spectral convolution — while the neural operator framework [4], error estimates [5][6], and nonlinear decoders [7] supply the analysis and the architectural refinements. The empirical record, from sub-percent turbulence prediction to FourCastNet's planetary forecasts [8], shows the paradigm working at scale.

The frontier is clear. Certified error bounds that hold outside the training distribution, architectures that natively handle arbitrary geometries without giving up spectral efficiency, data-efficient training that leans harder on physics, and hybrid solvers that use learned operators as preconditioners inside classical iterations — these are the problems that will decide whether operator learning becomes infrastructure or remains a spectacular demo. The mathematics says the maps are learnable; engineering them into trustworthy scientific software is the work ahead.
