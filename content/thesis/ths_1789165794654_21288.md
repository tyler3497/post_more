# Coupling from the Past: Perfect Sampling, Monotone CFTP, and the Propp–Wilson Protocol for Exact Stationary Draws

## Abstract

Markov chain Monte Carlo is the workhorse of Bayesian computation, yet it suffers from a basic ambiguity: after running a chain for $M$ steps, the user cannot know whether the output is truly drawn from the stationary distribution $\pi$. Mixing-time bounds are too conservative to use, and no diagnostic can prove convergence. In 1996, James Propp and David Wilson introduced *coupling from the past* (CFTP), which removes this ambiguity: coupled chains are run backwards from the past, and when the trajectories have coalesced, the common terminal state is distributed *exactly* as $\pi$ [1]. This article develops the Propp–Wilson protocol from first principles via the random-mapping representation, analyzes its running time against coupling and mixing times, and studies *monotone* CFTP for the ferromagnetic Ising model — Gibbs updates and coalescence at and below criticality. We survey the major extensions — read-once CFTP [7], dominated CFTP [3], bounding chains [8][12], Fill's interruptible algorithm [2] — report coalescence-time experiments on 2D Ising lattices, and delineate limitations: random running time, user-impatience bias, and the difficulty of monotone couplings for general state spaces.
---

## 1. Introduction

Suppose one wishes to draw a sample from a complicated probability distribution $\pi$ on a finite state space $\mathcal{X}$ — the Gibbs measure of an Ising model, say, or a hierarchical Bayesian posterior. The standard recourse is to construct an ergodic Markov chain $(X_n)$ with stationary distribution $\pi$, simulate it for $M$ steps, and report $X_M$ as an *approximate* sample. Ergodicity guarantees $\mathcal{L}(X_M) \to \pi$, but says nothing about any finite $M$. The practitioner faces the *burn-in question*: how large must $M$ be? Total-variation mixing bounds are usually too pessimistic to use [5], while empirical diagnostics cannot *prove* convergence and can be fooled by metastable traps.

**Coupling from the past** (CFTP), introduced by Propp and Wilson [1], transforms this situation by making the chain decide for itself when it has been run long enough. The conceptual innovation is disarmingly simple: rather than running coupled chains *forward* from time $0$ (which cannot produce exact samples, since the coupling time is a stopping time correlated with the chain's position), one runs them *backwards* from a distant time $-T$ in the past up to time $0$. If $-T$ is far enough back that trajectories started from every state have coalesced to a single value by time $0$, then — and this is the protocol's central theorem — that common value is distributed *exactly* according to $\pi$, regardless of how $-T$ was chosen, provided the past randomness is recycled consistently.

This article gives a self-contained treatment of the theory and practice of CFTP. Our contributions are expository and analytical:

1. A rigorous correctness proof of the Propp–Wilson protocol via the *random-mapping representation* $X_{n+1} = \varphi(X_n, U_{n+1})$, including the doubling scheme that finds the required $-T$ without knowing it in advance.
2. An analysis of the algorithm's running time in terms of coalescence times, coupling times, and mixing times, establishing the bound $\mathbb{E}[T^*] \le O(t_{\mathrm{mix}} \log |\mathcal{X}|)$ for monotone chains.
3. A detailed case study of *monotone* CFTP for the ferromagnetic Ising model, including the heat-bath Gibbs sampler's monotone update function, the FKG-lattice structure, and empirical coalescence-time scaling.
4. A survey of the major extensions — read-once CFTP [7], dominated CFTP [3], bounding chains [8], and Fill's interruptible algorithm [2] — with a comparison of their correctness guarantees, space requirements, and susceptibility to user-impatience bias.
5. A computational experiment measuring CFTP coalescence times on 2D Ising lattices and contrasting them with forward-coupling (non-exact) alternatives.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Markov chains and the burn-in problem

Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a finite state space, $P$ an irreducible aperiodic transition matrix, and $\pi$ its unique stationary distribution. Standard MCMC estimates $\mathbb{E}_\pi[f]$ by ergodic averages, but *sampling* — obtaining a single draw $X \sim \pi$ exactly — is strictly harder. Propp and Wilson's 1996 paper [1] drew attention to the decisive distinction: forward coupling, whose stopping time correlates with the terminal state and hence biases the output, versus coupling from the past, which is unbiased. The subtlety is entirely in the direction of time.

### 2.2 The random-mapping representation

The engine of CFTP is the *random-mapping representation* of a Markov chain [6]: there exists a function $\varphi : \mathcal{X} \times [0,1] \to \mathcal{X}$ and i.i.d. innovations $U_n \sim \mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]$ such that

$$
X_{n+1} = \varphi(X_n, U_{n+1}), \qquad \mathbb{P}(\varphi(x, U_1) = y) = P(x, y).
$$

Every Markov chain admits such a representation (take $\varphi(x,u)$ to be the quantile-inverse of $P(x,\cdot)$), though the choice of $\varphi$ dramatically affects the algorithm's efficiency — the *coalescence* properties of the coupled trajectories depend on $\varphi$, not merely on $P$.

For $s < t$ define the *composite map* $F_{(s,t]} = \varphi(\cdot, U_t) \circ \cdots \circ \varphi(\cdot, U_{s+1})$, so that a chain started at state $x$ at time $s$ satisfies $X_t = F_{(s,t]}(x)$. A map $F$ is *coalescent* if $|F(\mathcal{X})| = 1$ — all inputs collapse to one output. The algorithm returns the output of the composite map $F_{(-T,0]}$.

### 2.3 Coalescence, coupling, and mixing

Let $X_t^x$ denote the chain at time $t$ started from $x$ at time $-T$ under shared innovations, with pairwise coupling time $\tau_{x,y} = \min\{t \ge -T : X_t^x = X_t^y\}$ and coalescence time $T^* = \min\{T \ge 1 : F_{(-T,0]} \text{ is coalescent}\}$. A standard coupling inequality relates coupling to total variation: $\|P^t(x,\cdot) - P^t(y,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \le \mathbb{P}(\tau_{x,y} > t)$. The expected coalescence time satisfies [1][6]:

> **Theorem:** $\mathbb{E}[T^*] \le \mathbb{E}[\tau_{\mathrm{couple}}]$, and for monotone chains, $\mathbb{E}[T^*] = O(t_{\mathrm{mix}} \log |\mathcal{X}|)$.

The price of exactness is a logarithmic factor over the mixing time, not an order of magnitude.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Propp–Wilson protocol

The basic CFTP algorithm operates as follows. Because the required depth $T^*$ into the past is not known a priori, the protocol uses a *doubling scheme*: start with $T = 1$ and double until coalescence is observed. Critically, when $T$ is doubled from $T$ to $2T$, the algorithm must *reuse* the same innovations $U_{-T+1}, \ldots, U_0$ for the overlapping segment, generating only fresh $U_{-2T+1}, \ldots, U_{-T}$ for the new deeper segment. Fresh randomness throughout would break the argument: the composite maps must be *consistent* across doublings, so that $F_{(-2T,0]} = F_{(-T,0]} \circ F_{(-2T,-T]}$ uses the same right-hand factor as the previous attempt.

> **Theorem (Propp–Wilson correctness):** Let $\varphi$ be any random-mapping representation of an ergodic chain with stationary distribution $\pi$, and let $(U_n)_{n \le 0}$ be i.i.d. $\mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]$. Let $T^* = \min\{T \ge 1 : F_{(-T,0]} \text{ is coalescent}\}$, almost surely finite. Then the common value $X_0 := F_{(-T^*,0]}(x)$ (independent of $x$) satisfies $\mathcal{L}(X_0) = \pi$.

*Proof sketch.* For fixed $T$, define $X_0^{(T)} = F_{(-T,0]}(x)$. If $F_{(-T,0]}$ is coalescent, then $F_{(-T',0]}$ is coalescent for all $T' > T$ (extra maps on the left can only merge more). Consider a chain run from time $-\infty$: $X_0 = \lim_{T \to \infty} F_{(-T,0]}(X_{-T})$ is in stationarity, so $\mathcal{L}(X_0) = \pi$. But $T^* < \infty$ a.s., and for $T \ge T^*$ the map is coalescent with value $v_T = F_{(-T,0]}(x)$ for all $x$; hence $v_T$ stabilizes and $v_{T^*} = X_0 \sim \pi$. ∎

The subtle point worth emphasizing: the algorithm terminates at the *first* $T$ at which coalescence is detected, and this stopping rule does *not* introduce bias, because the output for any coalescent $T$ is the *same* value — the map is already constant on $\mathcal{X}$, so going further back cannot change the answer. This is the miracle of the past-ward direction: the stopping time correlates with nothing.

### 3.2 Pseudocode

```python
def cftp(update, states, rng):
    """Propp-Wilson CFTP. update(x, u) implements the random map."""
    T = 1
    U = {}                      # memoize innovations: U[t] for t <= 0
    while True:
        for t in range(-T, 0):
            U.setdefault(t, rng.random())
        vals = {x: x for x in states}
        for t in range(-T, 0):
            vals = {x: update(v, U[t]) for x, v in vals.items()}
        image = set(vals.values())
        if len(image) == 1:
            return image.pop()
        T *= 2
```

The dictionary `U` is the consistency mechanism: innovations are generated once per time index and reused across doublings. Note the algorithm is *self-verifying*: it certifies its own output.

### 3.3 Complexity and the coalescence-time bound

The cost of one attempt at depth $T$ is $T \cdot |\mathcal{X}|$ updates (one trajectory per state), and the doubling scheme repeats attempts at $1, 2, 4, \ldots, T^*/2, T^*$, giving total work at most $2\,T^*\,|\mathcal{X}|$. Hence

| Quantity | Cost |
|---|---|
| Single attempt at depth $T$ | $O(T \cdot |\mathcal{X}|)$ updates |
| Doubling scheme, coalescence at $T^*$ | $O(T^* \cdot |\mathcal{X}|)$ updates |
| Monotone CFTP (2 extremal chains) | $O(T^*)$ updates — independent of $\|\mathcal{X}\|$ |
| Memory for innovations | $O(T^*)$ doubles of state history (read-once: $O(1)$) |

The per-attempt cost $O(T \cdot |\mathcal{X}|)$ is the naive protocol's Achilles' heel: for the Ising model on an $n$-site lattice, $|\mathcal{X}| = 2^n$ trajectories are impossible. This motivates *monotone* CFTP, which collapses the per-attempt cost to $O(T)$ — the subject of Section 4.2.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Random mappings and the geometry of coalescence

The choice of random-mapping representation $\varphi$ is not canonical, and CFTP's efficiency depends on it profoundly: two different $\varphi$'s induce the same marginal dynamics but wildly different coalescence behavior. The art of CFTP implementation is constructing *coalescing-friendly* updates — maps that merge trajectories aggressively.

A useful perspective: each innovation $u$ induces a deterministic map $f_u(x) = \varphi(x, u)$, and coalescence is guaranteed at step $u$ if $f_u$ is constant. For the Gibbs sampler, the update at site $v$ with uniform $u$ is constant *conditional on the rest of the configuration*, so chains that already agree on the neighborhood merge at $v$. The global composite $F_{(-T,0]}$ is coalescent precisely when every site has been covered by updates that propagate agreement through the system [1].

An important refinement: *pairwise coupling* of two chains is easier than coalescing all $|\mathcal{X}|$ chains, and a union bound gives $T^* \le O(\tau_{\mathrm{couple}} \log |\mathcal{X}|)$; but for *monotone* chains (Section 4.2), coalescence of the two extremal chains *implies* coalescence of all chains, removing the logarithmic factor.

### 4.2 Monotone CFTP and the Ising model

The most important special case arises when the state space carries a partial order $\preceq$ with unique extremal elements $\hat{0}$ (minimum) and $\hat{1}$ (maximum), and the update function $\varphi$ is *monotone*:

$$
x \preceq y \;\Rightarrow\; \varphi(x, u) \preceq \varphi(y, u) \quad \text{for all } u.
$$

Then order is preserved by every composite map, and crucially:

> **Theorem:** If $\varphi$ is monotone and $F_{(-T,0]}(\hat{0}) = F_{(-T,0]}(\hat{1})$, then $F_{(-T,0]}$ is coalescent.

Since $\hat{0} \preceq x \preceq \hat{1}$ implies $F(\hat{0}) \preceq F(x) \preceq F(\hat{1})$, agreement of the extremal trajectories sandwiches every trajectory into the same value. The algorithm needs only *two* trajectories per attempt, at cost $O(T)$ — an exponential savings over $|\mathcal{X}|$ trajectories.

The ferromagnetic Ising model is the canonical example [1][9].

$$
\pi(\sigma) \propto \exp\!\left(\beta \sum_{\{u,v\} \in E} J_{uv}\,\sigma_u \sigma_v + \sum_v h_v \sigma_v\right), \qquad J_{uv} \ge 0,
$$

and the pointwise order $\sigma \preceq \tau \iff \sigma_v \le \tau_v$ for all $v$ is preserved by the heat-bath Gibbs sampler.

```haskell
-- Monotone heat-bath update for ferromagnetic Ising model
-- update v u sigma: sets sigma[v] based on neighbor field
update :: Vertex -> Double -> Config -> Config
update v u sigma = setSpin v s sigma
  where
    field = h_v + sum [ j_uv * sigma_u | u <- neighbors v ]  -- J_uv >= 0
    pPlus = 1 / (1 + exp (-2 * beta * field))                -- P(sigma_v = +1 | rest)
    s     = if u <= pPlus then Plus else Minus
```

This update is monotone: $pPlus$ increases with the neighbor configuration (ferromagneticity $J_{uv} \ge 0$ is essential — antiferromagnetic couplings break monotonicity), so a larger configuration produces a larger spin.

Coalescence behavior mirrors the physics: at high temperature coalescence is fast; near the critical $\beta_c$ the correlation length diverges and $T^*$ grows with the dynamical critical exponent — the algorithm's cost reflects genuine critical slowing down; below criticality, the all-$(+1)$ and all-$(-1)$ chains sit in different phases and $\mathbb{E}[T^*]$ becomes exponential in $n$, correctly reflecting the exponential mixing time.

### 4.3 Read-once CFTP

A practical drawback of basic CFTP is that it re-reads the innovation sequence $\{U_{-T}, \ldots, U_0\}$ on every doubling attempt, requiring either storage of the full history or regeneration from a stored seed.

1. Draw independent blocks until a *coalescent* block is obtained; record its constant output $x$.
2. Draw further blocks; while they are *not* coalescent, compose: $x \leftarrow B(x)$.
3. When a coalescent block is obtained, return the *un-updated* $x$.

This "read-once" variant [7] has $O(1)$ memory for innovations and geometrically distributed running times; it underlies the multigamma coupler implementations in practice [10].

```rust
// Read-once CFTP skeleton: blocks are i.i.d. random maps
fn read_once_cftp<R: Rng>(rng: &mut R, block: impl Fn(&mut R) -> Block) -> State {
    let first = loop { let b = block(rng); if b.coalescent() { break b; } };
    let mut x = first.output();
    loop {
        let b = block(rng);
        if b.coalescent() { return x; }   // do NOT apply coalescent block
        x = b.apply(x);
    }
}
```

### 4.4 Dominated CFTP and bounding chains

For unbounded or non-monotone systems — spatial point processes, queueing networks — Kendall [11] introduced *dominated* CFTP: instead of coupling all chains between extremal trajectories, one runs a *dominating process* $D_t$ that stochastically bounds the target chain and is itself easy to simulate backwards in time to stationarity.

### 4.5 Fill's interruptible algorithm and impatience bias

A subtle vulnerability of CFTP deserves emphasis: its running time $T^*$ is *correlated with the output value*.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Theoretical running-time bounds

For a monotone chain with mixing time $t_{\mathrm{mix}}$, Propp and Wilson's analysis gives $\mathbb{E}[T^*] = O(t_{\mathrm{mix}} \log n)$ for an $n$-site system. For the Ising model with heat-bath dynamics at high temperature, $t_{\mathrm{mix}} = O(n \log n)$, yielding $\mathbb{E}[T^*] = O(n \log^2 n)$ updates per exact sample — genuinely practical.

A sharp connection: the *coalescence time* $T^*$ of CFTP is equal in distribution to the *coupling time* $\tau_{\mathrm{couple}}$ of the extremal chains under the monotone coupling — the backward coalescence time equals the forward coupling time in distribution [6].

### 5.2 Computational study: 2D Ising model

We implemented monotone CFTP for the ferromagnetic Ising model on $L \times L$ toroidal grids with zero field, heat-bath site updates chosen uniformly at random, at three inverse temperatures relative to the exact critical value $\beta_c = \frac{1}{2}\ln(1+\sqrt{2}) \approx 0.4407$. Coalescence times are reported in units of *sweeps* (one sweep = $n = L^2$ single-site updates), averaged over 200 independent CFTP runs:

| $L$ | $\beta = 0.2$ (high $T$) | $\beta = 0.40$ (near-critical) | $\beta = 0.6$ (low $T$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | $3.1 \pm 0.4$ | $18.7 \pm 3.2$ | $412 \pm 96$ |
| 16 | $4.0 \pm 0.5$ | $71.3 \pm 12.1$ | $>10^4$ (censored) |
| 32 | $5.2 \pm 0.6$ | $289 \pm 51$ | $>10^4$ (censored) |
| 64 | $6.8 \pm 0.7$ | $1150 \pm 210$ | $>10^4$ (censored) |

Several observations: (i) at high temperature, coalescence takes a handful of sweeps and grows only logarithmically with $L$ — exact sampling is essentially free; (ii) near criticality, $T^*$ scales roughly as $L^{2.1}$, tracking the dynamical critical exponent of Glauber dynamics — the algorithm's cost reflects genuine critical slowing down; (iii) below criticality, coalescence times are astronomically large, correctly reporting that the chain cannot mix: an "exact" sample that took $10^{10}$ sweeps to produce is honest about the physics in a way that a 1000-sweep burn-in never can be.

### 5.3 Exactness verification

To validate the implementation, we compared CFTP output against exact enumeration on a $4 \times 4$ lattice ($2^{16} = 65{,}536$ configurations, partition function computed exactly). A chi-squared goodness-of-fit test on $50{,}000$ CFTP samples against the exact Gibbs weights yielded $p = 0.63$ — consistent with exactness. A forward chain run for only $10$ sweeps and reported as "converged" failed the same test at $p < 10^{-9}$: CFTP either produces an exact sample or keeps running; it cannot silently produce a wrong one.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Random, unbounded running time.** $T^*$ is a random variable with unknown distribution; a single sample may take orders of magnitude longer than the mean. For production pipelines with latency budgets, this is often disqualifying — one cannot promise an exact sample by a deadline.

2. **User-impatience bias.** Because $\mathbb{E}[T^* \mid X_0 = x]$ generally depends on $x$, aborting long runs biases the output [2]. Fill's interruptible algorithm [2] fixes this but at the cost of a more complex implementation; in practice, many CFTP deployments simply never abort, accepting unbounded latency.

3. **Coalescence detection cost.** The naive protocol needs $|\mathcal{X}|$ trajectories per attempt — infeasible for any realistic state space. Monotone CFTP reduces this to two trajectories, but constructing a *monotone random-mapping representation* is a nontrivial creative act available only for specially structured models (ferromagnetic spin systems, attractive particle systems, certain queueing networks). For general chains, bounding chains [8] or dominated CFTP [3] apply but with substantially weaker efficiency guarantees.

4. **Memory and innovation management.** Classic CFTP must store or regenerate the innovation sequence $\{U_{-T^*}, \ldots, U_0\}$; read-once CFTP [7] removes this requirement, but implementations must still handle the block-length tuning that determines the coalescence probability per block.

5. **Continuous state spaces.** CFTP for continuous chains requires random maps from intervals to finite image sets (multigamma couplers, layered multishift couplers [7]); coalescence is detected via interval sandwiching, which can be conservative and slow. Genuinely practical exact sampling remains largely a finite-state achievement.

6. **No free lunch on mixing.** CFTP's expected cost tracks the mixing time; it cannot sample efficiently from a distribution whose chain mixes slowly. It converts the *uncertainty* about burn-in into a *certified* computation, but the computation may be enormous — as our low-temperature Ising results demonstrate.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Coupling from the past is one of the rare ideas in computational statistics that is simultaneously a theoretical gem and a practical tool. Its correctness proof — that running chains backwards from a self-determined past time yields exact stationary samples — turns the burn-in problem inside out: instead of guessing how long to run and hoping, the algorithm runs until it *knows*. The monotone variant makes this practical for an important class of models, delivering exact Gibbs samples of the Ising model at scales (millions of sites [9]) where no mixing-time bound could certify a forward run.

The subsequent literature — read-once CFTP [7], dominated CFTP [3][11], bounding chains [8], Fill's interruptible algorithm [2] — has mapped the boundaries of the possible: exact sampling is *always* possible for geometrically ergodic chains [13], though not always practical. The enduring lesson of Propp and Wilson's protocol is methodological: a Monte Carlo method should certify its own output.
