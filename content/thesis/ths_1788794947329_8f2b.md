# Zero-Copy Packet Processing at Line Rate: The Architecture of DPDK Poll-Mode Drivers, Hugepage Memory Subsystems, and Run-to-Completion Pipelines

## Abstract

The Data Plane Development Kit (DPDK) has become the de facto substrate for high-performance software packet processing, enabling commodity servers to forward packets at hundreds of gigabits per second with per-packet latencies in the low microseconds. Its performance rests on a deliberate inversion of conventional operating-system I/O wisdom: replace interrupt-driven kernel stacks with userspace poll-mode drivers, replace per-packet heap allocation with pre-allocated mbuf pools mapped onto hugepages, replace lock-protected queues with lock-free rings, and replace migratable thread scheduling with NUMA-pinned run-to-completion pipelines. This thesis presents a systematic treatment of that architecture. We analyze the cost model that makes kernel bypass worthwhile, dissect the poll-mode driver and descriptor-ring mechanics of modern NICs, formalize the zero-copy buffer lifecycle through mempool abstractions, quantify the TLB and cache effects of 2 MiB/1 GiB hugepages, derive the progress guarantees of the rte_ring enqueue/dequeue protocols, examine longest-prefix-match and ACL acceleration structures, and define a rigorous benchmarking methodology for 100 Gbps+ forwarding. We further prove bounds on polling overhead and show empirically grounded expectations for wire-rate forwarding at 64-byte packets.


## 1 Introduction

Network function virtualization has forced an uncomfortable fact into the open: the general-purpose OS networking stack, designed for fairness and flexibility, was never engineered for line-rate forwarding. At 100 Gbps the smallest Ethernet frames arrive every 6.72 ns, and a single 3 GHz core offers roughly twenty clock cycles per packet if it were to process one port alone [4]. Every cache miss, interrupt, copy, and contended lock is a structural barrier. Traditional stacks spend thousands of cycles per packet on interrupt handling, socket buffer management, and protocol bookkeeping before application logic even begins [1].

DPDK answers by removing the kernel stack from the forwarding path entirely. The network interface controller (NIC) is unbound from its kernel driver and claimed by a userspace Poll-Mode Driver (PMD) [3]. A dedicated logical core spins in a tight loop, interrogating the NIC's receive descriptor ring; arrived packets are handed to the application as descriptors pointing into pre-allocated, hugepage-backed memory — the *zero-copy* invariant.

This thesis develops the architecture from first principles. **Section 2** reviews the cost model of conventional stacks. **Section 3** states our methodology. **Section 4** is the deep dive — polling versus interrupts, zero-copy buffer management, hugepages, lock-free rings, and lookup acceleration under NUMA pinning. **Section 5** presents the empirical methodology and bounds at 100 Gbps and beyond.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The cost of the conventional stack

A packet traversing the Linux receive path follows a well-documented sequence: the NIC raises an interrupt, the driver allocates an `sk_buff`, the frame ascends through the IP and transport layers, and a `copy_to_user` delivers it to the socket [6]. Each step exacts measurable overhead:

1. **Interrupt overhead.** Context switches and deferred-work scheduling cost microseconds per event, even with coalescing.
2. **Copy overhead.** Kernel-to-user copies touch every byte twice, evicting application working sets from caches.
3. **Allocation overhead.** Per-packet `sk_buff` allocation stresses the slab allocator and serializes on shared freelists.
4. **Cache pollution.** Cold packet data and metadata chase each other through the memory hierarchy, defeating prefetching.

Metronome's authors formalize the central tradeoff: polling-based frameworks avoid interrupts entirely, but continuous polling burns CPU cycles and energy even when the link is idle [1]. DPDK embraces polling *unconditionally* — a design choice we analyze in §4.1.

### 2.2 The kernel-bypass lineage

DPDK is distinguished by going furthest: it *owns the NIC*, the descriptor rings, the memory allocator, and the scheduling policy. Benchmarks comparing I/O modes in cloud-native 5G user-plane functions confirm DPDK as the raw-throughput reference, at the cost of the heaviest footprint — hugepages, IOMMU, dedicated cores, and a NIC invisible to the kernel [3].

> **Theorem: Kernel-Bypass Amortization.** *Let C_k be the per-packet kernel-stack cost, C_p the per-packet polling cost including busy-wait overhead, and λ the packet arrival rate. Kernel bypass is strictly beneficial iff λ · (C_k − C_p) > P_idle, where P_idle is the power/resource cost of the polling core at zero load. At high λ, the left-hand side dominates; at low λ, polling wastes energy — the exact tradeoff quantified by the Metronome adaptive-polling model [1].*

### 2.3 Run-to-completion versus pipeline models

DPDK applications admit two scheduling disciplines. In the **pipeline model**, stages map to distinct cores connected by `rte_ring` queues. In the **run-to-completion model**, one core executes RX → process → TX for a burst with no inter-core communication on the fast path. The latter dominates because it keeps all per-packet state in one core's L1/L2 caches.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis follows a three-pronged methodology:

- **Architectural deconstruction.** We trace the DPDK data path from the PCIe layer to the application, identifying every eliminated abstraction and the invariant replacing it, with key algorithms in executable pseudocode [7].
- **Formal analysis.** We derive lower bounds on per-packet cycle budgets at 100/200/400 Gbps, prove the lock-freedom progress guarantees of the ring protocol, and state the lookup structures' asymptotic complexity.
- **Empirical benchmarking protocol.** §5 defines a *methodology* — traffic profiles, isolation requirements, metric definitions, and statistical treatment — yielding wire-rate claims with stated confidence, in the spirit of published 100 Gbps DPDK mempool studies [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Poll-Mode Drivers: Polling versus Interrupts

#### The interrupt pathology

Interrupt-driven I/O multiplexes CPU attention across events: the NIC signals *after* work has arrived — optimal when arrivals are rare. In forwarding workloads, arrivals are the only thing the machine does, and under sustained load interrupt overhead collapses into **interrupt livelock**: more cycles spent entering handlers than processing packets. NAPI mitigates this by polling under load, but the mode transition plus per-packet `sk_buff` management preserves a substantial fixed cost [1][4].

#### The PMD contract

A DPDK Poll-Mode Driver inverts the responsibility: the driver *asks* the NIC whether work exists, in a tight loop, on a dedicated core that does nothing else. The canonical receive burst has this structure:

```c
/* Simplified ixgbe-class PMD receive burst (illustrative) */
uint16_t
pmd_rx_burst(struct rx_queue *q, struct rte_mbuf **bufs, uint16_t nb)
{
    uint16_t nb_rx = 0;
    uint16_t idx = q->rx_tail;

    while (nb_rx < nb) {
        volatile union rx_desc *d = &q->desc[idx];
        /* DD (descriptor done) bit set by NIC via DMA write */
        if (!(d->status & RXD_STAT_DD))
            break;                      /* no more packets */
        bufs[nb_rx++] = q->sw_ring[idx];/* zero-copy: take the mbuf */
        q->sw_ring[idx] = mempool_get(q->pool); /* refill */
        d->addr = mbuf_data_iova(q->sw_ring[idx]);
        d->status = 0;                  /* return descriptor to NIC */
        idx = (idx + 1) & q->mask;
    }
    if (nb_rx)
        mmio_write(q->rdt_reg, (idx - 1) & q->mask); /* one tail update */
    return nb_rx;
}
```

Four properties make this fast: **batching amortizes MMIO** (one tail-register write per burst); the **DD bit is a cache-line handshake**; **zero-copy is structural** (a pointer transfer, not bytes); and there are **no syscalls or context switches** — the pinned core never traps to the kernel [3].

#### The price of polling: energy and latency under load variance

Unconditional polling burns a full core even at zero traffic. The Metronome work shows that *adaptive* intermittent retrieval — sleeping briefly when the queue is empty — preserves most throughput while substantially reducing CPU and energy consumption, backed by an analytical model of the arrival process [1]. Modern DPDK supports interrupt-assisted wakeup for low-rate queues, but the fast path remains poll-driven: *the interrupt is the wrong synchronization primitive for a device that is never idle*.

| Mechanism | Wakeup latency | CPU at zero load | CPU at line rate | Determinism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MSI-X interrupts + NAPI | 1–10 µs | ~0% | High, variable | Poor (jitter) |
| Continuous polling (PMD) | <100 ns | 100% of one core | 100% of one core | Excellent |
| Adaptive polling (Metronome) | 100 ns – 1 µs | Proportional to λ | ≈ continuous | Good |
| Interrupt + poll hybrid | 1–5 µs | Low | High | Moderate |

### 4.2 Zero-Copy Buffer Management: mbuf Pools and Descriptor Rings

#### The mbuf as a universal currency

Every DPDK packet is represented by an `rte_mbuf`: a fixed-size metadata header prepended to a data buffer, carrying length, port, offload flags, RSS hash, and a **reference count** enabling multi-segment chains. The mbuf is never allocated on the fast path: at initialization the application creates a *mempool* — a contiguous array of mbuf objects already backed by DMA-capable memory — from which the PMD draws and to which the transmit path returns buffers [2][7].

The lifecycle is a closed loop: `rte_mempool_create` allocates *N* mbufs in hugepage memory at startup; the PMD pops free mbufs and programs their I/O virtual addresses into RX descriptors; the NIC DMAs frame bytes into the mbuf data area and sets the DD bit; the application mutates headers *in place*; the transmit burst programs mbuf addresses into TX descriptors for DMA-out; on TX completion the mbufs return to the mempool in bulk [2][7].

No `memcpy` of payload bytes occurs anywhere in this loop. That is the entire content of the phrase *zero-copy*: ownership transfer of pre-allocated buffers between the NIC and the application, mediated by descriptor rings.

#### Descriptor-ring mechanics

The RX and TX rings are the hardware/software contract. Each is a power-of-two array of descriptors in DMA-visible memory, with a producer index owned by one side and a consumer index owned by the other. Correctness hinges on *exactly one writer per index* and on memory ordering: the NIC must observe the descriptor's address before the tail-register update, and the CPU must observe the DD bit before reading frame bytes. On weakly ordered architectures (ARM), explicit `rte_wmb()`/`rte_rmb()` fences are mandatory — a frequent source of porting bugs.

```python
# Model of a single-producer/single-consumer descriptor ring
class DescRing:
    def __init__(self, size):
        assert size & (size - 1) == 0
        self.desc = [None] * size
        self.mask = size - 1
        self.head = 0
        self.tail = 0

    def produce(self, buf):                   # NIC side
        assert self.desc[self.tail] is None
        self.desc[self.tail] = buf
        self.tail = (self.tail + 1) & self.mask

    def consume(self):                        # PMD side
        buf = self.desc[self.head]
        if buf is None:
            return None
        self.desc[self.head] = None
        self.head = (self.head + 1) & self.mask
        return buf
```

The invariant `desc[i] is None ⟺ slot i is producer-owned` mirrors the hardware DD-bit protocol, letting the PMD run lock-free on the data path [4].

#### Bulk allocation and per-core caches

`rte_mempool` adds **per-lcore caches**: each core holds a small private stack of free objects, so the uncontended fast path never touches shared atomics. Objects move between the private cache and the shared ring in bulk, amortizing synchronization. The TurboMem study shows that at 100 Gbps with 64-byte frames, mempool throughput itself can become the binding constraint, and that the pool's backing-page TLB behavior measurably shifts forwarding rate [2].

### 4.3 The Hugepage Memory Subsystem

#### Why page size is a data-plane parameter

Every DMA mapping and every page-table walk pays a cost proportional to TLB pressure. With 4 KiB pages, a 1 GiB mempool needs 262,144 page-table entries — far beyond any TLB — so each new page risks a page walk of tens of cycles. With 2 MiB hugepages it needs 512; with 1 GiB pages, exactly one [2][7]. DPDK's Environment Abstraction Layer therefore allocates *all* of its memory from hugepages via `hugetlbfs` and pins it (`mlock`) against swapping.

The benefits compound: a 64-entry DTLB covers 128 MiB with 2 MiB pages versus 256 KiB with 4 KiB pages; hugepages reduce IOVA fragmentation for VFIO/IOMMU mappings; locked, pre-faulted pages eliminate minor page faults from the fast path — a tail-latency requirement.

#### NUMA placement and the first-touch rule

On multi-socket servers, a hugepage physically resides on one NUMA node, and cross-node access pays 1.5–2× the latency plus interconnect contention. DPDK's memory subsystem is NUMA-aware by construction: `rte_malloc_socket` and `rte_mempool_create` with an explicit `socket_id` place each structure on the node local to the cores and PCIe device that will use it [3][7].

> **Theorem: NUMA-Local Processing Optimality.** *For a run-to-completion pipeline on a two-socket system, let L_local be local DRAM latency and L_remote ≈ 1.8 · L_local be cross-node latency. If a packet's mbuf, descriptor rings, and lookup tables all reside on the socket of the processing core, per-packet memory latency is bounded by k · L_local for k cache-line touches; any design that migrates packets across sockets pays at least L_remote per crossing, independent of compute. Hence socket-local placement is asymptotically optimal for memory-bound forwarding [2][3].*

The TurboMem evaluation provides the empirical counterpart: even the choice between explicit 1 GiB pages and transparent-hugepage auto-merging changes measured TLB-miss rates and forwarding throughput at 100 Gbps — page-size policy belongs in the performance model, not the deployment checklist [2].

### 4.4 Lock-Free Inter-Core Communication: rte_ring

When cores must exchange packets — pipeline stages, TX scheduling, exception-path delivery — DPDK provides `rte_ring`: a bounded multi-producer/multi-consumer FIFO using compare-and-swap (CAS) on head/tail indices, *without locks* [7]. The enqueue protocol reserves *n* slots with one CAS, writes the pointers, then publishes the tail — synchronization cost per packet *O(1/n)*, which is why DPDK insists on burst-oriented APIs.

The protocol is lock-free: a thread stalled mid-enqueue never blocks the progress of others — strictly stronger than mutex behavior, which is catastrophic on non-preemptible fast-path cores. The reservation step in Rust-like pseudocode:

```rust
// Lock-free slot reservation (single CAS loop, multi-producer)
fn reserve_slots(ring: &Ring, n: u32) -> Option<u32> {
    loop {
        let head = ring.prod_head.load(Ordering::Acquire);
        let tail = ring.cons_tail.load(Ordering::Acquire);
        let free = ring.capacity - (head - tail);
        if free < n { return None; }              // ring full: backpressure
        let new_head = head + n;
        if ring.prod_head.compare_exchange(
                head, new_head,
                Ordering::AcqRel, Ordering::Acquire).is_ok() {
            return Some(head);
        }
        // CAS failed: another producer won; retry
    }
}
```

The COREC work pushes this further, showing that even the NIC-facing receive path — traditionally a critical section per queue — can be restructured into non-blocking algorithms coordinated purely by read-modify-write atomics, eliminating queue locks without sacrificing correctness [4].

### 4.5 Lookup Acceleration and NUMA-Affinity Run-to-Completion

#### The forwarding decision

A minimal L3 forwarder must map each packet's destination IP to an output port: a **longest-prefix-match (LPM)** over the routing table. DPDK's `rte_lpm` implements the DIR-24-8 algorithm: a 2^24-entry direct table handles prefixes of length ≤ 24 in one memory read, longer prefixes spill into 256-entry `tbl8` groups — *O(1)* worst-case lookup with at most two dependent reads, trading 16 MiB of memory for deterministic latency [7]. The lookup is vectorized over four addresses per iteration with SIMD gathers, so the effective per-packet cost is a fraction of the two-read worst case on a cache-warm core.

#### Run-to-completion with NUMA affinity

The complete fast path on one core is therefore: poll a burst, LPM-lookup each mbuf, rewrite headers in place, hand the burst to the NIC. No queues between stages, no locks, no cross-core traffic, no syscalls. The NIC's RSS distributes flows across RX queues so *n* cores scale near-linearly; each queue is owned by exactly one core, preserving the single-producer/single-consumer invariant of §4.2 [3][7]. Memory, cores, and PCIe devices all sit on the same NUMA node, so the packet's entire lifetime touches only local resources — the architecture that forwards 148.8 Mpps of 64-byte frames per 100 Gbps port with a handful of cores [2][3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Cycle-budget analysis

For link rate *R* bits/s and minimum on-the-wire frame size *F* (64 B + 20 B preamble/IFG = 84 B), the packet rate is *P = R / (8F)* and the per-packet budget on *c* cores at frequency *f* is:

| Link rate | Mpps (64 B) | Cycles/pkt (1 core @ 3 GHz) | Cycles/pkt (4 cores) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 Gbps | 14.88 | 201.6 | 806.5 |
| 100 Gbps | 148.81 | 20.2 | 80.6 |
| 200 Gbps | 297.62 | 10.1 | 40.3 |
| 400 Gbps | 595.24 | 5.0 | 20.2 |

At 100 Gbps a single core has **≈ 20 cycles per packet** — less than one DRAM access. By contradiction with any design that touches DRAM per packet, wire-rate 64-byte forwarding *requires* the entire fast path to hit in cache: pinned cores, hugepages, warm lookup tables, zero per-packet allocation — the quantitative justification for every mechanism in §4 [2][4].

> **Theorem: Cache-Residency Necessity.** *At 100 Gbps with 64-byte frames, any forwarding path whose per-packet working set exceeds the core's L1/L2 capacity, or that performs per-packet DRAM accesses, cannot sustain line rate on fewer than ⌈P · t_DRAM⌉ cores, where t_DRAM is the DRAM access latency in packet-times — an order of magnitude more cores than a cache-resident design. Hence hugepage-backed, NUMA-local, allocation-free data paths are necessary, not optional [2].*

### 5.2 Benchmarking methodology for 100 Gbps+

Credible DPDK benchmarks share a discipline, exemplified by 100 Gbps mempool studies [2] and dataplane I/O comparisons [3]:

1. **Isolation.** `isolcpus`, `nohz_full`, no hyperthreading, fixed CPU frequency, IRQs steered off fast-path cores.
2. **Topology honesty.** Report the NUMA node of every NIC, core, and memory pool; a benchmark that silently crosses sockets measures the interconnect, not the software.
3. **Traffic generation.** An *external* line-rate generator; in-host loopback inflates results through shared caches.
4. **Profiles.** 64-byte fixed (worst-case pps), IMIX, and 1518-byte (bandwidth ceiling); ≥ 60 s runs, ≥ 5 repetitions with reported variance.
5. **Metrics.** Mpps/Gbps, latency percentiles (p50/p99/p999), cycles per packet from hardware counters, TLB/cache-miss rates from PMU profiling [2].
6. **Zero-loss criterion.** Report the *maximum loss-free rate*; a forwarder dropping 0.1% at "line rate" is not at line rate.

### 5.3 Expected results envelope

Under this methodology, a tuned run-to-completion L3 forwarder on modern x86 hardware exhibits: **64 B** — line-rate 148.8 Mpps per 100 G port with 2–4 cores, ~50–80 cycles/packet, p99 latency under 10 µs [2][3]; **IMIX** — line rate with fewer cores, bandwidth-bound rather than pps-bound; **1518 B** — line rate trivially, often on a single core; **scaling** — near-linear with cores up to the NIC's queue count and PCIe lane budget, sublinear beyond.

Deviations diagnose specific faults: low 64 B pps implicates per-packet overhead (allocation, locking, cross-NUMA); high p99 latency implicates interrupts, page faults, or power management; poor scaling implicates shared state.

## 6 Limitations

DPDK's design is a bundle of deliberate tradeoffs.

1. **CPU dedication.** Polling consumes whole cores regardless of load; adaptive schemes [1] mitigate but do not eliminate the waste.
2. **Kernel detachment.** The NIC disappears from the kernel: no `iptables`, no `tc`, no standard observability on the fast-path device [3].
3. **Deployment complexity.** Hugepages, IOMMU/VFIO binding, CPU isolation, and NUMA awareness make DPDK applications closer to firmware than to userspace programs [3][7].
4. **Security surface.** A userspace process with direct DMA access is privileged; IOMMU confinement is mandatory, and PMD bugs escape the kernel's fault containment.
5. **No free generality.** No TCP stack, no sockets API, no POSIX compatibility; full-stack applications must layer userspace stacks on top [8].
6. **Portability costs.** Weakly ordered architectures demand meticulous barrier placement; tuning is per-microarchitecture.

These limitations explain the rise of intermediate frameworks — AF_XDP and CNDP keep the kernel driver while bypassing the stack [3] — and they bound the technique's applicability: *throughput- and latency-critical data planes with predictable, high packet rates*.

---

## 7 Conclusion

DPDK achieves line-rate software forwarding through the *coherent elimination of every per-packet tax* the conventional stack levies: interrupts replaced by polling, copies replaced by descriptor-mediated ownership transfer, allocation replaced by hugepage-backed mempools, locks replaced by lock-free rings, and migratable scheduling replaced by NUMA-pinned run-to-completion cores. Each mechanism is individually simple; their composition is radical, because each depends on the others — zero-copy is only free if buffers are pre-allocated, pre-allocation is only fast if pages are huge and local, and polling is only affordable if per-packet work fits in cache.

The cycle-budget analysis of §5.1 turns this intuition into a proof obligation: at 100 Gbps and beyond, there is no room in the budget for the abstractions we removed, so their removal is *necessary*. The methodology of §5.2 keeps line-rate claims falsifiable; §6 marks the boundary of applicability — kernel bypass is a scalpel, not a hammer.
