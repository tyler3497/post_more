# The LCF Architecture and the de Bruijn Criterion: Trustworthy Kernels for Higher-Order Logic Proof Assistants

## Abstract

This thesis examines the two dominant architectural answers to the central epistemological question of mechanized mathematics: *why should we believe a proof assistant?* The Edinburgh LCF architecture, introduced by Robin Milner and collaborators in the 1970s, answers by reducing trust to a small logical kernel encapsulated in an abstract data type of theorems, so that the soundness of arbitrarily complex proof automation rests only on the correctness of the kernel and the host language's type discipline. The de Bruijn criterion, articulated by Freek Wiedijk in the tradition of N. G. de Bruijn's Automath, answers instead by demanding that proof assistants emit independently checkable proof objects verifiable by a small, separately written program. We trace the lineage from Stanford and Edinburgh LCF through HOL88, HOL Light, Isabelle, and Coq; formalize the precise trust arguments each architecture supplies; survey the historical record of soundness-critical kernel bugs and the verification efforts that responded to them; and compare LCF-style abstract kernels with proof-object architectures, deep and shallow embeddings, and logical frameworks such as Dedukti. Our central claim is that the LCF kernel and the de Bruijn criterion are complementary rather than competing: the former economizes *what must be trusted during proof construction*, the latter *what must be trusted after proof construction*.

## 1. Introduction

A proof assistant is, famously, "just another program" [8]. Its output—claims of the form *theorem T has been proved*—is valuable only insofar as the program itself is trustworthy. But proof assistants are among the most complex programs ever written for a mathematically demanding purpose: parsers, pretty-printers, higher-order unification, decision procedures, rewriting engines, counterexample search, elaborate user interfaces. No human can audit all of this code, and it changes constantly. The foundational problem of proof-assistant architecture is therefore one of *trust localization*: how to arrange the system so that its soundness depends on as small and as stable a portion of code as possible.

Two architectural ideas dominate the answer. The first is the **LCF approach**: implement the primitive inference rules of the object logic as the *only* constructors of an abstract data type `thm` in a strongly typed metalanguage, letting the metalanguage's type checker guarantee that no value of type `thm` can be manufactured by any other route [1][2][4]. The second is the **de Bruijn criterion**, named by Freek Wiedijk after N. G. de Bruijn of Automath: a proof assistant satisfies the criterion when it can export its proofs in a form checkable by a small program that a skeptical user could write independently [5][8]. The two are often conflated; both concentrate trust, but at different points in the proof lifecycle, with different engineering trade-offs.

> **Thesis claim:** The LCF architecture and the de Bruijn criterion provide *complementary* trust arguments: LCF guarantees *correctness by construction* of theorems during interactive proof, while the de Bruijn criterion guarantees *correctness by independent re-checking* after the fact. Systems combining both achieve the strongest currently practical assurance.

Section 2 surveys the history from Automath through Edinburgh LCF to the modern HOL, Isabelle, and Coq families. Section 3 describes our comparative methodology. Section 4 develops the technical core: the abstract theorem type, the de Bruijn criterion, kernel extensions and trust costs, the soundness-bug record, the proof-search/checking distinction, and LCF-style versus embedding-based architectures. Section 5 presents the empirical record; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Automath and the idea of independent checking

The story begins with N. G. de Bruijn's **Automath** project at Eindhoven (1967–): the first proof assistant in the modern sense, a language for expressing complete formal proofs checkable by a mechanical *checker* program. Its landmark was Jutting's 1979 verification of Landau's *Grundlagen der Analysis*—143 pages of German text, checked overnight in 1979 and re-checkable in under half a second today [6][9]. Automath embodied the *proof-object* philosophy: trust resides in the small checker, not in the large proof-construction machinery.

### 2.2 Stanford LCF, Edinburgh LCF, and the abstract theorem type

The LCF lineage begins with **Stanford LCF** (Milner, 1972) for Scott's Logic of Computable Functions—not itself LCF-style in the modern sense, since the "LCF" of its name denoted the object logic, not the architecture [4]. The **Edinburgh LCF** breakthrough (Gordon, Milner, and Wadsworth, 1979; LNCS 78) [2] introduced a cluster of now-standard innovations: backward proof via *tactics*, the theory hierarchy, and—a general-purpose functional programming language, **ML** (for *meta language*), as the vehicle for operating on formulas of the object language [1][4].

Milner's key insight was driven by a practical crisis: in early experiments he kept running out of memory storing explicit proofs [4]. The solution was an **abstract type of theorems**, `thm`, whose operations were exactly the inference rules of the logical calculus:

```ocaml
(* Sketch of the LCF idea in modern OCaml *)
module Kernel : sig
  type thm                             (* abstract: representation hidden *)
  val assume : term -> thm
  val modus_ponens : thm -> thm -> thm
  val gen : term -> thm -> thm          (* universal generalization *)
  (* ... one function per primitive inference rule ... *)
end
```

Because ML's type discipline forbids code outside the `Kernel` module from manufacturing values of the abstract type `thm`, *every* value of type `thm` is guaranteed to have been produced by applying inference rules to axioms [1][4]. Users may write arbitrarily complex ML programs, but the validity of the theorems they produce depends only on the soundness of the kernel and the correctness of the ML compiler.

### 2.3 The HOL, Isabelle, and Coq families

The LCF idea proved extraordinarily fertile. Mike Gordon's **HOL** (1980s) adapted LCF technology to Church's classical higher-order logic; HOL88, HOL90, and HOL4 followed, and John Harrison's **HOL Light** (1990s–) pushed minimalism to its extreme with a kernel of roughly **430 lines of OCaml** implementing ten primitive inference rules plus definitional principles [6][7]. Lawrence Paulson's **Isabelle** (1986–) generalized the architecture to a *generic* proof assistant: a small inference kernel for a meta-logic in which diverse object logics—FOL, ZF, and above all HOL—could be embedded [11]. The **Coq** family (1984–), rooted in the Calculus of Constructions, took the proof-object route: tactics construct proof terms in dependent type theory, and a kernel type-checker validates them [13]—closer in spirit to Automath.

### 2.4 Wiedijk and the de Bruijn criterion

Freek Wiedijk's formulation, canonically stated in Barendregt and Wiedijk's "The Challenge of Computer Mathematics" [10], is:

> **Definition (de Bruijn criterion).** A proof assistant *satisfies the de Bruijn criterion* if it generates proofs that can be checked, independently of the system that created them, by a *simple* program—one that a skeptical user could write herself [8].

The criterion separates the *powerful proof engine* from the *simple proof checker*. It is satisfied paradigmatically by Coq (proof terms checked by the kernel) and Mizar (articles checked by a small verifier), and increasingly by systems exporting to **Dedukti**, a logical framework based on the λΠ-calculus modulo rewriting proposed as a *universal* proof checker for HOL, Coq/Matita, PVS, and others [5].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds by **comparative architectural reconstruction**: rebuilding the trust arguments of Edinburgh LCF from the Gordon–Milner–Wadsworth volume [2] and Paulson's account [1][4], of HOL Light from Harrison's kernel description [6], and of the de Bruijn criterion from Wiedijk's writings and the Dedukti literature [5][9]; accounting the *trusted computing base* (TCB) of each architecture in size and structure; surveying the documented history of soundness-critical bugs as natural experiments; and reviewing the landmark kernel-verification efforts—Harrison's "Towards Self-verification of HOL Light" [12] and the CakeML-verified HOL kernel [7 in-text].

Kernel line counts (e.g., "430 lines") are cited from authors' own statements [6][7]; they are *indicative* rather than exact, since counting conventions vary. Soundness-bug claims are restricted to cases documented in the archival literature or acknowledged by maintainers.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The abstract theorem type: how ML's type system becomes a soundness argument

The LCF trust argument must establish that *no execution* of *any* user program can produce a value of type `thm` that does not correspond to a genuine derivation. It decomposes into three lemmas:

1. **Abstraction lemma.** In a correct ML implementation, an abstract type's representation is accessible only within its defining module. No client code can forge or mutate a `thm` value except through the exported operations.
2. **Rule-fidelity lemma.** Each exported operation implements a *sound* primitive inference rule: applied to genuine theorems, it yields a genuine theorem. This is argued informally by the kernel author and proved formally in the verification efforts of §5.
3. **Composition lemma.** By induction over the evaluation of any ML program, every `thm` value it produces is a finite composition of exported operations—hence of sound primitive inferences—applied to axioms.

The conclusion: **proof search is untrusted but proof construction is trusted-by-construction**. A tactic may be buggy, may loop, may throw exceptions, but it *cannot* produce a false theorem, because the only theorems it can produce are those the kernel's rules already sanction [1][2].

Note the argument's subtle dependencies. It assumes the *correctness of the ML compiler and runtime*—a forged `thm` could in principle arise from a compiler bug, from unsafe features (`Obj.magic` in OCaml, which HOL Light's kernel discipline must carefully avoid), or from memory corruption. The LCF argument therefore *localizes* trust rather than eliminating it; Harrison is explicit that the "critical code" of HOL Light is ~430 lines *plus* the OCaml compiler and runtime [6].

### 4.2 The de Bruijn criterion, precisely stated

Wiedijk's criterion has two components [8][10]:

- **Exportability.** The system must *emit* its proofs in a complete, self-contained form—proof objects, proof terms, or fully detailed scripts.
- **Independent checkability.** There must exist a *small* checker, *independent* of the producing system, that accepts exactly the valid proof objects.

The philosophical payoff is **trust portability**: a theorem proved in Coq can be believed by someone who trusts only a small independently written checker for the Calculus of Inductive Constructions. The Dedukti project pushes this to its conclusion: a *single* small checker for the λΠ-calculus modulo rewriting that re-verifies proofs originating in HOL, Coq, Matita, PVS, and FoCaLiZe alike [5].

The criterion's cost is **proof-object size and elaboration complexity**. Fully explicit proof terms for substantial developments can reach gigabytes [5], and *producing* them requires the elaborator to record every step the automation took—engineering LCF-style systems avoided because Milner's abstraction made it unnecessary [4].

| Dimension | LCF architecture | de Bruijn-criterion architecture |
|---|---|---|
| Trust anchor | Abstract `thm` type + kernel rules | Small independent checker |
| Proof artifact | None required (type system suffices) | Explicit proof object required |
| Buggy tactic consequence | Failure/exception, never false theorem | Invalid object rejected by checker |
| Kernel size (indicative) | ~430 lines (HOL Light) [6][7] | Checker: hundreds–few thousand lines |
| Trusts the ML/compiler | Yes (type discipline is load-bearing) | Only for proof *search*, not checking |
| Cross-system verification | Hard (kernels differ) | Natural (export to common checker) |

### 4.3 Kernel extensions and their trust cost: Isabelle's small kernel, Coq's large one

Not all kernels are as spartan as HOL Light's. **Isabelle** follows the LCF pattern—a small inference kernel for Isabelle/Pure, with tactics built outside it—but its kernel necessarily encompasses more: schematic variables, higher-order unification in rule composition, and the definitional packages. It is *small relative to the whole system* but an order of magnitude larger than HOL Light's [8][11].

**Coq's kernel**, implementing the Calculus of Inductive Constructions with universe polymorphism, cumulativity, and a conversion checker with efficient reduction machines, has historically been *much* larger—tens of thousands of lines—and harder to audit [8]. This is the price of Coq's expressive logic: every feature of the type theory is load-bearing for soundness and must live in the TCB. The contrast illustrates a general law:

> **Kernel economy law.** The trusted base grows with the complexity of the *logic*, not the complexity of the *automation*. LCF lets automation be arbitrarily complex for free; only the logic's primitive rules and definitional principles cost trust.

This is why HOL-family kernels stay tiny while supporting enormously powerful automation—Isabelle's **Sledgehammer**, which farms proof search out to external ATPs and *replays* results through the kernel [11], is the extreme case: untrusted supercomputer-scale search, kernel-checked result.

### 4.4 The soundness-bug record: what actually failed

The most instructive evidence comes from failures. Every major proof assistant has, at some point, accepted something it should not have; soundness-critical bugs have been found and fixed in the kernels or definitional mechanisms of Coq, Isabelle/HOL, and the HOL family alike. Three patterns recur:

1. **Definitional-extension bugs.** The most dangerous class. LCF-style systems extend the logic by *definitional principles* (constant definitions, type definitions) that must be conservative. Bugs here—where a "definition" smuggles in inconsistency—have historically been the leading cause of kernel-level unsoundness.
2. **Kernel-rule implementation bugs.** Coding errors in a primitive rule (bound variables, assumption sets, universe levels). HOL Light's tiny kernel makes these *auditable*—the entire kernel can be read in one sitting [6].
3. **Elaborator/checker bugs.** In proof-object systems, elaborator bugs are harmless (the checker rejects bad terms), but *checker* bugs in conversion or unification are fatal. The de Bruijn criterion's value is exactly that it makes this distinction crisp: only checker bugs matter.

### 4.5 Proof checking versus proof search: the fundamental asymmetry

Both architectures exploit a deep asymmetry: **checking a proof is easy; finding one is hard** (checking is decidable and often low-polynomial; search is undecidable in general). LCF internalizes this in the type system—search lives in untrusted ML code; only the *result*, a kernel-built `thm`, is trusted. The de Bruijn criterion externalizes it—search lives in the prover; only the *certificate* is re-checked.

This asymmetry also explains why **tactics** were LCF's great practical invention [1][2]. A tactic maps a goal to subgoals *plus a justification*: a kernel-level function rebuilding the theorem from the subgoals' proofs. Because justifications are ordinary kernel rule applications, a tactic performing "hundreds of primitive inferences in a single step" [2] costs no additional trust. Modern descendants—Isabelle's Eisbach, Coq's Ltac2, Lean's metaprogramming—are all elaborations of this 1979 idea.

### 4.6 LCF-style versus embeddings: deep, shallow, and the Edinburgh LF

A third architectural axis: how an *object logic* is represented inside a *framework*. In a **deep embedding**, the object logic's syntax and rules are formalized as datatypes inside the framework, and soundness must be proved about the embedding. In a **shallow embedding**, object-logic constructs map directly onto framework constructs, inheriting the framework's guarantees. LCF-style systems are, in a precise sense, *shallow embeddings of their own kernel's logic into ML*: the abstract type `thm` *is* the embedding, and ML's type system does the soundness work [4].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Kernel sizes across the LCF lineage

| System | Kernel size (indicative) | Language | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Edinburgh LCF (1979) | Small kernel (abstract `thm`) | ML | [2][4] |
| HOL88 / HOL90 | Few thousand lines | ML | [4][6] |
| **HOL Light** | **~430 lines** ("critical code") | OCaml | [6][7] |
| Isabelle (kernel) | Small core, larger than HOL Light | Standard ML | [8][11] |
| Coq (kernel) | Tens of thousands of lines (historically) | OCaml | [8] |
| Dedukti checker | Small (λΠ-modulo checker) | OCaml/Haskell | [5] |

The HOL Light figure is the empirical anchor of the minimalist position: Harrison reports that the kernel—ten primitive rules (REFL, TRANS, MK_COMB, ABS, BETA, ASSUME, EQ_MP, DEDUCT_ANTISYM_RULE, INST, INST_TYPE) plus definitional principles—is about 430 lines of OCaml, and teaching materials worldwide quote: *"HOL Light is just 430 lines of the critical code"* [6][7]. Everything else—the tactics, decision procedures, the Flyspeck formalization of the Kepler conjecture—is untrusted automation whose output the kernel re-verifies rule by rule.

### 5.2 The verification of kernels: Harrison and CakeML

If the kernel is the trust anchor, the next step is to *verify the kernel itself*:

- **Harrison's self-verification (2006).** In "Towards Self-verification of HOL Light" [12], Harrison formalized the HOL Light kernel *within* HOL Light and proved its soundness—a bootstrap in which the system reasons about its own trust anchor. Its significance is methodological: the LCF kernel is small enough to be a feasible verification target at all.
- **The CakeML-verified HOL kernel.** Kumar, Myreen, Norrish, and Owens mechanized the semantics and soundness proof of the HOL Light kernel *including its definitional principles*—extending Harrison's result—and showed that a synthesized implementation of the kernel in the verified CakeML compiler's ML refines the inference system, yielding a verified machine-code implementation of the kernel [7 in-text]. This closes the loop the original LCF argument left open: the TCB is not only small, it is *proved correct* down to machine code.

These results are the strongest trust argument currently available for any proof assistant.

### 5.3 The de Bruijn criterion in practice: Dedukti as universal checker

On the proof-object side, the landmark is **Dedukti**: translations now exist from HOL-family systems, Coq/Matita, PVS, FoCaLiZe, and automated provers into Dedukti's λΠ-calculus modulo rewriting, letting a single small checker re-verify proofs from mutually untrusting systems [5]. Recent work has addressed gigabyte-scale proof objects with concurrent proof checking on multicore hardware while keeping the checker small [5]. The de Bruijn criterion has moved from philosophical desideratum to deployed infrastructure.

## 6. Limitations and Threats to Validity

**The compiler and hardware remain trusted.** Both architectures bottom out in compilers, runtimes, and silicon. The CakeML work [7 in-text] pushes the verified boundary to machine code, but hardware is outside any formal argument.

**Small kernels can still be wrong.** Auditability is not correctness. The soundness-bug record (§4.4) shows that even small, carefully maintained kernels have harbored soundness-critical defects, particularly in definitional principles. The LCF argument guarantees that *if* the kernel rules are sound *then* all theorems are genuine; that the rules are sound remains a claim requiring proof, as §5.2 recognizes.

**The de Bruijn criterion's "simple program" is an idealization.** Writing a genuinely independent checker for a rich logic like the Calculus of Inductive Constructions is a major undertaking, and in practice "independent" checkers often share code, specifications, or authors with the prover. Dedukti mitigates this with a deliberately weak checker logic (λΠ-modulo), but translation *into* Dedukti is itself trust-relevant.

**Line counts are approximate.** Kernel sizes in §5.1 depend on counting conventions and evolve with each release; read them as order-of-magnitude indicators of auditability.

**Scope.** This thesis addresses classical higher-order-logic kernels in the LCF tradition and the de Bruijn-criterion literature around them. Constructive type-theoretic systems (Agda, Lean's kernel, Nuprl) and SMT-solver proof production raise related but distinct trust questions not treated here.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Fifty years after Milner's memory crisis and de Bruijn's Automath, the two great answers to *"why believe a proof assistant?"* have converged in practice while remaining distinct in principle. The **LCF architecture** says: make the kernel so small and the abstraction barrier so rigid that soundness follows from the host language's type discipline—*correctness by construction*, with HOL Light's ~430-line OCaml kernel as the existence proof that radical minimalism is practical [6][7]. The **de Bruijn criterion** says: make proofs exportable and checkable by a small program a skeptic could write—*correctness by independent re-checking*, with Dedukti emerging as the universal checker [5].

The architectures are complementary, not competing. LCF economizes trust *during* proof construction; the de Bruijn criterion economizes trust *after* it. The systems with the strongest assurance stories—HOL Light with its verified kernel, Coq with its proof-term discipline, Isabelle with kernel-checked Sledgehammer reconstruction—combine elements of both. The frontier is clear: shrink kernels further, verify them down to machine code, and make proof export to independent checkers routine rather than heroic.
