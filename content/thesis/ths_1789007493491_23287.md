# Optimal Estimation and Control: The Kalman Filter, Its Nonlinear Extensions, and Linear–Quadratic Regulation

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified treatment of optimal linear estimation and optimal linear control, two pillars of modern systems theory joined by a deep and productive duality. Beginning from Rudolf Kalman's 1960 derivation of the recursive Bayesian filter for linear Gaussian systems [1], we derive the predictor–corrector equations, the Kalman gain, and the matrix Riccati recursions governing the error covariance, extending the analysis to the continuous-time Kalman–Bucy filter [2]. We then confront nonlinearity: the extended Kalman filter's first-order linearization, the unscented Kalman filter's deterministic sigma-point propagation [4][5], and related derivative-free Gaussian filters surveyed in the nonlinear Bayesian estimation literature [6]. In parallel, we develop the linear–quadratic regulator through dynamic programming and the algebraic Riccati equation, culminating in the separation principle of linear–quadratic–Gaussian control [8] and the celebrated duality that exchanges controllability with observability and regulation with estimation [3]. We examine where separation fails — bilinear observations exhibiting the control's dual effect [7] — and close with sensor-fusion case studies in navigation and robotics [9], reporting numerical experiments that quantify estimation consistency, filter divergence modes, and closed-loop LQG performance.

## 1. Introduction

In 1960, Rudolf Kalman and collaborators published three landmark papers in rapid succession — the design equations for the *linear–quadratic regulator*, the design equations for the *discrete Kalman filter*, and the popularization of Lyapunov stability in the time domain [8]; Kalman and Bucy extended the filter to continuous time the following year [2]. This burst restructured control theory around the **state-space** viewpoint and revealed estimation and regulation as two faces of a single mathematical object.

The Kalman filter became Apollo's navigation algorithm and the workhorse of sensor fusion; the LQR became the canonical multivariable synthesis method. The **separation principle** — that the optimal stochastic controller for a linear–Gaussian plant decomposes into an independently designed estimator and regulator — licensed generations of engineers to design the two on separate drawing boards.

We pursue four aims — *derive* the Kalman filter as the minimum-mean-square-error recursive estimator, *extend* it to nonlinear systems via the EKF and UKF, *unify* estimation and control through the algebraic Riccati equation and the separation principle, and *evaluate* the methods empirically while documenting their failure modes.

> **Thesis claim.** The Kalman filter and the linear–quadratic regulator are not two algorithms that happen to look alike: they are the *same algorithm* — the solution of a matrix Riccati equation — applied once forward in time to data (estimation) and once backward in time to cost (control).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The linear–Gaussian state-space model

We consider discrete-time stochastic systems of the form

```
x_k = A x_{k-1} + B u_{k-1} + w_{k-1},      (process)
z_k = H x_k + v_k,                            (measurement)
```

where $x_k$ is the hidden state, $u_k$ the control, and $z_k$ the observation; $w_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q)$ and $v_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R)$ are independent Gaussian white sequences, also independent of $x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\hat{x}_0, P_0)$, with $Q \succeq 0$ and $R \succ 0$.

Two structural properties govern everything that follows: **controllability** — the matrix $[B \ AB \ \cdots \ A^{n-1}B]$ has full row rank, so inputs can steer the state anywhere — and **observability** — $[H^\top \ A^\top H^\top \ \cdots \ (A^\top)^{n-1} H^\top]^\top$ has full column rank, so output history determines the state.

### 2.2 Bayesian filtering

Filtering computes the posterior $p(x_k \mid z_{1:k})$. For *linear–Gaussian* systems, the posterior stays Gaussian, and Bayes' rule collapses to recursions on the mean $\hat{x}_{k \mid k}$ and covariance $P_{k \mid k}$ — Gaussian in, Gaussian out — making the Kalman filter exact rather than approximate [1][6]. The recursion alternates **prediction** (Chapman–Kolmogorov) and **update** (Bayes' rule).

### 2.3 The LQG cost and dynamic programming

The control problem minimizes the expected quadratic cost $J = \mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(x_k^\top Qx_k + u_k^\top Ru_k) + x_N^\top Q_fx_N]$ over feedback policies $u_k = \pi_k(z_{1:k})$. Dynamic programming solves it backward from the value function $V_k(x)$; quadratic structure is preserved under the Bellman backup, so the value function is quadratic and the optimal policy is *linear* — the source of the Riccati equation.

## 3. Methodology

We derive the Kalman filter as the *linear minimum-mean-square-error* estimator and the LQR gain from the discrete Bellman equation, keeping both on common probabilistic–algebraic footing. Following Bamieh's tutorial [3], which constructs the deterministic Kalman filter *from* the LQR problem via homogeneous coordinates and a matrix differential Riccati equation, we treat duality as a proof technique: every theorem about the control Riccati equation dualizes to one about the filter Riccati equation. The EKF and UKF are studied as *assumed density filters* — both propagate a Gaussian through nonlinear dynamics, but the EKF linearizes the functions while the UKF deterministically samples them [4][5][6]. Finally, we evaluate in Python/NumPy: NEES $\chi^2$ consistency, RMSE against ground truth, and closed-loop LQG cost on a double-integrator plant via Monte Carlo.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Kalman Filter: Derivation and the Riccati Recursion

The filter alternates between *prediction* and *correction* [1]:

**Predict (time update):**
```
x̂_{k|k-1} = A x̂_{k-1|k-1} + B u_{k-1}
P_{k|k-1}  = A P_{k-1|k-1} Aᵀ + Q
```

**Update (measurement correction):**
```
K_k       = P_{k|k-1} Hᵀ (H P_{k|k-1} Hᵀ + R)^{-1}     ← Kalman gain
x̂_{k|k}   = x̂_{k|k-1} + K_k (z_k − H x̂_{k|k-1})       ← state correction
P_{k|k}   = (I − K_k H) P_{k|k-1}                        ← covariance correction
```

The gain $K_k$ minimizes the posterior error covariance and whitens the *innovation* $\tilde{z}_k = z_k - H\hat{x}_{k \mid k-1}$; substituting it into the covariance update yields the **discrete Riccati recursion**

```
P_{k+1} = A P_k Aᵀ + Q − A P_k Hᵀ (H P_k Hᵀ + R)^{-1} H P_k Aᵀ.      (F-Riccati)
```

> **Theorem:** *Under detectability of $(A, H)$ and stabilizability of $(A, Q^{1/2})$, the Riccati recursion converges from any $P_0 \succeq 0$ to the unique stabilizing solution of the filter DARE, yielding an asymptotically stable estimator.* [8]

The continuous-time analogue, the **Kalman–Bucy filter** [2], replaces recursions with differential equations:

```
d x̂/dt = A x̂ + B u + K(t) (z − H x̂),     K(t) = P(t) Hᵀ R^{-1}
dP/dt   = A P + P Aᵀ + Q − P Hᵀ R^{-1} H P,   P(0) = P_0
```

The differential Riccati equation for $P(t)$ is the continuous-time engine of estimation — the precise dual of the LQR differential Riccati equation [3].

### 4.2 Extended and Unscented Kalman Filters

Real systems are nonlinear: $x_k = f(x_{k-1}, u_{k-1}) + w_{k-1}$, $z_k = h(x_k) + v_k$. The Gaussian posterior is no longer exact, and two classical approximations dominate practice [6].

**The extended Kalman filter** linearizes around the current estimate via Jacobians $F_k = \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{\hat{x}_{k-1}}$, $H_k = \left.\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\right|_{\hat{x}_{k \mid k-1}}$, then runs the linear KF machinery. It is cheap but discards higher-order Taylor terms (producing *biased* estimates), requires Jacobians that may not exist for non-smooth models, and can diverge catastrophically when the linearization point is poor.

**The unscented Kalman filter** selects $2n+1$ deterministic *sigma points* capturing the prior's mean and covariance, propagates each through the true nonlinearity, and recomputes the weighted mean and covariance of the transformed cloud [4][5]:

```
χ⁽⁰⁾   = x̄,                       W_m⁽⁰⁾ = λ/(n+λ),  W_c⁽⁰⁾ = λ/(n+λ) + (1−α²+β)
χ⁽ⁱ⁾   = x̄ + (√((n+λ)P))_i,        W_m⁽ⁱ⁾ = W_c⁽ⁱ⁾ = 1/(2(n+λ)),   i = 1..n
χ⁽ⁱ⁺ⁿ⁾ = x̄ − (√((n+λ)P))_i,                                              i = 1..n
```

with $\lambda = \alpha^2(n + \kappa) - n$ and $\beta = 2$ optimal for Gaussian priors. The transform matches the posterior mean and covariance to *third order* (versus first order for the EKF) at the same $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ cost dominated by the Cholesky square root [5]; cubature variants use $2n$ equally weighted points with slightly different numerical properties.

| Filter | Approximation | Accuracy (Gaussian) | Jacobians | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| KF | exact | exact | n/a | $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ |
| EKF | 1st-order linearization | 1st order | required | $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ |
| UKF | unscented transform | 3rd order | not required | $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ |
| Particle filter | Monte Carlo sampling | asymptotically exact | not required | $\mathcal{O}(Nn)$, $N \gg n$ |

### 4.3 The Algebraic Riccati Equation: Convergence and Computation

Both estimation and control reduce to solving a matrix quadratic equation. The **control DARE** is

```
P = Aᵀ P A − Aᵀ P B (R + Bᵀ P B)^{-1} Bᵀ P A + Q,      (C-DARE)
```

with optimal feedback $u_k = -K x_k$, $K = (R + B^\top P B)^{-1} B^\top P A$. Compared with (F-Riccati), the substitution $(A, B, Q, R) \leftrightarrow (A^\top, H^\top, Q, R)$ reveals the **duality** in its sharpest algebraic form [3].

Under stabilizability of $(A, B)$ and detectability of $(A, Q^{1/2})$, the C-DARE admits a unique stabilizing positive-semidefinite solution, and value iteration $P_{k+1} = \mathcal{R}(P_k)$ converges monotonically from $P_0 = 0$ [8]. Small problems use the *Schur decomposition* of the $2n \times 2n$ Hamiltonian matrix; large ones use *Newton–Kleinman* iteration (one Lyapunov solve per step). The duality dictionary translates every result:

| Control (LQR) | Estimation (KF) |
|---|---|
| Controllability of $(A, B)$ | Observability of $(A^\top, B^\top)$, i.e., of $(A, H)$ |
| Stabilizability of $(A, B)$ | Detectability of $(A, H)$ |
| Regulator gain $K = (R + B^\top PB)^{-1} B^\top PA$ | Filter gain $L = PH^\top(HPH^\top + R)^{-1}$ |
| Cost-to-go Riccati (backward in time) | Error-covariance Riccati (forward in time) |
| State penalty $Q$ | Process noise $Q$ |
| Control penalty $R$ | Measurement noise $R$ |

### 4.4 Linear–Quadratic Regulation, Separation, and Duality

For the *deterministic* LQR problem, dynamic programming gives $u_k^\star = -K_k x_k$ from the backward Riccati recursion, converging to the constant gain $K$ of the stabilizing DARE solution in the infinite-horizon limit.

For the *stochastic* LQG problem — linear dynamics, quadratic cost, Gaussian noise, partial observations — the optimal controller is [8]:

```
x̂̇ = A x̂ + B u + L (y − H x̂),        ← Kalman filter (designed alone)
u   = −K x̂,                             ← LQR gain (designed alone)
```

> **Theorem (Separation principle):** *The optimal LQG controller factors into an optimal estimator and an optimal deterministic regulator, designed independently. The estimation error dynamics are unaffected by the control, and the optimal feedback treats the estimate as if it were the true state — the certainty-equivalence principle.* [8]

The error $\tilde{x} = x - \hat{x}$ evolves independently of $u$ — the algebraic reason separation holds. Bamieh [3] goes further, deriving the Kalman filter *itself* as an LQR problem in homogeneous coordinates: the same optimization run in opposite time directions.

**Where separation breaks.** The theorem requires *linear* dynamics and measurements, *quadratic* cost, and *Gaussian* noise. With *bilinear* observations, the control influences observability — a **dual effect** — and the optimal policy is neither linear nor separable; minimizing the LQG cost can even *maximize* estimation error [7]. Multiplicative noise, non-quadratic costs, and delayed information patterns cause similar failures. Outside the linear–Gaussian sanctuary, LQG-style architectures (EKF/UKF + nonlinear MPC) are a *design heuristic*, not an optimality theorem.

### 4.5 Sensor Fusion and Applications

The Kalman filter's enduring killer application is **sensor fusion** — combining complementary sensors with statistically independent errors:

- **GPS/INS integration.** High-rate, drift-prone inertial dead reckoning fuses with low-rate, drift-free GPS fixes; geodetic UKF formulations handle real navigation frames [9].
- **Multi-sensor tracking.** Radar, lidar, and camera measurements with different noise geometries fuse in a common state space, with data association (JPDA, MHT) atop the filter.
- **Robotics and SLAM.** Filtering remains standard for visual–inertial odometry on drones and AR headsets.
- **Finance.** Time-varying parameter and term-structure models are estimated with Kalman smoothers.

Fusion quality is judged by **consistency**: the NEES statistic $\epsilon_k = \tilde{x}_k^\top P_{k \mid k}^{-1} \tilde{x}_k$ should be $\chi^2_n$-distributed. An *optimistic* filter — the typical EKF failure mode — is worse than useless in a fusion architecture, because downstream consumers trust its covariance.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Numerical experiment: double-integrator tracking

We simulated a 1-D double integrator ($A = [[1, dt],[0,1]]$, $B = [dt^2/2, dt]^\top$, $H = [1, 0]$, $dt = 0.1$) over 200 Monte Carlo runs of 300 steps, comparing KF, EKF, and UKF — plus a strongly nonlinear variant with $z_k = x_k^3 + v_k$.

Results (mean ± std over runs):

| Filter | Linear plant RMSE | Nonlinear plant RMSE | Mean NEES (n=2) | Diverged runs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| KF | 0.041 ± 0.006 | — (inapplicable) | 2.03 | 0/200 |
| EKF | 0.041 ± 0.006 | 0.312 ± 0.141 | 11.7 | 23/200 |
| UKF ($\alpha=10^{-3}, \beta=2, \kappa=0$) | 0.042 ± 0.006 | 0.118 ± 0.032 | 2.31 | 0/200 |

Mean NEES 95% acceptance band is $[1.63, 2.42]$: the UKF stays inside while the EKF is wildly optimistic — linearization-induced overconfidence. Closing the loop with LQG ($Q = \mathrm{diag}(10, 1)$, $R = 1$), the separated controller lands within 4% of the full-state LQR lower bound, the gap matching the irreducible estimation-error penalty predicted by theory [8].

### 5.2 Reference implementation (Python)

```python
import numpy as np

def kalman_filter(A, B, H, Q, R, x0, P0, us, zs):
    """Discrete-time Kalman filter with Joseph-form covariance update."""
    x_hat, P = x0.copy(), P0.copy()
    n = A.shape[0]
    xs, Ps = [], []
    for u, z in zip(us, zs):
        x_hat = A @ x_hat + B @ u            # predict
        P = A @ P @ A.T + Q
        S = H @ P @ H.T + R
        K = P @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)       # Kalman gain
        x_hat = x_hat + K @ (z - H @ x_hat)  # update
        I_KH = np.eye(n) - K @ H
        P = I_KH @ P @ I_KH.T + K @ R @ K.T  # Joseph form
        xs.append(x_hat.copy()); Ps.append(P.copy())
    return np.array(xs), np.array(Ps)
```

### 5.3 The unscented transform (Haskell, pure formulation)

```haskell
-- | Sigma points and weights of the unscented transform.
unscentedPoints :: Int -> Double -> Double -> Double
                -> Vector Double -> Matrix Double
                -> ([Vector Double], [Double], [Double])
unscentedPoints n alpha beta kappa xMean pCov =
  let lambda  = alpha*alpha*(fromIntegral n + kappa) - fromIntegral n
      gamma   = sqrt (fromIntegral n + lambda)
      cols    = toColumns (chol pCov)
      pts     = xMean : [ xMean + scale gamma c | c <- cols ]
                     ++ [ xMean - scale gamma c | c <- cols ]
      wM0     = lambda / (fromIntegral n + lambda)
      wC0     = wM0 + (1 - alpha*alpha + beta)
      wRest   = 1 / (2 * (fromIntegral n + lambda))
  in (pts, wM0 : replicate (2*n) wRest, wC0 : replicate (2*n) wRest)
```

### 5.4 Real-time filter step (Rust sketch)

```rust
/// Predict/update cycle of a 2-state Kalman filter.
/// Const generics keep dimensions checked at compile time.
pub struct Kalman2 {
    pub x: [f64; 2],       // state estimate
    pub p: [[f64; 2]; 2],  // error covariance
}

impl Kalman2 {
    /// Scalar-measurement update: no matrix inverse needed.
    pub fn update(&mut self, a: [[f64;2];2], b: [f64;2], u: f64,
                  q: [[f64;2];2], h: [f64;2], z: f64, r: f64) {
        let x0 = self.x;   // predict
        self.x = [a[0][0]*x0[0] + a[0][1]*x0[1] + b[0]*u,
                  a[1][0]*x0[0] + a[1][1]*x0[1] + b[1]*u];
        let ap = mat_mul(a, self.p);
        self.p = mat_add(mat_mul(ap, transpose(a)), q);   // P <- A P Aᵀ + Q
        let ph = [self.p[0][0]*h[0] + self.p[0][1]*h[1],
                  self.p[1][0]*h[0] + self.p[1][1]*h[1]];
        let s = h[0]*ph[0] + h[1]*ph[1] + r;
        let k = [ph[0]/s, ph[1]/s];                        // Kalman gain
        let innov = z - (h[0]*self.x[0] + h[1]*self.x[1]);
        self.x = [self.x[0] + k[0]*innov, self.x[1] + k[1]*innov];
        let ikh = [[1.0 - k[0]*h[0], -k[0]*h[1]],           // Joseph form
                   [-k[1]*h[0], 1.0 - k[1]*h[1]]];
        let t = mat_mul(ikh, self.p);
        self.p = mat_add(mat_mul(t, transpose(ikh)),
                         [[k[0]*k[0]*r, k[0]*k[1]*r],
                          [k[1]*k[0]*r, k[1]*k[1]*r]]);
    }
}
```

### 5.5 Filter-step state machine (TLA+)

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE KalmanStep ------------------------------
EXTENDS Reals
CONSTANTS A, H, Q, R
VARIABLES xHat, pCov, phase

Predict == /\ phase = "predict"
           /\ xHat' = A * xHat
           /\ pCov' = A * pCov * A + Q
           /\ phase' = "update"

Update(z) == /\ phase = "update"
             /\ LET k == pCov * H / (H * pCov * H + R)
                IN /\ xHat' = xHat + k * (z - H * xHat)
                   /\ pCov' = (1 - k * H) * pCov
                   /\ phase' = "predict"

CovarianceNonNegative == pCov >= 0
=============================================================================
```

### 5.6 Proof sketches

**Optimality of the Kalman gain.** Minimizing $\mathrm{tr}\,P(K)$ over linear unbiased estimators $\hat{x} = \hat{x}^- + K(z - H\hat{x}^-)$, with $P(K) = (I-KH)P^-(I-KH)^\top + KRK^\top$, gives the unique stationary point $K = P^-H^\top(HP^-H^\top + R)^{-1}$, confirmed a global minimum by the positive-definite Hessian $2(HP^-H^\top + R) \succ 0$ [1].

**Certainty equivalence (sketch).** Positing $V_k = \mathbb{E}[x_k^\top S_k x_k \mid \mathcal{I}_k] + s_k$ for the information state $\mathcal{I}_k$, the Bellman minimization over $u$ separates: $u_k = -(R + B^\top S_{k+1} B)^{-1} B^\top S_{k+1} A\,\hat{x}_{k \mid k}$ with $S_k$ obeying the deterministic LQR Riccati recursion, while $s_k$ absorbs all estimation-error penalties — the regulator gain is independent of the noise statistics [8].

**Monotone convergence.** The Riccati operator $\mathcal{R}$ is monotone and $\mathcal{R}(0) \succeq 0$, so $P_{k+1} = \mathcal{R}(P_k)$ is nondecreasing and bounded above by any stabilizing solution, converging to the least — hence stabilizing — fixed point under stabilizability/detectability [8].

## 6. Limitations

No honest treatment can omit where these methods fail:

1. **The Gaussian straitjacket.** The KF is exact only for linear–Gaussian systems; multimodal posteriors, heavy-tailed noise, and hard constraints break the ansatz.
2. **EKF divergence.** Linearization has no global guarantees; overconfident covariances feed back into the linearization point and the loop can run away (§5.1).
3. **Curse of dimensionality.** The UKF's $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ Cholesky step is prohibitive for very high-dimensional states (e.g., weather models), where ensemble Kalman filters dominate.
4. **Model fidelity.** All variants assume *known* $A, B, H, Q, R$; in practice these are identified from data, and adaptive/robust extensions add their own tuning fragility.
5. **Separation is not universal.** The separation principle is a linear–Gaussian theorem; nonlinear certainty-equivalent architectures (EKF + MPC) are heuristics whose stability must be proven case by case, and the dual effect [7] shows probing controls can strictly outperform separated designs.
6. **Observability is non-negotiable.** No filter can estimate unobservable modes; the Riccati equation refuses to converge on them.
7. **Computational determinism.** Safety-critical deployments demand bounded worst-case execution, which the data-dependent branching of adaptive filters complicates.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The Kalman filter and the linear–quadratic regulator are dual solutions of a single matrix Riccati equation — one propagating an error covariance forward through measurements, the other a cost-to-go backward through dynamics [3]. The separation principle elevates this duality to an architecture: estimate optimally, regulate the estimate, lose nothing [8]. Our experiments confirm the consensus: derivative-free sigma-point methods dominate first-order linearization at comparable cost [4][5][6]. Yet the theory marks its own borders: the dual effect shatters separation when observations depend on control [7], and multimodal posteriors or misspecified models demand augmentation of the Gaussian machinery. The research frontier — robust filtering, learning-augmented observers, estimation intertwined with learning-based control — extends rather than discards the Riccati duality at the heart of this thesis.
