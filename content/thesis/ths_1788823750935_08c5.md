# Persistent Homology for High-Dimensional Scientific Data: Filtrations, Stability Theorems, Zigzag Persistence, and the Mapper Algorithm

## Abstract

This thesis presents a rigorous, self-contained treatment of **persistent homology** as applied to high-dimensional scientific data. Beginning with simplicial complexes (Vietoris–Rips, Čech, and alpha) and filtrations of a point cloud, we develop the algebra of *persistence modules* over the polynomial ring 𝔽[t], prove the structure theorem decomposing them into interval modules, and define *persistence diagrams* and *barcodes*. We prove the **stability theorems** of Cohen–Steiner, Edelsbrunner, and Harer: the bottleneck distance between persistence diagrams is bounded by the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between the underlying metric spaces, and refinements of this bound via filtered chain complexes [1]. We then generalize to *zigzag persistence* (Carlsson–de Silva), where maps may reverse direction, with applications to level-set analysis and dynamic point clouds [2]. We analyze the **Mapper algorithm** of Singh, Mémoli, and Carlsson as a nerve-theoretic summarization of pull-back covers [3], discuss **multi-parameter persistence** and its inherent non-decomposability, survey **vectorizations** (persistence landscapes [4] and persistence images [5]) enabling statistical learning, and review applications in materials science and neuroscience. Computational complexity, benchmarking on synthetic data with Ripser-class algorithms, and fundamental limitations conclude the work.

---

## 1. Introduction

The central difficulty of modern scientific data analysis is that the datasets of interest — single-cell RNA expression profiles, cryo-electron microscopy ensembles, cosmological simulations, fMRI voxel fields — inhabit spaces of dimension far beyond human intuition, yet are conjectured to concentrate near *low-dimensional stratified manifolds*. Persistent homology supplies an algebraic language for extracting the multiscale topological invariants of such data [6].

The *persistent homology pipeline* may be summarized by the composition:

$$\text{Metric spaces} \xrightarrow{\text{VR filtration}} \text{Simplicial filtrations} \xrightarrow{H_k(-;\mathbb{F})} \text{Persistence modules}$$

where the second arrow denotes simplicial homology with coefficients in a field $\mathbb{F}$. A finite point cloud $X$ in a metric space $(M,d)$ determines, for each scale parameter $\varepsilon \ge 0$, a simplicial complex $\mathrm{VR}_\varepsilon(X)$; as $\varepsilon$ grows, these complexes nest into a *filtration*. Applying the $k$-th homology functor yields a diagram of vector spaces and linear maps

$$H_k(\mathrm{VR}_{\varepsilon_1}(X)) \longrightarrow H_k(\mathrm{VR}_{\varepsilon_2}(X)) \longrightarrow \cdots \longrightarrow H_k(\mathrm{VR}_{\varepsilon_n}(X)),$$

whose decomposition into interval summands — the *barcode* or *persistence diagram* — records precisely the births and deaths of $k$-dimensional topological features (connected components, loops, voids, cavities).

Three properties elevate this construction from a curiosity to a scientific instrument:

1. **Stability.** Small perturbations of the input produce only small perturbations of the output, measured by the *bottleneck distance*. For finite metric spaces $X, Y$,
   $$d_B(\mathcal{B}_k(X), \mathcal{B}_k(Y)) \le 2\, d_{GH}(X, Y),$$
   where $d_{GH}$ is the Gromov–Hausdorff distance [1]. This theorem converts persistent homology into a *Lipschitz* observable — the crucial ingredient for any robust statistical methodology.
2. **Generality.** The filtration paradigm extends beyond Vietoris–Rips: to alpha complexes for computational geometry, to zigzag sequences where inclusions alternate direction [2], and to multi-parameter filtrations for bifiltrations by density and scale.
3. **Learnability.** Persistence diagrams can be *vectorized* — via landscapes [4] or images [5] — into Banach or Hilbert spaces where standard machine-learning machinery applies, with stability preserved.

This thesis treats each of these pillars with mathematical precision, states the principal theorems with proofs or proof sketches, surveys algorithms and software, and evaluates empirical performance and applications in materials science (porosity, defect analysis) and neuroscience (functional connectivity).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Simplicial complexes

A **simplicial complex** $K$ on a finite vertex set $V$ is a collection of subsets of $V$ closed under taking subsets: if $\sigma \in K$ and $\tau \subseteq \sigma$, then $\tau \in K$. A $k$-simplex is a set of cardinality $k+1$. The *abstract* point of view suffices for data analysis: we never embed the complex geometrically, we only need the combinatorics.

Given a finite metric space $(X, d)$ and a scale $\varepsilon \ge 0$, the **Vietoris–Rips complex** is defined by

$$\mathrm{VR}_\varepsilon(X) = \{\sigma \subseteq X : d(x, y) \le \varepsilon \text{ for all } x, y \in \sigma\}.$$

It is the *clique complex* of the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood graph of $X$: a simplex enters as soon as all its pairwise distances are below $\varepsilon$. The complex is computable from the distance matrix alone, which explains its dominance in applications despite its exponential worst-case size [1].

> **Theorem (Nerve lemma for Čech).** Let $X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be finite and $\varepsilon > 0$. The **Čech complex** $\check{C}_\varepsilon(X) = \{\sigma : \bigcap_{x \in \sigma} B(x, \varepsilon/2) \neq \emptyset\}$ is homotopy-equivalent to the union of balls $\bigcup_{x \in X} B(x, \varepsilon/2)$, by the nerve theorem.

The **alpha complex**, a subcomplex of the Delaunay triangulation, carries the same homotopy type as the Čech complex while remaining dramatically smaller in low dimensions; it is the filtration of choice for three-dimensional materials-science data.

The two classical complexes interleave: $\mathrm{VR}_\varepsilon(X) \subseteq \check{C}_{2\varepsilon}(X) \subseteq \mathrm{VR}_{2\varepsilon}(X)$, so Rips and Čech filtrations approximate each other with a factor of two — the origin of the factor 2 in the stability inequality [1].

### 2.2 Homology and filtrations

Fix a field $\mathbb{F}$ (typically $\mathbb{Z}_2$ or $\mathbb{Q}$). The $k$-th simplicial homology $H_k(K; \mathbb{F})$ is a vector space counting $k$-dimensional holes: $\dim H_0$ counts connected components, $\dim H_1$ counts independent loops, $\dim H_2$ counts enclosed voids. Functoriality: an inclusion $K \hookrightarrow L$ induces linear maps $H_k(K) \to H_k(L)$.

A **filtration** is a nested family $K_1 \subseteq K_2 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq K_n$. A *persistence module* is a diagram of vector spaces and linear maps

$$V_1 \xrightarrow{\phi_1} V_2 \xrightarrow{\phi_2} \cdots \xrightarrow{\phi_{n-1}} V_n.$$

> **Theorem (Structure theorem for persistence modules).** *Every pointwise finite-dimensional persistence module over a field decomposes uniquely, up to isomorphism and ordering, as a direct sum of interval modules $\mathbb{I}[b, d]$, where $\mathbb{I}[b,d]_i = \mathbb{F}$ for $b \le i \le d$ and $0$ otherwise, with identity maps internally.*

This is the representation theory of the $A_n$ quiver with all arrows oriented identically; indecomposables are intervals by Gabriel's theorem [2]. The multiset of intervals $[b_i, d_i)$ is the **barcode**; plotted as points $(b_i, d_i)$ above the diagonal, it is the **persistence diagram** $D_k(X)$.

### 2.3 Distances between diagrams

The **bottleneck distance** between two diagrams $D, D'$ is

$$d_B(D, D') = \inf_{\gamma} \sup_{p} \|p - \gamma(p)\|_\infty,$$

the infimum over all partial matchings $\gamma$ between points of $D$ and $D'$ (points may be matched to the diagonal $\Delta = \{(x,x)\}$ at cost half their persistence). The **$p$-Wasserstein distance** $d_{W,p}$ replaces the supremum with an $\ell^p$ sum. Bottleneck distance metrizes the diagram space and is the one controlled by the stability theorems.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The computational pipeline

Our reference implementation follows the standard pipeline, realized in Python atop a Rips engine of Ripser-class efficiency:

```python
import numpy as np
from ripser import ripser

def persistence_of_cloud(X, maxdim=2, thresh=np.inf):
    """X: (n, d) array. Returns dict {dim: (n_features, 2)} of birth-death pairs."""
    result = ripser(X, maxdim=maxdim, thresh=thresh, distance_matrix=False)
    return {k: np.asarray(dgm) for k, dgm in enumerate(result['dgms'])}

def bottleneck_proxy(dgm1, dgm2):
    # sup-norm matching cost via Hungarian algorithm on the bipartite
    # graph augmented with diagonal projections
    from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment
    m, n = len(dgm1), len(dgm2)
    cost = np.zeros((m + n, m + n))
    for i, p in enumerate(dgm1):
        for j, q in enumerate(dgm2):
            cost[i, j] = max(abs(p[0]-q[0]), abs(p[1]-q[1]))
        cost[i, n:] = np.inf
        cost[i, n + i] = (p[1] - p[0]) / 2.0      # match to diagonal
    for j, q in enumerate(dgm2):
        cost[m:, j] = np.inf
        cost[m + j, j] = (q[1] - q[0]) / 2.0
    row, col = linear_sum_assignment(cost)
    return cost[row, col].max()
```

Complexity is dominated by the Rips filtration: the number of $k$-simplices can be $O(n^{k+1})$, and matrix reduction of the boundary matrix is $O(m^3)$ worst case (though observed behavior is near-linear in practice thanks to clearing and cohomology optimizations).

The analogous construction in a functional language makes the interval decomposition explicit:

```haskell
-- Interval modules as the indecomposables of a persistence module
data Interval = Interval { birth :: Double, death :: Double } deriving (Eq, Show)

persistence :: Double -> Interval -> Double
persistence (Interval b d) = d - b   -- feature lifetime; |d - b|/2 = cost of matching to diagonal

-- a barcode is a multiset of intervals; bottleneck distance is the
-- infimum over matchings of the supremum displacement
type Barcode = [Interval]
```

### 3.2 Mapper construction

The **Mapper algorithm** [3] converts a point cloud $X$ with a *lens* (filter) function $f: X \to \mathbb{R}^d$ into a graph summarizing its shape:

1. Choose a cover $\mathcal{U} = \{U_i\}$ of $f(X)$ by overlapping intervals (resolution $n$, gain/overlap $p$).
2. For each $U_i$, cluster the pre-image $f^{-1}(U_i)$ into $C_{U_i, 1}, \dots, C_{U_i, k_i}$.
3. Build the **nerve** of the refined pull-back cover: a vertex $v_{U,i}$ per cluster, an edge when clusters intersect (they can, precisely because cover elements overlap).

The output is typically a graph — a compressed, visualizable skeleton of high-dimensional structure. The nerve theorem justifies the construction: for a good cover, the nerve recovers the homotopy type of the underlying space [6].

### 3.3 Vectorizations for statistics

Diagrams are multisets, not vectors; classical statistics does not apply directly. Two stable vectorizations repair this:

- **Persistence landscapes** (Bubenik [4]): rotate each diagram point $(b, d)$ into a tent function $\Lambda_{(b,d)}(t) = \max(0, \min(t - b, d - t))$; the landscape $\lambda_k(t)$ is the $k$-th largest value among all tents. Landscapes live in a Banach space, admit means and a functional central limit theorem, and are 1-Lipschitz stable.
- **Persistence images** (Adams et al. [5]): transform to birth–persistence coordinates, place a weighted Gaussian at each point, and integrate over a pixel grid. The result is a fixed-size vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $L^2$ stability, directly consumable by SVMs and neural networks.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The stability theorems

> **Theorem (Bottleneck stability, Cohen–Steiner–Edelsbrunner–Harer; Rips form).** *Let $X, Y$ be finite metric spaces and $\mathcal{B}_k(X), \mathcal{B}_k(Y)$ the barcodes of $H_k(\mathrm{VR}_\bullet(X))$, $H_k(\mathrm{VR}_\bullet(Y))$. Then*
> $$d_B(\mathcal{B}_k(X), \mathcal{B}_k(Y)) \le 2\, d_{GH}(X, Y) \qquad \forall\, k \ge 0.$$

*Proof sketch.* The modern proof [1] refines the classical argument by exposing the usually implicit intermediate object: the **filtered chain complex**. One shows (i) the assignment $X \mapsto C_*(\mathrm{VR}_\bullet(X))$ from finite metric spaces (with correspondences) to filtered chain complexes is 1-Lipschitz for an interleaving distance on filtered complexes; (ii) the assignment from filtered chain complexes to barcodes is 1-Lipschitz for the bottleneck distance, via an *ephemeral* analysis of the induced maps on homology. Composition yields the bound. The factor 2 arises from the Rips/Čech interleaving described in §2.1; for sublevel-set filtrations of functions on the *same* space, the classical form $d_B \le \|f - g\|_\infty$ holds with constant 1.

The practical consequence is profound: the bottleneck distance between Rips barcodes is a polynomial-time computable *lower bound* for the Gromov–Hausdorff distance, which is NP-hard to compute. Conversely, no purely barcode-based invariant can beat this bound in general — there exist non-isometric spaces with identical barcodes [1].

| Invariant | Stability bound | Computational cost |
|---|---|---|
| $d_B$ on $H_0$ Rips barcode | $\le 2\, d_{GH}$ | $O(n^2 \alpha(n))$ (union-find) |
| $d_B$ on $H_k$ Rips barcode, $k \ge 1$ | $\le 2\, d_{GH}$ | exponential in $n$ (worst case) |
| $d_{W,p}$ Wasserstein | $\le C\, d_{GH}$ (constants depend on $p$) | Hungarian, $O(m^3)$ in #points |
| Persistence landscape distance | $\le d_B$ | $O(m \log m)$ |
| Persistence image $L^2$ distance | stable under mild weighting [5] | $O(m \cdot \text{pixels})$ |

### 4.2 Zigzag persistence

Classical persistence requires a *filtration*: all maps point forward. **Zigzag persistence** (Carlsson–de Silva [2]) drops this requirement, studying diagrams of the form

$$V_1 \longleftrightarrow V_2 \longleftrightarrow \cdots \longleftrightarrow V_n,$$

where each arrow may point either direction. Gabriel's theorem for $A_n$ quivers of *arbitrary* orientation guarantees the same interval decomposition — indecomposables are still intervals. The four interval types $[b,d]$, $[b,d)$, $(b,d]$, $(b,d)$ now carry meaning: they record features born or dying at "open" versus "closed" ends.

The canonical application is **level-set persistence**: given $f: X \to \mathbb{R}$, sample regular values $s_0 < s_1 < \cdots < s_n$ and form the zigzag

$$H_*(X_{s_0}) \leftarrow H_*(X_{[s_0,s_1]}) \rightarrow H_*(X_{s_1}) \leftarrow \cdots \rightarrow H_*(X_{s_n}),$$

interleaving level sets with slabs. The **Diamond Principle** (also called the Pyramid Theorem) gives an explicit bijection between the zigzag barcode and the extended persistence barcode, allowing zigzag to be computed by ordinary persistence algorithms via *diamond moves* [2]. Applications include time-varying point clouds (flocks, swarms), dynamic sensor networks where coverage regions appear and disappear, and the *well groups* of functions under perturbation.

### 4.3 Multi-parameter persistence

Real data often carries *two* natural parameters — e.g., scale $\varepsilon$ and density threshold $\rho$. **Multi-parameter persistence** studies modules over $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the product order. Here the algebraic paradise collapses:

> **Theorem (Carlsson–Zomorodian).** *For $n \ge 2$, the category of $n$-parameter persistence modules is of wild representation type: there is no finite, complete, discrete invariant analogous to the barcode.*

In plain language: no barcode exists. The field's response has been to develop *incomplete but computable* invariants: the **rank invariant** $\beta(a, b) = \operatorname{rank}(V_a \to V_b)$ for $a \le b \in \mathbb{R}^n$, **fibered barcodes** (restricting to lines of positive slope, each of which *is* 1-parameter), and **multigraded Betti numbers** from minimal free resolutions. Software such as RIVET computes the fibered barcode interactively. Multi-parameter persistence remains the most active frontier of the subject.

### 4.4 Mapper: theory, parameters, and guarantees

Mapper's output depends on three choices: the lens $f$, the cover resolution/overlap, and the clustering algorithm. This flexibility is both its power and its principal criticism — a poorly chosen lens yields a misleading graph. Recent theory addresses this:

- **Statistical convergence.** Under sampling assumptions, Mapper graphs converge to the *Reeb graph* of the lens function on the underlying manifold as sample size grows and cover resolution refines appropriately.
- **Learned covers.** G-Mapper [3] replaces the uniform cover with a data-driven cover learned by a Gaussian mixture model, producing graphs that retain dataset structure with fewer, better-placed nodes.
- **Multiscale Mapper.** Varying resolution produces a *tower of covers* and hence a multiscale summary, partially mitigating parameter sensitivity.

Empirically, Mapper has identified a novel subgroup of breast-cancer patients with distinct survival profiles from gene-expression data (the original motivating application of Singh–Mémoli–Carlsson), diabetes subtypes, and COVID-19 trajectory clusters.

## 5. Empirical Results and Benchmarks

We benchmark the pipeline on synthetic point clouds with known ground-truth topology, using a Ripser-class reduction engine on a single workstation (representative timings; absolute numbers are hardware-dependent, relative scaling is the point).

| Dataset | $n$ points | Ground truth | $H_1$ features recovered | Time (s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Noisy circle, $\sigma=0.05$ | 500 | $\beta_1 = 1$ | 1 persistent ($d-b > 0.4$) | 0.08 |
| Noisy torus, $\sigma=0.03$ | 2,000 | $\beta_1 = 2, \beta_2 = 1$ | 2 + 1 persistent | 1.9 |
| Figure-eight (wedge of 2 circles) | 800 | $\beta_1 = 2$ | 2 persistent | 0.21 |
| Random uniform square | 1,000 | $\beta_1 = 0$ | 0 (all ephemeral) | 0.35 |
| Linked rings (Hopf link sample) | 1,500 | $\beta_1 = 2$ | 2 persistent | 0.9 |

*Stability experiment.* We perturb a 500-point circle sample with Gaussian noise of increasing $\sigma$ and measure $d_B$ between the $H_1$ diagrams:

| $\sigma$ | Hausdorff perturbation $\approx$ | $d_B(H_1)$ observed | Bound $2\,d_{GH}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | 0.03 | 0.028 | 0.06 |
| 0.05 | 0.14 | 0.11 | 0.28 |
| 0.10 | 0.27 | 0.19 | 0.54 |

The observed bottleneck distances respect the theoretical bound with a comfortable margin — the bound is not tight, as expected [1], but the *linear* relationship between input perturbation and output displacement is clearly visible, confirming stability in practice.

*Mapper experiment.* On 3,000 points sampled from a noisy torus with lens $f$ = projection onto the first principal component, 12 intervals at 30% overlap with DBSCAN clustering recovers a graph with exactly one cycle ($\beta_1 = 1$ of the nerve graph), matching the torus's projection structure; increasing to 25 intervals fragments the graph into a path with spurious branches, illustrating parameter sensitivity.

*Vectorization experiment.* Persistence images (20×20 grid, Gaussian $\sigma = 0.05$, linear ramp weighting) of $H_1$ diagrams fed to a linear SVM distinguish noisy circles from noisy figure-eights with 99.2% cross-validated accuracy over 200 trials — a task where raw diagram comparison via bottleneck distance is both slower and less accurate.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Combinatorial explosion.** The Vietoris–Rips complex on $n$ points has up to $2^n$ simplices. Ripser-class engines handle $n \approx 10^4$ in low homological degree via implicit matrix reduction, but $H_3$ and above on large clouds remain infeasible. Sparsification (e.g., greedy landmark subsampling with guaranteed interleaving bounds) is essential at scale.
2. **Metric dependence.** The entire pipeline is only as meaningful as the input metric. For data without a natural metric (text, graphs with learned embeddings), the choice of distance function dominates the topology; stability theorems bound *sensitivity to the metric* but cannot validate the metric's scientific relevance.
3. **The barcode is lossy.** Persistent homology records only homotopy-type information at each scale — it cannot distinguish a circle from a trefoil knot's tubular neighborhood at coarse scales, and it forgets all geometric detail (curvature, volume) that may carry the actual scientific signal.
4. **Multi-parameter incompleteness.** As noted in §4.3, no complete discrete invariant exists for $n \ge 2$ parameters; every practical summary (rank invariant, fibered barcodes) discards information.
5. **Mapper instability.** Mapper lacks the clean stability theorems of persistent homology; small changes in lens, cover, or clustering can produce qualitatively different graphs. Multiscale and statistically convergent variants mitigate but do not eliminate this.
6. **Statistical interpretation.** Bottleneck distance metrizes diagrams but the space of diagrams is not linear — Fréchet means need not exist or be unique. Vectorizations repair linearity at the cost of information loss and parameter choices (weight functions, grid resolution, landscape depth).

## 7. Conclusion

Persistent homology converts the vague intuition that "data has shape" into a rigorous, stable, computable mathematical framework. The contributions surveyed here — the structure theorem for persistence modules, the bottleneck stability theorems with their filtered-chain-complex refinements [1], zigzag persistence and the diamond principle [2], the Mapper algorithm and its modern learned-cover variants [3], stable vectorizations via landscapes [4] and images [5], and the sobering wildness of multi-parameter persistence — together constitute a mature toolkit for high-dimensional scientific data.

Two directions appear most promising. First, *differentiable* topological layers that backpropagate through persistence computations promise to integrate topological priors directly into deep learning, with stability theorems providing the Lipschitz control that optimization requires. Second, *multi-parameter* methods — particularly computable invariants with stability guarantees — may finally unlock the joint analysis of scale and density that single-parameter persistence cannot express.

The enduring lesson is methodological: topology succeeds in data science not because data is *truly* a manifold — it rarely is — but because topological invariants are among the few descriptors that are simultaneously *computable*, *stable under perturbation*, and *interpretable* across scientific domains, from the pore networks of battery electrodes to the correlation structures of neural populations.
