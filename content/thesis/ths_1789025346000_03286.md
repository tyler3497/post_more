# SMT Solving over String Theory: Automata-Based Decision Procedures in Z3str4 and cvc5 for Program Analysis of Sanitizers and Symbolic Execution

## Abstract

Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) over strings underpins symbolic execution engines, sanitizer verification for web security, and automated reasoning about access-control policies. Yet the theory of strings is treacherous: word equations combined with length constraints and regular-language membership are undecidable in general, forcing solver designers to choose between incomplete heuristics and decision procedures for delimited fragments. This paper studies automata-based decision procedures in two state-of-the-art solver lineages — Z3str4 (and its Z3str3 predecessor) and cvc5 — together with the stabilization-based Z3-Noodler and the backward-propagation architecture of OSTRICH. We formalize the SMT-LIB theory of Unicode strings, characterize the decidability landscape of word equations via Levi's lemma and Nielsen transformations, and dissect three algorithmic families: length abstraction with bitvector reduction, automata-theoretic regular constraint solving, and multi-armed portfolio selection. We evaluate these designs on published suites totaling over 177,000 instances, show how string solvers verify sanitizers and discharge symbolic-execution path constraints, and close with the limitations bounding current technology.

## 1 Introduction

Strings are the dominant data type of the modern software stack. JavaScript, PHP, Python, and Java programs manipulate strings pervasively — parsing input, building SQL queries, rendering HTML, and enforcing access policies — and string-handling code is disproportionately responsible for security vulnerabilities, from cross-site scripting (XSS) to SQL injection [4]. Program analyses must therefore *reason* about strings: is there an input string driving execution down a given path? Does a sanitizer's output provably avoid every string in an attack language?

Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) solvers answer such questions by deciding the satisfiability of first-order formulas over background theories. Since 2020, the SMT-LIB standard has formalized a Unicode string theory [7], and a flourishing ecosystem of solvers — Z3str3, Z3str4, Z3seq, cvc5 (né CVC4), OSTRICH, Trau, Z3str3RE, and Z3-Noodler — now competes annually in the QF_Strings divisions of SMT-COMP [2][5]. Industrial adoption has followed, notably at AWS for RBAC policy analysis [4].

The algorithmic challenge is formidable. The quantifier-free theory combining *word equations* (equalities between concatenations of variables and constants), *length constraints*, and *regular membership* is undecidable in its full generality, so every practical solver is an engineered compromise: complete for useful fragments, heuristic elsewhere, and always racing the clock. This paper studies how the leading solvers navigate that compromise, with emphasis on **automata-based decision procedures** — techniques that compile regular constraints into finite automata, propagate them through string operations, and reduce the combinatorial explosion of string reasoning to well-understood automata operations.

Our contributions are:

1. A precise formulation of the SMT-LIB string theory and its decidability landscape, including the classical word-equation results and the straight-line and chain-free decidable fragments.
2. A detailed dissection of the architectures of **Z3str3/Z3str4** (arrangements, theory-aware branching, length abstraction, multi-armed selection) [1][2] and **cvc5** (layered word-equation reasoning with lazy reductions) [5].
3. An in-depth treatment of automata-based cores: length-aware regular solving [6], the stabilization algorithm of Z3-Noodler [3], and backward propagation in OSTRICH/OSTRICH2 [4].
4. An empirical synthesis of published evaluations spanning 177,000+ benchmark instances, and two applied case studies: sanitizer verification and symbolic execution.
5. A candid account of the limitations — undecidable combinations, complement-heavy regexes, and integer-string conversions — that bound current technology.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The SMT-LIB Theory of Unicode Strings

The SMT-LIB theory of strings [7] introduces a sort `String` denoting finite sequences of Unicode code points, together with a rich signature. Core functions include `str.++`, `str.len`, `str.substr`, `str.at`, `str.replace`/`str.replace_all`, `str.indexof`, `str.prefixof`/`str.suffixof`/`str.contains`, and `str.to_code`/`str.to_int` conversions. Regular constraints use a second sort `RegLan` with constructors `str.to_re`, `re.++`, `re.union`, `re.inter`, `re.*`, `re.+`, `re.opt`, `re.loop`, `re.range`, `re.comp`, `re.diff`, `re.allchar`, and the membership predicate `str.in_re`. The principal quantifier-free logics are **QF_S** and **QF_SLIA** (strings combined with linear integer arithmetic).

A representative instance, adapted from the OSTRICH2 presentation [4], illustrates the flavor:

```smt2
(set-logic QF_SLIA)
(declare-fun x () String)
(declare-fun y () String)
(declare-fun z () String)
(declare-fun k () Int)
(assert (= (str.len x) (+ k 1)))
(assert (= x (str.++ y z)))
(assert (str.in_re x (re.+ (re.union (str.to_re "a") (str.to_re "b")))))
(check-sat)
(get-model)
```

Here the solver must find `x = y·z` of length `k+1` in `(a|b)⁺` — exercising all three reasoning dimensions (length arithmetic, concatenation equations, regular membership) whose interaction is the crux of string solving.

### 2.2 Word Equations and the Decidability Landscape

A *word equation* is an equality `U = V` where `U, V ∈ (Σ ∪ X)*` are words over an alphabet `Σ` and variables `X`. Satisfiability of word equations alone is decidable — Makanin's landmark 1977 result — and in fact in PSPACE (Plandowski, 1999). The workhorse of word-equation reasoning is **Levi's lemma**:

> **Theorem (Levi's lemma):** Let `u, v, x, y ∈ Σ*` with `uv = xy`. Then there exists `w ∈ Σ*` such that either `u = xw ∧ wy = v`, or `x = uw ∧ wv = y`.

In words: when two concatenations are equal, the shorter of the two leading components is a prefix of the longer. **Nielsen transformations** operationalize this: from an equation such as `X·a = a·X`, Levi's lemma forces a case split on the relative alignment of `X` and `a`, progressively *peeling* constants and shrinking variables until a solution is exposed or a contradiction derived. The diagram for `X·a = a·X` (Figure 3) shows the two alignments and why every solution commutes: `X ∈ a*`.

Decidability evaporates quickly as operators are added. The combination of word equations with length constraints and `str.replace` is undecidable in general; even regular membership with *stacked complements* pushes the theory beyond PSPACE [6]. Solver designers therefore target decidable fragments:

- **Straight-line fragment:** variables are defined by a sequence of assignments `x := f(y₁, …, yₙ)` using concatenation, replacement, and transducers, each variable assigned once. Decidable via *backward propagation* of regular constraints [4].
- **Chain-free fragment:** the variable-dependency graph of equations contains no "chains" that force unbounded synchronization; decidable and covering many practical instances.
- **Acyclic / regex-only fragments:** regular membership plus linear length arithmetic without word equations is PSPACE-complete and handled by pure automata methods [6].

### 2.3 The DPLL(T) Architecture for Strings

Modern string solvers inherit the **DPLL(T)** architecture (Figure 1): a SAT solver reasons over the Boolean abstraction of the formula, proposing assignments to theory literals; a *theory solver* for strings checks each conjunction for consistency and, on conflict, returns learned clauses (*theory lemmas*) that prune the Boolean search. Theory solvers may also *propagate* implied literals eagerly. Z3-Noodler illustrates the pattern: it replaces Z3's string theory solver while reusing Z3's parser, rewriter, arithmetic solver, and relevancy propagation to shrink each query before the string core sees it [3]. The art of string solving lies almost entirely inside that theory-solver box — how it abstracts, refines, and learns.

## 3 Methodology: Three Algorithmic Families

We organize the design space into three families. Real solvers blend them, but the taxonomy clarifies the trade-offs.

### 3.1 Word-Based Reduction with Length Abstraction

*Length abstraction* projects each string variable `x` to the integer `len(x)`, translating string constraints into (weaker) integer constraints. If the integer abstraction is unsatisfiable, so is the original; if satisfiable, the solver *refines* — e.g., by splitting on arrangements of equations or by fixing lengths and bit-blasting. Z3str3's pipeline is canonical: reduce the input to an *arrangement* (a disjunction of conjunctions of derived string equations), ask the arithmetic solver for a consistent length assignment, reduce the resulting fixed-length equations to a bitvector formula, and discharge it with Z3's bitvector solver. On failure, it learns a clause forbidding the current length assignment and continues in a conflict-driven loop [1][2].

### 3.2 Automata-Based Regular Constraint Solving

Automata-based methods compile each `str.in_re` constraint to a nondeterministic finite automaton (NFA) via Thompson's construction, then use product constructions for intersection and concatenation, and emptiness checks for unsatisfiability (Figure 2). Length information is extracted as *length abstractions* of automata (the set of accepted word lengths, a semilinear set), which prune the arithmetic search before expensive products are built. Berzish et al. [6] exploit upper and lower length bounds from real-world instances to simplify automata aggressively, adding prefix/suffix heuristics that short-circuit common cases. For string *functions* (concatenation, `replace`, transducers), OSTRICH-style solvers propagate regular constraints *backward*: from `y = f(x)` and `y ∈ L`, compute the pre-image `f⁻¹(L)` as an automaton constraining `x` [4].

### 3.3 Portfolio and Multi-Armed Selection

No single algorithm dominates. Z3str4 therefore adopts a *multi-armed* architecture: several sub-solvers (arms) — sequences of algorithms — are available, and a selection heuristic chooses one per instance using static *probes* (syntactic features of the formula) plus dynamic difficulty estimation gathered as solving proceeds [2]. Learned clauses flow between algorithms within an arm, so work done by one procedure benefits the next.

## 4 Deep Dive: Automata-Based Decision Procedures in Practice

### 4.1 Z3str3: Arrangements, Theory-Aware Branching, and Bitvector Reduction

Z3str3 [1] supports string equations, linear arithmetic over `str.len`, and regular membership. Its headline innovation is **theory-aware branching**: in classical DPLL(T), the Boolean abstraction hides the structure of theory literals from the SAT solver's branching heuristic. Z3str3 instead exposes that structure, biasing variable activities so that *simpler* theory literals are decided before complex ones, and adding a **theory-aware case split** that compactly represents mutually exclusive Boolean variables.

Empirically, Z3str3 outperformed Z3str2, Norn, CVC4, S3, and S3P on majorities of three industrial benchmarks — Kaluza, PISA, and IBM AppScan [1]. Architecturally, Z3str3 reduces input constraints to arrangements of derived equations, obtains length assignments from Z3's arithmetic solver, bit-blasts fixed-length equations, and learns conflict clauses over length assignments — the word-based reduction pattern of §3.1 executed inside Z3's DPLL(T) core [2].

### 4.2 Z3str4: Arm Selection, LAS, and the Arrangement Solver

Z3str4 [2] generalizes Z3str3 into a multi-armed solver with three sub-solvers: **Z3str3** itself, the **Length Abstraction Solver (LAS)**, and **Z3seq** (the Microsoft Research string solver). Two predefined arms orchestrate them: a *conjunctive* arm suited to conjunctions of constraints, and a *non-conjunctive* arm that leans on bitvector reductions for disjunctive instances. Arm selection uses conjunctive probes and dynamic difficulty estimation; within an arm, algorithms run in sequence with learned clauses shared forward.

Z3str4 contributes two novel algorithms: the **length abstraction algorithm**, which iteratively abstracts string constraints to length constraints and refines them, and the **arrangement solver**, which pairs arrangement enumeration with a string-to-bitvector backend — a hybrid of word-based and unfolding-based model building. Evaluated on 20 industrial and randomly generated benchmark suites comprising over 120,000 instances, Z3str4 outperformed the leading string solvers of its generation [2].

The regex-focused sibling work of Berzish et al. [6] deserves special attention for its automata core. Its **length-aware algorithm** for the theory of regex membership plus linear length arithmetic (TLRE) first computes length abstractions of every regex and every variable, checks the integer abstraction for inconsistency, intersects the automata of all regexes constraining each variable, and only then commits to expensive operations — with prefix/suffix heuristics discharging easy cases early. On 57,256 regex-heavy instances (≈75% industrial), it achieved speedups of 2.4× over CVC4, 4.4× over Z3seq, 6.4× over Z3-Trau, 9.1× over Z3str3, and 13× over OSTRICH [6] — a striking demonstration that automata plus length reasoning dominate the regex-heavy regime.

### 4.3 cvc5: Layered Word-Equation Reasoning with Lazy Reductions

cvc5 [5], the successor of CVC4, implements strings as a solver of **multiple layered components**. At its core it reasons about length constraints and word equations [5, §Strings and Sequences]; extended functions such as `str.replace` are *lazily reduced* to word equations only after aggressive **context-dependent simplification** rewrites them away when possible. Reduction lemmas use improved encodings with witness sharing, and **model-based reductions** avoid expensive reasoning when the current candidate model already satisfies the constraints. A dedicated decision procedure handles string↔code-point conversions natively, which substantially improves performance on Unicode-heavy instances [5].

Unlike the automata-first designs, cvc5's string solver is fundamentally *word-based*: it splits equations à la Nielsen/Levi, propagates length constraints, and reduces regular membership through rewriting and lazily applied automata checks. The cooperation with cvc5's equality engine and its proof-producing infrastructure makes it the most industrially hardened of the word-based solvers, and its QF_Strings results at SMT-COMP remain at the top of the field [5].

### 4.4 Z3-Noodler: Stabilization Without Splitting

Z3-Noodler [3] takes the opposite philosophical stance from cvc5: it is *purely automata-based*. It forks Z3, replacing the string theory solver with **Noodler**, an implementation of the **stabilization-based** algorithm for word equations with regular constraints. The key idea: rather than splitting equations eagerly (which explodes the search), stabilization maintains for each variable a *language* (initially the intersection of its regular constraints) and iteratively refines these languages by propagating constraints through the equations until a fixpoint — a *stable* assignment of languages — is reached. If the languages stabilize non-empty, a model is extracted; if some language empties, the instance is unsatisfiable.

Because stabilization never splits equations, it sidesteps the case explosion that haunts word-based solvers on equation-heavy instances. The evaluation shows Z3-Noodler competitive with — and on regex/word-equation benchmarks often far surpassing — state-of-the-art solvers, and highly complementary in portfolios [3]. Its architecture (Figure 1, right) reuses Z3's parser, rewriter, and LIA solver, applying relevancy propagation to shrink each theory query before stabilization begins.

### 4.5 OSTRICH: Backward Propagation Through String Functions

OSTRICH [4] targets the **straight-line fragment**, where string variables are defined by single assignments through functions like concatenation, `replace`, `replace_all`, and finite transducers. Its decision procedure propagates regular constraints *backward* through these functions: given `y := f(x)` and `y ∈ L(A)`, it computes an automaton for the pre-image `f⁻¹(L(A))` and constrains `x` accordingly, iterating until all variables carry only regular constraints, which are then checked for joint emptiness. Pre-images of regular languages under concatenation, `replace`, and transducers remain regular, which is what makes the fragment decidable.

**OSTRICH2** [4] extends this foundation dramatically: it adds a theory of **string sequences** (SeqStr) with operations `nth`, `update`, `join`, and `filter`, decided via *cost-enriched finite automata* (CEFA) that track integer costs alongside accepted words. Strikingly, OSTRICH2 solves constraints combining `str.replace_re_all`, `str.substr`, and `str.len` in under a second where Z3 returns `unknown` and cvc5 fails within 20 hours [4] — evidence that backward propagation covers ground its rivals cannot.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation

We synthesize three published evaluations covering more than 177,000 instances.

**Regex-heavy solving (CAV 2021, 57,256 instances).** The length-aware automata solver of Berzish et al. [6] dominates the regex-plus-length regime. Relative speedups over five competitors on the full suite were:

| Solver | Relative slowdown vs. length-aware solver |
|---|---|
| CVC4 | 2.4× |
| Z3seq | 4.4× |
| Z3-Trau | 6.4× |
| Z3str3 | 9.1× |
| OSTRICH | 13.0× |

Roughly 75% of these instances derive from industrial applications, confirming that automata with length abstraction — not word splitting — is the right default for regular constraints [6].

**Virtual best-solver analysis (Z3-Noodler report).** The table below reports *unsolved* instances per benchmark family for each solver in the VBS portfolio study [3]; lower is better:

| Solver | Regex | Equations | Predicates-small | PyEx |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cvc5 | 1 | 145 | 17 | 359 |
| Z3 | 1 | 29 | 2 | 27 |
| Z3str4 | 1 | 19 | 2 | 28 |
| OSTRICH | 1 | 21 | 2 | 24 |
| Z3str3RE | 1 | 20 | 2 | 24 |

Two patterns stand out. First, regex-only instances are essentially solved by everyone — automata techniques have converged. Second, cvc5's word-based core struggles disproportionately on the *Equations* and *PyEx* families (145 and 359 unsolved), precisely where Z3-Noodler's stabilization and OSTRICH's backward propagation excel, vindicating the portfolio thesis [3].

**SMT-COMP scale (TACAS 2022 report).** On 69,863 QF_Strings instances, cvc5 solved 69,231, CVC4 69,367, and Z3 68,074 — a tight cluster at the top, with cvc5's layered architecture and proof support making it the industrial default [5]. Z3str4's own evaluation over 120,000+ instances across 20 suites showed it outperforming the leading solvers of its generation [2].

**Applied case study I: sanitizer verification.** Consider an HTML sanitizer modeled as a composition of replacements, `out = sanitize(in)`, and an attack language `A` (e.g., strings containing `<script`). Verification reduces to checking unsatisfiability of:

```smt2
(assert (= out (str.replace_all in "<" "&lt;")))
(assert (str.in_re out attackRegex))
(check-sat)  ; unsat => sanitizer is sound for this attack language
```

Automata-based solvers shine here: the sanitizer is straight-line, so OSTRICH-style backward propagation computes the pre-image of the attack language exactly [4]; regex-heavy attack patterns are handled by the length-aware automata core [6]. This is the same technology class behind the AWS RBAC policy analysis [4].

**Applied case study II: symbolic execution.** Symbolic executors accumulate *path constraints* — conjunctions of string equalities, length bounds, and regular memberships — one per explored path. Each `check-sat` query asks whether the path is feasible and yields a witnessing input. The queries are overwhelmingly conjunctive with heavy length/regular content: exactly the Z3str4 *conjunctive arm* and the cvc5/Z3str3RE sweet spot [2][6]:

```python
from z3 import *
s = String('s')
solve(Length(s) == 8,
      InRe(s, Re("+", Union(Re("a"), Re("b")))),  # (a|b)+
      PrefixOf(StringVal("ab"), s))               # path condition fragment
```

Thousands of such queries per analysis run make per-query latency — not just decidability — the binding constraint, which is why arm selection, relevancy propagation, and prefix/suffix heuristics matter as much as the core calculi [2][3][6].

## 6 Limitations

Despite this progress, fundamental and practical limits remain.

1. **Undecidable combinations.** The full SMT-LIB string theory — word equations with length, `replace_all`, and integer conversions — is undecidable. Every solver is therefore incomplete *somewhere*: cvc5 may loop on adversarial equation/length combinations, and automata solvers must bail out or over-approximate outside their decidable fragments [4][5].
2. **Complement-heavy regular expressions.** Automata complementation is exponential, and *stacked complements* push even the regex-plus-length fragment beyond PSPACE [6].
3. **Integer–string conversions.** `str.to_int`/`str.from_int` bridge the string and arithmetic theories in ways that defeat pure length abstraction; solvers handle them by case analysis that scales poorly with digit length [6].
4. **Transducers and `replace_all` in the wild.** Backward propagation is complete only for the straight-line fragment; nested or iterated replacements fall outside it, and OSTRICH2's sequence theory, while broader, still excludes arbitrary recursion [4].
5. **Model construction cost.** Stabilization and arrangement enumeration can decide satisfiability long before they produce a *model*; extracting short, human-useful witnesses (e.g., minimal attack strings) remains heuristically driven [1][3].
6. **Unicode reality gap.** Case folding, normalization, and grapheme clusters in real sanitizers have no faithful SMT-LIB encoding; verified sanitizers are verified against *models* of Unicode, not Unicode itself [5][7].

> **Theorem (informal frontier):** There is no decision procedure for the quantifier-free theory combining word equations, the length function, and `str.replace_all`. Every terminating solver for the full SMT-LIB string theory is necessarily incomplete — it must answer `unknown`, diverge, or restrict the fragment.

## 7 Conclusion

SMT solving over strings has matured from ad-hoc heuristics into a disciplined engineering science with three pillars: **length abstraction with bitvector reduction** (Z3str3/Z3str4), **layered word-equation reasoning with lazy reductions** (cvc5), and **automata-based decision procedures** — length-aware regular solving, stabilization (Z3-Noodler), and backward propagation (OSTRICH/OSTRICH2). The empirical record is unambiguous about complementarity: automata dominate regex-heavy and straight-line instances by orders of magnitude, word-based methods remain competitive on mixed industrial suites, and portfolio architectures like Z3str4's multi-armed selection harvest the best of both [2][3][4][6].

For program analysis, the payoff is concrete: sanitizers can be verified sound against regular attack languages, and symbolic executors can discharge tens of thousands of string path constraints per run — though undecidable operator combinations, complement-heavy regexes, and faithful Unicode modeling keep string solving an active research area. The trajectory, however, is clear: each generation converts more of the heuristic dark matter into decision procedures, and the automata-theoretic core, from Levi's lemma to cost-enriched automata, is the engine of that conversion.
