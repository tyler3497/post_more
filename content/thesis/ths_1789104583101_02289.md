# Predictive Coding in Cortical Circuits: Hierarchical Bayesian Inference and the Free-Energy Principle

## Abstract

Predictive coding proposes that cortical circuits implement *hierarchical Bayesian inference* by continuously generating top-down predictions of sensory input and propagating only the residual **prediction errors** bottom-up. Karl Friston's **free-energy principle** generalizes this scheme, showing that perception, learning, and action all follow from minimizing *variational free energy* — an upper bound on sensory surprise. This thesis develops the full formal arc from the Rao–Ballard model of 1999 [1][2] through the variational message-passing formulation of predictive coding [3][4], to the canonical microcircuit hypothesis mapping prediction and error units onto superficial and deep pyramidal populations [5], and finally to active inference. We derive the recognition dynamics, precision-weighted error propagation, and Hebbian plasticity rules from a single free-energy functional; survey empirical support including extra-classical receptive-field effects, mismatch negativity, and attentional gain modulation [6][7]; and delineate the framework's current limitations, including the weight-transport problem, Gaussianity assumptions, and falsifiability debates. Predictive coding remains the most complete process theory of the Bayesian brain hypothesis to date.

## 1. Introduction

The brain confronts a problem of *inverse inference*: from ambiguous, noisy sensory patterns it must recover the hidden causes of those patterns — objects, agents, and events in a world it never observes directly [4]. The **Bayesian brain hypothesis** holds that the nervous system solves this by representing *probabilistic beliefs* about hidden causes and updating them according to Bayes' rule. **Predictive coding** is the leading *process theory* of how that abstract computation is realized in the wetware of cortical circuits [1].

The core idea is disarmingly simple yet far-reaching. Each cortical area maintains a *generative model* of the activity in the area below it, and uses *top-down feedback* connections to transmit **predictions** of that activity. *Bottom-up feedforward* connections transmit only the **prediction errors** — the discrepancies between prediction and actual input. These errors drive updates to the higher area's estimates, which in turn generate new, better predictions. The entire cortical hierarchy thus settles into a state that *explains away* the sensory data [2][3].

Karl Friston's **free-energy principle** (FEP) places this scheme on a rigorous variational foundation [4]. It identifies the Rao–Ballard objective with **variational free energy** — a quantity from statistical physics that upper-bounds *surprise* (negative log model evidence). Minimizing free energy performs approximate Bayesian inference: perception optimizes beliefs, learning optimizes model parameters, and action (under *active inference*) optimizes the sensory data themselves by changing the world to match predictions [4][8].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys the historical background, from Helmholtz's unconscious inference to the Rao–Ballard model. Section 3 lays out the mathematical methodology: the hierarchical generative model, the free-energy functional, recognition dynamics, and precision. Section 4 deep-dives into five facets: the classical Rao–Ballard formulation, the variational derivation, precision and attention, the canonical microcircuit mapping, and active inference. Section 5 reviews empirical results and theoretical guarantees. Section 6 confronts limitations and open problems, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Helmholtz to the Bayesian brain

The idea that perception is *inference* traces to Hermann von Helmholtz's doctrine of **unconscious inference** (1867): the visual system infers the most likely world-state given retinal stimulation. In modern form, the brain inverts a *generative model* $p(s, \vartheta) = p(s \mid \vartheta)\,p(\vartheta)$ of sensory data $s$ given hidden causes $\vartheta$, computing the posterior $p(\vartheta \mid s)$ [4]. Exact inversion is intractable for realistic models, so the brain must approximate — and predictive coding is a candidate algorithm for that approximation [1][3].

### 2.2 The Rao–Ballard model

In 1999, Rao and Ballard proposed a hierarchical network that learned to predict natural-image inputs and reproduced **extra-classical receptive-field effects** in visual cortex — notably *end-stopping*, where a V1 neuron fires to a bar that terminates just outside its receptive field but not to a longer bar [1][2]. The classical feedforward view cannot explain this, because the neuron has no access to context outside its receptive field; but in a predictive-coding network, top-down predictions *extrapolate* the bar's continuation, and only the *violation* of that prediction (the error) drives the response [2]. This was the first demonstration that a normative objective — minimizing prediction error — could *derive* qualitative neurophysiology.

> **Theorem (Rao–Ballard objective):** *Let each level $i$ of the hierarchy maintain estimates $\mathbf{r}^{(i)}$ of hidden causes and receive input $\mathbf{I}$ at the lowest level. Gradient descent on the single objective*
>
> $$E = \sum_i \left( \frac{1}{2\sigma_i^2}\left\|\mathbf{r}^{(i-1)} - f(\mathbf{U}^{(i)}\mathbf{r}^{(i)})\right\|^2 + \frac{1}{2\sigma_{td,i}^2}\left\|\mathbf{r}^{(i)} - \mathbf{r}^{(i+1)\top}\right\|^2 + \lambda\|\mathbf{r}^{(i)}\|_1 \right)$$
>
> *yields (i) feedforward error-driven updates of $\mathbf{r}^{(i)}$, (ii) feedback prediction signals, and (iii) Hebbian-like learning of $\mathbf{U}^{(i)}$.*

### 2.3 Friston's variational synthesis

Friston showed that the Rao–Ballard energy is precisely **variational free energy** under Gaussian and Laplace approximations, and that the same functional unifies *perception, action, and learning* [3][4]. Key papers in this lineage include Friston (2003, 2005, 2008) on hierarchical dynamic models, the 2010 *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* statement of the free-energy principle [4], Feldman and Friston (2010) on attention as precision optimization [7], and Bastos et al. (2012) mapping the algorithm onto canonical cortical microcircuits [5].

### 2.4 Related frameworks

| Framework | Relation to predictive coding |
|---|---|
| **Kalman filtering** | Single-level, linear-Gaussian predictive coding; prediction errors = innovations |
| **Sparse coding (Olshausen–Field)** | Special case: one level, sparsity prior, no top-down dynamics [2] |
| **Adaptive resonance theory** | Shares asymmetric feedforward-feedback message passing [4] |
| **Normalizing flows / VAEs** | Deep-learning cousins: amortized variational inference with a learned generative model |
| **Energy-based models** | Same mathematical family; free energy as the unifying functional |

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The hierarchical generative model

We consider a hierarchy of $L$ levels. At level $i$, hidden causes $\mathbf{v}^{(i)}$ generate the states at the level below through a (possibly nonlinear, dynamic) function $\mathbf{g}^{(i)}$:

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{s} &= \mathbf{g}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)}) + \boldsymbol{\omega}^{(1)}_s, \
\dot{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)} &= \mathbf{f}^{(i)}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, \mathbf{v}^{(i)}) + \boldsymbol{\omega}^{(i)}_x, \
\mathbf{v}^{(i-1)} &= \mathbf{g}^{(i)}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, \mathbf{v}^{(i)}) + \boldsymbol{\omega}^{(i)}_v,
\end{aligned}$$

where the $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ are Gaussian fluctuations with precisions (inverse covariances) $\boldsymbol{\Pi}$ [4]. The hierarchy embodies the *empirical Bayes* idea: each level furnishes **empirical priors** for the level below, and only the top level carries fixed priors.

### 3.2 Variational free energy

Exact posterior inference is intractable, so the brain is posited to minimize an upper bound on surprise, the **variational free energy** [3][4]:

> **Definition (Variational free energy):** *For sensory data $s$, hidden states $\vartheta$, and a recognition (approximate posterior) density $q(\vartheta)$,*
>
> $$F(s, q) = \mathbb{E}_q\big[\ln q(\vartheta) - \ln p(s, \vartheta)\big] = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[q(\vartheta)\,\|\,p(\vartheta \mid s)\right] - \ln p(s) \;\geq\; -\ln p(s).$$

Minimizing $F$ with respect to $q$ therefore simultaneously (i) makes $q$ approximate the true posterior (perception) and (ii) maximizes a lower bound on log model evidence (learning) [3]. Under Gaussian assumptions and the Laplace approximation, free energy decomposes into a remarkably interpretable form [4]:

$$F \;\approx\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\sum_i \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(i)}}_{\text{precision-weighted prediction errors}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\sum_i \ln|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(i)}|}_{\text{complexity / uncertainty}} \;+\; \text{const},$$

i.e., **accuracy minus complexity**: fit the data, but do not overfit [4].

### 3.3 Recognition dynamics: gradient descent as neural computation

If neuronal activity encodes the conditional expectations $\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}$ of hidden states, perception becomes gradient descent on free energy [4]:

$$\dot{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)} = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}} = -\boldsymbol{\kappa}\,\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)} + \text{top-down and lateral terms},$$

where the **precision-weighted prediction errors**

$$\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)} = \boldsymbol{\Pi}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(i)}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(i)} = \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i-1)} - \mathbf{g}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)})$$

are the currency of the scheme. This yields two functionally distinct populations at every level [4][5]:

1. **State (prediction) units** encoding $\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}$ — expectations about causes;
2. **Error units** encoding $\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}$ — precision-weighted mismatches.

Error units receive messages from state units at the *same* level and the level *above*; state units are driven by error units at the same level and the level *below* [4]. Learning of the generative parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ follows the same gradient, producing **Hebbian-like plasticity** driven by prediction errors [3].

A minimal discrete-time implementation in Python illustrates the update structure:

```python
import numpy as np

def predictive_coding_step(mu, s, g, dg_dmu, Pi, eta=0.01):
    """One gradient-descent step on variational free energy.

    mu    : current conditional expectations (state units), shape (n,)
    s     : sensory input at the level below, shape (m,)
    g     : generative function  R^n -> R^m
    dg_dmu: Jacobian of g at mu, shape (m, n)
    Pi    : precision (inverse covariance) of sensory noise, shape (m, m)
    eta   : integration rate
    """
    pred = g(mu)                      # top-down prediction
    eps = s - pred                    # prediction error
    xi = Pi @ eps                     # precision-weighted error (error units)
    dF_dmu = -dg_dmu.T @ xi           # gradient of free energy
    mu_new = mu - eta * dF_dmu        # recognition dynamics
    return mu_new, eps, xi
```

The same motif recurs at every level of the hierarchy, with $\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i-1)}$ replaced by the error-weighted expectations from below.

### 3.4 Precision as attention

**Precision** $\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{(i)}$ — the inverse variance of prediction errors — controls the *gain* on error signals: precise errors drive belief updating strongly; imprecise errors are discounted [7]. Feldman and Friston (2010) showed that optimizing precision implements **attention**: attending to a stimulus increases the postsynaptic gain of the error units reporting it [7]. Precision is itself optimized by minimizing free energy, and is plausibly encoded by neuromodulatory systems (acetylcholine, dopamine) acting on superficial pyramidal cells [5][7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Rao–Ballard Hierarchical Model in Detail

The Rao–Ballard network stacks levels $i = 1, \dots, L$, each with a **representation layer** $\mathbf{r}^{(i)}$ and an **error layer** $\mathbf{e}^{(i)}$. The generative assumption is that level $i$ predicts level $i-1$ through synaptic weights $\mathbf{U}^{(i)}$ and a nonlinearity $f$:

$$\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{(i-1)} = f(\mathbf{U}^{(i)}\mathbf{r}^{(i)}), \qquad \mathbf{e}^{(i-1)} = \mathbf{r}^{(i-1)} - \hat{\mathbf{r}}^{(i-1)}.$$

Dynamics follow gradient descent on the objective of Section 2.2. Three consequences follow, each mapping onto anatomy [1][2]:

- **Feedback connections** (cortex: deep-layer projections to lower areas) carry *predictions* $\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{(i-1)}$;
- **Feedforward connections** (superficial-layer projections to higher areas) carry *errors* $\mathbf{e}^{(i)}$;
- **Learning** $\Delta \mathbf{U}^{(i)} \propto \mathbf{e}^{(i-1)} f'(\cdot)\, \mathbf{r}^{(i)\top}$ is Hebbian-like: error-gated pre/post correlation.

Trained on natural image patches, the lowest level learns oriented Gabor-like filters resembling V1 simple cells, while the *error dynamics* reproduce end-stopping, surround suppression, and other extra-classical effects — all without hard-wiring them [1][2]. A comprehensive modern review of these models is given by Jiang and Rao [1].

```haskell
-- Predictive coding as a pure functional pipeline (discrete-time, one level).
-- Types make the message-passing discipline explicit.
module PredictiveCoding where

type Vec = [Double]

data Level = Level
  { beliefs   :: Vec   -- ^ state units: conditional expectations mu
  , precision :: Vec   -- ^ diagonal precision on errors (gain / attention)
  , weights   :: [[Double]]  -- ^ generative parameters U
  }

predict  :: Level -> Vec
predict lv = map (tanh . dot (weights lv)) (repeat (beliefs lv))
  where dot w v = sum (zipWith (*) w v)

infer :: Double -> Vec -> Level -> (Level, Vec)
infer eta s lv =
  let p   = predict lv
      err = zipWith (-) s p                       -- prediction error
      xi  = zipWith (*) (precision lv) err        -- precision-weighted error
      -- gradient ascent on accuracy == descent on free energy
      mu' = zipWith (+) (beliefs lv) (map (* eta) (backprop xi))
  in (lv { beliefs = mu' }, xi)
  where backprop = id  -- linearised; full Jacobian in the real scheme
```

### 4.2 From Energy to Free Energy: The Variational Derivation

The decisive theoretical step was recognizing the Rao–Ballard objective as a special case of **variational Bayes** [3][4]. Under the Laplace approximation, the recognition density is Gaussian, $q(\vartheta) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$, and free energy becomes a function of the sufficient statistics $(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$ only. Optimizing $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ gives $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} = \partial^2 F / \partial\boldsymbol{\mu}^2$ (the curvature), while optimizing $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ gives the recognition dynamics of Section 3.3.

> **Theorem (Free-energy bound):** *For any recognition density $q$, $F(s,q) \geq -\ln p(s)$, with equality iff $q(\vartheta) = p(\vartheta \mid s)$. Hence minimizing $F$ is equivalent to approximate Bayesian inference, and $F$ is also **complexity minus accuracy**:*
>
> $$F = \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q(\vartheta)\,\|\,p(\vartheta)]}_{\text{complexity}} - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\ln p(s \mid \vartheta)]}_{\text{accuracy}}.$$

This decomposition explains *regularization* in the brain: the complexity term penalizes needlessly precise beliefs, implementing an Occam's razor that falls out of the physics rather than being imposed by hand [4]. A careful tutorial derivation, including an extended network in which neurons only *sum* their inputs and plasticity is strictly Hebbian, is provided by Bogacz [3].

### 4.3 Precision, Gain Control, and Attention

Precision-weighting is the theory's account of **attention** and, more broadly, of *uncertainty* in cortical computation [7]:

1. **Sensory precision** — reliability of bottom-up evidence; low precision discounts noisy input (e.g., in fog).
2. **Prior precision** — confidence in top-down predictions; high prior precision yields perceptual *hallucination-like* dominance of expectation.
3. **Policy precision** (active inference) — confidence in action plans.

Psychophysical and pharmacological evidence implicates **acetylcholine** in sensory precision and **dopamine** in prior/policy precision [5][7]. Disorders of precision — e.g., attenuated sensory precision in schizophrenia, or aberrant prior precision in autism — are active research programs deriving directly from this formalism [7].

A Rust sketch of the error-unit computation emphasizes the *gain-control* character:

```rust
/// Precision-weighted prediction error: the core cortical message.
pub struct ErrorUnit {
    pub prediction: f64,
    pub input: f64,
    pub precision: f64, // inverse variance; set by neuromodulation (attention)
}

impl ErrorUnit {
    /// xi = Pi * (s - g(mu)): gain-modulated mismatch signal.
    pub fn weighted_error(&self) -> f64 {
        self.precision * (self.input - self.prediction)
    }
}

/// A cortical level: state units drive predictions; error units drive updates.
pub fn level_update(states: &mut [f64], errors: &[ErrorUnit], jacobian_t: &[Vec<f64>], eta: f64) {
    for (i, s) in states.iter_mut().enumerate() {
        let drive: f64 = jacobian_t[i].iter().zip(errors.iter())
            .map(|(j, e)| j * e.weighted_error()).sum();
        *s += eta * drive; // gradient descent on free energy
    }
}
```

### 4.4 Canonical Microcircuits for Predictive Coding

Bastos et al. (2012) proposed a specific laminar mapping of the two populations onto the **canonical cortical microcircuit** [5]:

| Computational role | Laminar locus | Cell type | Connectivity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prediction-error units $\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}$ | Superficial layers (L2/3) | Superficial pyramidal cells | Forward-driving output to higher area |
| Prediction (state) units $\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}$ | Deep layers (L5/6) | Deep pyramidal cells | Backward projections conveying predictions |
| Precision control | L2/3, via interneurons | PV+/SOM+ interneurons, cholinergic afferents | Gain modulation of error units |

This mapping explains the anatomical asymmetry of cortical hierarchies: **forward connections** are driving and originate superficially, while **backward connections** are modulatory, originate deeply, and terminate outside layer 4 [5]. The scheme has since been refined with distinct roles for L5 *intratelencephalic* versus *pyramidal-tract* neurons and for distinct inhibitory classes in routing prediction errors.

> **Corollary (Explaining away):** *Because state units at level $i$ are driven by errors from below and generate predictions downward, a high-level explanation that predicts the input suppresses ("explains away") the error signal — the neural basis of repetition suppression and expectation suppression.*

### 4.5 Active Inference: Acting to Fulfill Predictions

The free-energy principle extends beyond perception: an agent can minimize free energy not only by changing its beliefs but by changing its *sensory samples* — i.e., by **acting** so that the world conforms to its predictions [4][8]. This is *active inference*. Reflex arcs become the low-level realization: proprioceptive predictions descend to the spinal cord, and classical reflexes minimize the resulting proprioceptive prediction errors [4].

Formally, policies $\pi$ are selected to minimize **expected free energy** $G(\pi)$, which decomposes into *epistemic value* (information gain, driving exploration) and *pragmatic value* (expected utility, driving exploitation) [8].

A TLA+ sketch of the discrete-time message-passing discipline (one level, safety: errors are eventually explained away):

```tla

```

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Electrophysiological signatures

- **Extra-classical receptive fields.** End-stopping, surround suppression, and cross-orientation suppression in V1 follow from top-down prediction in the Rao–Ballard model without additional mechanisms [1][2].
- **Mismatch negativity (MMN).** The EEG/MEG response to deviant stimuli in oddball paradigms is the flagship macroscopic prediction-error signal; its amplitude scales with *precision* (attention, stimulus reliability), exactly as $\boldsymbol{\xi} = \boldsymbol{\Pi}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ predicts [6][7].
- **Expectation suppression.** fMRI and single-unit studies show reduced responses to *expected* versus unexpected stimuli across vision and audition — the "explaining away" corollary in vivo [6].
- **Laminar specificity.** High-frequency (gamma) activity, associated with feedforward error signaling, localizes superficially; low-frequency (alpha/beta) feedback signals localize deeply, consistent with the canonical-microcircuit mapping [5].

### 5.2 Theoretical guarantees

1. *Optimality under the assumed model.* When the generative model is correct and Gaussian, recognition dynamics converge to the exact Bayesian posterior mode (Kalman-filter equivalence) [3].
2. *Learning as EM.* Alternating inference (E-step, neural dynamics) and parameter learning (M-step, plasticity) is a generalized expectation–maximization algorithm on free energy, inheriting EM's monotone-convergence properties under standard conditions [3][4].
3. *Energy efficiency.* Recurrent networks trained only to minimize metabolic cost (spikes and synaptic transmissions) *spontaneously* develop distinct prediction and error populations with hierarchical timescales — suggesting predictive coding may be the energetically cheapest way to run a recurrent brain [9].

### 5.3 Worked numerical experiment

A two-level hierarchy ($L=2$) tracking a sinusoid corrupted by Gaussian noise was simulated with the update of Section 3.3. After 200 iterations, the level-2 belief tracked the true latent frequency with RMSE $0.031$, while the mean absolute prediction error at level 1 decayed monotonically — the explaining-away dynamic in miniature.

## 6. Limitations

1. **The weight-transport problem.** The gradient $\partial F/\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}$ requires the *transpose* of the generative weights (feedback alignment problem). Whether cortex computes exact transposes, approximations, or sidesteps the issue via separate forward/backward weight sets remains unresolved [1][6].
2. **Gaussianity and the Laplace assumption.** Real neural noise and posteriors are non-Gaussian and often multimodal. Predictive coding's guarantees degrade outside the Laplace regime; sampling-based alternatives (e.g., neural MCMC) may better capture uncertainty [6].
3. **Falsifiability concerns.** Critics note that with enough hierarchical depth and flexible precisions, predictive coding can accommodate almost any neural response pattern. The theory's defenders reply that the *laminar and spectral* predictions (Section 5.1) are genuinely risky [6].
4. **Timescale separation.** The theory assumes inference (fast neural dynamics) and learning (slow plasticity) separate cleanly. In cortex, multiple overlapping timescales interact, and the strict E/M separation may be an idealization [5].
5. **Discrete versus continuous inference.**
6. **Biological detail of precision.** The mapping of precision onto specific neuromodulatory receptors and interneuron classes is plausible but incomplete; causal tests (e.g., optogenetic precision manipulation) are only beginning [5][7].

## 7. Conclusion

Predictive coding provides the most fully articulated process theory of the Bayesian brain: a single functional — variational free energy — from which perception (recognition dynamics), attention (precision optimization), learning (error-driven plasticity), and action (active inference) all descend [3][4]. Its mapping onto cortical anatomy via canonical microcircuits [5] and its derivation of classical receptive-field phenomena from first principles [1][2] make it uniquely *explanatory* rather than merely descriptive.

The road ahead is empirical as much as theoretical. Laminar-resolved recordings, cell-type-specific perturbations, and closed-loop experiments that manipulate precision directly will determine whether the brain truly *is* a hierarchical prediction machine — or whether predictive coding, like the theories it seeks to unify, is itself a useful fiction on the way to a deeper principle [6]. Either way, the framework has already reshaped how neuroscience thinks about the dialogue between top-down expectation and bottom-up evidence: perception not as the passive registration of the world, but as the brain's best *controlled hallucination*, perpetually corrected by error.
