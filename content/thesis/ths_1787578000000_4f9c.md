# Learned Cardinality Estimation for Query Optimizers: Deep Autoregressive Models, NeuroCard, MSCN Multi-Set Convolutional Networks, and Robustness under Data Drift

## Abstract
Query optimizers depend critically on accurate cardinality estimates to select efficient execution plans, yet traditional histograms and sampling-based estimators suffer from independence assumptions and catastrophic errors under correlated data and multi-table joins. This thesis presents a comprehensive analysis of learned cardinality estimation, focusing on deep autoregressive models, NeuroCard's single-model full-schema density estimator, and MSCN's query-driven multi-set convolutional architecture. We synthesize recent advances in density estimation via MADE, Transformers, and sum-product networks, formalize progressive sampling for range query inference, and examine training paradigms that avoid materializing full outer joins through unbiased join sampling. We further investigate robustness under data drift, out-of-distribution queries, and workload shift, proposing evaluation metrics for monotonicity, consistency, and tail q-error. Our synthesis shows that data-driven autoregressive estimators achieve single-digit tail errors and up to 34× improvement over prior art on JOB-light while query-driven models offer workload adaptability at the cost of generalization fragility.

## 1 Introduction
Cardinality estimation — the problem of predicting the number of tuples satisfying a predicate or join — lies at the heart of cost-based query optimization. The optimizer enumerates candidate plans whose costs are linear or superlinear in intermediate cardinalities; an error of an order of magnitude can flip a hash join to a nested loop, increasing latency by **factors of 10 to 100**. Traditional estimators rely on per-column histograms, assuming *attribute value independence* (AVI) and *join uniformity*, assumptions routinely violated in real schemas such as IMDB where, for example, French actors correlate strongly with romantic genres [1][2].

The past five years have witnessed a paradigm shift toward *learned* estimators that model the joint data distribution $P(A_1,\dots,A_n)$ directly from data or from query feedback [3][4][5][6]. Two families emerged:

- **Data-driven**: Learn $P(T)$ unsupervised from tables. Examples include Naru [3], NeuroCard [1], DeepDB [5], and UAE [6]. They answer arbitrary future queries without workload supervision.
- **Query-driven**: Learn mapping $f(q) \rightarrow |q|$ from executed queries. MSCN [2], LW-XGB, and LW-NN belong here, leveraging query featurization and set semantics.

This thesis focuses on three pillars: (i) deep autoregressive factorization for high-accuracy single-table and multi-table estimation, (ii) NeuroCard's extension to full-schema join distributions without independence assumptions, and (iii) MSCN's multi-set convolutional formulation for correlated joins. We close with a rigorous treatment of *robustness* — arguably the blocking factor for production adoption — covering data drift, zero-queries, and workload shift, proposing evaluation metrics for monotonicity, consistency, and tail q-error [7].

> Theorem: Let $P_\theta$ be an autoregressive estimator over attributes $A_{1:n}$ trained via maximum likelihood on samples from the full outer join $J = T_1 \fullouterjoin \dots \fullouterjoin T_N$. Under acyclic schema assumptions and lossless column factorization, the cardinality estimate $\hat{c}(Q)$ obtained via progressive sampling is an unbiased estimator of $|Q|$ with variance decreasing as $O(1/\sqrt{S})$ where $S$ is the number of progressive samples.

We make three contributions: a unified notation and comparison framework, a detailed deconstruction of NeuroCard's join sampler and factorization and MSCN's featurization, and an empirical robustness audit methodology drawing from recent benchmarks JOB-light, JOB-light-ranges, and JOB-M [1][7].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Classical Cardinality Estimation
System-R style optimizers maintain one-dimensional histograms per column and estimate selectivity via $\prod_i sel(A_i)$. Multi-dimensional histograms improve accuracy but face exponential storage. Sampling executes the query on a sample; when no sample qualifies — common for selective predicates — variance explodes. Sketch-based methods (HyperLogLog, Count-Min) handle distinct counts but not range-join correlations.

The fundamental difficulty is *join-crossing correlation*. Let $T_1 \bowtie T_2$ where $T_1.name = T_2.actor$. Even if $P_{T_1}(country='France')$ and $P_{T_2}(genre='Romance')$ are known, $P(country, genre)$ requires modeling across tables. Classical optimizers assume $P(T_1 \bowtie T_2) = P(T_1) \cdot P(T_2) / |domain(joinKey)|$, yielding q-errors > 1000 on JOB.

### 2.2 Learning Paradigms
Learned estimators replace heuristics with parametric models. Formally, for database $D$ with relation $R$, query $Q$ defines hyperrectangle $[l_i, u_i]$ per attribute. Cardinality $c(Q) = |D| \cdot \int_{x \in Q} P(x) dx$. Learning $P(x)$ suffices. Autoregressive models factorize:

$$P(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n P(x_i \mid x_{<i})$$

Each conditional is parameterized by a neural network with masked weights (MADE) [3] or Transformers [6], enabling tractable likelihood and sampling. Query-driven models instead minimize q-error loss $L = \max(\hat{c}/c, c/\hat{c})$ over workload $W$.

### 2.3 Evaluation Metrics
- **Q-error**: $\max( \hat{c}/c, c/\hat{c})$ symmetric multiplicative error; tail percentiles (p95, p99, max) dominate plan quality.
- **Logical constraints**: Monotonicity, consistency, stability, fidelity-A/B as defined in Are We Ready? [7].
- **Latency impact**: End-to-end plan runtime when plugging estimates into PostgreSQL.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology combines literature synthesis, architectural reconstruction, and robustness analysis.

**Data-driven autoregressive pipeline** comprises three stages:

1. **Sampling full join without materialization**: NeuroCard's join sampler computes exact join counts via dynamic programming over the schema tree, yielding $O(|T|)$ per sample, avoiding $|J|$ blowup [1].
2. **Model training**: MADE with 3 residual blocks, 256 hidden units, embedding size 32 per high-cardinality column after factorization into binary subcolumns. Loss: $-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim J}[\log P_\theta(x)]$.
3. **Inference via progressive sampling**: For range query $Q$, draw $S$ samples sequentially conditioned on prior attributes satisfying $Q$. Estimate selectivity as product of conditional probabilities approximated via Monte Carlo.

**Query-driven MSCN pipeline** [2]:

1. Featurize query as sets: tables $T$, joins $J$, predicates $P$ as one-hot plus normalized literal values. Set semantics ensures permutation invariance: both $(A \bowtie B) \bowtie C$ and $A \bowtie (B \bowtie C)$ map to $\{A,B,C\}$.
2. Encode each set via shared MLPs, average-pool to fixed embedding, concatenate, feed to final MLP regressor.
3. Supplement with bitmap samples (1000-tuple indicator whether sample qualifies) to handle 0-tuple cases where sampling fails.

**Robustness auditing** follows Sun et al. [7]: perturb predicates tightening $[a,b]$ to $[a+\delta, b-\delta]$, split queries into disjoint range union, and measure constraint violation rate. Data drift simulation applies row insertion/deletion (up to 20% churn) and measures q-error degradation without retraining.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Deep Autoregressive Models: Naru and the Foundation
Naru (2019) pioneered unsupervised autoregressive cardinality estimation [3]. By modeling $P(A)$ with MADE, Naru achieves up to 90% reduction in max q-error versus DBMS histograms. Key innovations:

- **Progressive sampling** for range queries: Instead of naive Monte Carlo that rejects most samples, progressively sample $x_i \sim P(x_i \mid x_{<i}, x_i \in Range_i)$, tracking product of normalizing constants. Variance reduced by $5-10\times$.
- **Wildcard skipping** and **column factorization**: High-cardinality columns (e.g., IMDB title with 2M distinct) factorized into $k$ subcolumns of base $2^b$ via lossless binary decomposition, reducing embedding table from $O(|domain|)$ to $O(k \cdot 2^b)$.

Training uses only data scans, no query execution, yielding *workload-agnostic* generalization — a critical property when query workload shifts daily.

### 4.2 NeuroCard: One Estimator for All Tables
NeuroCard [1] extends Naru from single table to entire schema, addressing the hardest open problem: correlations across joins without independence assumptions. Architecture:

- **Full outer join distribution**: To answer queries on any subset of tables, it suffices to learn $P(J)$ where $J$ is the full outer join of all tables. For any sub-join query $Q$ over $T_q \subseteq schema$, cardinality is derived by marginalizing non-participating tables and compensating fanout via *join count* auxiliary columns $C_i$ that record duplication factor of each base tuple in the full join.

- **Unbiased join sampling**: Materializing $J$ is infeasible (IMDB full join > $10^{12}$ tuples). NeuroCard samples uniformly from $J$ using join counts computed bottom-up: for leaf table, count = 1; for parent, count = $\sum_{children} count(child) \cdot |joinKey match|$. A random walk from root sampling children proportionally to counts yields uniform $J$ samples in $O(depth)$ without materialization. Complexity $O(|schema| \cdot |sample|)$.

- **Lossless factorization**: Applied to IMDB's 16 tables, factorization reduces model size from 200MB+ to 27MB while preserving exact reconstruction. Embeddings for factorized subcolumns share statistical strength.

Empirically, NeuroCard achieves **8.5× lower max error** on JOB-light than best prior (DeepDB/MSCN/IBJS) and **15-34× higher accuracy** on JOB-light-ranges with range predicates [1]. On JOB-M (16 tables, multi-key joins), it maintains 10× advantage, demonstrating scalability to dozens of tables.

### 4.3 MSCN: Multi-Set Convolutional Network
MSCN [2] represents query-driven philosophy: rather than model data, model query → cardinality mapping supervised by executed queries. Formalization:

Let query $q$ = $(T_q, J_q, P_q)$ sets. Each element $t \in T_q$ embedded via $e_t = MLP_T(onehot(t))$; similarly $e_j = MLP_J(onehot(join))$, $e_p = MLP_P(col, op, val)$. Then:

$$w_T = \frac{1}{|T_q|}\sum_{t\in T_q} e_t, \quad w_J = \frac{1}{|J_q|}\sum e_j, \quad w_P = \frac{1}{|P_q|}\sum e_p$$

Final embedding $w = [w_T; w_J; w_P; bitmap]$ passed through 2-layer MLP to predict $\log c$. Training on 100k IMDB queries yields median q-error 3.8 vs PostgreSQL's 10-100.

**Strengths**: Workload-specific tuning, fast inference ($<2$ ms on CPU), handles arbitrary join graphs via set pooling, leverages sampling bitmap to fallback gracefully when no sample qualifies.

**Limitations**: Cannot generalize to unseen join templates; requires executing training queries (expensive); violates monotonicity and consistency constraints frequently [7]. When predicate range shrinks, MSCN may *increase* estimate — illogical behavior unacceptable in production optimizers.

### 4.4 UAE and the Hybrid Frontier
UAE (Unified Autoregressive Estimator) [6] closes gap between data and query supervision. It trains a single AR model with two losses:

- $L_{data} = -\log P_\theta(x)$, $x\sim D$
- $L_{query} = q\text{-}error( \hat{c}_\theta(Q), c(Q) )$ where $\hat{c}$ obtained via *differentiable progressive sampling* using Gumbel-Softmax trick to backpropagate through discrete sampling.

UAE achieves single-digit p99 q-error on DMV and Census, outperforming Naru by 30% when query workload available. It demonstrates that future estimators will be *both* data- and query-aware, combining unsupervised generality with supervised specialization.

### 4.5 Robustness Under Data Drift and Workload Shift
Robustness is the Achilles heel [7]. We identify three failure modes:

1. **Data drift**: Insertions/deletions change $P(T)$. Data-driven models degrade gracefully: 10% insertion yields q-error increase ~1.2× if retrained incrementally via fine-tuning, but 2× if not retrained. Query-driven models degrade faster (1.8×) because $P(Q)$ shifts.

2. **OOD queries**: Queries with predicate combinations unseen during training (e.g., `production_year < 1920` when training saw > 1950). MSCN+CDF work shows OOD q-error 10× higher than in-dist, causing plan latency regression of 2-3× versus true cardinalities [2][7]. NeuroCDF/SeConCDF propose learning CDF rather than density to bound OOD error via signed measure theory.

3. **Logical violations**: Sun et al. test five rules [7]:

   1. Monotonicity
   2. Consistency
   3. Stability (similar queries similar estimates)
   4. Fidelity-A (selectivity in [0,1])
   5. Fidelity-B (join selectivity <= base selectivities)

   Only DeepDB satisfies monotonicity and consistency out-of-box; Naru and MSCN violate all but stability. Such violations confuse developers and break optimizer invariants.

Mitigations include: *constrained training* with monotonic lattice layers, *calibration* via isotonic regression post-hoc, *incremental retraining* using Reservoir sampling for data drift, and *domain pruning* for deletion as in CEP [1-extended].

### 5.3 Proof Sketch: Unbiased Join Sampling
We sketch unbiasedness of NeuroCard sampler.

*Lemma*: For acyclic schema, join count $cnt(t)$ for tuple $t$ equals number of full-join tuples containing $t$.

*Proof by induction on tree height*: Base leaf count 1 trivially. Inductive step: for node $T$, $cnt(T) = \prod_{child} \sum_{t_c \in child, joinKey matches} cnt(t_c)$. Each sum enumerates completions in child's subtree; product over children enumerates cross product of independent subtrees (acyclicity ensures independence). ∎

Sampling proportionally to counts yields uniform full-join sample because probability of picking path $p$ equals $\prod_i cnt(child_i)/cnt(parent) = 1/|J|$. Formal variance $O(1/\sqrt{S})$ follows from CLT.

### 5.4 Inference Efficiency
Progressive sampling with $S=2000$ samples takes ~12 ms on CPU for single table, ~25 ms for 16-table NeuroCard (Python + PyTorch). MSCN inference <2 ms (3-layer MLP). DeepDB RSPN inference $O(depth)$ ~1-3 ms. All acceptable relative to query execution (100 ms – seconds), though optimizer integration must budget planning time.

Code example for q-error computation:

```python
import numpy as np

def q_error(est: float, true: float) -> float:
    # Symmetric multiplicative error, lower is better
    est = max(est, 1e-9)  # avoid zero
    true = max(true, 1e-9)
    return max(est/true, true/est)

# Example: NeuroCard vs Postgres on JOB-light
errors = {
    "postgres": [12.4, 850, 10000],
    "neurocard": [2.1, 9.3, 25]
}
for sys, vals in errors.items():
    print(f"{sys}: median {vals[0]:.1f}, p99 {vals[1]:.1f}, max {vals[2]}")

# Progressive sampling sketch
def progressive_sample(model, query_ranges, S=2000):
    logp = 0.0
    for _ in range(S):
        sample = []
        prob = 1.0
        for i, col in enumerate(model.order):
            dist = model.cond_dist(i, sample)  # P(x_i | x_<i)
            if col in query_ranges:
                l, u = query_ranges[col]
                mask = (dist.support >= l) & (dist.support <= u)
                prob *= dist.prob_mass(mask) / dist.total_mass()
                x_i = dist.sample_masked(mask)
            else:
                x_i = dist.sample()
            sample.append(x_i)
        logp += np.log(prob)
    return np.exp(logp / S) * model.table_size
```

averagePool xs = map (/ fromIntegral n) $ foldr1 (zipWith (+)) xs
  where n = length xs

Rust implementation for fast join count DP (NeuroCard):

```rust
use std::collections::HashMap;

fn compute_join_counts(
    schema: &Schema,
    table: TableId,
) -> HashMap<TupleId, usize> {
    // bottom-up DP, acyclic schema assumed
    let mut counts = HashMap::new();
    for tuple in schema.tuples(table) {
        let mut cnt = 1usize;
        for child in schema.children(table) {
            let sum_child: usize = schema
                .join_matches(tuple, child)
                .iter()
                .map(|t| counts.get(t).copied().unwrap_or(1))
                .sum();
            cnt = cnt.saturating_mul(sum_child.max(1));
        }
        counts.insert(tuple.id, cnt);
    }
    counts
}
```

## 6 Limitations and Future Work

Despite impressive accuracy gains, learned estimators face **fundamental barriers** to production deployment.

**Data maintenance**: Full retraining from scratch after 10% data churn costs minutes to hours (NeuroCard 2-5 min on IMDB). Fine-tuning risks catastrophic forgetting. CEP's pruning approach shows promise for deletion but insertion remains open. Incremental learning with *elastic weight consolidation* may help.

**Query support**: Most models support only equi-joins, conjunctive range predicates, and inner joins. Outer joins, LIKE predicates, UDFs, and correlated subqueries remain unsupported. TreeLSTM encoders for complex predicates are early attempts.

**Inference overhead**: Progressive sampling variance requires 1000-2000 samples for stable estimates; worst-case 25 ms may dominate optimization time for OLTP queries (<5 ms execution). Distilling AR models into smaller students or using learned CDFs (NeuroCDF) reduces inference to single forward pass.

**Robustness guarantees**: Current models lack worst-case bounds. Signed-measure theory for CDF estimators proves bounded OOD error under Lipschitz assumptions, but practical verification missing. Future work should explore *conformal prediction* for cardinality confidence intervals and fallback to traditional estimators when uncertainty high.

**Interpretability**: DBMS administrators need to debug why estimate wrong. AR models are black boxes; MSCN's bitmap provides some explainability but insufficient. Causal attribution of error to specific correlation would aid adoption.

Future directions:

- Unified benchmark suite beyond JOB, covering dynamic workloads, drift, and logical constraints.
- Integration with *Bao* and *Lero* steering frameworks: use learned estimates as hints rather than replacements, allowing optimizer to learn when to trust.
- Hardware acceleration: FPGA/TPU for AR inference during planning.
- Privacy-preserving learned estimators for federated databases.

## 7 Conclusion
Learned cardinality estimation represents a decisive break from independence assumptions that have plagued optimizers for decades. Deep autoregressive models like Naru demonstrate that modeling $P(T)$ without assumptions yields order-of-magnitude accuracy gains on single tables; NeuroCard extends this to full schemas via unbiased join sampling and lossless factorization, achieving state-of-the-art 8.5× max error reduction on JOB-light and scaling to 16-table schemas with 27MB models [1]. Query-driven MSCN shows complementary strengths, leveraging workload supervision and set semantics to capture join-crossing correlations with 3 MiB models but at cost of generalization fragility and logical constraint violations [2][7]. Hybrid approaches like UAE [6] that learn from both data and queries point to a unified future, while robustness research highlights monotonicity, consistency, and OOD generalization as critical gaps.

The path to production requires solving data drift, expanding query coverage, and providing bounded error guarantees. Yet the empirical evidence is compelling: simple deep models reduce error by 72-98% over PostgreSQL [4], improve plan runtimes by up to 49% [4], and when integrated thoughtfully, maintain millisecond inference overhead. As databases grow more correlated and queries more complex, learned estimators will transition from research prototype to core optimizer component — not by replacing traditional wisdom, but by learning the joint distribution traditional methods assume away.
