# Maximal Extractable Value and Proposer-Builder Separation in Ethereum: Auction Theory, Order-Flow Auctions, and Enshrined PBS

## Abstract

Maximal extractable value (MEV) is the profit a privileged actor can extract by controlling the inclusion, exclusion, or ordering of transactions within a block. In Ethereum's proof-of-stake era, proposer-builder separation (PBS) institutionalized this extraction as a market: specialized builders compete in first-price sealed-bid auctions to sell the right to fill a block, first through trusted off-chain relays (MEV-Boost) and prospectively through the consensus layer itself (enshrined PBS, EIP-7732). This thesis develops an auction-theoretic account of the full MEV supply chain. We model builder competition as a first-price auction with affiliated values, derive equilibrium bid shading and winner's-curse dynamics, analyze order-flow auctions that return a share of extracted value to transaction originators, and formalize the commit-reveal exchange of enshrined PBS, including its free-option pathology and the Payload Timeliness Committee that polices builder revelation. Empirically, over 90% of blocks now flow through MEV-Boost while three builders produced nearly 80% of blocks between late 2023 and early 2024 [1][2], implying that the auction market, while allocatively efficient, concentrates structural power and demands protocol-level remedies.

---

## 1 Introduction

Every Ethereum block is a small economy. Within a twelve-second slot, the actor who decides the *order* of pending transactions decides who captures their latent arbitrage, liquidation, or sandwich value. Originally *miner* extractable value, now generalized to *maximal* extractable value, this is an unavoidable tax on users of any expressive blockchain: wherever a privileged party orders state transitions, the ordering right has a price.

Proposer-builder separation (PBS), first articulated by Vitalik Buterin, is Ethereum's architectural answer: rather than demanding every validator be a sophisticated MEV extractor — which would centralize validation — it splits the roles. *Builders* compete to construct the most valuable block; *proposers* (validators) sell the right to fill their slot to the highest bidder. The mechanism connecting them is an auction, and auctions have equilibria, and equilibria have winners and losers. Understanding PBS is therefore, at its core, an exercise in auction theory.

This thesis treats the MEV supply chain as nested auctions and asks: how should the builder auction be modeled, and what does equilibrium predict about bidding, revenue, and concentration? Can *order-flow auctions* (OFAs) return MEV to the users who create it? What changes when the auction is *enshrined* via EIP-7732 [3][4], and what pathologies does enshrinement introduce? And what does the empirical record say about who actually wins?

Our contributions: (i) a formal affiliated-values model of the builder auction with equilibrium bid shading; (ii) analysis of OFAs as a second auction layer that redistributes but can entrench value; (iii) a precise account of the ePBS commit-reveal protocol, its PTC, and the *free option problem* [1]; (iv) a Monte Carlo simulation reproducing observed concentration dynamics [2].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 A taxonomy of maximal extractable value

MEV arises wherever transaction ordering is discretionary. The canonical strategies include:

- **Frontrunning.** Observing a pending victim transaction in the public mempool and inserting one's own transaction ahead of it — for example, buying a token before a large swap moves its price.
- **Backrunning.** Placing a transaction immediately *after* a victim transaction to capture the arbitrage its price impact creates. Backrunning is generally considered the "benign" form of MEV, since it corrects prices rather than degrading the victim's execution.
- **Sandwich attacks.** The composition of the two: frontrun and backrun around a victim swap, extracting the slippage tolerance as profit.
- **DEX–DEX and DEX–CEX arbitrage.** Exploiting price discrepancies across venues before arbitrageurs in the public mempool can react.
- **Liquidations.** Competing to be the first to liquidate an undercollateralized lending position and claim the liquidation bonus.
- **Time-bandit attacks.** Reorganizing recently finalized history to retroactively capture MEV — a consensus-layer attack that PBS-era designs seek to make uneconomical.

Most user order flow now bypasses the public mempool via private RPC channels, making order flow itself a tradeable asset — a dynamic central to §4.2.

### 2.2 The PBS pipeline

In the production PBS design, relay-based and operated through MEV-Boost [5], five roles interact:

1. **Users** originate transactions, increasingly via private order flow.
2. **Searchers** detect MEV opportunities and package them into *bundles* — atomic sequences of transactions.
3. **Builders** assemble full blocks from bundles, mempool transactions, and private order flow, and submit block *headers* together with *bids* to relays.
4. **Relays** are trusted intermediaries that run the auction: they collect builder bids and forward only the highest-bidding header to the proposer, withholding the block body until the proposer commits.
5. **Proposers** (validators) request the best header via `getHeader`, sign it blindly, and submit the signed header; the relay then reveals the full block.

The relay's commit-reveal discipline is load-bearing — the proposer commits blindly so neither side can defect — but the relay is itself a trusted third party that can censor bids or extract rents, which is precisely what enshrined PBS aims to remove.

### 2.3 The centralization puzzle

Yet the builder market has concentrated sharply: between late 2023 and early 2024, just three builders produced nearly 80% of all Ethereum blocks [2], even as over 90% of blocks flow through MEV-Boost auctions [1]. The auction is efficient; the market structure around it is not obviously competitive. Explaining that gap — and whether enshrinement closes or widens it — is the central task of what follows.

## 3 Methodology

We model each slot as a single-object auction using auction theory extended to affiliated values: (i) a formal game-theoretic model of the builder auction; (ii) a Monte Carlo simulation of equilibrium bidding; (iii) calibration against published MEV-Boost measurements [1][2][5].

### 3.1 The builder auction as a game

With $n$ builders, builder $i$ observes a private signal $s_i$ of available MEV — a function of order-flow access, searcher relationships, and latency. These signals are *affiliated* (Milgrom–Weber): a high signal for one builder makes high signals for others more likely, since all draw on overlapping mempools and correlated private flow. Each builder submits a sealed bid $b_i \geq 0$; the highest bidder wins, pays its bid to the proposer (a first-price rule), and keeps the residual MEV.

Let builder $i$'s true block value be $v_i$, with $v_i = \mathbb{E}[V \mid s_i]$ under affiliation. The symmetric Bayes–Nash equilibrium bidding strategy $\beta(\cdot)$ satisfies the standard first-order condition, and in the benchmark case of independent private values drawn from distribution $F$,

$$ \beta(v) = v - \frac{\int_{0}^{v} F(x)^{\,n-1}\,dx}{F(v)^{\,n-1}}, $$

the familiar bid-shading formula: each builder shades its bid below its true value by exactly the expected information rent it must retain to make truthful bidding incentive-compatible. With affiliation, the *linkage principle* applies: public revelation of affiliated information (e.g., relays streaming real-time bids) raises proposer revenue — good for validators — but disproportionately rewards the best-informed builders, i.e., those with exclusive order flow.

### 3.2 Simulation design

Builder valuations combine a common MEV component with an idiosyncratic skill/flow component, parameterized by correlation $\rho$. Builders play the equilibrium shading strategy; we measure proposer revenue, builder surplus, win concentration (Herfindahl–Hirschman index), and the effect of asymmetric order-flow endowments.

```python
import numpy as np

def affiliated_values(n_builders, n_slots, rho=0.7, seed=42):
    """Draw affiliated builder valuations for each slot.

    v_i = rho * common + (1 - rho) * idiosyncratic_i
    rho -> 1 means builders see nearly the same MEV opportunity set.
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    common = rng.lognormal(mean=0.5, sigma=0.6, size=(n_slots, 1))
    idiosyncratic = rng.lognormal(mean=0.5, sigma=0.6,
                                  size=(n_slots, n_builders))
    return rho * common + (1 - rho) * idiosyncratic

def equilibrium_bids(values, n_builders):
    """Symmetric equilibrium shading for lognormal-ish values.

    Uses the order-statistic approximation b(v) = E[second-highest | win],
    i.e. each builder bids its expected payment conditional on winning,
    which implements the first-price equilibrium shading factor.
    """
    n_slots = values.shape[0]
    bids = np.empty_like(values)
    for t in range(n_slots):
        v = values[t]
        # shading factor from the n-1 opponents' expected max
        shade = (n_builders - 1) / n_builders
        # affiliated correction: shade less when values are correlated
        # (linkage principle intensifies competition)
        corr = np.corrcoef(values[max(0, t-50):t+1].T).mean() \
            if t > 5 else 0.7
        bids[t] = v * shade * (0.85 + 0.15 * corr)
    return bids

def run_auction(n_builders=6, n_slots=20000, rho=0.7,
                exclusive_flow_share=0.0):
    """Simulate the per-slot builder auction; returns market statistics."""
    values = affiliated_values(n_builders, n_slots, rho)
    if exclusive_flow_share > 0:
        # builder 0 holds exclusive private order flow: a value premium
        values[:, 0] *= (1.0 + exclusive_flow_share)
    bids = equilibrium_bids(values, n_builders)
    winners = bids.argmax(axis=1)
    winning_bids = bids[np.arange(n_slots), winners]
    proposer_revenue = winning_bids.mean()
    builder_surplus = (values[np.arange(n_slots), winners]
                       - winning_bids).mean()
    shares = np.bincount(winners, minlength=n_builders) / n_slots
    hhi = (shares ** 2).sum()  # Herfindahl-Hirschman index
    return {
        "proposer_revenue": proposer_revenue,
        "builder_surplus": builder_surplus,
        "top3_share": np.sort(shares)[-3:].sum(),
        "hhi": hhi,
        "mean_winning_bid": winning_bids.mean(),
    }

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for rho in (0.3, 0.7, 0.95):
        s = run_auction(rho=rho)
        print(f"rho={rho}: proposer_rev={s['proposer_revenue']:.3f} "
              f"builder_surplus={s['builder_surplus']:.3f} "
              f"top3={s['top3_share']:.2%} hhi={s['hhi']:.3f}")
    s = run_auction(rho=0.7, exclusive_flow_share=0.35)
    print(f"exclusive flow: top3={s['top3_share']:.2%} hhi={s['hhi']:.3f}")
```

The simulation is stylized — abstracting timing games, multi-block MEV, and relay fees — but isolates the mechanism-design question: how do correlation and asymmetric information map to concentration?

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The builder auction as a first-price auction with affiliated values

Three theoretical results organize the analysis of builder competition.

*First*, the first-price sealed-bid rule is the format MEV-Boost relays implement, and it governs surplus division between proposers and builders. Under independent private values, the *revenue equivalence theorem* equates proposer revenue to the expected second-highest valuation — but revenue equivalence fails once values are affiliated or bidders asymmetric, which is exactly the MEV setting. With affiliation, the linkage principle predicts that public bid disclosure (practiced by several relays that stream bids in real time) raises proposer revenue relative to a sealed format, because it weakens the winner's curse and lets builders bid more aggressively.

*Second*, the winner's curse dominates builder strategy: conditional on winning — having the most optimistic signal — the block is worth less than the signal suggested, so rational builders shade. The practical consequence is a *timing game*: relays accept bids until the last millisecond, so builders delay final bids to incorporate the freshest mempool data, compressing the auction into a latency contest that favors the best-provisioned infrastructure.

*Third*, asymmetry compounds: exclusive order flow gives *better information* about the common component, letting its holder shade less while still winning — the informed-bidder advantage. Our simulation (§5) shows even a modest exclusive-flow premium converts rapidly into market-share dominance, matching observed concentration [2].

### 4.2 Order-flow auctions: redistributing the MEV tax

If the builder auction sells the right to fill a block, the *order-flow auction* (OFA) sells the right to backrun a single transaction: a user submits a transaction to an OFA, searchers observe a *hint* and compete to append a backrun, the winner pays the user, and the bundle goes to builders — letting the transaction capture a share of the MEV it creates.

Flashbots' SUAVE generalizes this into a decentralized block-building stack: a *confidential compute* layer where encrypted order flow emits selective hints and auctions over execution rights run as smart contracts ("SUAPPs") [6]. The logic is MEV *redistribution* toward originators plus *credible neutrality* — rules enforced by cryptography, not relay goodwill.

But OFAs cut both ways [7]. Order flow feeds *every* downstream auction; whoever aggregates the most exclusive flow wins the builder auction most often, earns the most, and can therefore *pay the most* for yet more flow — via payment-for-order-flow deals or superior privacy/refund guarantees. This is the *centralization flywheel*: exclusive flow → auction dominance → revenue → more exclusive flow. A dominant builder can simply outbid everyone in each OFA too, converting downstream advantage into upstream control. Whether OFAs decentralize or entrench depends on the venues' own market structure — a competitive OFA layer redistributes; a monopolized one merely launders the same concentration.

### 4.3 Enshrined PBS: commit–reveal, staked builders, and the PTC

EIP-7732, enshrined proposer-builder separation (ePBS), moves the builder–proposer exchange into the consensus protocol and is slated for Ethereum's Glamsterdam upgrade [1][3]. The design replaces the relay's off-chain commit-reveal with protocol messages [3][4]:

1. **Commit.** At slot start ($t = 0$), the proposer publishes a beacon block containing a `SignedExecutionPayloadBid`: the winning builder's signed commitment to a block hash and a *bid value* payable to the proposer. The bid is debited directly from the builder's beacon-chain balance — builders are staked in-protocol entities (stakeable for as little as 1 ETH, exempt from validator churn queues), so payment is trustless rather than relay-guaranteed.
2. **Attest.** By $t = 1.5$s, attestors vote on the beacon block containing the commitment.
3. **Reveal.** The winning builder later reveals the full execution payload via a `SignedExecutionPayloadEnvelope`, plus blob data.
4. **Police.** A *Payload Timeliness Committee* (PTC) — a subset of the beacon committee — attests to whether the payload and blobs were revealed *on time*. At $t = 4$s the PTC enforces the *payload deadline*: if the payload has not been observed, the slot is treated as empty and no state transition occurs. At $t = 10$s it enforces the *blob deadline* over both payload and blob availability [1].

Execution validation is thus *decoupled, temporally and logically*, from consensus validation: the PTC checks timeliness without executing, deferring execution to the next block. The design removes relay trust and creates *pipelining* headroom — slow-propagating blobs get a later deadline than the payload, improving L1 scalability.

The ePBS auction preserves MEV-Boost's first-price structure [2]: the bid is still a sealed take-it-or-leave-it offer, the proposer still takes the maximum. What changes is *settlement and enforcement* — cryptoeconomic rather than reputational — and, crucially, *timing*: the builder commits at $t=0$ but reveals later, holding a valuable option in the interim.

### 4.4 The free option problem and timing games

That interim option is the sharpest strategic pathology in the enshrined design. Between committing and revealing, the builder watches MEV evolve and may find the block worth *less* than its committed bid. It then holds a *free option*: reveal at a loss, or withhold the payload, forfeit the slot (flagged empty by the PTC), and cap the loss at foregone profit plus penalty. With truncated downside and full upside, the option has positive value — and rational builders exercise it exactly when the network needs the payload most: during volatility, when MEV is hardest to forecast [1].

Formally, with committed bid $B$, realized block value $V_t$, and withholding penalty $c$, the builder reveals iff $V_t \geq B - c$ — exercising the option whenever $V_t < B - c$, with exercise probability rising in bid aggressiveness and MEV volatility. Two mitigations are analyzed [1]: *shortening the option window* (earlier deadlines shrink the variance of $V_t$) and *penalizing exercise* (raising $c$ via stake slashing). Both reduce exercise probabilities, but both cost: tighter deadlines reintroduce latency centralization, and harsh penalties raise entry barriers for small builders.

The lesson is general: *any* commit-reveal auction with delayed settlement grants the committer an option whose value must be priced into the mechanism. Relay PBS had the same structure, but relay discretion acted as an informal $c$. Enshrinement must replace informal discipline with explicit cryptoeconomics — the PTC and builder stake — whose calibration remains an open research question [1][2].

### 5.2 Simulation results

Running the affiliated-values simulation of §3.2 yields the following market statistics (20,000 slots, 6 builders):

| Scenario ($\rho$ = value correlation) | Proposer revenue (norm.) | Builder surplus (norm.) | Top-3 win share | HHI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\rho = 0.30$ (weak correlation) | 1.000 | 0.214 | 58.4% | 0.221 |
| $\rho = 0.70$ (baseline) | 1.183 | 0.176 | 61.9% | 0.238 |
| $\rho = 0.95$ (near-common value) | 1.312 | 0.141 | 64.2% | 0.251 |
| $\rho = 0.70$ + 35% exclusive-flow premium (builder 0) | 1.264 | 0.203 | 89.7% | 0.612 |

Three patterns emerge. *First*, higher correlation raises proposer revenue (the linkage principle) while compressing builder surplus. *Second*, even symmetric builders concentrate moderately (top-3 ≈ 62%), since affiliation plus shading inherently favors the best-informed. *Third*, a 35% exclusive-flow premium explodes concentration to a top-3 share near 90% and HHI 0.61 — highly concentrated by any antitrust standard. The flywheel is not a narrative; it is the equilibrium comparative static.

### 5.3 Theoretical result

> Theorem: *(Builder auction equilibrium and the order-flow advantage.)* Consider a symmetric first-price builder auction with $n \geq 2$ risk-neutral builders whose block valuations are affiliated. Let $\beta^*$ be the symmetric Bayes–Nash equilibrium strategy. Then: (i) $\beta^*(v) < v$ for all $v > 0$ — every builder shades; (ii) expected proposer revenue is non-decreasing in the degree of affiliation (linkage principle); and (iii) if one builder receives an exclusive order-flow endowment that first-order stochastically dominates rivals' information, its equilibrium win probability strictly exceeds $1/n$ and is increasing in the endowment, with the proposer's revenue gain strictly smaller than the endowed builder's surplus gain — i.e., exclusive flow concentrates profits upstream faster than it raises auction revenue.

*Proof sketch.* (i) Truthful bidding cannot be optimal: a marginal bid cut loses only with probability $o(dv)$ while saving $dv$ when winning; the FOC yields the §3.1 shading formula. (ii) Milgrom–Weber's linkage principle: affiliation plus public information raises the expected second-order statistic, which is the first-price winner's expected payment. (iii) The endowed builder's dominating signal distribution implies a higher posterior value at every signal, hence uniformly more aggressive best responses; the endowment raises the winner's *value* one-for-one but the *price* only through the shading-muted competitive response. ∎

The theorem rationalizes the empirical record: the dominant builders [2] are those with the largest private order-flow franchises, and the auction format, while maximizing proposer revenue given the information structure, cannot itself redistribute the structural advantage.

## 6 Limitations

This analysis has several important limitations. **First**, the model assumes risk-neutral builders in a static one-shot game; real builders are risk-averse, compete repeatedly (enabling tacit collusion), and face multi-block MEV that breaks the single-slot abstraction. **Second**, empirical grounding inherits market opacity: relay data covers only relayed auctions, private flow is unobservable, and builder profits are estimated. The ≈80% concentration figure [2] is robust, but its mechanism — skill vs. flow vs. latency — remains partially identified. **Third**, ePBS remains a draft specification for Glamsterdam [3]; PTC size, penalties, and deadlines are not final, and the free-option analysis [1] studies the design as specified, not as shipped. **Fourth**, we largely bracket cross-domain MEV, L2 sequencing, and the interaction between L1 PBS and rollup-level auction designs, each of which could amplify or dampen the dynamics described here. **Finally**, the simulation is illustrative, not calibrated: it shows comparative statics, not magnitudes, and is not a forecast of any builder's market share.

## 7 Conclusion

PBS turned MEV from a validator side hustle into a market — and markets have microstructure. The builder auction, modeled as a first-price auction with affiliated values, is allocatively efficient and revenue-maximizing for proposers; bid shading, the winner's curse, and the linkage principle describe it precisely. But efficiency is not decentralization: exclusive flow, latency edges, and the informed-bidder advantage compound into extreme concentration, and OFAs redistribute only insofar as their venues are themselves competitive.

Enshrined PBS [3][4] replaces trusted intermediaries with cryptoeconomic mechanism design: staked builders, trustless payment, and a PTC policing revelation. Its central novelty — the free option of delayed settlement [1] — reminds us that every commit-reveal auction must price the committer's optionality in advance. Whether ePBS decentralizes block building or merely re-platforms the same concentrated market will depend less on auction-theoretic elegance than on unglamorous parameters: who can afford the stake, who wins the latency race to the reveal deadline, and who owns the order flow. The auction is solved; the market structure is not.
