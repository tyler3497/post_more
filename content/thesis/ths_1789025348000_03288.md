# Optical Circuit Switching for Datacenter Fabrics: MEMS-Based OCS, RotorNet Demand-Oblivious Scheduling, and Hybrid Electro-Optical Topologies for AI Training Clusters

## Abstract
*Optical circuit switching (OCS)* — passive, data-rate-independent redirection of light between fiber ports — draws one to two orders of magnitude less power per bit than electrical packet switching (EPS) and can rewire a fabric's logical topology at runtime. This thesis treats OCS in datacenter fabrics across three architectural lineages. First, we analyze micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS)-based OCS: the 3D free-space crossbar geometry behind Google's *Apollo* deployment and Jupiter's evolution from Clos to direct-connect topologies [1]. Second, we formalize *demand-oblivious* scheduling via RotorNet's rotor switches, which cycle through a predetermined permutation sequence and eliminate the hotspot-estimation control loop [2][3], through its microsecond-scale realization [4]. Third, we survey hybrid electro-optical designs — Helios and c-Through [5][6], Sirius's nanosecond tunable-laser fabric [7], and Opera's rotor-plus-expander synthesis [8] — partitioning *elephant* and *mice* flows across media. We compare power, cost, reconfiguration latency, and radix scaling, and argue OCS is becoming the primary high-bandwidth substrate of AI training clusters.

## 1 Introduction

Electrical packet switches sit at the center of every modern datacenter, yet their dominance is increasingly qualified. CMOS switch silicon follows a slowing Moore's-law trajectory; every radix or serdes generation demands more energy per bit, more cooling, and more cost per port [1][7]. Optical fibers, by contrast, carry enormous and essentially free capacity — the fiber itself imposes no energy cost on transmission, and its capacity grows through wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) and modulation advances at the transceiver rather than through the switch core. The missing link is *switching*: redirecting light in the optical domain without the energy penalty of optical-electrical-optical (OEO) conversion.

Three generations of research have attacked this problem. The first generation, beginning around 2010, proposed *hybrid* architectures: a conventional packet-switched network augmented by a circuit-switched optical overlay that offloads large, persistent *elephant* flows, leaving bursty *mice* flows to EPS [5][6]. The second generation asked whether the optical fabric could stand alone, and the answer required rethinking control. Demand-aware reconfiguration — estimating traffic matrices, solving a maximum-weight matching, and steering mirrors — introduces control-plane latencies of 100 ms to seconds, which restricts the optical layer to workloads stable over multi-second epochs [5]. *Demand-oblivious* designs break this coupling: rotor switches cycle through a fixed sequence of matchings, and hosts schedule traffic *onto* the sequence rather than reconfiguring switches to match traffic [2][4]. The third generation co-designs device, topology, and protocol to reach nanosecond reconfiguration: tunable lasers plus passive diffraction gratings collapse the network into a flat, single-hop optical core [7].

The stakes have risen dramatically with AI training. Large-scale gradient synchronization (all-reduce) generates structured, high-volume, collective traffic across thousands of GPUs — traffic whose demands are *knowable* from the parallelization strategy yet *bursty* at the collective granularity. Optical switching is a natural fit: TopoOpt demonstrates that topology optimization for distributed DNN training over an all-fiber OCS fabric can measurably reduce training time [9], and hyperscale operators are now sampling 300×300-port MEMS OCS devices explicitly for GPU clusters.

This thesis makes three contributions:

1. A device-level and system-level analysis of **3D MEMS OCS**, including the two-mirror-array crossbar geometry, insertion-loss budgets, reconfiguration dynamics (10–100 ms), and the production lessons of Google's Apollo/Jupiter deployment [1].
2. A formal treatment of **demand-oblivious optical scheduling**: rotor permutations, cycle-time analysis, Valiant load balancing (VLB) over multi-hop indirect paths, and the Opera synthesis of rotor plus packet-switched expanders [2][3][4][8].
3. A comparative evaluation of **hybrid electro-optical topologies** (Helios, c-Through, Sirius, Opera) on power, cost, latency, and radix scalability, with a projection onto AI-training-cluster requirements.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Optical circuit switching fundamentals

An optical circuit switch maps *N* input fibers to *N* output fibers at the physical layer. The switching fabric is *passive* in the data plane: once configured, photons traverse the switch without OEO conversion, so the power draw is dominated by the control electronics (milliwatts to watts) rather than by data-plane energy per bit. This yields the two headline properties of OCS: **data-rate independence** (upgrading from 400G to 1.6T requires transceiver changes, not switch changes) and **protocol/format independence** (any modulation, any WDM grid, traverses the same fabric).

### 2.2 Datacenter traffic structure

Datacenter traffic is *skewed*: a small fraction of flows (elephants) carry the majority of bytes, while the vast majority of flows (mice) are short, latency-sensitive RPCs [6]. Elephant flows are persistent (seconds to minutes); mice are bursty (microseconds to milliseconds). This bimodality is the fundamental justification for hybrid designs: circuits amortize their reconfiguration cost over elephants, while packets handle mice [5][6].

### 2.3 Reconfiguration timescales

The control plane of demand-aware OCS performs three steps: **demand estimation** (100 ms – 1 s of traffic observation), **matching computation** (solving a maximum-weight *b*-matching on the traffic matrix), and **mirror steering** (10–100 ms for 3D MEMS, settling time included). The reconfiguration timescale thus constrains the optical layer to traffic stable over *seconds* — adequate for diurnal shifts and bulk transfers but blind to bursty structure [5][10].

Demand-oblivious rotor switching collapses the first two steps to zero: the switch cycles through *N* (or *N*−1) matchings continuously, and each host maintains a transmission schedule synchronized to the cycle [2][4]. Reconfiguration cost is then purely a device parameter (microseconds for rotor switches), and traffic is *scheduled onto* the fabric rather than the fabric *reconfigured for* traffic.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in three methodological strands: (i) device-physics modeling of 3D MEMS crossbars to derive loss budgets, radix limits, and control-loop dynamics; (ii) formal scheduling analysis of rotor networks, including throughput bounds under Valiant load balancing and multi-hop indirect routing; and (iii) comparative system evaluation of hybrid architectures using published prototype measurements and analytical cost models. Throughout, we ground claims in measured production data (Google Jupiter [1]), prototype demonstrations (Sirius [7], RotorNet [4], Mordia [11]), and trace-driven simulation results reported in the cited works.

### 3.1 MEMS crossbar model

A 3D MEMS OCS with *N* ports uses two *N*-element arrays of mirrors, each mirror tiltable on two axes by electrostatic actuation (electrodes beneath the mirror form a capacitor; applied voltage pulls the mirror toward the electrode, with tilt angle set by voltage balance [10]). The signal path is:

$$\text{fiber}_i \rightarrow \text{collimator} \rightarrow \text{mirror}_i^{(in)} \rightarrow \text{free space} \rightarrow \text{mirror}_j^{(out)} \rightarrow \text{collimator} \rightarrow \text{fiber}_j$$

The free-space geometry gives **path-independent loss**: every input-output pair traverses an equal-length path, so signal strength is inherently balanced across the crossbar — a decisive advantage over 2D MEMS or semiconductor optical amplifier (SOA) fabrics where loss compounds with path length [10]. Typical specifications: insertion loss 1.5–3 dB, return loss >40 dB, polarization-dependent loss <0.2 dB, and crosstalk below −50 dB.

### 3.2 Rotor scheduling model

A rotor switch implements the *cyclic shift* permutation family. For *N* nodes, the rotor cycles through matchings

$$\pi_k : i \mapsto (i + k) \bmod N, \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, N-1$$

spending a *slot* of duration $\tau$ on each, giving a full cycle time $T_c = N\tau$. With *R* rotor switches operating in parallel on staggered schedules, each node simultaneously connects to *R* distinct destinations. Traffic engineering becomes **slot assignment**: each host, synchronized to the global schedule, transmits to destination *d* during the slot in which a direct matching to *d* (or an indirect multi-hop path) is active. No centralized controller observes traffic or computes matchings — the control plane complexity that plagued demand-aware designs vanishes [2][3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 MEMS-based OCS: device architecture and production deployment

The decisive production datapoint is Google's Jupiter evolution [1]. Between 2015 and 2022, Jupiter scaled from 1.3 Pb/s to over 6 Pb/s of aggregate bandwidth, delivering 5× higher speed and capacity with a 30% capex reduction and 41% power reduction. A *key enabler* was the introduction of the **Apollo OCS** intermediation layer: MEMS-based circuit switches placed between aggregation blocks, each mapping 136 input fibers to 136 output fibers via two 2D MEMS mirror arrays (the signal bounces from a collimator array, off a splitter, onto the MEMS mirrors, and out through the output collimators).

> **Theorem: Arbitrary logical topologies.** An OCS layer between packet-switched blocks realizes any logical topology whose degree sequence respects the physical port budget: configuring each OCS device with a permutation of its input-output fibers instantiates the target interconnection graph without recabling. ∎

The commercial ecosystem has converged on MEMS as the dominant switching technology for AI clusters (MarketsandMarkets projects the OCS market growing from $0.56B in 2026 to $2.52B in 2032, with the >320×320 segment the fastest-growing) [10]. Representative products: Lumentum's R300 (300×300 ports, ~65% power savings claimed for large GPU deployments), and Eoptolink's NX200/NX300 (140 and 320 ports) built on self-developed MEMS mirrors.

**Reconfiguration dynamics.** A 3D MEMS reconfiguration involves voltage settling (mirror mechanical resonance ~kHz), optical power feedback convergence, and control-plane round trips: 10–100 ms end-to-end. This is *four orders of magnitude* slower than a packet-switch forwarding decision, which is why demand-aware MEMS fabrics require stable elephants: workloads persisting seconds to minutes amortize the reconfiguration tax [5][10].

### 4.2 RotorNet: demand-oblivious scheduling and the rotor abstraction

RotorNet's central insight is that the *control plane* is the barrier to OCS adoption, not the optics [2]. Prior proposals (Helios, c-Through, OSA) required network-wide demand estimation, centralized circuit assignment, and tight synchronization — a "complex and unwieldy control plane." RotorNet replaces demand-aware reconfiguration with a **demand-oblivious** rotor: the switch cycles through its permutation sequence regardless of traffic, and endpoints schedule packets onto the cycle.

Key design parameters from the original SIGCOMM '17 paper [2]:

- **Rotor switch**: a 2,048-port optical device based on imaging relays, switching 1,000× faster than MEMS crossbars (microseconds vs. tens of milliseconds).
- **Cycle time**: for a 2,048-rack fabric with 128 rotor switches, $T_c \approx 3.2$ ms — below the 10 ms MEMS reconfiguration floor, so traffic sees the *full* permutation cycle within one reconfiguration-equivalent window.
- **Hybrid for latency**: latency-sensitive mice are served by a parallel low-latency packet-switched network (a "hybrid" at the fabric level, not the flow level).

**Realization.** "Realizing RotorNet" (SIGCOMM '24) [4] demonstrates an end-to-end implementation: custom NICs synchronized to the rotor switch, microsecond-scale switching, commodity transceivers, and *zero packet loss* for user-space application traffic. The engineering crux is **synchronization**: hosts and switches must agree on slot boundaries within sub-microsecond tolerance; the paper reports a practical design achieving this with commodity protocols.

### 4.3 Sirius: nanosecond switching via tunable lasers and passive gratings

Sirius attacks reconfiguration from the opposite direction: instead of moving mirrors or cycling permutations, it moves the *wavelength* [7]. Each node carries fast tunable lasers; the fabric core is a single layer of passive gratings that diffract each wavelength to a fixed output port. Changing the laser's wavelength changes the destination — and the laser tunes in **< 912 ps**, yielding **3.84 ns end-to-end reconfiguration** over 50-Gbps channels in the prototype.

1. **No moving parts, no switch power.** The gratings are entirely passive; the only powered elements are the transceivers. Simulations show Sirius approximating ideal non-blocking performance with **74–77% lower power** than an electrically switched fabric [7].
### 4.4 Hybrid electro-optical topologies: Helios, c-Through, Mordia, and Opera

The hybrid lineage begins with two SIGCOMM 2010 papers [5][6]. **Helios** proposes a hybrid electrical/optical *switch* for modular data centers: pod switches retain a mix of electrical and optical uplinks; a central scheduler identifies persistent high-volume pod-pair demands and steers them through MEMS OCS, achieving the performance of a fully provisioned packet network at significantly lower cost, complexity, and power. **c-Through** ("part-time optics") frames the same idea as a *control plane*: a hybrid network where the optical component is provisioned for the long-lived portion of the demand, with a provably near-optimal scheduler. Both identify the same fundamental tradeoff: optical capacity is cheap but reconfigures slowly; electrical capacity is flexible but expensive and power-hungry.

**Opera** [8] synthesizes the rotor and expander traditions. The fabric combines rotor switches (for bulk, scheduled traffic) with a packet-switched expander graph (for mice), connected to the same ToR uplinks. The expander provides static low-latency connectivity; the rotor provides high-throughput demand-oblivious capacity; a unified ToR scheduler assigns traffic between them. Conceptually, Opera is RotorNet matured: it keeps demand-oblivious scheduling but repairs RotorNet's latency tail with the expander, and it keeps MEMS-free optics but at commodity transceiver cost.

### 4.5 OCS for AI training clusters: collectives on a reconfigurable fabric

AI training traffic is the most compelling modern driver. Distributed data-parallel training issues **collective** operations (all-reduce, all-gather, reduce-scatter) whose communication graphs are known *a priori* from the parallelization strategy, yet whose phases alternate between compute and burst communication at collective granularity (tens of milliseconds to seconds). This structure is ideal for OCS: circuits can be provisioned per collective phase, and the all-to-all or ring patterns of collectives map naturally onto permutation-based rotor schedules.

Industry has followed: Lumentum's R300 and Eoptolink's NX300 are explicitly positioned for AI clusters, with deployment models that *replace the EPS spine* with OCS, eliminating OEO at the spine and cutting both power and predictable latency.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Power and cost scaling

The power argument for OCS rests on the absence of per-hop OEO conversion. Measurements and vendor data converge on the following:

- A MEMS OCS port draws on the order of **1 W** (control electronics and mirror drivers), versus **10–30 W** per port for a high-radix EPS port at 400G–800G including serdes and switch ASIC. At equal bisection bandwidth, the optical layer consumes **two orders of magnitude less power** [6][10].
- Google's production data: 41% reduction in fabric power and 30% reduction in capex across the Jupiter evolution, attributed substantially to OCS-enabled direct-connect topologies and incremental upgrades without overprovisioned spines [1].
- Sirius simulations: 74–77% lower power than an ideal non-blocking electrical fabric at equal performance [7].
- Opera cost model: ~1.3× per-port cost versus a static network — the premium is bounded because rotor optics reuse commodity multimode/single-mode transceivers [8].

### 5.2 Throughput under demand-oblivious scheduling: a sketch

Consider *N* nodes, *R* rotor switches, slot time $\tau$, and cycle $T_c = (N-1)\tau$ (excluding the identity shift). In each cycle, every ordered pair $(i, j)$ receives *R* direct slots. Under Valiant load balancing, a packet from *i* to *j* first travels to a random intermediate *k* (slot $(i,k)$), then to *j* (slot $(k,j)$). The load on any directed slot is uniform by symmetry, and each slot carries at most the uniform rate. The per-node goodput is therefore $\Theta(R \cdot C / N)$ for link capacity $C$ — within a constant factor of the ideal non-blocking rate, with the constant determined by the VLB 2× tax and the slot duty cycle [2]. Multi-hop indirect routing (Opera's expander paths) tightens the constant for skewed demands at the cost of additional transceiver traversals [8].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Reconfiguration vs. burstiness.** MEMS-based demand-aware fabrics remain blind to sub-second traffic structure; they are capacity-planning tools, not packet schedulers. Workloads without persistent elephants gain little.
2. **Synchronization fragility.** Rotor networks trade control-plane complexity for *synchronization* complexity: slot-boundary agreement within microseconds across thousands of hosts is a hard distributed-systems problem, and clock drift translates directly into packet loss [4].
3. **VLB bandwidth tax.** Demand-oblivious routing pays a 2× bandwidth penalty and extra latency under VLB; for already-saturated fabrics this is a real cost, not a free lunch [2].
4. **Tunable-laser cost and reliability.** Sirius-class designs concentrate complexity and cost in the transceiver (fast tunable lasers are telecom-grade devices); a laser failure blinds a node's entire connectivity, unlike MEMS where a mirror fault degrades one port pair [7].
5. **Cabling and operational complexity.** Direct-connect and flat optical topologies replace structured Clos cabling with dense fiber plants and OCS patch panels; misconfiguration is harder to diagnose, and optical power budgets must be managed per path.
6. **Control-plane maturity.** SDN-driven topology engineering at Google scale [1] required a decade of operational tooling (Orion controller, automated fiber inventory, mirror-health monitoring). Smaller operators cannot trivially replicate this.
7. **Security and fault isolation.** A shared reconfigurable optical core is a shared fate domain: a mis-steered mirror or compromised controller can silently redirect traffic between tenants, and optical-layer faults are invisible to packet-layer diagnostics.

## 7 Conclusion

Optical circuit switching has completed a fifteen-year journey from research curiosity to production substrate. The 3D MEMS OCS — embodied in Google's Apollo deployment and Jupiter's direct-connect evolution — proved that large-scale, manufacturable, reliable optical switching is commercially viable, delivering double-digit percentage reductions in capex and power at petabit scale [1]. RotorNet proved that the control plane need not be the barrier, replacing demand estimation with demand-oblivious permutation cycling and Valiant load balancing [2], and its microsecond-scale realization brought the design within reach of practical deployment [4]. Sirius proved that the reconfiguration timescale itself can be collapsed by six orders of magnitude, reaching nanoseconds through tunable lasers and passive gratings [7]. The hybrid architectures — Helios, c-Through, Mordia, Opera — mapped the middle ground, partitioning traffic by timescale across media [5][6][8][11].
