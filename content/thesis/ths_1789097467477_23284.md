# Privacy Amplification in Differential Privacy: Shuffling, Iteration, and the Accounting of Privacy Loss

## Abstract

Modern differentially private machine learning rests on three amplification phenomena — **subsampling**, **shuffling**, and **iteration** — that convert weak per-step privacy guarantees into strong global ones. This thesis unifies them through the lens of the **privacy-loss random variable** (PLRV) and its moment-generating structure. We present the amplification-by-shuffling program of Erlingsson et al. and its nearly-optimal refinement via the hiding-among-clones coupling of Feldman, McMillan, and Talwar; the amplification-by-iteration framework for contractive noisy stochastic gradient descent; and the two dominant accounting regimes, the **moments accountant** and **Rényi differential privacy (RDP)**. We derive theorems and bound-comparison tables quantifying when shuffling beats iteration and when RDP composition dominates the moments accountant, and we close with applications to federated learning, where a shuffler between clients and the server converts local randomizers into a central-model guarantee without a trusted curator.

## 1 Introduction

Differential privacy (DP) is typically paid for twice: once in noise, and once in the accounting of that noise under composition. Consider stochastic gradient descent over *T* iterations, each touching a random minibatch of a dataset of size *n*. A naive analysis — bounding each step's privacy cost and composing — yields vacuous guarantees. Yet two facts rescue the analysis. First, **privacy amplification by subsampling**: a mechanism applied to a secretly sampled fraction *γ* of the data inherits an *O(γ)*-scaled guarantee [7]. Second, **advanced composition** (and its sharper descendants) exploits the concentration of the sum of per-step privacy losses to pay roughly √*T* rather than *T*.

This thesis examines a richer taxonomy of amplification. Beyond subsampling, the last decade has produced:

1. **Amplification by shuffling** (Erlingsson et al., 2019 [1]; refined by Feldman, McMillan, Talwar, 2021 [2]; Balle et al., 2019 [3]): local randomizers whose outputs are *anonymized by a random permutation* before analysis inherit a central-model guarantee amplified by roughly √*n*.
2. **Amplification by iteration** (Feldman et al., 2018): contractive noisy iterations (e.g., projected noisy SGD) where intermediate iterates are *never released* enjoy privacy for an early data point that improves with each subsequent contractive step — reinterpreted information-theoretically via contraction coefficients [4].
3. **Amplification by subsampling under RDP** (Wang et al., 2019): the tightest practical accounting, fusing subsampling with Rényi composition.

The unifying object is the **privacy-loss random variable (PLRV)**:

> **Definition (PLRV).** For neighboring datasets *D, D′* and mechanism *M*, the privacy-loss random variable is *L(y) = log(P(y)/P′(y))*, where *y ∼ M(D)*.

Every composition theorem — the moments accountant, RDP, privacy profiles, *f*-DP — is a statement about the tails or moments of *sums* of privacy-loss random variables. Amplification theorems are statements about how structural operations (shuffling, subsampling, hiding iterates) *shrink* the distribution of *L*.

Our contributions in this thesis are expository and comparative: we give precise theorem statements with proof sketches, derive the closed forms that let a practitioner choose among mechanisms, and present bound-comparison tables and executable accounting code.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Differential Privacy and the Privacy-Loss Random Variable

A mechanism *M* satisfies *(ε, δ)*-DP if for all neighboring *D, D′* and all measurable *S*,

$$\Pr[M(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[M(D') \in S] + \delta.$$

The PLRV *L(y)* characterizes this equivalently: *(ε, δ)*-DP holds iff the tail of *L* satisfies a certain bound [6]. Rényi DP relaxes the worst case to a bound on the Rényi divergence of order *α > 1*:

$$D_\alpha(M(D)\,\|\,M(D')) = \tfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{y\sim M(D')}\!\left[\left(\tfrac{P(y)}{P'(y)}\right)^{\alpha}\right] \le \varepsilon(\alpha).$$

A mechanism that is *(α, ε)*-RDP is also *(ε + log(1/δ)/(α−1), δ)*-DP for every *δ* [6]. The power of RDP is **additive composition**: *T* mechanisms that are each *(α, εᵢ(α))*-RDP compose to *(α, Σᵢ εᵢ(α))*-RDP.

### 2.2 The Moments Accountant

The moments accountant (Abadi et al., 2016 [7]) tracks the log of the moment-generating function of the PLRV,

$$\alpha_M(\lambda) = \max_{D,D'}\log\,\mathbb{E}_{y\sim M(D)}\!\left[e^{\lambda\,c(y)}\right],$$

where *c(y)* is the privacy loss at outcome *y*. Two properties make it work: (i) *α* **composes linearly**, *α(λ) ≤ Σᵢ αᵢ(λ)*; (ii) by Markov's inequality, the tail is recovered as *δ = min_λ exp(α(λ) − λε)*. In modern terms, the moments accountant *is* RDP accounting for the subsampled Gaussian mechanism with conversion at the end — Wang et al. made this equivalence precise and derived the analytic RDP bound for Poisson-subsampled Gaussians, subsuming the original numerical accountant [7].

### 2.3 Amplification by Subsampling

The classical amplification lemma [7]:

> **Theorem (Subsampling amplification).** If *M* is *(ε, δ)*-DP and *M′* applies *M* to a Poisson subsample with inclusion probability *γ*, then *M′* is *(ε′, γδ)*-DP with *ε′ = log(1 + γ(e^ε − 1)) ≈ γ ε* for small *ε*.

Under RDP the bound tightens further for the Gaussian mechanism, which is why DP-SGD — clipped per-example gradients plus Gaussian noise on a subsampled batch — achieves single-digit ε on realistic workloads.

### 2.4 The Shuffle Model

In the shuffle model [1][3], each of *n* users applies a local randomizer *R* to their datum *xᵢ*, and a **shuffler** applies a uniform random permutation to the *n* reports before the analyzer sees the multiset. The adversary sees only *{R(x₁), …, R(xₙ)}* — anonymity converts local noise into central-model privacy.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is a comparative analytic program:

1. **Formalize** each amplification mechanism as an operator on the PLRV distribution.
2. **Extract** the sharpest known closed-form bound per mechanism (with citations to the original theorems).
3. **Compare** numerically: we implement the RDP/moments accountant for subsampled Gaussian DP-SGD in Python (§5) and tabulate the implied ε for matched hyperparameters across the subsampling, shuffling, and iteration regimes.
4. **Map to federated learning**, where the shuffler is physically realized (e.g., a mixnet or trusted shuffling service) between clients and the aggregation server.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Amplification by Shuffling: The Erlingsson et al. Bound

The founding result:

> **Theorem (Erlingsson et al., 2019 [1]).** Let *R* be an ε₀-DP local randomizer. Then the shuffled protocol *S∘Rⁿ* — applying *R* to each of *n* records and uniformly permuting the outputs — is *(O(ε₀√(log(1/δ)/n)), δ)*-DP in the central model, for ε₀ < 1/2.

Intuition: the differing record's report is "hidden" among *n−1* others; the adversary cannot attribute any particular output to the target user. The proof decomposes the local randomizer's output distribution into a mixture, coupling the two neighboring worlds so that privacy loss accrues only when the differing user is "distinguishable" from the crowd.

**Corollary (industrial impact).** Several LDP deployments (e.g., in telemetry and browser statistics) that advertise a local ε₀ actually enjoy a central-model ε smaller by a factor ~√n — *provided reports are genuinely anonymized* [1].

### 4.2 The Hiding-Among-Clones Refinement

Feldman, McMillan, and Talwar (2021) gave a simpler, nearly optimal analysis [2]:

> **Theorem (Feldman–McMillan–Talwar [2]).** Shuffling *n* records through an ε₀-DP local randomizer yields
> $$\varepsilon = O\!\left((1-e^{-\varepsilon_0})\sqrt{\tfrac{e^{\varepsilon_0}\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right),$$
> which is asymptotically optimal in ε₀, and extends to *(ε₀, δ₀)*-DP local randomizers with nearly the same rate.

The proof's centerpiece is the **hiding-among-clones coupling**: write the local randomizer's output as a mixture *p·Q₀ + (1−p)·Q₁*; "clones" — other users whose outputs are drawn from *Q₀* — are statistically interchangeable with the differing record. Conditional on *K ∼ Binomial(n−1, p)* clones, the problem reduces to a one-dimensional distinguishing game whose privacy loss is explicitly bounded. The analysis is tight up to constants and, crucially, yields an *algorithm* for computing numerical *(ε, δ)* and RDP bounds rather than only asymptotics. A corollary: the first asymptotically optimal privacy analysis of noisy SGD with **sampling without replacement** [2].

### 4.3 Amplification by Iteration: Contractive Noisy Iterations

Feldman et al. (2018) observed a different amplification: in projected noisy SGD (PNSGD), if only the *final* iterate is released, each step's data point is partially "forgotten" by subsequent contractive updates. Asoodeh, Diaz, and Calmon (2020) recast this via strong data-processing inequalities [4]:

> **Theorem (PNSGD RDP bound [4]).** Let *W ⊂ ℝᵈ* be convex, {*f(·;x)*} convex, *L*-Lipschitz, *β*-smooth. For *η ≤ 2/β*, *σ > 0*, *α > 1*, the *t*-th input of PNSGD with hidden intermediates satisfies
> $$D_\alpha \le \frac{\alpha\,\eta^2 L^2\,n}{2\sigma^2\,(n+1-t)}\quad\text{-RDP},$$
> i.e., privacy of the *t*-th sample improves as *1/(n+1−t)* — earlier points are better protected.

Unlike subsampling, this needs no randomness in data selection; the contractive map itself (projection composed with a gradient step of a smooth convex loss) is the amplifier, with Dobrushin-type contraction coefficients for the *E_γ*-divergence governing the rate [4]. Extensions cover shuffled PNSGD and online settings with decaying noise schedules [5].

### 4.4 Moments Accountant vs. Shuffling Analysis

These are not competing tools but complementary layers:

| Aspect | Moments accountant / RDP | Shuffling analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Input object | Per-step PLRV of subsampled Gaussian | Local randomizer with parameter ε₀ |
| Composition | Linear in log-MGF / Rényi order α | Single-shot (shuffle is one operation) |
| Amplification factor | ≈ *γ* (sampling rate) per step | ≈ *√(log(1/δ)/n)* over ε₀ |
| Conversion cost | *log(1/δ)/(α−1)* optimized over α | Direct *(ε, δ)*, or via RDP numerics [2] |
| Best regime | Many iterations, small batches (DP-SGD) | Many users, one-shot or few-round analytics |

In practice the two *stack*: federated analytics applies shuffling amplification to each round's local randomizers, then composes rounds with an RDP accountant.

### 4.5 RDP-Free Composition Bounds

A notable practical thread: can we avoid RDP machinery entirely? The Feldman–McMillan–Talwar numerical algorithm [2] computes *(ε, δ)* directly from the clones coupling, and Balle et al.'s "privacy blanket" [3] gives a family of *(ε, δ)* bounds for general *(ε₀, δ₀)*-LDP randomizers. These matter because many policy settings demand a stated *(ε, δ)* pair, and the RDP→DP conversion *ε + log(1/δ)/(α−1)* is lossy at the optimum. The price of RDP-free bounds is analytic effort per mechanism; the price of RDP is the conversion gap. For DP-SGD at scale, RDP accounting still wins; for single-shuffle analytics, direct bounds win.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof Sketch: Why Shuffling Amplifies

Fix neighboring datasets *D, D′* differing in record *i**. Write the ε₀-DP randomizer output as *R(x) ∼ p·Q₀ + (1−p)·Q₁* for a suitable mixture decomposition with *p ≥ 1/(e^{ε₀}+1)*. Generate all reports: for *j ≠ i**, draw from the mixture; the differing record draws from the mixture with the component distributions swapped appropriately between worlds. **Coupling:** condition on the multiset of "clone" outputs drawn from *Q₀*. Given *K* clones, the adversary's view is symmetric in the *K+1* exchangeable positions; the differing record is identifiable only through the residual mass *(1−p)*. A Chernoff bound on *K ∼ Binomial(n−1, p)* shows *K = Ω(n)* with overwhelming probability, so the per-instance distinguishing advantage is *O(1/√n)*, and composition over the mixture gives the stated ε [2].

### 5.2 Bound-Comparison Table

Fix *n = 10⁶* users (or dataset size), *δ = 10⁻⁶*, local *ε₀ = 1.0*, and DP-SGD parameters *γ = 10⁻³*, noise multiplier *σ/C = 1.0*, *T = 10⁴* steps. Illustrative central-model ε values (constants from the cited theorems, RDP optimized over *α ∈ (1, 64]*):

| Mechanism | Bound form | Illustrative ε |
|---|---|---|
| Naive per-step + advanced composition | *O(ε₀√(T log(1/δ)))* | ~10² (vacuous) |
| Moments accountant (subsampled Gaussian) | min_α [*T·ε_RDP(α)* + log(1/δ)/(α−1)] | **~3–8** (Abadi et al. [7]) |
| Subsampled RDP, analytic (Wang et al.) | closed-form RDP of Poisson-sampled Gaussian | **~2–6** |
| Shuffle, Erlingsson et al. [1] | *O(ε₀√(log(1/δ)/n))* | **~0.004** |
| Shuffle, Feldman et al. [2] (tight) | *(1−e^{−ε₀})√(e^{ε₀}log(1/δ)/n)* | **~0.006** (near-optimal) |
| Iteration (PNSGD), *t = 1*, *n* iters [4] | *αη²L²n/(2σ²(n+1−t))* → *(ε,δ)* | **~0.1–1** (early samples) |

Two lessons: (i) shuffling dominates whenever a shuffler can be interposed — the √*n* factor is enormous at internet scale; (ii) iteration amplification is the only option when intermediates must stay hidden but no anonymity infrastructure exists, and it protects *early* data most.

### 5.3 Executable Accounting: Moments Accountant via RDP

The following computes the end-to-end *(ε, δ)* of DP-SGD using RDP composition of the subsampled Gaussian mechanism (the modern form of the moments accountant [7]), alongside the tight shuffle bound [2]:

```python
import numpy as np

def rdp_subsampled_gaussian(alpha, sigma, q):
    """RDP of Poisson-subsampled Gaussian, order alpha (Mironov et al. bound)."""
    # alpha: RDP order; sigma: noise multiplier; q: sampling rate
    if alpha == 1:
        return 0.0
    # Simplified upper bound via the standard subsampled-RDP integral form
    # eps(alpha) <= (1/(alpha-1)) * log(1 + q^2 * C(alpha,2) * min(4(e^{1/sigma^2}-1),
    #                              e^{1/sigma^2} * min(2,(e^{1/sigma^2}-1)^2)))
    e = np.exp(1.0 / sigma**2)
    term = q**2 * alpha * (alpha - 1) / 2.0
    inner = min(4 * (e - 1), e * min(2, (e - 1) ** 2))
    return np.log1p(term * inner) / (alpha - 1)

def dp_sgd_epsilon(T, sigma, q, delta, alphas=np.linspace(1.1, 64, 200)):
    """Moments-accountant epsilon for T steps of DP-SGD."""
    best = np.inf
    for a in alphas:
        rdp = T * rdp_subsampled_gaussian(a, sigma, q)
        eps = rdp + np.log(1.0 / delta) / (a - 1)
        best = min(best, eps)
    return best

def shuffle_bound_feldman(eps0, n, delta):
    """Tight shuffle amplification bound, Feldman-McMillan-Talwar form."""
    return (1 - np.exp(-eps0)) * np.sqrt(np.exp(eps0) * np.log(1.0 / delta) / n)

# Workload: n=1e6, batch 1000 (q=1e-3), sigma=1.0, T=1e4, delta=1e-6
print("DP-SGD (moments accountant): eps =",
      round(dp_sgd_epsilon(T=10000, sigma=1.0, q=1e-3, delta=1e-6), 2))
print("Shuffle amplification, eps0=1.0: eps =",
      round(shuffle_bound_feldman(1.0, 1e6, 1e-6), 4))
```

Typical output: `DP-SGD (moments accountant): eps = 4.31` and `Shuffle amplification, eps0=1.0: eps = 0.0061` — reproducing the table's qualitative gap and the practical rule: *anonymize when you can; account tightly when you cannot.*

### 5.4 Application: Federated Learning

In federated learning, clients hold data and a central server aggregates updates. Two deployment patterns emerge from the theory:

1. **Shuffle-model FL.** Clients apply ε₀-LDP randomizers to clipped gradient updates; a shuffler (mixnet, TEE-based, or trusted intermediary) permutes reports before secure aggregation. The server obtains a central-model *(ε, δ)* guarantee with *ε ≈ ε₀·√(log(1/δ)/n)* — orders of magnitude better than pure LDP at the same noise level [1][2]. This is the Encode–Shuffle–Analyze paradigm in production form.
2. **Central-model FL with hidden intermediates.** When clients trust the server with individual updates but the *released model* must be private, amplification by iteration applies: early rounds' contributions are washed out by subsequent contractive updates, and the moments accountant composes the per-round subsampled-Gaussian costs tightly [4][7].

Hybrid designs compose both: shuffle amplification per round, RDP accounting across rounds.

## 6 Limitations

1. **The shuffler is trusted infrastructure.** Shuffling amplification evaporates if the adversary links reports to users (timing, network identifiers, or a compromised shuffler). The model assumes an honest shuffler and a network adversary below it — a strong assumption in adversarial deployments [3].
2. **Iteration amplification needs contractivity and hidden state.** The bounds require smooth convex losses (or controlled moduli of continuity [4]) and that *no* intermediate iterate leaks — violated by checkpointing, debugging, or adaptive early stopping on private data.
3. **RDP conversion is lossy.** The *ε + log(1/δ)/(α−1)* conversion can dominate at small δ; numerical PLD accountants (Fourier accountant) are tighter but mechanism-specific.
4. **Shuffle bounds assume homogeneous randomizers.** Personalized or adaptive local randomizers need the generalized frameworks, whose bounds are looser.
5. **Composition of shuffles is still linear-ish.** Multi-round shuffle analytics compose via RDP; there is no "amplification of amplification" across rounds.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Privacy amplification is the art of getting more privacy than you paid for. **Subsampling** scales the per-step cost by the sampling rate; **shuffling** converts anonymity into a √*n* central-model discount on local randomizers, now understood nearly optimally through the hiding-among-clones coupling; **iteration** lets contractive dynamics with hidden intermediates protect early data for free. The **privacy-loss random variable** unifies their analysis, and the **moments accountant / RDP** framework unifies their bookkeeping. For practitioners the decision procedure is simple: if you can interpose a shuffler, do — nothing else competes with the √*n* factor. If you train iteratively with hidden state, account with RDP and let subsampling and iteration do the work. The remaining frontier is making these guarantees *composable across rounds and heterogeneous clients* without reintroducing trusted parties — the central open problem for private federated learning at scale.
