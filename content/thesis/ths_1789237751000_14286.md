# Shannon's Noisy-Channel Coding Theorem: Random Coding, Error Exponents, and the Architecture of Reliable Communication

## Abstract

Claude Shannon's 1948 paper founded information theory and proved one of its most counterintuitive results: any communication rate strictly below a channel's capacity can be achieved with arbitrarily small probability of error, no matter how noisy the channel [1]. This thesis reconstructs the full intellectual edifice of the noisy-channel coding theorem, from the entropy and mutual information that measure uncertainty, through the source coding theorem that establishes the compression limit, to the dual machinery of the achievability proof — random coding with joint-typicality decoding — and the converse, proved via Fano's inequality. We then develop the theory of error exponents, including Gallager's random-coding and expurgated exponents, the sphere-packing converse bound, and the critical rate where lower and upper bounds meet [6]. Modern developments are surveyed: list decoding, Arikan's polar codes as the first explicit low-complexity capacity-achieving construction [5], the rediscovery of LDPC codes, the finite-blocklength framework of Polyanskiy, Poor, and Verdú [2], feedback capacity, and the zero-error capacity Shannon introduced in 1956 [7].

## 1 Introduction

In 1948, Claude Elwood Shannon published "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" in the *Bell System Technical Journal*, a paper that electrical engineer Robert G. Gallager would later call a "blueprint for the digital era" and that *Scientific American* dubbed the "Magna Carta of the Information Age" [1]. The paper introduced the *bit* — a term Shannon credited to John Tukey — and gave precise mathematical form to an engineering intuition that had, until then, been treated as folk wisdom: noise does not set an absolute floor on communication fidelity; it sets a limit on *rate*.

> **Theorem (Shannon's noisy-channel coding theorem, informal):** For any discrete memoryless channel with capacity $C$ and any rate $R < C$, there exists a sequence of block codes of rate approaching $R$ whose maximum probability of decoding error tends to zero. Conversely, no sequence of codes with rate $R > C$ can have vanishing error probability.

The theorem's most startling feature is not merely its statement but its proof technique. Shannon did not *construct* a good code; he showed that a code chosen at random from an appropriate ensemble is good with high probability. This *probabilistic method* — existence proved through randomness — was radical, and it established a pattern that recurs throughout coding theory: the best codes we know are often random, and explicit constructions play catch-up with the random-coding benchmark.

This thesis reconstructs that argument in full rigor, then follows the research program it launched: **how fast** does the error probability vanish (error exponents), **which explicit codes** achieve capacity in practice (polar and LDPC codes), what happens at **finite blocklength** where asymptotics no longer apply, and what changes when the channel has **feedback** or when errors must be exactly zero rather than vanishing.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Entropy, Mutual Information, and the Quantification of Uncertainty

Shannon's theory begins by *measuring* information. For a discrete random variable $X$ with probability mass function $p(x)$ over a finite alphabet $\mathcal{X}$, the **entropy** is

$$H(X) = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} p(x)\log_2 p(x),$$

measured in bits. Entropy is the expected number of binary digits needed to describe the outcome — the fundamental unit of uncertainty [4].

For a pair $(X, Y)$, the **conditional entropy** $H(Y|X)$ measures the residual uncertainty in $Y$ after $X$ is known, and the **mutual information**

$$I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y|X) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}$$

measures the *reduction* in uncertainty about one variable upon observing the other. It is symmetric, non-negative, and zero exactly when $X$ and $Y$ are independent. The celebrated **chain rules** — $H(X_1,\dots,X_n) = \sum_i H(X_i|X^{i-1})$ and $I(X_1,\dots,X_n;Y) = \sum_i I(X_i;Y|X^{i-1})$ — turn these definitions into a computational engine [4].

| Quantity | Definition | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Entropy $H(X)$ | $-\sum p(x)\log p(x)$ | Intrinsic uncertainty |
| Conditional entropy $H(Y\|X)$ | $\mathbb{E}_X[H(Y\|X=x)]$ | Residual uncertainty after side information |
| Mutual information $I(X;Y)$ | $H(Y)-H(Y\|X)$ | Information shared by $X$ and $Y$ |
| KL divergence $D(p\\|q)$ | $\sum p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ | Cost of mistaking $q$ for $p$ |

### 2.2 The Source Coding Theorem: Compression Has a Fundamental Limit

Before attacking noisy channels, Shannon solved the noiseless problem: a discrete memoryless source with entropy $H$ can be compressed to $H$ bits per symbol, and no further [1]. The proof introduces the **asymptotic equipartition property (AEP)**: among the $|\mathcal{X}|^n$ sequences of length $n$, a *typical set* of roughly $2^{nH}$ sequences carries nearly all the probability mass, and each is nearly equiprobable. Encoding only the typical set achieves rate $H$; the converse shows no scheme can beat it. The source coding theorem is the prototype for everything that follows: an **achievability** argument based on typicality plus a **converse** argument showing the limit is fundamental.

### 2.3 The Discrete Memoryless Channel and Its Capacity

A **discrete memoryless channel (DMC)** is a triple $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y}, W)$ where $W(y|x)$ is the probability of output $y$ given input $x$, and successive uses are independent. The **capacity** is defined information-theoretically as

$$C = \max_{p(x)} I(X;Y).$$

For the binary symmetric channel with crossover probability $p$, $C = 1 - H_2(p)$; for the binary erasure channel, $C = 1 - p$ [6]. Operationally, capacity is the supremum of rates at which arbitrarily reliable communication is possible — and the theorem's burden is to prove that these two definitions coincide.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is the classical Shannon-theoretic pipeline, which we reconstruct and then extend:

1. **Random coding (achievability).** Generate codewords independently according to a capacity-achieving input distribution; decode by joint typicality. Show the *average* error probability over the ensemble vanishes, hence at least one good code exists.
2. **Converse (Fano).** Relate any code's error probability to mutual information via Fano's inequality, proving no rate above capacity can sustain vanishing error.
3. **Error-exponent analysis (Gallager).** Refine the achievability with the $\rho$-trick to show error decays *exponentially* in blocklength; bound the exponent from above via sphere packing.
4. **Constructive and finite-length refinements.** Survey list decoding, polar codes, LDPC codes, and the Polyanskiy–Poor–Verdú finite-blocklength framework [2][5].
5. **Edge cases.** Feedback and the zero-error capacity, where the theory changes character [7].

We adopt the convention that logarithms are base 2 unless noted; blocklength is $n$, the number of messages $M = 2^{nR}$, and error probability is denoted $P_e$.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Achievability: Random Coding and Joint Typicality

Fix a distribution $p(x)$ achieving capacity. Generate a **random codebook**: $M = 2^{nR}$ codewords, each an i.i.d. draw $X^n \sim p^{\otimes n}$. This codebook is revealed to both encoder and decoder — a shared resource that makes the analysis tractable.

> **Theorem (Achievability):** For any $R < C$, there exist $(n, 2^{nR})$ codes with $P_e \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.

*Proof sketch.* The decoder uses **joint typicality**: upon receiving $y^n$, it declares the message $m$ whose codeword $x^n(m)$ is jointly typical with $y^n$; if none or more than one, an error is declared. Two error events matter: (i) the transmitted codeword is not jointly typical with the received sequence — vanishingly likely by the joint AEP; (ii) some *incorrect* codeword is jointly typical — for a random incorrect codeword, this occurs with probability at most $2^{-n(I(X;Y)-3\epsilon)}$, and there are $2^{nR}-1$ competitors. The union bound gives $P_e \leq \epsilon + 2^{nR}2^{-n(I(X;Y)-3\epsilon)}$, which vanishes when $R < I(X;Y) - 3\epsilon$. Since $\epsilon$ is arbitrary and $p$ achieves $C$, rates below capacity are achievable. ∎

Three features deserve emphasis. First, the decoder is **suboptimal**: maximum-likelihood decoding would do at least as well. Second, the proof bounds the *ensemble-average* error, so a *deterministic* good code exists by the probabilistic method — but the proof gives no recipe for finding it. Third, the codebook is enormous and unstructured, foreshadowing the central tension of the field: random codes achieve capacity, but we want *explicit* codes with *efficient* decoding.

### 4.2 The Converse: Fano's Inequality and the Weak Converse

The converse shows that rates above $C$ are impossible with vanishing error. The key tool is **Fano's inequality**: for any estimator $\hat{W}$ of a uniform message $W$ from observation $Y^n$,

$$H(W|Y^n) \leq 1 + P_e \cdot nR,$$

where $P_e = \Pr[\hat{W} \ne W]$. Uncertainty about the message given the observation is controlled by the error probability.

The **weak converse** follows: $nR = H(W) = I(W;Y^n) + H(W|Y^n) \leq I(X^n;Y^n) + 1 + P_e nR \leq nC + 1 + P_e nR$, where the last step uses the memorylessness of the channel, $I(X^n;Y^n) \leq \sum_i I(X_i;Y_i) \leq nC$. Rearranging,

$$P_e \geq 1 - \frac{C}{R} - \frac{1}{nR},$$

so if $R > C$, the error probability is bounded away from zero — the weak converse. The **strong converse** sharpens this: for $R > C$, $P_e \to 1$, not merely bounded away from zero. Together, achievability and converse pin capacity down exactly: it is both the supremum of achievable rates and the infimum of rates forcing error.

### 4.3 Error Exponents: Gallager's $\rho$-Trick and the Reliability Function

Shannon's theorem tells us $P_e \to 0$ for $R < C$, but *how fast*? Define the **reliability function**

$$E(R) = \limsup_{n\to\infty} -\frac{1}{n}\log P_e^*(n, 2^{nR}),$$

the best achievable exponential decay rate. Determining $E(R)$ exactly remains one of information theory's great open problems; what we know are tight bounds in part of the range [6].

Gallager's breakthrough was to analyze the *maximum-likelihood* decoder rather than the suboptimal typicality decoder, using a deceptively simple union-bound refinement — the **$\rho$-trick** — which weakens $\Pr[\cup_j \mathcal{E}_j] \leq \min\{1, \sum_j \Pr[\mathcal{E}_j]\}$ to a $\rho$-th power version with $0 \le \rho \le 1$. Optimizing over $\rho$ yields the **random-coding exponent**

$$E_r(R) = \max_{0\le\rho\le 1}\max_{p(x)}\left[E_0(\rho, p) - \rho R\right],\quad E_0(\rho,p) = -\log\sum_y\left(\sum_x p(x)W(y|x)^{1/(1+\rho)}\right)^{1+\rho},$$

with the guarantee $P_e \leq 2^{-nE_r(R)}$ [6]. At low rates, $E_r(R)$ can be improved by **expurgation** — discarding the worst codewords from the random ensemble — yielding the expurgated exponent $E_x(R)$, which exceeds $E_r(R)$ below a threshold rate $R_x$.

From above, the **sphere-packing bound** of Shannon, Gallager, and Berlekamp gives $E(R) \le E_{sp}(R)$ by counting how many decoding regions can be packed around codewords. A **critical rate** $R_{\text{crit}} \in (0, C)$ exists where random-coding and sphere-packing exponents coincide, so $E(R)$ is *exactly* determined for $R \in (R_{\text{crit}}, C)$; below $R_{\text{crit}}$ even the existence of the exponent is unknown in general [6].

### 4.4 From Theory to Practice: List Decoding, Polar Codes, and LDPC Codes

Shannon's random codebook is a mathematical existence proof, not an engineering artifact. Three developments bridge the gap:

- **List decoding.** Instead of demanding a unique decoded message, the decoder may output a short *list* guaranteed to contain the transmitted message. List decoding strictly increases achievable rates against adversarial noise and underpins modern concatenated and folded Reed–Solomon constructions.
- **Polar codes.** In 2009, Erdal Arıkan introduced **channel polarization**: recursively combining and splitting $N = 2^n$ copies of a binary-input channel synthesizes $N$ bit-channels that polarize — a fraction $I(W)$ become nearly noiseless and the rest nearly useless [5]. Transmitting data only on the good channels achieves the symmetric capacity with encoding and decoding complexity $O(N\log N)$ under successive-cancellation decoding, with block error probability scaling as $2^{-N^\beta}$ for $\beta < 1/2$. Polar codes were the **first explicit, low-complexity, capacity-achieving** construction — adopted by 3GPP for the 5G NR control channels.
- **LDPC codes.** Robert Gallager's 1962 invention of low-density parity-check codes lay dormant for three decades until their rediscovery in the 1990s; decoded by iterative belief propagation on sparse factor graphs, irregular LDPC ensembles approach capacity within hundredths of a decibel and dominate modern standards (Wi-Fi, DVB-S2, 10GBase-T).

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 The Finite-Blocklength Regime: Polyanskiy–Poor–Verdú

Asymptotics ($n \to \infty$) say little about a 5G control packet of 32 bytes. Polyanskiy, Poor, and Verdú launched the systematic finite-blocklength program in 2010, deriving achievability bounds (random-coding union, dependence-testing) and a minimax converse that are tight even for $n$ below 200 [2]. Their signature result is the **normal approximation**: the maximum rate at blocklength $n$ and error $\epsilon$ satisfies

$$R^*(n,\epsilon) = C - \sqrt{\frac{V}{n}}\,Q^{-1}(\epsilon) + O\!\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right),$$

where $V$ is the **channel dispersion** — the variance of the information density — and $Q^{-1}$ is the inverse Gaussian tail function [2]. Dispersion is the second-order fundamental limit: capacity governs the mean, dispersion governs the penalty for finiteness. The following Python sketch computes the normal approximation for a binary symmetric channel:

```python
import math

def binary_entropy(p):
    return -p*math.log2(p) - (1-p)*math.log2(1-p)

def qinv(eps):
    # Acklam's approximation to the inverse normal CDF tail
    a = [-3.969683028665376e+01, 2.209460984245205e+02,
         -2.759285104469687e+02, 1.383577518672690e+02,
         -3.066479806614716e+01, 2.506628277459239e+00]
    b = [-5.447609879822406e+01, 1.615858368580409e+02,
         -1.556989798598866e+02, 6.680131188771972e+01,
         -1.328068155288572e+01]
    c = [-7.784894002430293e-03, -3.223964580411365e-01,
         -2.400758277161838e+00, -2.549732539343734e+00,
          4.374664141464968e+00, 2.938163982698783e+00]
    d = [7.784695709041462e-03, 3.224671290700398e-01,
         2.445134137142996e+00, 3.754408661907416e+00]
    p = eps if eps < 0.5 else 1.0 - eps
    if p < 0.02425:
        q = math.sqrt(-2.0*math.log(p))
        x = (((((c[0]*q+c[1])*q+c[2])*q+c[3])*q+c[4])*q+c[5]) / \
            ((((d[0]*q+d[1])*q+d[2])*q+d[3])*q+1.0)
    else:
        q = p - 0.5; r = q*q
        x = (((((a[0]*r+a[1])*r+a[2])*r+a[3])*r+a[4])*r+a[5])*q / \
            (((((b[0]*r+b[1])*r+b[2])*r+b[3])*r+b[4])*r+1.0)
    return x if eps < 0.5 else -x

def normal_approx_rate(p, n, eps):
    """R*(n, eps) for BSC(p) via PPV normal approximation."""
    C = 1.0 - binary_entropy(p)
    V = p*(1-p)*(math.log2((1-p)/p))**2   # dispersion, bits^2/use
    return C - math.sqrt(V/n)*qinv(eps)

for n in (128, 1024, 8192):
    print(n, round(normal_approx_rate(0.11, n, 1e-3), 4))
```

For a BSC with crossover $p = 0.11$ ($C \approx 0.5$), the approximation yields rates $0.5 - 3.09\sqrt{V/n}$: at $n = 128$ and $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$ the achievable rate drops to roughly $0.28$, quantifying the finite-blocklength penalty that asymptotics hide.

### 5.2 Feedback Does Not Increase Capacity — But Changes the Story

Shannon showed that for a DMC, **feedback does not increase capacity**: $C_{\text{FB}} = C$. Yet feedback transforms the engineering: with noiseless feedback, the error exponent can be dramatically improved (the Schalkwijk–Kailath scheme achieves doubly-exponential error decay on the AWGN channel), variable-length coding with stop-feedback attains the normal-approximation rate with far simpler schemes, and in the *zero-error* setting feedback genuinely increases capacity [7].

### 5.3 The Zero-Error Capacity

In 1956, Shannon introduced $C_0$, the **zero-error capacity**: the supremum of rates achievable with *exactly zero* probability of error [7]. The theory changes character from probabilistic to combinatorial. Two inputs are *confusable* if some output can arise from both; the confusability graph's vertices are input symbols with edges joining confusable pairs, and $C_0 = \lim_n \frac{1}{n}\log\alpha(G^{\boxtimes n})$, where $\alpha$ is the independence number and $\boxtimes$ the strong graph product. Shannon computed $C_0$ for the five-cycle noisy typewriter only as a lower bound ($\log\sqrt{5}$); the matching upper bound waited for Lovász's 1979 theta-function breakthrough. For cycles $C_7$ and beyond, the zero-error capacity remains open — a humbling reminder that demanding *zero* error is vastly harder than demanding *vanishing* error.

## 6 Limitations

The theory's power comes with clearly marked boundaries:

1. **Asymptotics vs. practice.** Capacity is a statement about $n \to \infty$; the PPV program quantifies the finite-$n$ gap, but code design at short blocklengths remains partly heuristic.
2. **The reliability function is incomplete.** The exact value of $E(R)$ below the critical rate is unknown for general DMCs — the oldest open problem in the field [6].
3. **Random codes are not explicit.** The achievability proof gives no construction; polar codes resolved the existence of explicit capacity-achieving codes only in 2009, and only for symmetric channels under successive cancellation.
4. **Memorylessness and synchrony.** The classical theory assumes i.i.d. channel uses with perfect synchronization; channels with memory, insertions/deletions, or adversarial states require substantially different machinery.
5. **Zero-error capacity is intractable in general.** Computing $C_0$ is combinatorial and, as shown in later work, even the computability of the quantity is delicate [7].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Shannon's noisy-channel coding theorem did more than solve a problem — it created a discipline and a method. The **random-coding argument** taught theorists to prove existence through probability; the **converse** via Fano's inequality taught them to certify impossibility; **Gallager's exponents** taught them to ask not just *whether* error vanishes but *how fast*; and **Arıkan's polarization** finally delivered the explicit construction Shannon's proof had only promised. The finite-blocklength program of Polyanskiy, Poor, and Verdú [2] and the combinatorial world of zero-error capacity [7] show the theory still expanding at both ends — toward engineering reality on one side and mathematical depth on the other.

The deepest lesson is philosophical: Shannon showed that noise is not an enemy to be eliminated but a parameter to be *coded around*. Randomness — the very phenomenon that corrupts the channel — became, in the form of random codebooks, the instrument of its defeat. That inversion, from noise as obstacle to randomness as method, remains the founding gesture of modern information theory.
