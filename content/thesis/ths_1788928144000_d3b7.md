# Statistical Watermarking of Large Language Model Text: Green-List Logit Biasing, Tournament Sampling, Detection Statistics, and Robustness under Paraphrase Attacks

## Abstract

As large language models (LLMs) generate text indistinguishable from human writing, watermarking has emerged as the principal technical countermeasure for provenance attribution. This thesis develops a unified treatment of **statistical watermarking schemes** — decoding-time interventions embedding a key-dependent pseudorandom signal into generated text without retraining. We analyze the **Kirchenbauer–Geiping–Wen (KGW) green-list logit-biasing scheme** [1] with its z-score detector and the quality–detectability trade-off in (δ, γ); the **SynthID-Text tournament-sampling construction** of Dathathri et al. [4], achieving near non-distortionary embedding via a keyed knockout tournament; the **cryptographic undetectability framework** of Christ, Gunn, and Zamir [8]; and the **robustness frontier** from reliability studies [2], spoofing and paraphrase attacks [3, 5], and forensic evaluations [7]. We derive detection statistics from first principles, prove concentration bounds, compare distortion properties, and taxonomize attacks — paraphrase, token substitution, translation round-trips, spoofing — with measured effects on true-positive rate. No single scheme dominates: KGW offers simplicity and strong detection, SynthID offers deployable non-distortionary quality, and robustness against determined paraphrasing remains open.

## 1. Introduction

The deployment of large language models capable of fluent, long-form generation has created a *provenance crisis*: text published online can no longer be presumed human-authored. Concerns span academic integrity, disinformation at scale, and the pollution of future training corpora [7]. Among proposed mitigations, **watermarking** — embedding a statistically detectable, human-imperceptible signal at generation time — is the only approach that attributes text to its generator *without* post-hoc classification, which is known to be fundamentally unreliable [5].

Statistical watermarking rests on a simple but powerful observation: autoregressive generation samples each token from a distribution; a party holding a secret key can *shape* that sampling so that the resulting sequence correlates with key-derived pseudorandom values. A detector holding the key can then re-score any candidate text and perform hypothesis testing. Two landmark constructions define the field:

1. **Logit-biasing (KGW).** At each step, the vocabulary is pseudorandomly split into a "green list" (fraction γ) and "red list" via a hash of preceding tokens; a bias δ added to green logits shifts sampling toward green tokens. Detection counts green tokens and computes a z-score [1].
2. **Tournament sampling (SynthID-Text).** Candidates drawn from the model's own distribution compete in a keyed knockout tournament over random watermarking functions *g₁, …, gₘ*. Because the key only *chooses among* model-proposed candidates, the scheme is (nearly) non-distortionary [4].

These constructions sit within a richer design space: distortion-free sampling [6], cryptographic undetectable watermarks [8], and multi-bit payload schemes. Deployment reality — Google's Gemini, Anthropic's Claude — has converged on tournament-style schemes, while KGW remains the workhorse of open research and the MarkLLM benchmark [7].

This thesis makes four contributions. First, a unified mathematical formulation of the green-list and tournament schemes. Second, a self-contained derivation of the detection statistics — the z-score, likelihood-ratio, and mean *g*-value tests — with concentration bounds quantifying detection power versus sequence length. Third, a comparative analysis of distortion, robustness, and security. Fourth, a systematized taxonomy of attacks and their measured effects on true-positive rate, drawing on reliability studies [2] and forensic evaluations [7].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Autoregressive generation

An autoregressive language model defines a distribution *pₗₘ(· | x₁…xₜ)* over a vocabulary *V* of size |*V*|. Generation proceeds by computing logits *lₜ ∈ ℝ^|V|* and sampling — typically via softmax with temperature, top-*k*, or top-*p* truncation — until a stopping condition. A watermarking scheme consists of three algorithms:

- **KeyGen(1^λ)** → secret key *sk*;
- **WatermarkGen(sk, prompt)** → text *x₁…x_T* generated with a keyed modification to decoding;
- **Detect(sk, x₁…x_T)** → *{0, 1}* indicating watermark presence.

The desiderata are: (i) **low distortion** — watermarked quality near unwatermarked; (ii) **low false-positive rate**; (iii) **high true-positive rate**; (iv) **robustness** to edits; (v) **security** — no forgery or removal without the key.

### 2.2 The KGW scheme

Kirchenbauer et al. [1] proposed the first practical decoding-time watermark. Given context window width *h*, at step *t* a pseudorandom function *f: ℕ^h → ℕ* seeded by the previous *h* tokens partitions *V* into green list *Gₜ* of size γ|*V*| and red list *Rₜ = V \ Gₜ*. Logits are then modified as

$$l_{tk} = \begin{cases} l_{tk} + \delta & \text{if } k \in G_t \ l_{tk} & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \qquad (1)$$

The bias δ shifts probability mass toward green tokens while leaving the relative order within each list unchanged. Detection replays the same hash computation over the candidate text, counts green tokens |*s*|*G*, and forms the z-score

$$z = \frac{|s|_G - \gamma T}{\sqrt{T\gamma(1-\gamma)}}. \qquad (2)$$

Under the null hypothesis (text generated independently of the key), |*s*|*G* is approximately Binomial(*T*, γ), so *z* is approximately standard normal; *z > 4* gives a one-sided false-positive rate of roughly 3 × 10⁻⁵ [1].

### 2.3 Distortion-free and undetectable watermarks

A watermark is **distortion-free** if, for any single query, the marginal distribution of the watermarked output equals the base model's. Kuditipudi et al. [6] achieve this by coupling generation to a keyed random-number sequence, detecting via correlation or Levenshtein-distance alignment for robustness. Christ, Gunn, and Zamir [8] introduced the stronger notion of **undetectability**: no polynomial-time adversary, even with adaptive oracle access to the watermarked model, can distinguish it from the unwatermarked model except with negligible advantage — defined via an IND-CPA-style game. Any scheme introducing distributional distortion is detectable in this sense, which places KGW outside the undetectable class while tournament sampling approaches it [4].

> **Theorem:** (Undetectability implies distortion-freeness.) If a watermarking scheme is undetectable in the sense of Christ et al., then for every prompt the watermarked output distribution is computationally indistinguishable from the base model's distribution. *Proof sketch:* an adversary querying a single prompt and applying a statistical test distinguishing the two distributions would otherwise constitute a distinguishing attack. ∎

### 2.4 SynthID-Text

Dathathri et al. [4], published in *Nature* and deployed in Google's Gemini, introduced **tournament sampling**. At each step: (1) a seed *rₜ = h(xₜ₋ₕ…xₜ₋₁, k)* is derived from the recent *H* = 4 tokens and a secret key; (2) *m* = 3 random watermarking functions *g₁…gₘ* map (token, seed) pairs to Bernoulli values; (3) 2^*m* = 8 candidates are sampled from *pₗₘ(· | x<t)*; (4) candidates compete pairwise in *m* knockout layers, layer ℓ winners selected by *gℓ*, until one token survives. Detection computes a **mean g-value score** and compares against a threshold. Because the key only selects among tokens the model itself proposed, the expected output distribution is preserved: the tournament is *non-distortionary* on average over keys [4]. An independent theoretical analysis [9] derives closed-form expressions for detection statistics and confirms the near non-distortionary property at depth *m*.

### 2.5 The unigram variant and the robustness–security tension

Zhao et al. [3, 5] observed that the KGW scheme with context width *h* = 0 — a **fixed, context-independent** green/red partition — achieves strong robustness to edit attacks, since changing a token cannot "ripple" through subsequent partitions. However, Sadasivan et al. [5] demonstrated a **spoofing attack**: with a fixed partition, an adversary can recover the green list via token-frequency analysis and forge watermarked text. This exposes a fundamental tension: context-dependent partitions (*h* ≥ 1) resist spoofing but are fragile to edits; context-independent partitions resist edits but leak the green list [5].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Formal setup

Fix vocabulary *V*, security parameter λ, and key *k*. For KGW, the green-list generator *G(xₜ₋ₕ…xₜ₋₁; k)* is defined via a PRF *f*; for SynthID, the seed generator *rₜ = h(xₜ₋ₕ…xₜ₋₁, k)* and tournament functions *gℓ(·; rₜ)*. Generation follows the base sampler with the keyed modification applied to logits (KGW) or candidate selection (SynthID).

### 3.2 Detection as hypothesis testing

Detection is a one-sided hypothesis test. The null *H₀*: the text is independent of *k*; the alternative *H₁*: the text was produced by WatermarkGen(*k*, ·). Three test statistics are in use:

| Scheme | Statistic | Null distribution | Decision rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| KGW | z-score (2) | ≈ N(0, 1) | *z > τ* (τ = 4 typical) |
| KGW variant | Likelihood ratio of green counts | χ² mixture | LR > τ_α |
| SynthID | Mean g-value score | centered at 0.5 under no watermark | score > τ (e.g., 0.5 with deadband ±0.03) |
| Christ et al. | Correlation with keyed sequence | standard normal | correlation > τ |
| Kuditipudi et al. | Levenshtein alignment score | permutation null | p-value < α |

For SynthID, the MarkLLM implementation uses a weighted-mean detector with three states — WATERMARKED, NOT_WATERMARKED, UNCERTAIN — rather than a binary threshold [7]. Detection needs only the key (and, for KGW, the tokenizer), not model weights, enabling third-party auditing [2].

### 3.3 Experimental protocol (reference design)

Following the reliability study [2] and the MarkLLM evaluation harness [7], a rigorous evaluation proceeds as:

1. **Generation.** Produce *N* = 200–500 completions (*T* ≈ 200–300 tokens) with and without watermarking on a fixed model (OPT-6.7B in [1]; Gemma in [4]).
2. **Quality.** Compute perplexity under a reference model and, ideally, human or LLM-judge fluency ratings.
3. **Detection.** Compute z-scores / g-value scores for watermarked, human, and attacked texts; sweep τ for ROC curves and AUC; report TPR at fixed FPR (1%, 0.1%).
4. **Attacks.** Apply paraphrase (DIPPER [5]), synonym substitution, token deletion/insertion, and translation round-trips; re-run detection.
5. **Sweeps.** Vary δ ∈ {1, 2, 5}, γ ∈ {0.25, 0.5}, *H*, and tournament depth *m* to map the trade-off surface.

```python
import math

def kgw_detect(token_ids, green_list_fn, gamma=0.25, threshold=4.0):
    green = sum(1 for t, tok in enumerate(token_ids)
                if tok in green_list_fn(token_ids[max(0, t-1):t]))
    T = len(token_ids)
    z = (green - gamma * T) / math.sqrt(T * gamma * (1 - gamma))
    return z > threshold, z
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The geometry of logit biasing: how δ shapes the green surplus

The bias δ enters the softmax multiplicatively: a green token's probability is scaled by *e^δ* relative to red tokens. Kirchenbauer et al. [1] analyze the expected z-score via the model's entropy: the green surplus per token is largest when the base distribution is *flat* (high entropy) — many plausible candidates for the bias to swing — and smallest when the model is nearly deterministic, where the argmax token dominates regardless of δ. The paper's spike-entropy measure *S* quantifies this. Empirically [1, 2], δ = 2 with γ = 0.25 yields mean z-scores of 8–15 on 200-token OPT-6.7B generations — far above τ = 4 — while increasing perplexity by only ~5–10%. Larger δ (e.g., 5) pushes z-scores above 20 but degrades fluency; the trade-off curve is convex, with diminishing detection returns past δ ≈ 2 [2].

### 4.2 Concentration of the detection statistic

> **Theorem:** (KGW detection concentration.) Under *H₁* with per-token green probability γ′ > γ, the z-score (2) satisfies *z* ≥ (γ′ − γ)√*T*/√(γ(1−γ)) − O(√log(1/β)) with probability ≥ 1 − β. Under *H₀*, *z* ≤ O(√log(1/β)) with probability ≥ 1 − β.

*Proof sketch.* Write |*s*|*G* = Σₜ Xₜ with Xₜ ∈ {0,1}; under *H₀*, E[Xₜ] = γ (up to negligible PRF bias); under *H₁*, E[Xₜ] = γ′. Hoeffding's inequality, P(|ΣXₜ − Tμ| ≥ εT) ≤ 2exp(−2ε²T), yields the bounds after rearranging. The √*T* signal growth under *H₁* versus bounded noise under *H₀* is why detection becomes near-certain for *T* ≳ 100–200 tokens [1]. ∎

A subtlety: the Xₜ are *not* independent, because the green list at position *t* depends on preceding tokens, which are themselves watermark-influenced. Kirchenbauer et al. note the dependence is weak — the hash decorrelates successive partitions — and empirical null distributions on human text match N(0,1) closely [1, 2]. The likelihood-ratio variant, modeling the exact binomial tail rather than the normal approximation, tightens power for short texts (*T* < 50) [2].

### 4.3 Tournament sampling: non-distortionary embedding

The key theoretical result of SynthID-Text [4, 9] is that tournament sampling preserves the base distribution in expectation over the key. For *m* = 1 (single layer, *N* = 2 candidates *a, b* drawn i.i.d. from *pₗₘ*), the winner is *a* iff *g₁(a, r)* ≥ *g₁(b, r)* (ties broken randomly). Since the *g₁* values are i.i.d. fair coins independent of the tokens, P(*a* wins) = P(*b* wins) = 1/2 *conditional on the candidate pair*; the emitted token's distribution thus equals *pₗₘ* exactly — the tournament is a fair coin the key cannot bias. For *m* > 1, each layer's selection is conditionally fair given the survivors, so by induction the full tournament with *N* = 2 is exactly non-distortionary [9] (with *N* > 2, distortion is measured below 0.3% [9]).

Detection exploits the *conditional* structure: given the key, the winner of each match is the candidate with the higher *g*-value, so the emitted token correlates with *gℓ* under the true seed and the mean g-value exceeds 0.5. Under *H₀*, tokens are independent of the keyed *g*-functions, so the score concentrates at 0.5. Dathathri et al. report detection AUC > 0.99 on Gemini outputs with no measurable quality degradation, and better paraphrase robustness than KGW at matched quality [4].

### 4.5 Quality–detectability Pareto analysis

The three schemes occupy different points on the quality–detectability–robustness Pareto frontier:

| Property | KGW (*h*=1, δ=2) | KGW unigram (*h*=0) | SynthID-Text |
|---|---|---|---|
| Text quality (Δ perplexity) | +5–10% | +5–10% | ≈ 0% (non-distortionary) |
| Detection (TPR @ 1% FPR, *T*=200) | > 95% | > 95% | > 99% (AUC) |
| Paraphrase robustness | weak | strong | moderate |
| Spoofing resistance | strong | **weak** | strong |
| Public verifiability | yes | yes | yes (key only) |
| Cryptographic undetectability | no | no | approximately |

No scheme dominates: KGW is simplest and well-studied; the unigram variant trades security for robustness; SynthID achieves the best quality–detection balance and is the only one deployed at planetary scale — but independent forensic evaluation cautions that published robustness claims depend on configuration details not fully disclosed in production [7].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Detection power versus text length

Across studies [1, 2, 7], the empirical detection curve follows the √*T* law of §4.2 closely. Representative figures for KGW (OPT-6.7B, δ = 2, γ = 0.25, *T* = 200): mean z ≈ 10.5 on watermarked text versus ≈ 0 on human text; ROC AUC ≈ 0.999; TPR = 98.4% at FPR = 10⁻³. For *T* = 50, TPR at the same FPR falls to ~72%, confirming that short texts are the hard regime — motivating likelihood-ratio and higher-criticism detectors [2]. SynthID-Text on Gemma completions (*T* = 200) achieves TPR ≈ 99%+ at FPR = 1% with the mean g-value detector [4].

### 5.2 The δ–γ trade-off surface

Sweeping (δ, γ) reproduces the paper's trade-off figures [1] and independent replications [2]: z-score increases monotonically in δ and decreases in γ — smaller green lists concentrate the signal — while perplexity increases in δ and is roughly flat in γ. The knee of the curve — δ = 2, γ = 0.25 — is the community-standard operating point. A *hard* watermark variant (δ → ∞, always sampling the argmax green token) maximizes detection but visibly degrades text; the *soft* watermark (finite δ) is the practical choice [1].

### 5.3 Attack effectiveness (measured)

| Attack | KGW *h*=1 TPR loss | Unigram TPR loss | SynthID TPR loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| DIPPER paraphrase (lex=60) | −75 pp | −15 pp | −40 pp |
| Synonym substitution (20%) | −30 pp | −8 pp | −18 pp |
| Random deletion (10%) | −25 pp | −6 pp | −12 pp |
| Translation round-trip (EN→FR→EN) | −85 pp | −60 pp | −70 pp |
| Spoofing (forgery) | resistant | **vulnerable** | resistant |

Values are representative magnitudes from [2, 5, 7] (pp = percentage points; baseline TPR ≈ 95% at 1% FPR). The forensic-readiness evaluation [7] further notes that undisclosed production configurations make independent verification of vendor claims difficult, and that none of the tested configurations meets forensic evidentiary standards.

### 5.4 Security proofs: what is and isn't guaranteed

Kirchenbauer et al. [1] prove *detection soundness* under the PRF assumption: without the key, no efficient adversary produces text scoring above threshold except with negligible probability — *for text the adversary writes from scratch*. This does *not* cover removal attacks (the adversary starts from a watermarked sample) or spoofing, analyzed separately [5]. Christ et al. [8] prove undetectability assuming one-way functions, but their construction is not robust — a single paraphrase destroys it. Zhao et al. [3] prove the unigram scheme resists any edit-bounded adversary — at the price of the spoofing vulnerability found by Sadasivan et al. [5]. The field thus faces a **robustness–security–quality trilemma**, with each scheme sacrificing at least one vertex.

## 6. Limitations

First, **short and low-entropy text** fundamentally limits all statistical watermarks: with *T* < 30 tokens or near-deterministic generation (factual QA, code), the signal cannot reach significance without destroying quality [1, 4]. Adaptive schemes that skip low-entropy positions [6] help but reduce effective *T* further.

Second, **the paraphrase gap** remains: any watermark tied to surface tokens is removable by a sufficiently strong paraphraser, and semantic-level watermarks are nascent [5, 7]. As paraphrasers improve with model scale, deployed watermark robustness may *decrease* over time — an unusual security dynamic.

Third, **evaluation realism**: most published numbers use open models (OPT, Llama, Gemma) with known configurations; production systems (Gemini, Claude) disclose neither keys, thresholds, nor scoring details, so independent forensic validation is limited [7].

Fourth, **adversarial adaptation**: public detectors enable *oracle attacks* — an adversary queries the detector to hill-climb toward unwatermarked text. Private detection (key held by the provider) mitigates this but concentrates trust. Fifth, **equity and false accusations**: even a 10⁻⁵ FPR implies thousands of false accusations at web scale; watermark evidence alone is insufficient for high-stakes attribution [7].

## 7. Conclusion

Statistical watermarking has matured from a research curiosity into deployed infrastructure. The KGW green-list scheme [1] established that decoding-time logit biasing yields strong, publicly verifiable detection at modest quality cost, grounded in classical hypothesis testing. SynthID-Text [4] showed that tournament sampling embeds the signal *without* distributional distortion, enabling production deployment. Cryptographic formalizations [8] clarified the limits — undetectability, distortion-freeness, and robustness cannot all be maximized at once — and attack research [2, 5, 7] mapped the frontier: context-dependent hashes resist spoofing but fall to paraphrase; fixed partitions resist paraphrase but leak to spoofing; translation round-trips defeat everything.

The open problems are clear: (i) semantic-level watermarks robust to strong paraphrase; (ii) schemes simultaneously unforgeable and robust; (iii) standardized, independently auditable evaluation bridging open research and closed production systems. Until these are solved, watermarks should be treated as *one signal among many* in provenance attribution — powerful at scale, insufficient alone for forensic certainty.
