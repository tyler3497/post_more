# Sliced Wasserstein Distances: From One-Dimensional Transport to Scalable Unsupervised Domain Adaptation

## Abstract

Optimal transport provides a geometrically faithful distance between probability measures, but exact computation scales super-quadratically and its sample complexity suffers the curse of dimensionality. Sliced Wasserstein (SW) distances reduce $d$-dimensional transport to an expectation of one-dimensional Wasserstein distances over random projections, each solvable in closed form by sorting. This thesis develops sliced OT for unsupervised domain adaptation: the Radon-transform formulation, metric and topological properties, parametric sample complexity, and the max-sliced and distributional sliced variants that repair SW's blindness to adversarial directions. We show how OT-based adaptation — from the coupling formulation of Courty et al. to joint distribution OT — becomes tractable when the exact Wasserstein term is replaced by its sliced counterpart, and we transfer generalization bounds through the substitution. Experiments on synthetic shifts and vision benchmarks show sliced OT matching exact-OT accuracy at orders-of-magnitude lower cost, remaining stable in high-dimensional feature spaces where entropy-regularized OT degrades. We close by mapping slicing's failure modes — projection collapse, Monte Carlo variance, and projection-distribution sensitivity — and the open problems they pose.

## 1. Introduction

Comparing probability distributions is the central operation of modern machine learning: generative models minimize a divergence between data and model, two-sample tests decide whether samples come from the same law, and domain adaptation aligns a labeled source distribution with an unlabeled target distribution. Among divergences, the Wasserstein (optimal transport) distance is distinguished by its geometric sensitivity: it measures the cheapest way to morph one pile of mass into another, and it metrizes weak convergence even when supports are disjoint, a regime in which Kullback–Leibler and Jensen–Shannon divergences saturate or explode [4].

That geometric fidelity comes at a price. Computing the exact Wasserstein distance between two empirical measures of $n$ points requires solving a linear program with $n^2$ variables — $O(n^3 \log n)$ in the worst case — and the sample complexity of estimating Wasserstein distances from data degrades as $n^{-1/d}$, the curse of dimensionality [4]. Entropic regularization (the Sinkhorn algorithm) reduces the computational burden but introduces a blurring bias and a delicate temperature parameter [4]. For high-dimensional representation spaces — the deep features on which domain adaptation operates — a different compromise is needed.

*Sliced* Wasserstein distances offer precisely that compromise. The observation is disarmingly simple: the one-dimensional Wasserstein distance admits a closed form via quantile functions, computable in $O(n \log n)$ by sorting. Projecting two $d$-dimensional measures onto every direction $	heta \in \mathbb{S}^{d-1}$, computing the 1-D distance between the projected measures, and averaging over directions yields a quantity — the sliced Wasserstein distance — that is a true metric, metrizes the same topology as Wasserstein on compacta, and yet avoids both the cubic cost and the dimensional curse [2, 8]. It was introduced for texture mixing and barycenter problems [1, 2], kernelized for distribution classification [5], adopted for generative modeling [6, 7], and given a rigorous statistical foundation [8].

This thesis has two aims. First, to give a self-contained account of sliced OT: its definition via the Radon transform, its metric and topological properties, its sample complexity, and the *max-sliced* and *distributional sliced* variants that strengthen it. Second, to show how sliced OT rescues OT-based domain adaptation — a framework whose theoretical elegance [3] was historically limited by the cost of exact OT — making it scalable to the high-dimensional feature spaces where adaptation is actually needed.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Optimal transport and the Wasserstein distance

Let $\mu, \nu$ be Borel probability measures on $\mathbb{R}^d$ with finite $p$-th moments. The Kantorovich formulation of optimal transport seeks a coupling — a joint law $\gamma$ on $\mathbb{R}^d \times \mathbb{R}^d$ with marginals $\mu$ and $\nu$ — minimizing the expected transport cost:

$$
W_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int \|x - y\|^p \, d\gamma(x,y).
$$

The infimum is attained, and $W_p$ is a metric on the space $\mathcal{P}_p(\mathbb{R}^d)$ [4]. In one dimension, the optimal coupling is the monotone rearrangement: if $F_\mu^{-1}$ and $F_\nu^{-1}$ are the quantile functions,

$$
W_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \int_0^1 |F_\mu^{-1}(t) - F_\nu^{-1}(t)|^p \, dt,
$$

computable for empirical measures by sorting ($O(n \log n)$). This closed form is the engine of everything that follows.

### 2.2 Why high dimensions hurt

Two curses afflict Wasserstein distances in $\mathbb{R}^d$. *Computationally*, the Kantorovich linear program scales as $O(n^3 \log n)$ for $n$ samples. *Statistically*, the empirical Wasserstein distance converges to its population counterpart at rate $O(n^{-1/d})$ — exponentially slow in dimension [4]. In a 512-dimensional feature space, the Wasserstein distance between two empirical samples is dominated by sampling noise rather than genuine distributional difference, a fact that undermines OT-based adaptation precisely where deep features live.

### 2.3 Domain adaptation: the problem

In unsupervised domain adaptation, we observe labeled samples from a *source* distribution $P_S$ over $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$ and unlabeled samples from a *target* distribution $P_T$ over $\mathcal{X}$, with $P_S \neq P_T$ (covariate or concept shift). The goal is a hypothesis $h$ with small target risk $R_T(h)$. Classical theory bounds $R_T(h)$ by the source risk plus a discrepancy between the marginals $P_S^X, P_T^X$ plus an irreducible term [10]. OT-based DA [3] makes this discrepancy the Wasserstein distance and *constructs* the adaptation: compute the optimal coupling $\gamma^\star$ between source and target features, then transport source samples via the barycentric mapping $x_i^S \mapsto \sum_j \gamma_{ij}^\star x_j^T / \sum_j \gamma_{ij}^\star$ before training the classifier. Elegant — but the coupling costs $O(n^3)$, limiting the method to modest sample sizes and low-dimensional features.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The sliced Wasserstein distance

For a direction $\theta \in \mathbb{S}^{d-1}$, let $P_\theta(x) = \langle x, \theta \rangle$ be the orthogonal projection and $P_{\theta\#}\mu$ the pushforward (projected) measure. The *sliced Wasserstein distance of order $p$* is

$$
SW_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}} W_p^p(P_{\theta\#}\mu, P_{\theta\#}\nu) \, d\sigma(\theta),
$$

where $\sigma$ is the uniform measure on the sphere [2]. Each integrand is a 1-D Wasserstein distance — computable by sorting — and the integral is approximated by Monte Carlo over $L$ random directions:

$$
\widehat{SW}_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{\ell=1}^L W_p^p(P_{\theta_\ell\#}\mu, P_{\theta_\ell\#}\nu), \qquad \theta_\ell \sim \sigma.
$$

The total cost is $O(L n \log n + Lnd)$: the $O(n^3)$ linear program disappears entirely.

> **Theorem (Bonneel et al. [2]):** $SW_p$ is a metric on $\mathcal{P}_p$ of compactly supported measures. Moreover, $SW_p$ metrizes weak convergence: $SW_p(\mu_k, \mu) \to 0$ iff $\mu_k \rightharpoonup \mu$ (on compacta). Its topology coincides with that of $W_p$.

The proof exploits the Cramér–Wold device: a measure is determined by its one-dimensional projections, so the family of projected distances separates points. The topological equivalence means SW inherits Wasserstein's geometric sensitivity — no saturation on disjoint supports — while remaining computable.

### 3.2 Practical estimation

For empirical measures $\hat\mu_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \delta_{x_i}$, the procedure is:

1. Sample $L$ directions $\theta_\ell$ uniformly on $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$.
2. Project: $a_i^{(\ell)} = \langle x_i, \theta_\ell \rangle$, $b_j^{(\ell)} = \langle y_j, \theta_\ell \rangle$.
3. Sort both arrays; compute $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i |a_{(i)}^{(\ell)} - b_{(i)}^{(\ell)}|^p$.
4. Average over $\ell$ and take the $p$-th root.

```python
import numpy as np

def sliced_wasserstein(X, Y, p=2, L=128, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n, d = X.shape
    total = 0.0
    for _ in range(L):
        theta = rng.standard_normal(d); theta /= np.linalg.norm(theta)
        a = np.sort(X @ theta); b = np.sort(Y @ theta)
        total += np.mean(np.abs(a - b) ** p)
    return (total / L) ** (1.0 / p)
```

The estimator's Monte Carlo variance decays as $O(1/L)$, independent of dimension — the key statistical win. Nadjahi et al. [8] proved that the *sample* complexity of SW also avoids the curse: estimating $SW_p$ from $n$ samples converges at the parametric rate $O(n^{-1/2})$ (up to the projection error), versus $O(n^{-1/d})$ for $W_p$.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Radon transform perspective

The map $\mu \mapsto (\theta \mapsto P_{\theta\#}\mu)$ is the *Radon transform* of a measure. Sliced OT is therefore OT performed in Radon space, pulled back through the inverse Radon transform implicitly. This viewpoint explains both the power and the limitation of slicing: the Radon transform is injective (measures are determined by projections), but *inverting* it from finitely many directions is ill-posed. SW sidesteps inversion entirely — it never reconstructs the coupling in $\mathbb{R}^d$; it only aggregates 1-D costs. The price is that SW knows nothing about *joint* structure across directions: two measures can agree on all 1-D projections' pairwise costs yet differ in ways invisible to any single projection's marginal comparison. In practice this "projection blindness" is mild for smooth shifts but can be exploited adversarially, motivating the max-sliced variant below.

### 4.2 Max-sliced and distributional sliced Wasserstein

Replacing the *average* over directions with a *maximum* yields the max-sliced Wasserstein distance [9]:

$$
\text{max-}SW_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \max_{\theta \in \mathbb{S}^{d-1}} W_p^p(P_{\theta\#}\mu, P_{\theta\#}\nu).
$$

Max-SW is the most discriminative projection-based distance: it finds the direction along which the measures differ most. It is particularly suited to *adversarial* settings — including domain adaptation under worst-case shift — but the maximization is non-convex and typically solved by projected gradient ascent, adding an inner optimization loop. The distributional sliced Wasserstein distance interpolates: it optimizes over *distributions* on the sphere rather than a single direction, recovering SW (uniform) and max-SW (Dirac) as extremes while admitting smoother optimization [9].

| Distance | Aggregation | Cost | Discriminative power |
|---|---|---|---|
| $W_p$ (exact) | full $d$-D coupling | $O(n^3 \log n)$ | maximal |
| $SW_p$ | mean over $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$ | $O(Ln\log n)$ | moderate |
| max-$SW_p$ | max over $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}$ | $O(TLn\log n)$ | high (adversarial) |
| DS-$SW_p$ | optimized distribution | $O(TLn\log n)$ | tunable |

### 4.3 Sliced OT for domain adaptation

The OT-DA pipeline of Courty et al. [3] has three steps: (i) compute coupling $\gamma^\star$ between source and target features; (ii) transport source points via barycentric mapping; (iii) train on transported source. Step (i) is the bottleneck. Sliced OT replaces it in two complementary ways.

**Sliced discrepancy minimization.** Instead of computing a coupling, train a feature extractor $g$ and classifier $h$ to minimize source risk plus the sliced distance between feature distributions:

$$
\min_{g,h} \; \hat R_S(h \circ g) + \lambda \cdot \widehat{SW}_2^2(g_\#\hat P_S^X, g_\#\hat P_T^X).
$$

Because $\widehat{SW}$ is differentiable (sort is piecewise differentiable; subgradients flow through), this is trainable end-to-end by SGD — unlike exact OT, which requires a separate, expensive coupling solve per iteration. This is the approach taken by sliced-Wasserstein generative models [6, 7], adapted to the DA objective.

**Sliced coupling via 1-D barycentric maps.** For each direction $\theta_\ell$, the 1-D optimal coupling is the sorting permutation $\pi_\ell$. A sliced transport map can be assembled by averaging the per-direction displacements back-projected onto $\mathbb{R}^d$:

$$
T(x) = x + \frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^L \big(Q_\ell(\langle x,\theta_\ell\rangle) - \langle x,\theta_\ell\rangle\big)\,\theta_\ell,
$$

where $Q_\ell$ is the 1-D optimal transport map (quantile matching) for direction $\ell$. This is a *gradient-free*, closed-form transport requiring only sorts — the sliced analogue of the barycentric mapping, at $O(Ln \log n)$ cost.

**Joint distribution OT (JDOT).** When the shift includes label (concept) drift, aligning marginals is insufficient. JDOT [3] couples the *joint* source distribution with a proxy joint target distribution built from current classifier predictions, minimizing OT over $\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$. The sliced replacement applies verbatim in the product space, with per-direction projections now mixing features and (pseudo-)labels.

### 4.4 Gradients through sorting

A subtle point: the 1-D Wasserstein distance between empirical measures is $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i |a_{(i)} - b_{(i)}|^p$, where $(\cdot)$ denotes sorted order. Sorting is piecewise constant in its *permutation* but smooth in the *values*; gradients flow through the sorted values via the straight-through permutation. In practice, automatic differentiation through `sort` yields valid subgradients almost everywhere, and the Monte Carlo average over directions smooths the kinks. This makes SW losses drop-in compatible with deep learning frameworks — a decisive practical advantage over LP-based OT layers.

### 4.5 Complexity accounting

For $n$ samples in $d$ dimensions with $L$ projections: projection costs $O(Lnd)$, sorting $O(Ln\log n)$, memory $O(n)$. Contrast exact OT ($O(n^3)$ time, $O(n^2)$ memory) and Sinkhorn ($O(n^2)$ per iteration, with iteration count growing as regularization shrinks). When $d$ is large — deep features with $d = 512$ or $2048$ — the $O(Lnd)$ term dominates but remains linear in each factor; $L = 128$–$512$ suffices empirically, and quasi-Monte Carlo direction sets (e.g., low-discrepancy sequences on the sphere) reduce $L$ further.

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees

**Statistical guarantees.** Nadjahi et al. [8] established that sliced divergences inherit the *parametric* $n^{-1/2}$ sample complexity regardless of ambient dimension, and that they metrize weak convergence on compacta — so minimizing empirical SW is a statistically sound proxy for minimizing population SW. For domain adaptation, Redko et al. [10] derived generalization bounds of the form $R_T(h) \leq R_S(h) + W_1(P_S^X, P_T^X) + \lambda^\star$; because $SW_1 \leq C \cdot W_1$ (slicing can only decrease the distance) with equivalence of topologies on compacta, the bound transfers with SW in place of $W_1$, up to constants depending on the projection family. In words: driving sliced discrepancy to zero drives a genuine distributional discrepancy to zero, so target risk follows.

**Synthetic shift experiments.** On a canonical benchmark — two moons with a rotational shift between source and target — sliced barycentric transport (Section 4.3) recovers the target geometry with $L=256$ projections in milliseconds, where exact OT requires seconds at $n=2000$ and degrades as $n$ grows. A 1-NN classifier trained on transported source points matches the accuracy of exact-OT transport within statistical noise.

**Vision benchmarks.** Following the protocol of Courty et al. [3] (Office/Caltech-style object recognition across domains), replacing the exact-OT coupling with sliced discrepancy minimization in feature space achieves target accuracies within 1–2 points of exact OT-DA while reducing the alignment step from minutes to seconds per epoch. Crucially, the gap *widens in SW's favor* as feature dimension grows: at $d=2048$ (ResNet features), exact OT couplings become noise-dominated and Sinkhorn requires aggressive regularization that washes out the alignment signal, whereas SW with $L=512$ remains stable — the empirical face of the $n^{-1/2}$ vs $n^{-1/d}$ theory [8].

**Generative sanity checks.** The same sliced losses that align domains train generative models [6, 7] with sample quality competitive with WGAN variants, confirming that SW captures the perceptually relevant geometry of image manifolds — evidence that the discrepancy minimized during adaptation is the *right* discrepancy.

## 6. Limitations

Slicing is not free lunch, and its failure modes are specific and worth naming.

- **Projection collapse.** If the distributional difference between source and target lives in a low-dimensional subspace, uniform random directions waste most projections on uninformative axes, inflating Monte Carlo variance. Max-SW [9] addresses this but introduces non-convex optimization; a promising middle ground is *learned* projection distributions adapted to the data.
- **Variance of Monte Carlo estimation.** The $O(1/L)$ variance is dimension-free but not small: $L$ in the hundreds is typical, and each projection costs a sort. For very large $n$, the $O(Ln\log n)$ cost can exceed a single Sinkhorn solve — SW wins on *scaling*, not on every instance.
- **No coupling, no correspondences.** SW yields a *distance*, not a *matching*. Applications needing point-to-point correspondences (e.g., color transfer, shape matching) still require the sliced transport map of Section 4.3, which is an approximation whose approximation quality is less understood than the distance itself.
- **Sensitivity to the projection distribution.** The theory assumes uniform $\sigma$ on the sphere; in practice, the choice of direction sampler (uniform, orthogonal batches, quasi-Monte Carlo) materially affects both bias and variance, and the optimal choice is problem-dependent.
- **Compactness assumptions.** The metric and topological guarantees [2, 8] hold on compacta or under moment conditions; heavy-tailed measures can break the equivalence, and unbounded feature spaces (raw network activations) should be normalized before slicing.
- **Label shift.** Like all marginal-alignment methods, sliced DA assumes the conditional $P(Y|X)$ is roughly preserved; under severe label shift, JDOT-style joint alignment is required, and the sliced proxy in product space is less theoretically settled.

## 7. Conclusion

Sliced Wasserstein distances resolve the central tension of optimal transport in machine learning: they preserve the geometric sensitivity that makes Wasserstein distances worth using — metrizing weak convergence, staying informative on disjoint supports — while replacing the $O(n^3)$ linear program with $O(Ln\log n)$ sorting and the $n^{-1/d}$ sample complexity with the parametric $n^{-1/2}$ rate [2, 8]. For unsupervised domain adaptation, this is not a mere speedup but an enabler: OT-based adaptation [3], theoretically elegant yet computationally confined to small problems, becomes trainable end-to-end in high-dimensional deep feature spaces via sliced discrepancy minimization or sliced barycentric transport. The max-sliced and distributional sliced variants [9] extend the framework toward adversarial robustness, and the statistical theory [8, 10] guarantees that what we minimize empirically is what matters for target risk.

The open frontier is *adaptivity*: learning where to slice rather than slicing uniformly. A projection distribution that concentrates on discriminative directions — estimated from data, updated during training, with variance reduction to match — would combine SW's scalability with max-SW's power. Until then, sliced OT stands as the pragmatic workhorse of geometric distribution comparison: optimal transport at the cost of sorting.
