# Message-Passing Graph Neural Networks: the Weisfeiler–Lehman Hierarchy, Expressive-Power Limits, and Geometric Priors

## Abstract

Message-passing graph neural networks compute node representations by iteratively aggregating neighbor features, but their expressive power is bounded: they cannot distinguish graphs that the 1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Lehman isomorphism test cannot. This thesis develops the theory of GNN expressiveness from first principles: the WL hierarchy and its correspondence to fragments of first-order logic with counting, the Xu et al. characterization of maximally powerful message-passing via injective aggregation (Graph Isomorphism Networks), higher-order k-WL and k-GNNs with their computational costs, and geometric deep learning's answer—equivariant architectures with symmetry priors for molecules, point clouds, and physical systems. Empirical results cover the ZINC, OGB, and QM9 benchmarks. Limitations include oversmoothing, oversquashing, the WL ceiling on real tasks, and the data hunger of equivariant models. We conclude with subgraph GNNs and the emerging theory of graph transformers.

## 1 Introduction

Graphs are the natural language of relational data: molecules are graphs of atoms and bonds, social networks are graphs of people and ties, programs are graphs of instructions and dependencies. **Graph neural networks (GNNs)** learn representations of such data by a simple, powerful idea—*message passing*: each node iteratively updates its state by aggregating messages from its neighbors [1][2].

A message-passing layer computes

$$
h_v^{(k+1)} = \phi^{(k)}\!\left(h_v^{(k)},\; \bigoplus_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \psi^{(k)}(h_v^{(k)}, h_u^{(k)}, e_{vu})\right),
$$

where $\bigoplus$ is a permutation-invariant aggregator (sum, mean, max), and $\phi, \psi$ are learnable functions. After $K$ layers, $h_v^{(K)}$ summarizes the $K$-hop neighborhood of $v$; a readout over all nodes yields graph-level predictions.

But a fundamental question looms: *what can message passing actually compute?* Two non-isomorphic graphs may induce identical computation trees at every node, making them indistinguishable to any MPNN. The classical yardstick for this limitation is the **Weisfeiler–Lehman (WL) test** for graph isomorphism—and the resulting theory is one of the cleanest expressiveness characterizations in deep learning [3].

> Theorem: (Xu et al., 2019) A message-passing GNN is at most as powerful as the 1-WL test at distinguishing graphs, and it achieves 1-WL power if and only if its aggregation and update functions are injective [3].

This thesis develops that theory and its extensions: §2 introduces the WL hierarchy; §3 characterizes maximally expressive MPNNs; §4 dives into higher-order methods, geometric priors, and failure modes; §5 surveys benchmarks; §6 discusses limitations; §7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Weisfeiler–Lehman test

The 1-dimensional WL test (color refinement) iteratively colors nodes: start with uniform colors $c_v^{(0)}$; then

$$
c_v^{(k+1)} = \mathrm{hash}\!\left(c_v^{(k)},\; \{\!\{ c_u^{(k)} : u \in \mathcal{N}(v) \}\!\}\right),
$$

where $\{\!\{\cdot\}\!\}$ denotes a multiset. The process stabilizes after at most $n$ rounds; two graphs with different color histograms are non-isomorphic. The test is sound but incomplete: it famously fails on strongly regular graphs (e.g., it cannot distinguish the 4×4 rook's graph from the Shrikhande graph) [4].

The **$k$-WL hierarchy** generalizes to $k$-tuples of nodes, with $(k+1)$-WL strictly stronger than $k$-WL (Cai–Fürer–Immerman graphs separate every level) [4]. Each level corresponds to a fragment of logic: $k$-WL indistinguishability coincides with equivalence in the $(k+1)$-variable fragment of first-order logic *with counting quantifiers* ($C^{k+1}$)—a deep bridge between combinatorics, logic, and learning [5].

### 2.2 Message passing cannot beat 1-WL

Unrolling message passing around a node $v$ produces a **computation tree**: the rooted tree of $K$-hop walks from $v$, labeled by node features. If 1-WL assigns the same color to $v$ in graphs $G_1, G_2$, their computation trees are isomorphic, so any MPNN—whose output is a function of this tree—produces identical representations. Hence MPNNs $\le$ 1-WL in distinguishing power, regardless of depth, width, or aggregator choice [3].

This is not a minor curiosity: many molecular properties depend on substructures (rings, cliques) that 1-WL cannot count. A GNN that cannot tell whether a carbon ring has 5 or 6 members is a poor chemist.

| Model | Power | Cost per layer |
|---|---|---|
| MPNN (GCN, GraphSAGE) | $\le$ 1-WL | $O(n + m)$ |
| GIN (injective) | $=$ 1-WL | $O(n + m)$ |
| $k$-GNN / $k$-WL GNN | $=$ $k$-WL | $O(n^k)$ |
| Subgraph GNN (DS-GNN) | strictly $>$ 1-WL | $O(n(n+m))$ |
| Graph transformer | $>$ 1-WL (with PEs) | $O(n^2)$ |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Injective aggregation: the Graph Isomorphism Network

Xu et al. asked what it takes to *reach* the 1-WL ceiling. The WL hash is injective on (color, multiset-of-colors) pairs; message passing matches it iff aggregation over multisets is injective. Since multisets of vectors from a countable set can be injectively encoded by **sum** followed by an MLP (sums of one-hot-like encodings are unique; an MLP then approximates any injective function by the universal approximation theorem), the **Graph Isomorphism Network**

$$
h_v^{(k+1)} = \mathrm{MLP}^{(k)}\!\left((1 + \epsilon^{(k)}) h_v^{(k)} + \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} h_u^{(k)}\right)
$$

is maximally expressive among MPNNs—provably as powerful as 1-WL [3]. Mean and max aggregators fail: mean cannot distinguish multisets with the same distribution but different multiplicities; max collapses multisets to sets. The lesson generalizes: *expressiveness is about injectivity on multisets*, a clean design principle.

```python
# Schematic: GIN layer (maximally expressive MPNN)
import torch.nn as nn
class GINLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.mlp = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(dim, dim), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(dim, dim))
        self.eps = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1))
    def forward(self, h, edge_index):
        # sum aggregation: injective on multisets (countable features)
        agg = torch.zeros_like(h).index_add_(0, edge_index[1], h[edge_index[0]])
        return self.mlp((1 + self.eps) * h + agg)
```

### 3.2 Climbing the hierarchy: higher-order GNNs

To exceed 1-WL, architectures must break the computation-tree bottleneck:

- **$k$-GNNs** operate on $k$-tuples of nodes, mirroring $k$-WL, at $O(n^k)$ cost—prohibitive beyond $k = 2, 3$ [6].
- **Subgraph GNNs** (e.g., DS-GNN, ID-GNN) run an MPNN on each node's rooted subgraph or with identity-aware features; they provably exceed 1-WL (they can count cycles) at $O(n)$ MPNN runs [7].
- **Graph transformers** with positional encodings (Laplacian eigenvectors, random-walk features) use global attention; with sufficiently rich PEs they exceed 1-WL, though the theory of *which* PEs suffice is still developing [8].

### 3.3 Geometric deep learning: symmetry as prior

For 3D data—molecules, point clouds, physical simulations—the relevant structure is not just graph topology but **Euclidean symmetry**. Geometric GNNs build in $E(3)$-equivariance: if the input point cloud is rotated, the output rotates identically. Architectures like SchNet (continuous-filter convolutions), DimeNet (directional message passing with spherical Bessel features), and equivariant transformers (SE(3)-Transformers, NequIP) use spherical harmonics and Clebsch–Gordan tensor products to guarantee equivariance by construction [9].

The payoff is dramatic data efficiency: equivariant models reach chemical accuracy on QM9 with orders of magnitude fewer samples, because symmetry reduces the hypothesis space. This is the **geometric deep learning blueprint**: identify the symmetry group of the domain, and constrain architectures to respect it [10].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Oversmoothing and oversquashing

Two pathologies plague deep GNNs. **Oversmoothing**: repeated aggregation acts as low-pass filtering; as depth grows, node representations converge to a subspace spanned by the graph's dominant eigenvectors, erasing discriminative information. Formally, the Dirichlet energy $\mathcal{E}(h) = \sum_{(u,v)} \|h_u - h_v\|^2$ decays exponentially with depth for mean aggregation [11].

**Oversquashing**: information from exponentially many $K$-hop neighbors is compressed through fixed-size node vectors; the Jacobian $\partial h_v^{(K)} / \partial x_u$ decays exponentially in $\mathrm{dist}(u,v)$, bottlenecking long-range dependencies [12]. Graph rewiring (adding edges to improve the Cheeger constant), graph transformers (all-pairs attention bypasses the bottleneck), and state-space-inspired long-range layers are competing remedies.

### 4.2 The WL ceiling in practice

Does the WL limit matter for real tasks? On molecular benchmarks, the answer is nuanced: 1-WL already distinguishes almost all small molecules (the failure cases—strongly regular graphs—are rare in chemistry), so GIN's theoretical advantage over GCN translates to modest gains on ZINC and OGB. Where expressiveness bites is in *counting* substructures (pharmacophores, functional groups) and in combinatorial tasks (SAT, TSP encodings), where subgraph GNNs and higher-order methods win decisively [7].

### 4.3 Equivariance meets expressiveness

A subtle interaction: standard MPNNs on 3D graphs with only distances as edge features are $E(3)$-invariant but cannot distinguish certain chiral or symmetric configurations; directional features (angles, dihedrals) or full tensor-product equivariance restore completeness. Recent work characterizes *universality* of equivariant architectures: sufficiently rich tensor-product networks are universal approximators of equivariant functions, closing the loop between symmetry and expressiveness [9].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Result 1 (GIN maximality).** Xu et al.: GIN achieves 1-WL distinguishing power; mean/max-aggregation MPNNs are strictly weaker [3]. On the graph-classification benchmarks of the era, GIN set state of the art among MPNNs.

**Result 2 (OGB leaderboards).** On ogbg-molhiv and PCQM4M, graph transformers with structural encodings now dominate, but the gains over well-tuned GINs are incremental—suggesting that for molecular property prediction, features and scale matter more than WL level [8].

**Result 3 (Equivariant force fields).** NequIP and MACE achieve ab initio molecular-dynamics accuracy with 1000× less data than invariant models, enabling simulations previously requiring supercomputers—arguably geometric deep learning's greatest practical triumph [9].

**Result 4 (Subgraph counting).** Subgraph GNNs provably count cycles up to length 6+ and separate the CSL (circular skip-link) graphs that defeat 1-WL; on synthetic benchmarks the theory–practice match is exact [7].

## 6 Limitations

1. **The WL ceiling.** Plain message passing cannot count substructures or separate regular graphs; many real tasks (combinatorial optimization, program analysis) need more.
2. **Oversmoothing/oversquashing.** Depth—the standard route to long-range reasoning—fights the architecture; no consensus remedy exists.
3. **Cost of higher order.** $k$-WL power costs $O(n^k)$; subgraph methods cost $O(n)$ MPNN runs. Expressiveness is computationally expensive.
4. **Positional encodings are fragile.** Graph transformers' power hinges on PEs (Laplacian eigenvectors suffer from sign/basis ambiguity; random-walk PEs are more stable but weaker). A canonical, provably sufficient PE scheme is missing.
5. **Dynamic and heterogeneous graphs.** The WL theory is developed for static, homogeneous graphs; temporal, heterogeneous, and hypergraph extensions are fragmentary.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The expressiveness theory of GNNs is a model of what deep-learning theory can be: a crisp limitation (the 1-WL ceiling), a precise characterization of optimality (injective aggregation), a hierarchy of stronger methods with quantified costs, and a complementary design principle (equivariance) that sidesteps the hierarchy entirely for geometric data. Message passing is not all-powerful, but knowing *exactly* what it cannot do has been more productive than any amount of architecture search: it told us to build GINs, subgraph methods, graph transformers, and equivariant networks, each addressing a diagnosed weakness.

The open frontier—canonical positional encodings, depth without smoothing, and a WL-like theory for dynamic graphs—promises to keep the dialogue between combinatorics and learning as fruitful in the next decade as it has been in this one.
