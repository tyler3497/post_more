# Closed-Loop Co-Adaptive Neural Decoders with Manifold Stabilizer Constraints for Intracortical Brain–Computer Interfaces

![Closed-loop co-adaptive BCI architecture with CLDA feedback](public/thesis/ths_1788827368040_1886-0.webp)

## Abstract

Intracortical brain–computer interfaces map Utah-array population spiking to effector kinematics in real time, but static decoders degrade as recording conditions drift within and across days [1][7]. This article develops **closed-loop co-adaptive decoders** — systems in which the decoder adapts online while the human user simultaneously adapts to it — and introduces **manifold stabilizer constraints** that anchor adaptation to the low-dimensional neural manifold on which population dynamics are conserved [4][9]. We formalize closed-loop decoder adaptation (CLDA) as stochastic approximation with inferred-intent supervision, analyze the two-learner stability problem, and compare Kalman, RNN, LFADS, and transformer decoder families, showing how error-related potentials supply human-in-the-loop supervision without interrupting use [10][11]. We prove a stabilizer-contraction theorem bounding tracking error under representational drift, and review empirical milestones: multiplicative RNNs robust where Kalman filters fail [2], LFADS dynamics improving velocity decoding [3], handwriting iBCIs at 90 characters per minute [5], and pseudo-label recalibration holding 93.84% accuracy over 403 days [6].

## 1 Introduction

A cursor/typing iBCI is a *closed-loop* dynamical system with two adaptive agents. On each 20–50 ms step, ~100–200 motor-cortex electrodes yield a spike-count vector $\mathbf{x}_t$; a decoder $f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ converts it into a velocity or character command; the user observes the cursor and adjusts their neural activity. Classical practice calibrated $f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ once, from *open-loop* (OL) imagined control, then froze it. Jarosiewicz *et al.* showed this is doubly wrong: *closed-loop*-calibrated decoders outperform OL-calibrated ones even with data quantity and recency held constant, because neural tuning itself differs between OL and CL contexts [1]. The decoder must be calibrated — and continuously re-calibrated — in the loop in which it operates.

Continuous re-calibration is now necessary — intra-day instabilities degrade decoding within hours [7], unit turnover forces supervised recalibration that interrupts use [8] — and tractable: latent-variable models [3][12] and LM pseudo-label recalibration [6] maintain performance without supervised sessions. The question is *how to constrain adaptation* so it tracks genuine change without chasing noise — the stabilizer constraint.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The two-learner problem

Closed-loop decoder adaptation (CLDA) updates the decoder's parameters *during closed-loop BMI operation* so the decoder tracks the true mapping between neural activity and intended movement [10]. CLDA closes a second loop: the user learns to modulate activity to drive the decoder, and the decoder learns to interpret the user. This *co-adaptation* is powerful but delicate — an over-aggressive decoder learns the user's *errors* rather than their *intentions*. Stability requires timescale separation or explicit constraints (§3.3, §4.1).

### 2.2 The Kalman baseline and intention-trained variants

The workhorse clinical decoder remains a linear-Gaussian state-space model:

$$\mathbf{z}_t = A\mathbf{z}_{t-1} + \mathbf{w}_t, \qquad \mathbf{x}_t = H\mathbf{z}_t + \mathbf{q}_t,$$

with $\mathbf{z}_t$ the kinematic state (cursor position/velocity), $A$ the state transition, and $H$ the neural observation matrix fit by regressing firing rates onto kinematics. The steady-state Kalman gain maps spike-count innovations to velocity updates at millisecond latency with negligible compute. The *recalibrated feedback intention-trained* Kalman filter (ReFIT-KF) augments this pipeline by re-training on closed-loop data under the assumption that the user's intention points toward the target — an early instance of inferred-intent supervision that anticipates modern CLDA [13]. In monkey experiments ReFIT-KF outperformed existing prosthetic algorithms in every measured domain, halved target acquisition time, and sustained repeatable high performance for years after implantation [13].

The Kalman filter's weaknesses motivate this article: it assumes a fixed linear tuning model $H$, so drift in preferred directions or unit dropout breaks it, and it adapts only through discrete recalibration sessions.

### 2.3 Nonstationarity: what drifts and on what timescale

Recording conditions change on at least three timescales: **intra-day drift** — waveform and firing-rate instabilities within a single session that measurably degrade decoding over hours [7]; **day-to-day turnover** — stable, well-isolated units are the minority, so decoding features must be routinely re-estimated [8]; and **contextual remapping** — tuning curves shift between open-loop observation and closed-loop control, and again as the user learns the decoder [1]. Any fixed decoder is a depreciating asset; the question is how to re-estimate it *without* interrupting the user.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Closed-loop decoder adaptation as stochastic approximation

Let the decoder be $f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ with parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. On step $t$ the system observes spikes $\mathbf{x}_t$, emits $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_t = f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_t}(\mathbf{x}_t)$, and receives a supervision signal $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t$ — an estimate of the user's true intent — from one of four canonical sources:

1. **Task-goal inference.** In center-out tasks, intended velocity is the cursor-to-target vector; in typing, the intended key is the displayed target [1].
2. **Bayesian self-training.** Posterior beliefs about intent update from decoder-output history and task statistics [10].
3. **Language-model pseudo-labels.** In handwriting iBCIs, an LM corrects raw character outputs; the corrections supervise continual online recalibration without interrupting the user (the CORP framework) [6].
4. **Error-related potentials.** Neural error signals indicate when the user perceives a decoder mistake, providing signed feedback [10][11].

Given $(\mathbf{x}_t, \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t)$, the CLDA update is a stochastic-approximation step:

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\!\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta_t \, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\, \ell\!\left(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_t}(\mathbf{x}_t), \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t\right)\right],$$

where $\ell$ is a tracking loss, $\eta_t$ a step size, and $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$ projects onto the **stabilizer constraint set** $\mathcal{C}$ of §3.2 — the difference between stabilizer-constrained CLDA and naive online gradient descent.

```python
import numpy as np

def clda_step(theta, P, x, z_intent, eta, stabilizer):
    """One RLS CLDA update with stabilizer projection onto the constraint set."""
    y = x @ theta
    e = z_intent - y
    K = (P @ x) / (1.0 + x @ P @ x)
    theta = theta + eta * K * e
    P = P - np.outer(K, x @ P)
    return stabilizer(theta), P

def stabilizer_project(theta, U_stable, thresh=0.95):
    """Keep readout components inside span(U_stable); shrink orthogonal drift."""
    c = U_stable.T @ theta
    return U_stable @ c + (1.0 - thresh) * (theta - U_stable @ c)
```

### 3.2 The stabilizer constraint: anchoring adaptation to the neural manifold

Population activity is high-dimensional ($N \sim 10^2$ channels) but behaviorally relevant dynamics live on a low-dimensional neural manifold of dimension $k \ll N$, and that manifold is *preserved across motor behaviors*: demixed principal components reveal target-related but task-independent neural modes [4].

We formalize the stabilizer as follows. Degenhart *et al.* showed that aligning day-specific estimates of the neural manifold to a reference manifold recovers proficient cursor control under severe, abrupt recording instabilities across multiple days — without requiring knowledge of user intent, and in some conditions outperforming supervised recalibration [9]. Let $U \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times k}$ be an orthonormal basis of the reference manifold (estimated from a calibration session via factor analysis or the encoder of a sequential autoencoder [3]). Decompose any linear readout $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ into manifold-aligned and orthogonal parts:

$$\boldsymbol{\theta} = U\boldsymbol{\alpha} + U_\perp \boldsymbol{\beta}.$$

The stabilizer constraint set is

$$\mathcal{C} = \left\{ \boldsymbol{\theta} \;:\; \lVert \boldsymbol{\beta} \rVert_2 \le \rho \right\},$$

i.e., adaptation may re-estimate the readout *within* the manifold but is penalized for drifting into the orthogonal complement, where day-to-day variability concentrates.

> Theorem: **Stabilizer contraction.** *Let the true readout satisfy $\boldsymbol{\theta}^\star_t = U\boldsymbol{\alpha}^\star_t + U_\perp\boldsymbol{\beta}^\star_t$ with $\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^\star_t\rVert \le \rho$ for all $t$, and let the intent-inferred loss be $\mu$-strongly convex in the manifold coordinates $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ with $L$-Lipschitz gradients. Then stabilizer-constrained CLDA with constant step size $\eta < 2/(\mu + L)$ satisfies*
> $$\mathbb{E}\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \boldsymbol{\theta}^\star_t\rVert^2 \le (1 - \eta\mu)^t \lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_0 - \boldsymbol{\theta}^\star_0\rVert^2 + \frac{\eta \sigma^2}{\mu} + 4\rho^2,$$
> *where $\sigma^2$ bounds the supervision noise. In particular, the asymptotic tracking error is controlled by the stabilizer radius $\rho$ and the intent-estimation noise $\sigma^2$, independent of the magnitude of orthogonal neural drift.*

In words: because neural variability lives mostly in the orthogonal complement, and the stabilizer refuses to follow it there, the decoder's steady-state error no longer grows with drift — it is *bounded by construction* (proof in §5).

```haskell
-- Stabilizer-constrained CLDA as a state transition (Haskell sketch)
type Vec = [Double]; type Mat = [[Double]]
data Decoder = Decoder { theta :: Vec, invCov :: Mat, basis :: Mat }

cldaUpdate :: Double -> Vec -> Vec -> Decoder -> Decoder
cldaUpdate eta x zIntent d =
  let y      = theta d `dot` x
      k      = (invCov d `matVec` x) ./ (1 + x `dot` (invCov d `matVec` x))
      theta' = basis d `projectOnto` ((theta d) `add` scale eta (k `scaleBy` (zIntent `sub` [y])))
      p'     = invCov d `sub` outer k (x `matVecT` invCov d)
  in d { theta = theta', invCov = p' }
```

### 3.3 Human-in-the-loop error signals

Error-related potentials (ErrPs) are frontocentral EEG/ECoG signatures — a characteristic deflection 150–650 ms after a perceived error, generated near the anterior cingulate cortex — that single-trial classifiers detect with 75–80% accuracy [11]. In closed-loop iBCI they serve two functions:

1. **Error damping.** Detecting that the user perceives the last output as wrong lets the system immediately attenuate or undo the erroneous command, improving control without any decoder update [14].
2. **Signed supervision.** The ErrP labels the recent $(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{z}})$ pair — "this output was wrong" — enabling CLDA updates whose supervision comes from the user's own evaluative circuitry rather than task geometry [10].

Hybrid systems fuse a kinematic decoder with an error classifier (e.g., logistic regression combining a motor-imagery score and an ErrP score), relying more on whichever channel is more classifiable for a given user [14]. Crucially, ErrP-based supervision works when task goals are *unknown* — free typing, open-ended cursor use — where goal-inferred intent is unavailable.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 CLDA dynamics and the co-adaptation stability problem

The coupled system has two learners with asymmetric information. Naive simultaneous gradient descent on both sides can produce *positive feedback*: the decoder learns the user's compensatory strategy, the user compensates for the decoder's learning, and performance oscillates or collapses. Three remedies are established in theory and practice:

- **Timescale separation.** Adapt the decoder an order of magnitude faster (or slower) than the user's learning rate, so one side sees a quasi-static partner. CORP-style continual recalibration adopts the fast-decoder regime [6].
- **Stabilizer projection.** Restricting updates to the reference manifold prevents the decoder from fitting transient, non-behavioral neural fluctuations [9].
- **Intention-consistent gating.** Updates are applied only when inferred intent is high-confidence (e.g., LM pseudo-labels above a likelihood threshold), bounding the supervision noise $\sigma^2$ [6].

Formally, the contraction bound of §3.2 can be stated as a TLA+ safety invariant over the tracking error `thetaErr`: with `Eta < 2/(Mu+L)`, the error satisfies `thetaErr' ≤ (1 - Eta*Mu) * thetaErr + Eta*Sigma2 + 4*Rho^2` at every step.

### 4.2 RNN decoders: learning many mappings instead of one

Sussillo *et al.* attacked nonstationarity by *pre-training for variability*: a multiplicative RNN (MRNN) trained on months of neural data plus synthetic perturbations (dropout, gain changes, tuning shifts) [2]. The MRNN internalized a family of neural-to-kinematic mappings selected via recurrent state; under perturbations that *disabled a state-of-the-art Kalman filter* in a monkey BMI model, it maintained control [2] — *robustness-through-diversity* complementing CLDA's *robustness-through-tracking*, and composable with it: an MRNN can be the plant that CLDA fine-tunes [5][6].

### 4.3 Latent dynamics: LFADS and neural data transformers

Linear-Gaussian decoders see spikes; latent-dynamics decoders see the *manifold*. LFADS fits a sequential variational autoencoder whose generator is an RNN: an encoder compresses a trial's spike train into an initial condition, and the generator unrolls autonomous dynamics reproducing denoised firing rates [3]. Decoding LFADS-denoised rates with simple optimal linear estimation *vastly improves* hand-velocity prediction over Gaussian smoothing [3]. For CLDA, the LFADS encoder provides exactly the stabilizer basis $U$: a denoised, low-dimensional coordinate system for the readout.

Transformers drop the explicit dynamics model. The Neural Data Transformer applies masked self-attention across time bins at >6× lower inference latency than recurrent baselines [12]; STNDT adds spatial attention over neurons [12]. **NDT2** showed unsupervised *pre-training across sessions, subjects, and tasks* yields representations that adapt rapidly to novel recording contexts [12] — a pretrained transformer encoder supplies a *universal* reference manifold, and CLDA becomes low-dimensional readout adaptation on top of it.

### 4.4 Manifold stabilization and decoder-day generalization

The hardest practical problem is the *decoder-day* boundary: today's array is not yesterday's array. The approaches below address it:

| Approach | Mechanism | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Manifold preservation | Task-independent latent modes persist across behaviors | dPCA reveals preserved manifold across motor tasks [4] |
| Latent-space alignment | Day-$t$ factors affinely aligned to reference manifold; no user-intent knowledge needed | Stabilizer recovers control under severe instabilities across days [9] |
| Continual pseudo-label recalibration | LM-corrected outputs supervise online updates | 93.84% accuracy over 403 days, no supervised sessions [6] |
| Cross-context pretraining | Transformer encoder pretrained on many sessions/subjects | Rapid adaptation to novel recording contexts [12] |
| Robustness-through-diversity | RNN trained on months of data + synthetic perturbations | Survives conditions that disable the Kalman filter [2] |

![Neural manifold with stabilizer subspace and drifting latent dynamics](public/thesis/ths_1788827368040_1886-1.webp)

The synthesis — and the thesis of this article — is that these are *components of one system*: a reference manifold $U$ provides the constraint set $\mathcal{C}$; pseudo-labels or ErrPs provide the supervision $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t$; the projected update tracks intent within the manifold while ignoring orthogonal drift.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch of the Stabilizer Contraction Theorem

In manifold coordinates $\mathbf{e}_t = \boldsymbol{\alpha}_t - \boldsymbol{\alpha}^\star_t$, the projected update is SGD on a $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth objective with intent-estimation noise $\mathbb{E}\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}_t\rVert^2 \le \sigma^2$. Standard analysis gives, for $\eta < 2/(\mu+L)$:

$$\mathbb{E}\lVert\mathbf{e}_{t+1}\rVert^2 \le (1 - \eta\mu)\,\mathbb{E}\lVert\mathbf{e}_t\rVert^2 + \eta^2 \sigma^2,$$

i.e., a geometric transient plus an $\eta\sigma^2/\mu$ noise floor. Reassembling, $\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \boldsymbol{\theta}^\star_t\rVert^2 = \lVert\mathbf{e}_t\rVert^2 + \lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_t - \boldsymbol{\beta}^\star_t\rVert^2 \le \lVert\mathbf{e}_t\rVert^2 + 4\rho^2$ by the constraint. ∎ The $4\rho^2$ term is the *price of the stabilizer*: the worst-case bias from refusing to track orthogonal drift.

### 5.2 Decoder families under closed-loop co-adaptation

The table below compares decoder families on the dimensions that matter for closed-loop deployment; reported figures are from the cited primary sources.

| Decoder family | Adaptation mechanism | Reported performance | Multi-day evidence | Inference cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Velocity Kalman filter | Discrete recalibration sessions | Clinical baseline; linear-Gaussian | Degrades with unit turnover [8] | O(N·d), trivial |
| ReFIT-KF | Closed-loop recalibration on inferred intent | Halves target acquisition time; stable for years [13] | Requires supervised sessions | O(N·d) |

| MRNN (multiplicative RNN) | Pretrained on months of data + perturbations | Robust where KF fails [2] | Trained on months of data | Recurrent, moderate |

Two patterns emerge. *First*, every high-performing modern system uses closed-loop data — through calibration [1], perturbation training [2], or continual recalibration [6]. *Second*, long-horizon stability is achieved either by anchoring to conserved structure (manifolds [4][9], pretrained representations [12]) or by relentless re-supervision (pseudo-labels [6], ErrPs [11]) — and stabilizer-constrained CLDA is the combination of both.

### 5.3 A quantitative vignette: the CORP result through the stabilizer lens

Fan *et al.*'s CORP framework is the closest existing realization of stabilizer-constrained CLDA at scale [6]. A seed RNN handwriting decoder (day 0, CER 3.22%) was continually recalibrated using language-model-corrected pseudo-labels — *without any supervised data collection sessions*. Over 403 days the baseline's error rose and fluctuated with nonstationarity while CORP held a stable average CER of 6.35% ± 1.75%, reaching 93.84% decoding accuracy and 6.18% CER at day 405 [6]. CORP is thus CLDA with LM-supplied $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t$: confident corrections keep $\sigma^2$ small and the stable handwriting strategy keeps $U$ valid — the theorem's bounded-error regime in practice.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Intent-inference circularity.** CLDA supervision $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t$ is *inferred*, not observed. If the intent model is systematically wrong (a confident LM miscorrection, a misattributed goal heuristic), the decoder learns the wrong mapping. The theorem's $\sigma^2$ term assumes unbiased noise; bias is not covered.
2. **Stabilizer rigidity.** $U$ is estimated from historical data. If the user's neural strategy genuinely reorganizes — new effector, new task, cortical reorganization — the preserved-manifold assumption [4] fails and $\rho$ must grow or $U$ must be re-estimated, reintroducing the instability the stabilizer prevents.
3. **Thin human evidence.** Timescale-separation arguments are validated mostly in monkeys [2] and short human sessions [1]; the 403-day CORP result is a single-participant demonstration [6].
4. **ErrP reliability.** Single-trial error-potential detection runs at 60–80% accuracy [11], and error signals can overlap spectrally with the control signal, risking interference unless the classifiers are jointly designed [14].
5. **Compute at the bedside.** Pretrained transformers [12] and LM-in-the-loop recalibration [6] demand GPU-class compute inside a 20–50 ms control loop; the Kalman filter's trivial O(N·d) cost remains a practical embedded-systems advantage.
6. **Evaluation comparability.** Bits-per-second, characters-per-minute, CER, and WER are measured under different tasks and participants; §5.2 compares *properties*, not a single benchmark. A standardized closed-loop benchmark suite with drift injection is still missing.

## 7 Conclusion

The closed-loop iBCI is a *coupled adaptive system*. CLDA gives the update law; the neural manifold gives the constraint set; error-related potentials and language-model pseudo-labels give supervision that does not interrupt use. The stabilizer-contraction theorem converts unbounded drift into bounded tracking error — matching the record, from MRNNs surviving Kalman-killing perturbations [2] to a handwriting decoder at 93.84% accuracy across 403 days with no supervised recalibration [6]. Next: learn manifold *and* readout jointly online with adaptive stabilizer radius, fuse ErrP and LM supervision into one confidence-weighted intent estimator, and validate two-learner stability in multi-participant, multi-year deployments.
