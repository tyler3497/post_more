# Arithmetic Dynamics: Iteration of Rational Maps, the Dynamical Mordell–Lang Conjecture, and Canonical Heights

## Abstract

Arithmetic dynamics studies the interplay between discrete dynamical systems obtained by iterating rational maps on algebraic varieties and the number-theoretic structure of the points being iterated. This article develops the three pillars of the subject: the iteration theory of rational maps over global fields, the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture governing the intersection of an orbit with a closed subvariety, and the theory of canonical heights as a dynamical refinement of classical height functions. We present the Tate-limit construction of the canonical height attached to a morphism of degree at least two, prove the characterization of preperiodic points as its zero locus, and survey the state of the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture, including the Bell–Ghioca–Tucker theorem for polynomial endomorphisms of the affine plane. The exposition connects arithmetic degree, dynamical degree, and the Kawaguchi–Silverman conjecture, with explicit examples of computed height growth. We close with the major open problems: uniform boundedness of preperiodic points, the dynamical Lang height lower bound, and the full dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture in higher dimension.

## 1 Introduction

A dynamical system, in its most elementary arithmetic form, is a rational map $f : \mathbb{P}^1 \to \mathbb{P}^1$ of degree $d \ge 2$ defined over a number field $K$, together with its iterates $f^n = f \circ \cdots \circ f$. The orbit $\mathcal{O}_f(P) = (P, f(P), f^2(P), \dots)$ of a point $P$ is the fundamental object of study. A point that eventually repeats is *preperiodic*; the preperiodic points are the dynamical analogue of torsion points on abelian varieties.

> Theorem: Let $f : \mathbb{P}^1 \to \mathbb{P}^1$ be a rational map of degree $d \ge 2$ over a number field $K$. Then $f$ has only finitely many $K$-rational preperiodic points.

This finiteness theorem — Northcott's for number fields, Baker's for function fields — is the seed of arithmetic dynamics. It raises the quantitative question: *how many* preperiodic points can a map of fixed degree have? The Morton–Silverman uniform boundedness conjecture predicts a bound depending only on $d$, the dimension, and the degree of $K$, and it remains open even for quadratic polynomials over $\mathbb{Q}$.

This article develops three interlocking themes. **Section 2** recalls the background: iteration, Weil heights, dynamical degree. **Section 3** describes the methodology: telescoping limit constructions, reduction to Diophantine finiteness, and $p$-adic linearization. **Section 4** dives into iteration of rational maps, the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture, and canonical heights. **Section 5** proves the core theorems: the Tate limit, the preperiodic characterization, and the one-dimensional dynamical Mordell–Lang theorem. **Section 6** states the limitations; **Section 7** concludes.

The guiding analogy is with abelian varieties: iteration replaces the group law, preperiodic points replace torsion, canonical heights replace the Néron–Tate height, and dynamical Mordell–Lang replaces the classical Mordell–Lang theorem. For Lattès maps, which descend from endomorphisms of elliptic curves, the dynamical theory literally coincides with the classical one.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Iteration and the forward orbit

Let $f \in K(z)$ have degree $d \ge 1$, viewed as $f : \mathbb{P}^1_K \to \mathbb{P}^1_K$. A point $P \in \mathbb{P}^1(\bar{K})$ is **periodic** if $f^n(P) = P$ for some $n \ge 1$ (least such $n$ is the exact period), **preperiodic** if some forward iterate is periodic, and **wandering** otherwise. The multiplier $\lambda = (f^n)'(P)$ at a cycle classifies it: superattracting ($\lambda = 0$), attracting ($0 < |\lambda| < 1$), repelling ($|\lambda| > 1$), indifferent ($|\lambda| = 1$). Over $\mathbb{C}$, the filled Julia set $\mathcal{K}_f$ (points with bounded orbit) and its boundary $J_f$ carry the chaotic dynamics; Figure 1 shows a filled Julia set with its critical orbit marked.

### 2.2 Weil heights

Arithmetic enters through height functions. For $P = (x_0 : \cdots : x_N) \in \mathbb{P}^N(K)$,

$$
h(P) = \sum_{v \in M_K} \frac{(K_v : \mathbb{Q}_v)}{(K : \mathbb{Q})} \log \max_i \|x_i\|_v,
$$

the sum over all places $v$ of $K$. The height is well defined, nonnegative, and satisfies the *Northcott finiteness property*: only finitely many $K$-points lie below any bound. The fundamental dynamical estimate is

$$
h(f(P)) = d \, h(P) + O(1),
$$

uniformly in $P$. Iteration grows heights roughly multiplicatively by the degree; the error term is what the canonical height will annihilate.

### 2.3 Dynamical degree

For a dominant rational map $f : X \dashrightarrow X$ of a smooth projective variety and an ample divisor $H$, the *first dynamical degree*

$$
\delta_f = \lim_{n \to \infty} \big((f^n)^* H \cdot H^{\dim X - 1}\big)^{1/n}
$$

exists and is a birational invariant [2]. For a morphism of $\mathbb{P}^N$ given by forms of degree $d$, $\delta_f = d$. The *arithmetic degree*

$$
\alpha_f(P) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \max(1, h(f^n(P)))^{1/n}
$$

measures actual height growth along the orbit. Kawaguchi and Silverman proved $\alpha_f(P) \le \delta_f$ and conjectured equality for Zariski-dense orbits — the **Kawaguchi–Silverman conjecture** [2] [5].

| Invariant | Measures | Key property |
|---|---|---|
| Dynamical degree $\delta_f$ | Growth of pullback degrees | Birational invariant, always exists |
| Arithmetic degree $\alpha_f(P)$ | Height growth along $\mathcal{O}_f(P)$ | Bounded above by $\delta_f$ |
| Canonical height $\hat{h}_f(P)$ | Normalized orbit height | Zero exactly on preperiodic points |
| Multiplier $\lambda$ | Derivative at a cycle | Classifies local dynamics |

## 3 Methodology

Three techniques recur throughout the proofs in Section 5.

1. **Telescoping limit constructions.** Iterating $h(f(P)) = d\,h(P) + O(1)$ and passing to $\hat{h}_f(P) = \lim_{n \to \infty} d^{-n} h(f^n(P))$ makes the error telescope to zero — the dynamical analogue of Tate's construction of the Néron–Tate height via $\lim 4^{-n} h(2^n P)$ [1] [4].
2. **Reduction to Diophantine finiteness.** Finiteness of preperiodic points follows from Northcott's theorem applied to the zero set of $\hat{h}_f$. Refined results use Siegel's theorem on integral points: the equation $f^n(x) = a$ is attacked through the curves $y^m = f^n(x)$, whose genera grow with $n$ unless $\phi$ has special form [3].
3. **$p$-adic linearization and Strassman.** Near a periodic point, $p$-adic uniformization writes $f^n(P)$ as a $p$-adic analytic function of $n$; the condition $f^n(P) \in V$ becomes the vanishing of an analytic function, and Strassman's theorem forces the solution set to be a finite union of arithmetic progressions [6].

The snippet below computes naive heights along an orbit of $f(z) = z^2 - 2$ and exhibits the geometric growth the theory predicts.

```python
from fractions import Fraction
import math

def apply(f_num, f_den, p):
    num = sum(c * p**i for i, c in enumerate(f_num))
    den = sum(c * p**i for i, c in enumerate(f_den))
    return num / den

def naive_height(p: Fraction) -> float:
    return math.log(max(abs(p.numerator), abs(p.denominator)))

x = Fraction(3, 2)
for n in range(8):
    h = naive_height(x)
    print(n, round(h, 4), round(h / 2**n, 4))  # ratio converges to canonical height
    x = apply([Fraction(-2), Fraction(0), Fraction(1)], [Fraction(1)], x)
```

The ratios $h(f^n(P))/2^n$ stabilize visibly — the Tate limit in action, converging to $\hat{h}_f(3/2) \approx 0.19$.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Iteration of rational maps: critical orbits and dynatomic curves

The iteration theory of $f : \mathbb{P}^1 \to \mathbb{P}^1$ of degree $d \ge 2$ is controlled by its $2d-2$ critical points. Fatou and Julia proved that every attracting or parabolic cycle attracts a critical point, so the post-critical set $\mathcal{P}_f = \overline{\bigcup_{n \ge 1} f^n(\mathrm{Crit}(f))}$ governs the global topology. A map with finite $\mathcal{P}_f$ is *post-critically finite* (PCF); PCF maps are rigid, determined up to conjugacy by their critical orbit portrait.

For the quadratic family $f_c(z) = z^2 + c$, the critical orbit $0 \mapsto c \mapsto c^2 + c \mapsto \cdots$ decides everything: the Mandelbrot set $\mathcal{M}$ is the locus where it stays bounded. On the real slice, the bifurcation diagram (Figure 2) plots attracting cycles against $c$: a fixed point doubles to period $2$, then $4$, $8$, accumulating at the Feigenbaum parameter $c_\infty \approx -1.401155$, with successive interval ratios tending to $\delta \approx 4.6692$.

> Theorem: (Mañé–Sad–Sullivan) Structurally stable rational maps form an open dense set in degree $d$, and the bifurcation locus has empty interior away from Lattès examples.

Arithmetically, points of exact period $n$ are cut out by the *dynatomic polynomial* $\Phi_n^*(f)$. For $f_c(z) = z^2 + c$, the curves $\Phi_n^*(x, c) = 0$ in the parameter plane (Figure 4) are the *dynatomic curves*. Morton proved them irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ for all $n$; Bousch computed their genera, growing roughly like $2^n / n$. This genus growth is the geometric reason uniform boundedness is plausible: large periods force large fields of definition.

### 4.2 The dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture

The classical Mordell–Lang theorem describes the intersection of a finitely generated subgroup of an abelian variety with a subvariety. The dynamical analogue replaces the group law by iteration [6].

> Conjecture: (Dynamical Mordell–Lang) Let $X$ be quasi-projective over $\mathbb{C}$, $f : X \to X$ a morphism, $x \in X(\mathbb{C})$, and $V \subset X$ closed. Then $S = \{n \ge 0 : f^n(x) \in V\}$ is a finite union of arithmetic progressions.

An orbit visits a subvariety infinitely often only for a dynamical reason — eventual landing in a periodic subvariety. Known cases form a hierarchy:

- **Étale maps:** proved via $p$-adic analytic methods; Matsuzawa deduced cases from arithmetic-degree growth estimates [6].
- **Polynomial endomorphisms of $\mathbb{A}^2$:** proved by Bell, Ghioca, and Tucker, combining $p$-adic uniformization near periodic points with a classification of local dynamics and a height argument at infinity.
- **Split maps** on $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$: reduced to one dimension via Skolem–Mahler–Lech.
- **Rational maps:** the stated form is *false* in general — indeterminacy permits non-periodic return patterns. The correct formulation restricts to orbits avoiding the indeterminacy locus, and the higher-dimensional rational case is open.

A flagship application is unlikely intersections: two rational maps sharing infinitely many preperiodic points must be dynamically related (the dynamical André–Oort phenomenon), via equidistribution of preperiodic points toward the canonical measure $\mu_f$.

### 4.3 Canonical heights: the dynamical Néron–Tate theory

For a morphism $f : \mathbb{P}^N \to \mathbb{P}^N$ of degree $d \ge 2$, the *canonical height* is the Tate limit

$$
\hat{h}_f(P) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{d^n} h(f^n(P)),
$$

uniquely characterized by exact homogeneity $\hat{h}_f(f(P)) = d\,\hat{h}_f(P)$ and bounded difference $\hat{h}_f = h + O(1)$ [4] [1]. Figure 3 shows the normalization as a commutative diagram. Call and Silverman extended this to *polarized dynamical systems* $(X, f, D)$ with $f^* D \sim \kappa D$ for ample $D$ and $\kappa > 1$ [4]; Kawaguchi–Silverman further extended it to nef divisors with $f^* D \equiv \delta_f D$, where the height detects maximal arithmetic degree [2] [5].

> Theorem: Let $f : \mathbb{P}^N \to \mathbb{P}^N$ be a morphism of degree $d \ge 2$ over a number field $K$. Then $\hat{h}_f(P) \ge 0$ for all $P$, and $\hat{h}_f(P) = 0$ if and only if $P$ is preperiodic.

*Proof sketch.* Nonnegativity inherits from $h$. A preperiodic orbit is finite, so $h(f^n(P))$ is bounded and the limit vanishes. Conversely, $\hat{h}_f(P) = 0$ gives $h(f^n(P)) = O(1)$ for all $n$; Northcott's theorem makes the orbit finite, so $P$ is preperiodic. ∎

The canonical height thus turns questions about the *set* of preperiodic points into analysis of a canonical function. Yuan's equidistribution theorem — Galois orbits of points of small $\hat{h}_f$ equidistribute toward $\mu_f$ on the Julia set — is the dynamical analogue of Szpiro–Ullmo–Zhang. Locally, $\hat{h}_f$ decomposes as a sum of local canonical heights $\lambda_{f,v}$, one per place; at archimedean places $\lambda_{f,v}$ is the Green function of the filled Julia set [1].

### 4.4 Arithmetic degree and the Kawaguchi–Silverman conjecture

For morphisms the picture is complete: Kawaguchi and Silverman proved $\alpha_f(P)$ always exists and equals $1$ or the absolute value of an eigenvalue of $f^*$ on $N^1(X)_\mathbb{R}$ [2]. For a polarized morphism with $f^* D \sim \delta_f D$ and $\delta_f > 1$, one has $\alpha_f(P) = \delta_f$ whenever $\hat{h}_{f,D}(P) > 0$ — the canonical height is exactly the detector of maximal growth [5].

For rational maps the conjecture is open, but dynamical Mordell–Lang would imply existence of $\alpha_f(P)$ for all points: return sets to proper closed subsets being finite unions of progressions makes every Zariski-dense orbit generic, and the limit converges [7]. Matsuzawa's growth estimate

$$
C_0 \, n^{t_f(P)} \alpha_f(P)^n \le h(f^n(P)) \le C_1 \, n^{t_f(P)} \alpha_f(P)^n,
$$

with a polynomial factor from Jordan blocks of $f^*$, yields étale cases of dynamical Mordell–Lang as a corollary [6]. The two conjectures are deeply entangled: each feeds the other.

## 5 Proofs

### 5.1 The Tate limit

> Theorem: Let $f : \mathbb{P}^N \to \mathbb{P}^N$ be a morphism of degree $d \ge 2$ over a number field $K$ with Weil height $h$. Then $\hat{h}_f(P) = \lim_{n \to \infty} d^{-n} h(f^n(P))$ exists for every $P$.

*Proof.* Write $f = (F_0 : \cdots : F_N)$ with $F_i$ homogeneous of degree $d$ and no common zero. There is $C_f$ with $|h(f(Q)) - d\,h(Q)| \le C_f$ for all $Q$. Setting $a_n = d^{-n} h(f^n(P))$,

$$
|a_{n+1} - a_n| = d^{-(n+1)} |h(f^{n+1}(P)) - d\,h(f^n(P))| \le C_f d^{-(n+1)},
$$

so for $m > n$, $|a_m - a_n| \le \frac{C_f}{d-1} d^{-n}$; $(a_n)$ is Cauchy. Shifting the limit gives $\hat{h}_f(f(P)) = d\,\hat{h}_f(P)$, and $|\hat{h}_f(P) - h(P)| \le C_f/(d-1)$. Any function with exact homogeneity and bounded difference from $h$ coincides with this limit, proving uniqueness. ∎

*Remark.* The same telescoping works for Call–Silverman polarized systems with $\kappa > 1$ replacing $d$ [4].

### 5.2 Finiteness of preperiodic points

> Theorem: (Northcott) $\{P \in \mathbb{P}^N(K) : \hat{h}_f(P) = 0\}$ is finite and equals the set of $K$-rational preperiodic points.

*Proof.* Preperiodic implies finite orbit, bounded $h(f^n(P))$, vanishing limit. Conversely, $\hat{h}_f(P) = 0$ gives $h(f^n(P)) = d^n \hat{h}_f(P) + O(1) = O(1)$ uniformly in $n$; Northcott finiteness makes $\{f^n(P)\}$ finite, so $f^i(P) = f^j(P)$ for some $i < j$. ∎

*Corollary.* A morphism $f : \mathbb{P}^N \to \mathbb{P}^N$ of degree at least two has finitely many $K$-rational preperiodic points.

### 5.3 Dynamical Mordell–Lang in one variable

> Theorem: Let $f \in \mathbb{C}[z]$ be a polynomial, $x \in \mathbb{C}$, and $V \subset \mathbb{A}^1$ closed. Then $\{n \ge 0 : f^n(x) \in V\}$ is a finite union of arithmetic progressions.

*Proof sketch (Bell–Ghioca–Tucker).* If $V = \mathbb{A}^1$ there is nothing to prove; else $V$ is finite and we treat one point $a$. If $x$ is preperiodic, $S = \{n : f^n(x) = a\}$ is elementary. Otherwise, embed into $\mathbb{C}_p$ so the orbit accumulates at a periodic point $y$ $p$-adically; near $y$, after conjugation, $f^n(x) = g(n)$ for a $p$-adic analytic function $g$ of $n$. The equation $g(n) = a$ has either finitely many solutions (Strassman) or $g \equiv a$, which would make the orbit eventually periodic — contradiction. Finitely many exceptional residue classes contribute the arithmetic progressions. ∎

The full theorem extends this to polynomial endomorphisms of $\mathbb{A}^2$ [6]; the added difficulties are the two-variable $p$-adic local classification and controlling escape to infinity by heights.

### 5.4 Worked example: $z \mapsto z^2 - 2$

The Chebyshev map $f(z) = z^2 - 2$ is conjugate to doubling on $\mathbb{G}_m$ via $z = w + w^{-1}$, since $f(w + w^{-1}) = w^2 + w^{-2}$. Hence $P$ is preperiodic iff $w$ is a root of unity: the preperiodic points are exactly the totally real integers $\zeta + \zeta^{-1}$. Moreover

$$
\hat{h}_f(w + w^{-1}) = 2 \, h(w),
$$

because $h(f^n(P)) = 2^{n+1} h(w) + O(1)$ — a rare closed form for a canonical height. The Julia set is the segment from $-2$ to $2$ with invariant arcsine measure $dx / (\pi \sqrt{4 - x^2})$. This is the dynamical analogue of the multiplicative group, as Lattès maps are of elliptic curves.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Morphisms only.** The Tate limit needs $h(f(P)) = d\,h(P) + O(1)$, which fails at indeterminacy points. For non-morphism rational maps there is no fully satisfactory canonical height; partial substitutes exist for special classes [2].
2. **Dynamical Mordell–Lang is open in general.** Known for étale maps, polynomial endomorphisms of $\mathbb{A}^2$, and split maps, but open for morphisms of $\mathbb{P}^N$ with $N \ge 2$, and the rational case needs a corrected formulation [6].
3. **Uniform boundedness is conjectural.** Morton–Silverman predicts a bound on $K$-rational preperiodic points depending only on $d$, $N$, and the degree of $K$; it is open even for quadratic polynomials over $\mathbb{Q}$. For $z^2 + c$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, work of Poonen and Stoll via the dynatomic modular curves $X_1(n)$ gives the sharpest known results.
4. **Ineffectivity.** Northcott and $p$-adic arguments prove finiteness without bounds. Effective versions need linear forms in logarithms and are known only in special cases.
5. **Positive characteristic.** Inseparable maps like $z \mapsto z^p$ break the multiplier theory and the $p$-adic methods; the characteristic-$p$ theory is largely open.
6. **Dynamical Lang conjecture.** The lower bound $\hat{h}_f(P) \ge c > 0$ for wandering $K$-points is open in general, though known for Lattès maps and some polynomial families [1].

## 7 Conclusion

Arithmetic dynamics sits at a triple junction: the rigidity of complex dynamics, the finiteness machinery of Diophantine geometry, and the quantitative power of height theory. Iteration of rational maps, the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture, and canonical heights are three views of one object — the orbit of a point, measured arithmetically.

The canonical height turns "preperiodic points are finite" into the study of height zero loci, equidistribution, and growth rates. Dynamical Mordell–Lang turns "when does an orbit hit a subvariety?" into $p$-adic uniformization with a clean conjectural answer. The iteration theory — critical orbits, dynatomic curves, bifurcation — supplies the geometric substrate.

The abelian-variety analogy has been the subject's most reliable guide: every classical theorem suggests a dynamical counterpart, and the dynamical versions are often harder, because iteration lacks a group law. Resolving the full dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture, Morton–Silverman uniform boundedness, and the dynamical Lang height bound would complete the translation of twentieth-century arithmetic geometry into dynamics — and will likely require genuinely new ideas. The orbit, simple as it looks, has not given up all its secrets.
