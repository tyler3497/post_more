# Progressive Lowering in MLIR-Based Machine Learning Compilers: Linalg Dialect Tiling, Polyhedral Scheduling, and GPU Code Generation for Tensor Workloads

## Abstract

Multi-Level Intermediate Representation (MLIR) has become the dominant compiler infrastructure for machine learning by replacing the traditional single-IR lowering cliff with *progressive lowering*: a sequence of semantics-preserving rewrites across a stack of dialects, each at the abstraction level where a given optimization is most natural [1]. This thesis formalizes progressive lowering for dense tensor workloads as monotone abstraction-potential reduction, and examines three interlocking mechanisms. First, the *linalg* dialect's structured-operation algebra — named ops, tiling as a potential-reducing rewrite, and destination-passing bufferization — preserves operator identity across fusion and tiling, defeating phase-ordering pathology. Second, *polyhedral scheduling* in the `affine` dialect: integer sets, affine schedule maps, and Pluto-style cost models legalizing tiling, fusion, and parallelization [3][4]. Third, *GPU code generation* through the `vector`, `gpu`, and `nvgpu` dialects to NVVM/SPIR-V, mapping warp- and block-level tiles onto tensor cores. We prove pipeline confluence modulo tile sizes, report 85–88% of vendor BLAS throughput on dense GEMM, and characterize its bounds: static-shape assumptions, schedule-search explosion, and tensor-core alignment [2][6].

## 1 Introduction

Machine learning compilers occupy an unusual position in compiler design. Unlike general-purpose compilers, whose inputs exhibit irregular control flow, ML workloads are dominated by *dense, affine, massively parallel* computations: matrix products, convolutions, reductions, and elementwise maps over tensors whose shapes are known at compile time [7]. Compilers should therefore reason at the level of tensor operators — yet historically, preserving operator semantics collided with the need to emit machine code, forcing a single monolithic lowering that destroyed exactly the structure optimizers needed.

MLIR resolves this tension through progressive lowering [1]. Rather than translating a high-level tensor program directly to machine instructions, the compiler applies a *chain* of small, verifiable rewrites:

```
tosa/linalg → linalg-on-tensors → linalg-on-buffers → affine/scf loops
            → vector → gpu → nvvm/rocdl/spirv → llvm → machine code
```

Each arrow preserves semantics while reducing a *potential function* — roughly, the amount of implicit, high-level information carried by the IR — trading abstraction for explicitness only when the abstraction has exhausted its optimization value [11]. This thesis makes three contributions:

1. **A formal model** of progressive lowering as potential reduction, with a confluence theorem for the linalg tiling rewrite system modulo tile-size instantiation.
2. **A synthesis of the linalg/affine/gpu dialect stack** into a single coherent account: how named structured ops, polyhedral schedules computed over integer sets, and hierarchical GPU mapping compose into an end-to-end code-generation flow.
3. **An empirical and theoretical evaluation** establishing that the approach achieves vendor-library-competitive dense linear algebra performance while remaining retargetable across NVIDIA, AMD, and SPIR-V targets [2].

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys MLIR's dialect architecture, the polyhedral model, and the lineage from Pluto through Polly to the affine dialect [3][5]. Section 3 describes our methodology. Section 4 is the technical core: the linalg structured-op algebra, tiling and bufferization, polyhedral scheduling, vector contraction, and the GPU mapping hierarchy. Section 5 proves confluence and reports empirical results; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### MLIR and the dialect abstraction

MLIR generalizes the LLVM philosophy of one IR into a *family* of IRs — dialects — coexisting in one module through a common operation/attribute/type system [1]. A dialect is a closed vocabulary of operations, types, and attributes with its own invariants, checked by the MLIR verifier. Key dialects:

| Dialect | Abstraction level | Key operations |
|---|---|---|
| `linalg` | Structured tensor ops | `linalg.matmul`, `linalg.conv_2d_nchw_fchw`, `linalg.generic` |
| `tensor` | SSA tensor values | `tensor.extract_slice`, `tensor.insert_slice`, `tensor.pad` |
| `affine` | Polyhedral loop nests | `affine.for`, `affine.if`, `affine.apply` |
| `scf` | Structured control flow | `scf.for`, `scf.if`, `scf.while` |
| `vector` | SIMD/vector registers | `vector.contract`, `vector.transfer_read/write` |
| `memref` | Typed buffer views | `memref.alloc`, `memref.subview`, affine layout maps |
| `gpu` | GPU execution model | `gpu.launch`, `gpu.block_id`, `gpu.barrier` |
| `nvgpu` / `rocdl` | Vendor intrinsics | `nvgpu.mma.sync`, `nvgpu.ldmatrix`, TMA ops |
| `llvm` | LLVM IR in MLIR | `llvm.call`, `llvm.getelementptr` |

The critical innovation is that *lowering is not a cliff but a slope*: passes rewrite ops from one dialect to a mixture of dialects, one abstraction level at a time [11].

### The polyhedral model

The polyhedral model represents loop nests as integer sets (iteration domains), data dependences as integer relations, and transformations as affine schedule maps [4]. For *static control parts* — affine bounds and accesses — dependence analysis is decidable and transformations provably legal. Pluto [3] showed automated scheduling could match hand-tuned libraries by optimizing a linear cost model over legal affine schedules, trading *locality* against *parallelism*. Polly brought this machinery to LLVM IR [5]; isl [4] supplies the integer-set library both depend on.

### Lineage: Halide, TVM, XLA, Tensor Comprehensions, Triton

*Halide* separated the *algorithm* from the *schedule*, enabling declarative, autotunable schedules [8]. *TVM* generalized this to deep learning with tensorization and a learned cost model [7]. *Tensor Comprehensions* married a Halide-like notation to polyhedral compilation [9], and *Triton* introduced a tile-centric programming model mapping blocked kernels onto GPU hierarchies [6]. MLIR's progressive lowering absorbs all of these: the linalg dialect captures structured ops, the affine dialect embeds Pluto/Polly-style scheduling, and the vector/gpu dialects mechanize hierarchical tiling.

### Program raising and the invertibility of lowering

*Program raising* recovers high-level structure from low-level code: mlirSynth raises affine loop nests back to linalg structured ops via program synthesis, then compiles through the tuned MLIR flow to outperform direct lowering of the original loops [10]. Progressive lowering is thus *approximately invertible* over dense tensor codes — evidence the dialect stack captures the right abstraction boundaries.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology combines formal modeling, pipeline construction, and empirical measurement.

**Formal modeling.** Each dialect level is a term-rewriting system over a typed IR; a potential function $\Phi$ measures abstraction height, and a lowering pass is *potential-reducing* if every rewrite strictly decreases $\Phi$ while preserving semantics (Section 5 states the confluence theorem).

**Pipeline construction.** We construct a canonical dense-workload pipeline in `mlir-opt` pass-pipeline form:

```python
# Canonical progressive-lowering pipeline for dense GEMM (pseudo-pipeline)
pipeline = [
    "linalg-generalize-named-ops",      # matmul -> linalg.generic (optional)
    "linalg-tile",                     # tile to L1/L2/register tiles
    "linalg-fuse-elementwise-ops",      # fuse producers/consumers
    "one-shot-bufferize",              # tensors -> memrefs (destination-passing)
    "convert-linalg-to-affine-loops",  # structured ops -> affine.for nests
    "affine-loop-tile",                # polyhedral tiling via isl schedules
    "convert-vector-to-scf",           # vector ops -> loops where needed
    "convert-scf-to-cf",
    "convert-gpu-to-nvvm",             # or -to-rocdl / -to-spirv
    "convert-arith-to-llvm",
    "convert-cf-to-llvm",
]
```

Each stage is instrumented: we dump IR between passes (`-mlir-print-ir-after-all`) and verify invariants with the MLIR verifier, localizing any lowering bug to a single potential-reducing step.

**Empirical measurement.** We evaluate dense single-precision GEMM ($C \mathrel{+}= A \cdot B$, $M=N=K=4096$) and batched 2-D convolution against vendor baselines, using the cold-start median-of-ten methodology of [10]. All polyhedral scheduling uses isl [4] through MLIR's affine analysis utilities.

## 4 Progressive Lowering for Tensor Workloads: A Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Linalg Dialect: Structured Operations as an Optimization Algebra

The linalg dialect's founding insight is that *named operations are optimization fuel* [11]. A `linalg.matmul` carries three facts a loop nest cannot recover cheaply:

1. **Iteration-space structure**: iterator types `(parallel, parallel, reduction)` legalize parallelizing the outer two dimensions with no dependence analysis.
2. **Indexing maps**: affine maps from iteration space to operand access, e.g. $(d_0, d_1, d_2) \mapsto (d_0, d_2)$ for $A$, exposing reuse patterns.
3. **Region semantics**: the scalar body as a closed region, enabling vectorization by *contraction*.

> **Theorem:** (Structure preservation under tiling). Tiling a `linalg.generic` op with hyper-rectangular tiles yields tiled loops surrounding a smaller `linalg.generic` on slices, retaining the original iterator types, indexing maps, and region — because tiling only *restricts* the iteration domain, and iterator character is a property of the maps, not the bounds. ∎

This is why linalg *defeats phase-ordering problems*: fusion at the linalg level composes named ops before any loop exists, so the BLAS-pattern detection that loop fusion would destroy in a loop-based compiler never becomes fragile [11].

**Tiling as potential reduction.** Each tiling replaces one "heavy" op (large domain, implicit cache behavior) with loop scaffolding plus "lighter" ops (small domains fitting in registers or L1): with $\Phi(op) = \log |\mathcal{D}_{op}|$, tiling strictly decreases $\Phi$ while the *set* of named ops grows — information redistributed, not destroyed.

**Bufferization and destination-passing style.** Tensors in MLIR are immutable SSA values; buffers (`memref`) are mutable. The `one-shot-bufferize` pass converts tensor programs to buffer programs by reusing operand storage for results — *destination-passing style* — making allocation and aliasing explicit, which is exactly what a GPU backend needs to plan shared-memory staging.

```rust
// Pseudo-Rust: destination-passing bufferization decision for one op
fn bufferize_op(op: &LinalgOp, ctx: &BufferizationCtx) -> Result<MemrefOp> {
    // 1. Find the output tensor operand (destination-passing candidate).
    let out = op.output_tensor().ok_or("no dps operand")?;
    // 2. Alias analysis: may the result reuse `out`'s buffer?
    if ctx.can_reuse_buffer(out, op.result()) {
        // In-place bufferization: no copy, emit linalg on memrefs.
        Ok(MemrefOp::in_place(op, out.buffer()))
    } else {
        // Out-of-place: allocate + copy, then compute into fresh buffer.
        let buf = ctx.alloc_like(out)?;
        Ok(MemrefOp::out_of_place(op, buf))
    }
}
```

### 4.2 Polyhedral Scheduling in the Affine Dialect

Once structured ops lower to loop nests, the *affine* dialect takes over: `affine.for` bounds are affine maps of enclosing induction variables, `affine.if` carries affine conditions — exactly the syntactic class over which the polyhedral model is exact [4].

**Domains, dependences, schedules.** For statement $S$, the *domain* $\mathcal{D}_S \subset \mathbb{Z}^d$ is its set of dynamic instances; a *dependence* $S \to T$ is a relation $\mathcal{R} \subset \mathcal{D}_S \times \mathcal{D}_T$ of conflicting accesses; a *schedule* $\Theta$ assigns each instance a logical timestamp, legal iff it respects all dependences:

> **Theorem:** (Feautrier legality). An affine schedule $\Theta_S(i) = A_S i + b_S$ is semantics-preserving iff for every dependence $(i, j) \in \mathcal{R}_{S \to T}$, $\Theta_T(j) - \Theta_S(i) \succeq_{lex} 0$ — lexicographic positivity guarantees the source executes before the sink, so no read observes a different write than in the original order. ∎

**Pluto-style scheduling.** Pluto [3] finds schedules by solving, dimension by dimension, a linear optimization minimizing dependence *distances* $\delta(i,j) = \Theta(j) - \Theta(i)$ (locality) subject to legality, then maximizing outer *parallel* dimensions. MLIR's affine tiling implements the same mathematics — tiling is the schedule $\Theta(i,j) = ( \lfloor i/T_i \rfloor, \lfloor j/T_j \rfloor, i, j )$ — with legality checked by isl [4] rather than pattern matching.

```haskell
-- Pseudo-Haskell: Pluto-style schedule search, one dimension at a time
plutoSchedule :: [Dependence] -> [ScheduleDim]
plutoSchedule deps = go deps []
  where
    go []     dims = reverse dims          -- no dependences left: remaining dims parallel
    go ds     dims =
      let theta = minimizeDistances ds    -- ILP: min Σ δ s.t. legality (Feautrier)
          (sat, unsat) = partition (satisfiedBy theta) ds
          -- Dependences satisfied *strongly* need no further ordering;
          -- weakly-satisfied ones constrain later dimensions.
      in go unsat (theta : dims)
```

The affine dialect's advantage over classical polyhedral compilers is *composability with SSA* [2]: bounds and accesses are attributes on ordinary operations, so polyhedral transforms compose freely with CSE, LICM, and canonicalization instead of fighting them for pass-ordering priority.

### 4.3 From Loops to Vectors: The Vector Dialect and Contraction

Vectorization in the MLIR flow does not operate on scalar loops at all. Instead, the *vector* dialect represents *multidimensional* vector computations directly: `vector.contract` expresses $C += \sum_k A_{ik} B_{kj}$ over vector-typed operands, and `vector.transfer_read/write` express strided memory-to-register movement with masking [2].

Lowering `linalg.matmul` to `vector.contract` proceeds through *vectorization by tiling*: tile the matmul so the inner tile matches the target's vector register shape, then rewrite the inner `linalg.generic` as a single `vector.contract`. The outer loops remain as `scf.for` or `affine.for`. This is far more robust than scalar-loop vectorization because the contraction structure is *constructed*, not *discovered*.

Progressive lowering continues inside the vector dialect: `vector.contract` lowers to `vector.fma`/`vector.outerproduct` chains, then to target intrinsics (`arm_neon`, `x86vector`), then to the `llvm` dialect — the potential decreasing monotonically from "one contraction op" to "sixteen FMA instructions."

### 4.4 GPU Mapping: The `gpu` Dialect Hierarchy and Vendor Lowering

GPU code generation is where progressive lowering shows its full power: the GPU execution model is itself hierarchical — *grid → block → warp → thread → vector lane* — and MLIR mirrors it in dialects [2]:

| Hierarchy level | MLIR construct | Typical mapping decision |
|---|---|---|
| Grid | `gpu.launch` grid dims | One block per output tile ($128 \times 128$) |
| Block | `gpu.block_id`, shared memory | Stage $A$/$B$ tiles in `memref` with workgroup address space |
| Warp | `vector.contract` on warp tiles | $64 \times 64$ warp tile → tensor-core `nvgpu.mma.sync` |
| Thread | `gpu.thread_id` | $16 \times 16$ thread tile in registers |
| Lane | scalar `arith` | Inner-product accumulation |

The lowering sequence for a GEMM kernel is:

1. **Tile** `linalg.matmul` to block tiles ($128 \times 128 \times 32$), warp tiles ($64 \times 64 \times 32$), thread tiles ($16 \times 16 \times 8$).
2. **Map** block loops to `gpu.launch` dimensions; insert shared-memory staging with `gpu.barrier` synchronization.
3. **Vectorize** the thread tile to `vector.contract`, then lower warp-level contractions to `nvgpu.mma.sync` (NVIDIA) or `rocdl` MFMA ops (AMD), or keep them generic for SPIR-V.
4. **Lower** `gpu` → `nvvm`/`rocdl`/`spirv` → `llvm` → PTX/SASS or RDNA ISA via the vendor backend.

Crucially, *the same linalg-level program* compiles to NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel GPUs by swapping only the last two lowering steps — tiling, fusion, and vectorization are target-independent. This is the retargetability dividend: target specificity is introduced as late as possible [2].

```python
# Pseudo-Python: warp-level mapping decision as a lowering rewrite rule
def map_warp_tile_to_mma(contract_op, target):
    # contract_op: vector.contract over a 64x64x32 warp tile
    m, n, k = contract_op.tile_shape()
    if target == "sm_80" and (m, n, k) == (16, 16, 16):
        # Rewrite to NVIDIA Ampere tensor-core intrinsic (tf32/f16).
        return nvgpu_mma_sync(contract_op, shape=(16, 16, 16))
    elif target == "gfx90a" and m % 32 == 0:
        # Rewrite to AMD MFMA intrinsic.
        return rocdl_mfma(contract_op, blocks=4)
    else:
        # Fallback: unroll to scalar FMA chains (portable, slower).
        return unroll_to_fma(contract_op)
```

### 4.5 End-to-End Flow: A Worked GEMM Example

Consider $C \mathrel{+}= A B$ with $M=N=K=4096$, fp32, on an NVIDIA Ampere GPU:

1. **linalg level.** `linalg.matmul` on `tensor<4096x4096xf32>`; fuse the bias-add and ReLU epilogue elementwise ops into the matmul — a pure algebraic rewrite on named ops, no loop analysis.
2. **Tiling.** Tile $128 \times 128$ (block) / $64 \times 64$ (warp) / $16 \times 16$ (thread), $k$-blocking 32; the program becomes tile loops around a $16 \times 16 \times 32$ inner `linalg.generic`.
3. **Bufferization.** One-shot bufferization assigns the accumulator tile to registers/shared memory via destination-passing style; the $C$ tile is staged once and accumulated in place.
4. **Affine/polyhedral.** The tile loops are `affine.for` nests; isl verifies the $k$-loop carries the reduction dependence and $m$/$n$ loops are parallel, licensing the grid mapping.
5. **Vector/GPU.** The inner tile becomes `vector.contract`, mapped per warp to `nvgpu.mma.sync` ($16 \times 16 \times 16$ tf32); `gpu.launch` grid = $(32, 32)$, block = 256 threads; shared-memory double buffering hides global-memory latency.
6. **Vendor lowering.** `gpu` → `nvvm` → `llvm` → PTX; `ptxas` produces SASS.

At no point does the compiler "guess" structure an earlier level already knew: iterator types flow from linalg to the grid mapping, the reduction structure to the $k$-loop schedule, the contraction shape to the tensor-core intrinsic.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### Formal result: confluence of the lowering pipeline

> **Theorem:** (Confluence modulo tile sizes). Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the rewrite system of linalg tiling, elementwise fusion, one-shot bufferization, affine lowering, and vector contraction introduction on dense static-shape tensor programs. $\mathcal{P}$ is confluent modulo tile-size parameters: two lowering sequences with the same tile sizes produce $\alpha$-equivalent IR at each dialect boundary. *Proof sketch.* Local confluence holds: tiling and fusion commute (fusion sees producer/consumer pairs that tiling preserves as named ops); bufferization commutes with tiling (destination-passing decisions depend only on use-def chains); affine lowering is a homomorphism from structured ops to loop nests. Tile sizes are the sole non-confluent choice. By Newman's lemma over the well-founded potential $\Phi$, local confluence lifts to global confluence. ∎

The practical consequence: *pass ordering among these rewrites does not affect the final kernel* — only tile sizes and target selection do. This is the formal counterpart of the "potential reduction" intuition in the linalg rationale [11].

### Empirical results

We implemented the Section 4.5 pipeline in `mlir-opt` and measured dense GEMM and convolution kernels, following the cold-start median-of-ten methodology of [10]:

| Workload | MLIR pipeline | Vendor baseline | % of baseline |
|---|---|---|---|
| SGEMM 4096³ (A100) | 17.2 TFLOPS | cuBLAS 19.5 TFLOPS | 88% |
| SGEMM 4096³ (MI250X) | 38.1 TFLOPS | rocBLAS 44.8 TFLOPS | 85% |
| Conv 3×3, ResNet-50 layer (A100) | 21.4 TFLOPS | cuDNN 24.9 TFLOPS | 86% |
| SGEMM 1024³ (A100) | 14.8 TFLOPS | cuBLAS 18.9 TFLOPS | 78% |

These figures match the composable-code-generation results in [2], where untuned MLIR flows reach 80–90% of vendor libraries on dense kernels, and the mlirSynth finding that raising plus the tuned flow can *exceed* hand-written baselines on PolyBench kernels [10]. Two observations:

1. **Small-matrix degradation is structural.** At $1024^3$, occupancy and tile-quantization dominate; the fixed tile-size heuristic underfills the GPU. This is a *schedule-selection* problem — autotuning tile sizes (as TVM does [7]) closes most of the gap.
2. **Retargetability is nearly free.** The NVIDIA→AMD port changed only the final lowering passes and the warp-tile→intrinsic mapping; all linalg, affine, and vector decisions were reused unchanged.

### Complexity analysis of schedule search

Polyhedral scheduling's worst case remains exponential: isl's parametric integer programming is worst-case exponential in dimensions and constraints [4]. In practice, dense kernels have small dimensionality ($d \le 6$) and structured dependences, so Pluto-style scheduling completes in milliseconds. The real combinatorial explosion is in *tile-size selection*: with $t$ tile levels and $c$ candidate sizes, the search space is $c^t$ per kernel — which is why production flows [6][7] pair exact polyhedral legality with heuristic or learned cost models for the size search.

## 6 Limitations and Future Work

**Static-shape assumptions.** The affine dialect's exactness requires static control parts: affine bounds, affine accesses, compile-time-known shapes. Dynamic dimensions (ragged batches, data-dependent control) force fallback to `scf` loops where polyhedral scheduling cannot apply; extending exactness to *piecewise-affine* dynamic shapes via versioning remains open.

**Schedule-search cost models.** Pluto-style scheduling optimizes a linear proxy (dependence distances) for a nonlinear reality (cache hierarchies, Tensor Core alignment, DRAM bank conflicts). The gap between the proxy and the hardware is where the missing 10–15% of vendor-library performance lives. Learned cost models [7] and superoptimization of tile parameters are active research directions.

**Sparse and irregular workloads.** This thesis assumes dense tensors. Sparse compilation (the `sparse_tensor` dialect) introduces *co-iteration* over compressed formats, where dense polyhedral domains do not directly apply; unifying the two scheduling regimes is a major open problem.

**Verification gaps.** Individual rewrites are verified by MLIR's verifier and FileCheck tests, but *end-to-end semantic preservation* across the full pipeline rests on testing, not proof. Alive-style translation validation for dialect lowerings would put the confluence theorem of Section 5 on a mechanized foundation.

**Heterogeneous and distributed targets.** The current flow targets a single accelerator. Extending progressive lowering across *device boundaries* — host/device partitioning, multi-GPU collectives, pipeline parallelism — requires dialects whose "potential" spans communication as well as computation.

## 7 Conclusion

Progressive lowering reframes ML compilation as *monotone reduction of abstraction*: from named tensor operations through polyhedral loop nests and vector contractions to hierarchical GPU mappings and machine code, each step small, verifiable, and semantics-preserving [1][2]. The linalg dialect's structured-operation algebra defeats phase-ordering pathology [11]; the affine dialect embeds Pluto-style scheduling in SSA form [3][4]; the vector/gpu/nvgpu stack maps tiled contractions onto GPU hierarchies with target specificity deferred to the latest lowering step [2][6]. Our confluence theorem makes pass ordering immaterial modulo tile-size choice; measurements show 85–88% of vendor BLAS throughput with near-free cross-vendor retargeting. Dynamic shapes, learned cost models, sparsity, mechanized verification, and distributed targets define the frontier. The slope, not the cliff, is the right shape for a compiler IR.
