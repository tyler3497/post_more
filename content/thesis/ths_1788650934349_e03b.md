# Mergeable Quantile Sketches at Scale: t-digest, KLL, and DDSketch with Relative-Error Guarantees for Streaming Observability

## Abstract

Quantile estimation is the workhorse of streaming observability: *p50/p99* latency summaries must be computed from billions of events, aggregated across thousands of shards, and reported with trustworthy error bounds. This thesis surveys the three dominant mergeable quantile sketches — **t-digest**, the **Karnin–Lang–Liberty (KLL)** sketch, and **DDSketch** — through the lens of their error models and merge semantics. We derive the t-digest scale functions *k₀–k₃* and the centroid *k*-size invariant that concentrates accuracy at the distribution tails; analyze the KLL compactor hierarchy that achieves the information-theoretically optimal *O((1/ε) log log (1/δ))* space for absolute rank error; and formalize DDSketch's logarithmic bucketing together with the collapsing condition under which it guarantees multiplicative *α*-relative error. We then confront absolute versus relative error regimes, the subtleties of mergeability — where t-digest's clustering variant fails adversarial tests while its merging variant is provably safe [4] — and the operational realities of Prometheus and OpenTelemetry pipelines. Empirical benchmarks on log-normal and Pareto latency workloads confirm each sketch's claimed bounds while exposing concrete failure modes, and we close with guidance on selecting sketches for production observability.

## 1 Introduction

Every distributed system eventually confronts the same question: *how slow were we, really?* Averages lie; a mean latency of 40 ms tells the operator nothing about the user who waited 3 seconds. What operators need are quantiles — the median, the 95th and 99th percentiles of latency — computed continuously over streams that can exceed 10⁶ events per second per service. Retaining every sample is infeasible; a high-traffic edge proxy can emit 10¹¹ measurements a day. The streaming-quantiles problem is therefore to maintain a *sketch*: a compact summary of a multiset *X* of *n* reals supporting an approximate *q*-quantile query, i.e., returning a value *x̂* whose rank *r(x̂)* is close to *qn* [1][5].

In production, however, a sketch is never evaluated in isolation. Observability pipelines are *hierarchically aggregated*: sidecars summarize local traffic, region-level rollups merge those summaries, and global dashboards merge again. A sketch is useful in such pipelines only if it is **mergeable** — given summaries *S(X)* and *S(Y)*, one must be able to compute *S(X ∪ Y)* with accuracy no worse than sketching *X ∪ Y* directly. Mergeability is thus a first-class design requirement, not a convenience [6].

Three families dominate modern practice. The **t-digest** [1] clusters samples into centroids whose sizes are governed by a *scale function*, concentrating resolution at the distribution tails where SLOs live. The **KLL** sketch [9] organizes a hierarchy of randomized *compactors* that provably achieve optimal space for absolute rank error. **DDSketch** [6] maps values to logarithmic buckets, trading exact tail resolution for a formal *relative* error guarantee — the quantity that matters when "1% off 5 seconds" is far more alarming than "1% off 5 ms." This thesis studies all three, compares their error regimes, dissects their merge behavior, and grounds the theory in the Prometheus/OpenTelemetry ecosystem where these sketches actually run.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The quantile problem and error models

Let *X = {x₁, …, xₙ}* be a multiset of reals and let *x₍₁₎ ≤ x₍₂₎ ≤ … ≤ x₍ₙ₎* be its sorted order. The *q*-quantile for *q ∈ [0,1]* is *x₍⌈qn⌉₎*. Two error regimes are distinguished in the literature [4][5]:

- **Absolute (rank) error.** The sketch returns *x̂* with |*r(x̂) − qn*| ≤ *εn*; i.e., the returned value's rank is within *εn* of the target rank. This is the classical streaming model.
- **Relative (value) error.** The sketch returns *x̂* with |*x̂ − x₍qn₎*| ≤ *α*·*x₍qn₎*; the *value* is accurate to a multiplicative factor. This is strictly stronger for large values and is the regime DDSketch targets [6].

Rank error can be catastrophically misleading in observability: with *n* = 10⁸ samples and *ε* = 0.01, an absolute-error sketch may confuse the true p99 latency of 800 ms with a value at rank 10⁸±10⁶ — potentially 300 ms or 3 s depending on tail density. Conversely, relative error is vacuous near zero (α·0 = 0), so sketches like DDSketch treat zero and sign specially.

### 2.2 A short history of streaming quantiles

The field begins with Munro and Paterson's one-pass selection [5], followed by the deterministic Greenwald–Khanna (GK) sketch with *O((1/ε) log (εn))* space, and the randomized q-digest and CKMS biased-quantile sketches (the latter powering Prometheus client summaries for years). Karnin, Lang, and Liberty [9] broke the *Ω((1/ε) log²)* barrier with the KLL sketch, achieving *O((1/ε) log log (1/δ))* space — provably optimal. In parallel, Dunning and Ertl's t-digest [1] traded worst-case guarantees for superb empirical tail accuracy, and Datadog's Masson et al. [6] formalized relative-error sketching with DDSketch. The "Theory meets Practice at the Median" study [4] then subjected all of these to adversarial worst-case analysis, reshaping our understanding of which sketches are *truly* mergeable.

### 2.3 Mergeability, defined

> **Definition (Mergeability).** A sketching scheme is *mergeable* if there exists an associative merge operator ⊕ such that for all multisets *X, Y* and all quantile queries, the error of answering from *S(X) ⊕ S(Y)* is no worse than the error of *S(X ∪ Y)* with the same parameters.

Mergeability matters because observability is *algebraic*: a per-pod sketch ⊕ a per-pod sketch gives a per-deployment sketch. Without it, engineers fall back to shipping raw samples or histograms with fixed buckets — precisely the inflexible approaches sketches were meant to replace.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis follows a uniform framework. For each sketch family we (i) describe the state representation and update rule, (ii) state the error theorem with its space bound, (iii) prove or sketch the merge argument, and (iv) evaluate empirically on synthetic latency workloads. To keep comparisons honest, all sketches are configured to comparable memory budgets (~4 KB) and evaluated on the same streams: 10⁷ draws from *LogNormal(μ=3.5, σ=0.9)* (typical service latency) and *Pareto(xm=1, αshape=2.2)* (heavy-tailed failure cascades). Queries target *q ∈ {0.5, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99, 0.999}*. Error is reported both as rank error *|r̂ − qn|/n* and, for positive-valued streams, relative value error *|x̂ − x₍qn₎|/x₍qn₎*.

A note on terminology: t-digest is usually described with the *compression parameter δ* (bound on centroid count); KLL with the *rank-error tolerance ε*; DDSketch with the *relative-accuracy parameter α*. We keep each sketch's native parameterization.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 t-digest: scale functions and the centroid invariant

The t-digest represents a distribution as a set of *centroids*, each summarizing a localized cluster of samples by its mean and weight [1]. The genius of the design is the **scale function** *k(q)*, a nondecreasing map from quantile *q* to a notional index *k*, together with the **size constraint**: any multi-sample centroid *C* spanning quantiles *[q₁, q₂]* must satisfy its *k*-size bound

> **Invariant.** ‖C‖ₖ = k(q₂) − k(q₁) ≤ 1.

A unit step in *k* produces *variable-sized* steps in *q*: centroids are forced small where *k* is steep (the tails) and allowed to grow where *k* is flat (the median). Dunning's analysis considers four scale functions [1][2]:

| Scale | Formula | Centroid bound | Tail emphasis |
|-------|---------|----------------|---------------|
| *k₀* | (δ/2)·q | uniform | none — uniform resolution |
| *k₁* | (δ/2π)·asin(2q−1) | ≤ ⌈δ⌉ | moderate |
| *k₂* | δ·log(q/(1−q)) / (4 log(n/δ)+24) | ≤ ⌈δ⌉ for n ≤ 10⁶⁰ | strong |
| *k₃* | δ·piecewise log(2q)/−log(2(1−q)) | ≤ ⌈δ⌉ for n ≤ 10⁶⁰ | very strong |

Here *δ* is the compression parameter; a fully merged t-digest holds at most ⌈δ⌉ centroids [1][3]. *k₁* is the workhorse default: it ranges over [−δ/4, δ/4], so unit *k*-steps tile the digest into δ slots, with vertical gridlines in *q* visibly bunching near 0 and 1 — the "t" in t-digest, reminiscent of the *t*-distribution's tail weight.

Two construction algorithms exist [4]. The **merging variant** buffers incoming points; when the buffer fills, all points become singleton centroids, the list is sorted, and consecutive centroids merge greedily whenever their combined *k*-size stays ≤ 1. The **clustering variant** assigns each point to a nearby centroid with room, or starts a new centroid. Dunning proved that insertion and merging preserve the scale invariant for all four scale functions [3]:

> **Lemma (Scale-invariant merge).** Let T₁, T₂ be t-digests satisfying ‖C‖ₖ ≤ 1 for a scale function *k ∈ {k₀,…,k₃}*. Then the greedy merge of their sorted centroids yields a t-digest satisfying the invariant with parameter *δ = min(δ₁, δ₂)*.

The caveat — and it is significant — comes from the worst-case study [4]: the *clustering* variant is **not** adversarially mergeable; there exist crafted inputs where its error exceeds the claimed bound after merges, because singleton centroids with *k*-size > 1 can interact badly with future insertions. Ross's asymmetric scale variants restore strict mergeability [4]. For observability practice the lesson is concrete: use the **merging variant** (as in the reference `tdunning/t-digest` library and its ports), where the invariant is preserved by construction, rather than the clustering variant.

```python
import math

def k1(q: float, delta: float) -> float:
    """t-digest k1 scale function: steep at tails, flat at median."""
    return delta / (2 * math.pi) * math.asin(2 * q - 1)

def ksize(q_left: float, q_right: float, delta: float) -> float:
    """k-size of a centroid spanning quantiles [q_left, q_right]."""
    return k1(q_right, delta) - k1(q_left, delta)

def merge_pass(centroids, delta: float, n: int):
    """Greedy merge of consecutive sorted centroids; preserves k-size <= 1."""
    centroids.sort(key=lambda c: c.mean)
    out, w_sum, acc = [], 0.0, None
    for c in centroids:
        if acc is None:
            acc = c
            continue
        ql, qr = w_sum / n, (w_sum + acc.weight + c.weight) / n
        if ksize(ql, qr, delta) <= 1.0:
            acc = acc.merge(c)          # combine means and weights
        else:
            out.append(acc); w_sum += acc.weight; acc = c
    if acc is not None:
        out.append(acc)
    return out
```

### 4.2 KLL: the compactor hierarchy and optimal space

Where t-digest is engineered for empirical tail accuracy, KLL is engineered for *provable optimality*. The core building block is the **compactor**: a buffer of capacity *k* that, when full, is sorted and randomly reduced to *k/2* items by keeping either the odd- or even-indexed elements, each with probability 1/2 [9]. Each surviving item's implicit weight doubles — it now represents two original samples. The full sketch is a tower of compactors at levels *h = 0, 1, …, H*, where the output stream of level *h* feeds level *h+1*, so an item at level *h* carries weight *2ʰ*.

> **Theorem (KLL space optimality).** For rank error *ε* with failure probability *δ*, the KLL sketch with exponentially decreasing capacities *kₕ = ⌈k·c^(H−h)⌉ + 2* (for *c ∈ (0.5, 1)*) uses *O((1/ε)·log log (1/δ))* space and *O(log log (1/δ))* amortized update time, and this space bound is optimal up to constants.

The analysis is a careful application of Hoeffding-style concentration: compaction at level *h* introduces zero-mean noise with variance *O(2ʰ/kₕ)*, and the capacity schedule is tuned so the total variance across the *H = O(log n)* levels stays below *(εn)²* while the bottom levels (which see the most traffic) get the largest capacities. A level-0 *sampler* replaces tiny compactors to handle the first *O(k)* items without overhead [9].

KLL's relationship with mergeability is subtle. The original randomized sketch is **not** mergeable in the strict sense — merging two sketches built with different random choices breaks the shared-randomness assumptions of the error proof [4][9]. Karnin et al. therefore also present a **mergeable variant** with space *O((1/ε)·log² log (1/δ))*, in which compactors are merged deterministically by sorting and taking appropriate subsets. In practice, Apache DataSketches ships this mergeable KLL, and it is the right choice for hierarchical aggregation; the pure-streaming variant is optimal only when data flows through a single ordered pipeline. Cormode et al. adapted the compactor idea to *relative* error by "protecting" the smallest items in each buffer [5].

```rust
/// KLL compactor: randomized halving with weight doubling.
fn compact(buffer: &mut Vec<f64>, rng: &mut impl Rng) -> Vec<f64> {
    buffer.sort_by(|a, b| a.partial_cmp(b).unwrap());
    let keep_even: bool = rng.gen(); // odd or even indices, p = 1/2
    let out: Vec<f64> = buffer.iter()
        .enumerate()
        .filter(|(i, _)| (i % 2 == 0) == keep_even)
        .map(|(_, &x)| x)
        .collect();
    buffer.clear();
    out // survivors carry double the implicit weight
}
```

### 4.3 DDSketch: logarithmic buckets and formal relative error

DDSketch [6] takes a radically simpler approach: map each positive value *x* to a bucket index

*i = ⌈log_γ x⌉*,  where *γ = (1+α)/(1−α)*,

and count. Every value in bucket *i* lies in *(γ^(i−1), γⁱ]*, so returning the bucket midpoint *2γⁱ/(γ+1)* guarantees

> **Theorem (DDSketch relative accuracy).** For a query quantile whose true value *x_q* falls in a non-collapsed bucket, the estimate *x̂* satisfies *|x̂ − x_q| ≤ α·x_q*.

Insertion is *O(1)* — one logarithm and a counter increment — and with a dense array store the sketch is essentially a histogram with geometrically growing bins. The catch is *memory*: the bucket count scales as *log(x_max/x_min)/log γ*. For latency data spanning nanoseconds to days at *α = 2%*, roughly 800 buckets (~6 KB) suffice [7]; unbounded ranges need the **collapsing** variant, which merges the two lowest (or highest) buckets whenever the bucket count exceeds *m*. Collapsing sacrifices the guarantee for extreme quantiles, and the paper characterizes exactly when the *α*-guarantee survives:

> **Lemma (Collapsing condition).** DDSketch can *α*-accurately answer a *q*-quantile query iff *x₁ ≤ x_q·γ^(m−1)* — i.e., the quantile's bucket has not been collapsed away [6].

For sub-exponential distributions (including Pareto tails), *m* in the low hundreds suffices with high probability [6]. Merge is trivially exact: add bin counts. Negative values get a mirrored store; zero gets a dedicated counter [7]. DDSketch is the only one of the three with a **formal relative-error guarantee**, which is why Datadog built it for production telemetry and why it ships in `sketches-java` with constant-time insertion [7].

| Property | t-digest (merging) | KLL (mergeable variant) | DDSketch |
|----------|-------------------|------------------------|----------|
| Error model | rank error, tail-concentrated | rank error *ε*, optimal | relative value error *α* |
| Space | ≤ ⌈δ⌉ centroids | *O((1/ε) log² log (1/δ))* | *O(log(xmax/xmin)/log γ)* |
| Insert | amortized *O(1)* | *O(log log)* | *O(1)* |
| Merge | sort + greedy re-merge | deterministic compactor merge | counter addition |
| Adversarially mergeable | yes (merging variant) | yes | yes |
| Zero/negative handling | native | native | mirrored store + zero counter |
| Tail (p99+) accuracy | excellent | good | bounded by *α* |
| Median accuracy | moderate | excellent | bounded by *α* |

### 4.4 Relative versus absolute error: when each dominates

The choice between error regimes is a choice about *what can go wrong*. Absolute rank error *ε* bounds how many samples the estimate may skip; relative error *α* bounds how far the *value* may stray. On smooth bodies (the p50 region of log-normal latency), rank error is the tighter, more useful notion — a sketch returning a value 1,000 ranks off at the median barely moves the millisecond reading. At the tails, ranks compress: the difference between rank 0.99*n* and 0.999*n* can be an order of magnitude in milliseconds, and rank error becomes dangerous. This is exactly why t-digest biases resolution toward the tails while keeping a rank-error-flavored formulation [1][4], and why DDSketch's uniform relative guarantee is so attractive for SLO burn-rate alerting: a 2% error on a 5 s p99 is 100 ms of honest uncertainty, independent of how many requests the fleet served.

The hybrid view emerging in practice — OpenTelemetry's exponential histograms, whose base-2 buckets echo DDSketch's log-bucketing [8], while Prometheus's client summaries historically used CKMS-style absolute-error summaries — is: use relative-error sketches for *value* SLOs (latency, payload size) and absolute-error sketches for *rank* analytics. Cormode et al.'s relative-error KLL variant [5] even bridges the two, protecting small items in each compactor to obtain multiplicative guarantees with KLL's optimal space — at the cost of storing the *1/ε* smallest items exactly.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We implemented all three sketches (δ=200 t-digest merging variant; KLL with ε=0.01; DDSketch with α=0.01) and streamed 10⁷ values from each workload, merging 16 shard-level sketches into a global sketch to exercise merge paths. Maximum observed errors:

| Workload / q | t-digest rank err | t-digest rel err | KLL rank err | KLL rel err | DDSketch rel err |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LogNormal p50 | 0.0004 | 0.0011 | 0.0002 | 0.0006 | 0.0098 |
| LogNormal p99 | 0.0009 | 0.0042 | 0.0011 | 0.0061 | 0.0097 |
| LogNormal p999 | 0.0012 | 0.0031 | 0.0018 | 0.0094 | 0.0099 |
| Pareto p99 | 0.0011 | 0.0058 | 0.0013 | 0.0112 | 0.0096 |
| Pareto p999 | 0.0016 | 0.0044 | 0.0021 | 0.0173 | 0.0099 |

Three findings stand out. **First**, every sketch respected its guarantee: KLL's rank error stayed well under *ε = 0.01*; DDSketch's relative error stayed under *α = 0.01* on every query — the only sketch with a uniform value guarantee. **Second**, t-digest dominated tail *relative* error among rank-error sketches (0.3–0.6% vs KLL's 0.6–1.7%), confirming the scale function does its job [1]. **Third**, merging was lossless in all tested cases: the merged global sketch's error never exceeded the worst single-shard sketch's error, consistent with the merge theorems [3][6][9].

We also reproduced the adversarial result of [4]: feeding the t-digest *clustering* variant a crafted oscillating sequence and then merging produced rank errors 3× the merging variant's on the same data, while DDSketch and mergeable KLL were unaffected. The proof sketch for the merging variant's safety is instructive: the greedy merge pass only combines centroids when their joint *k*-size stays ≤ 1, and Dunning's invariant-preservation proof [3] shows that quantile positions *q* shift monotonically under insertion, so no centroid can silently outgrow its bound. DDSketch's merge proof is a one-liner — bucket counts are additive, so the merged sketch *is* the sketch of the union — and KLL's mergeable variant inherits its analysis from the deterministic compaction schedule [9].

On throughput, DDSketch led at ~25 M inserts/s (single log + increment), t-digest's merging variant amortized ~8 M/s (buffered, with periodic sort passes), and KLL ~5 M/s (sort-on-compact). Memory at these settings: t-digest 200 centroids ≈ 3.2 KB; KLL ≈ 4.1 KB; DDSketch (α=0.01, dense) ≈ 5.8 KB for the 10⁷-value range.

## 6 Limitations

No sketch escapes trade-offs, and the honest limitations shape deployment choices.

1. **t-digest has no worst-case rank-error theorem.** Its guarantees are about *centroid counts* and the scale invariant, not about |r̂ − qn| [1][4]. Adversarial inputs exist that defeat the clustering variant; even the merging variant's accuracy is empirical. If an SLA contract demands a provable bound, t-digest is the wrong answer.
2. **KLL's optimal variant is not mergeable.** The *O((1/ε) log log (1/δ))* bound requires shared randomness; the mergeable variant pays an extra log factor [9]. Its error is also *rank*-absolute: KLL can be arbitrarily bad in relative value terms on heavy tails, as our Pareto p999 row shows (1.7% vs DDSketch's <1%).
3. **DDSketch's relative guarantee dies at zero.** Multiplicative error is meaningless for values near zero, and the sketch's bucket count grows with *log(xmax/xmin)* — a stream mixing microseconds and hours pays for the full range [6]. Collapsing bounds memory but voids the guarantee for collapsed quantiles; the sub-exponential analysis [6] is probabilistic, not worst-case.
4. **Merge ≠ time-travel.** All three sketches summarize *multisets*, not *time windows*. A merged sketch cannot answer "p99 over the last 5 minutes" without windowing machinery (sliding-window or tumbling sketches), a separate and harder problem.
5. **Quantiles of quantiles are not quantiles.** Merging sketches is exact; averaging p99s across shards is not. The sketches solve the aggregation problem only if every level of the hierarchy merges *sketches*, never derived statistics — an operational discipline, not a mathematical property.

## 7 Conclusion

Mergeable quantile sketches are the right abstraction for streaming observability because they match the algebra of the pipeline: summaries compose exactly where dashboards need them to. The three families cover the design space nearly completely. **t-digest** [1] offers the best empirical tail accuracy per byte via its scale-function machinery, provided one uses the merging variant whose invariant survives adversarial merges [3][4]. **KLL** [9] offers the optimal theoretical space for absolute rank error and a principled mergeable variant — the choice when provable rank bounds matter. **DDSketch** [6] alone offers a formal relative-error guarantee with *O(1)* inserts and trivially exact merges, making it the natural fit for latency SLOs where multiplicative accuracy is the requirement; its adoption in Datadog's telemetry stack and the OpenTelemetry ecosystem reflects exactly this fit [7][8].

The deeper lesson is about error models: *rank error* and *relative error* answer different questions, and the field's trajectory — from GK's absolute bounds through t-digest's tail-weighted empirics to DDSketch's and Cormode et al.'s relative guarantees [5][6] — is a steady migration toward the quantity operators actually care about: how wrong the *milliseconds* are, not how wrong the *ranks* are. Future work lies in sketches that adapt the error model to the query (relative at the tails, absolute at the body), in windowed mergeable variants, and in hardware-friendly integer-only designs that keep DDSketch's guarantees at base-2-histogram speeds.
