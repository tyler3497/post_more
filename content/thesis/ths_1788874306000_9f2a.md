# The Domain Name System at Global Scale: Anycast Routing, the DNSSEC Chain of Trust, Encrypted DNS (DoH/DoT/DoQ), and Cache-Poisoning Defenses from Kaminsky to Port Randomization

## Abstract

The Domain Name System resolves an estimated two trillion queries per day through a distributed hierarchy whose correctness, availability, and confidentiality underpin virtually every Internet application. This thesis unifies four pillars of DNS at planetary scale: *(i)* anycast routing, by which thirteen logical root-server identities are instantiated as thousands of physical instances; *(ii)* DNSSEC, the public-key chain of trust anchored in hard-coded trust anchors that authenticates data from root to leaf; *(iii)* encrypted transports — DNS over TLS, HTTPS, and QUIC — that close the passive-surveillance channel of cleartext resolution while concentrating trust in a few large resolvers; and *(iv)* layered defenses against cache poisoning, from the Kaminsky birthday-paradox attack of 2008 through source-port randomization and bailiwick hardening. Synthesizing protocol specifications and probabilistic attack models, we derive quantitative forgery-resistance bounds, characterize the residual attack surface after each mitigation, and identify open problems in key management, algorithm agility, and the tension between privacy and operational visibility.

## 1 Introduction

Few artifacts of the early Internet have survived global scale as intact — and as repeatedly stressed — as the Domain Name System. Conceived by Paul Mockapetris in RFCs 1034 and 1035 (1987) as a three-component architecture of *namespace*, *name servers*, and *resolvers* [1], DNS was designed for correctness and delegation, not adversarial conditions. Its original threat model was essentially empty: queries and responses traveled as unauthenticated UDP datagrams, distinguishable only by a 16-bit transaction identifier (TXID), and any party able to spoof packets on the path could substitute answers at will.

Three decades of scaling have transformed that modest design into the most-queried database in existence while layering onto it four largely independent engineering regimes:

1. **Routing resilience.** The thirteen root-server identities cannot be thirteen machines; through IP anycast, each identity is announced from hundreds of physical instances, converting the root into a geographically smeared, self-healing mesh [6][7].
2. **Data authenticity.** DNSSEC (RFCs 4033–4035) adds a public-key signature hierarchy so resolvers can *verify* answers rather than merely *receive* them [5].
3. **Transport confidentiality.** DNS over TLS, HTTPS, and QUIC encrypt the stub-to-recursive path, eliminating passive eavesdropping — at the cost of concentrating trust in a few large public resolvers [2][3][4][12].
4. **Forgery resistance.** The 2008 Kaminsky vulnerability (CVE-2008-1447) showed the birthday paradox could collapse DNS's 16-bit forgery barrier; the emergency response — source-port randomization — raised the bar to roughly 32 bits, with bailiwick rules and response-rate limiting hardening the protocol's edges [8][9][10].

This thesis examines these four regimes as one system. Our contributions are: (a) a consolidated architectural account of how anycast, DNSSEC, encryption, and anti-poisoning defenses interact; (b) quantitative bounds on cache-poisoning resistance before and after each mitigation; (c) a comparative analysis of encrypted transports across latency, censorship resistance, and centralization; and (d) an explicit inventory of what remains unsolved.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The resolution model

DNS names form a tree read right-to-left. Resolving `www.example.com.` from a cold cache is three questions, not one [2]: the recursive resolver asks a root server, which returns a *referral* to the `.com` servers; it asks a `.com` server, which refers to the `example.com` servers; it asks one of those, which returns the `A` record. Every response carries a TTL, and caching at each layer is what converts this multi-roundtrip procedure into the sub-millisecond answers users experience. Caching is also what makes poisoning valuable: one forged response, accepted once, is served to every subsequent client until the TTL expires.

The original wire format constrained responses to 512 bytes over UDP (RFC 1035); oversized responses set the truncation bit and retry over TCP — a fallback firewall operators break at their peril, since DNSSEC answers routinely exceed the limit [2]. Extension Mechanisms for DNS (EDNS0, RFC 6891) let resolvers advertise larger buffers; current guidance advertises 1232 bytes to stay beneath common IPv6 fragmentation thresholds.

### 2.2 Scale figures

The thirteen root identities are operated by twelve independent organizations — Verisign (A and J), USC-ISI, Cogent, the University of Maryland, NASA, ISC, the U.S. Department of Defense, the U.S. Army Research Lab, Netnod, RIPE NCC, ICANN, and the WIDE Project [7]. The "13" is a legacy packet-size artifact: the priming response had to fit in one 512-byte UDP datagram [3]. In practice the root is served by on the order of two thousand anycast instances across more than 130 countries [7].

### 2.3 Threat model

We assume a network adversary that can send, receive, and spoof packets from arbitrary source addresses (subject to whatever source-address validation the path enforces), but cannot break cryptographic primitives. Three attacker positions matter:

- **Off-path.** Cannot observe the victim's traffic; must *guess* the TXID and source port to forge a response. This is the Kaminsky adversary [8][10].
- **On-path.** Observes queries and can race or rewrite them; transport encryption defeats passive observation, DNSSEC defeats active rewriting [5][12].
- **Resolver operator.** Sees all queries in cleartext regardless of transport encryption — the centralization concern of the DoH era [12].

> **Theorem (Forgery barrier):** Against an off-path adversary, a response is accepted only if it matches the 5-tuple *(source IP, destination IP, source port, destination port, TXID)* and arrives before the legitimate response. Every defense in §4.3 enlarges the space the adversary must guess or shrinks the window in which a guess counts.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a synthesis study. Our method has three strands:

1. **Specification analysis.** We trace normative requirements through RFC 1034/1035, RFCs 4033–4035 and 5155 (DNSSEC/NSEC3), RFC 7858 (DoT), RFC 8484 (DoH), RFC 9250 (DoQ), and RFC 5452 (port randomization) [1][2][3][4][5].
2. **Probabilistic attack modeling.** For cache poisoning we adopt the birthday-paradox framing of Kaminsky's attack and its formal treatment via probabilistic model checking (Alexiou *et al.*, 2010), computing expected time-to-success as a function of TXID entropy, port entropy, and the race window against the legitimate response [8][10][11].
3. **Comparative transport analysis.** For DoT, DoH, and DoQ we compare handshake latency, head-of-line blocking, port identifiability, and the resulting censorship-resistance and centralization trade-offs [2][3][4][12].

We do not claim novel measurements; the empirical results in §5 consolidate published figures (patch timelines, instance counts, adoption statistics) into a single quantitative picture.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Anycast routing at the root and TLD edge

Anycast is routing's answer to "which server?": the *same* IP prefix is announced via BGP from many locations, and each router forwards toward the lowest-cost announcement. A query to `198.41.0.4` (A-root) is answered by whichever instance BGP deems closest [6].

Three properties make anycast load-bearing for DNS at scale:

- **DDoS absorption.** Attack traffic is partitioned across instances by the same mechanism that partitions legitimate traffic. The 2002 and 2007 DDoS events against the root system were absorbed largely because anycast contained the blast radius of each targeted instance [6].
- **Latency minimization.** Clients are served by nearby instances; early K-root anycast deployments demonstrated order-of-magnitude latency drops for local communities [6].
- **Failure containment.** If an instance fails, BGP reconverges and traffic shifts to the next-nearest announcement — *without any DNS-protocol involvement*.

Anycast is not free. Because successive packets may land on different instances, anycast is a poor fit for long-lived stateful connections — one reason DNS's stateless UDP query model synergizes with it, and one reason encrypted transports (§4.4) are deployed primarily on the *stub-to-recursive* path. Operators also contend with *route flapping* and the opacity of debugging: a traceroute shows *an* instance, never *the* instance set.

| Property | Unicast authoritative | Anycast authoritative |
|---|---|---|
| Failure domain | Single site / prefix | Partitioned across instances |
| DDoS handling | Must absorb full volume at one point | Volume diluted by BGP topology |
| Latency | Determined by one location | Near-minimal for most clients |
| Session state | Natural affinity | Requires care; favors stateless UDP |
| Debuggability | Deterministic target | Instance identity is opaque |

### 4.2 The DNSSEC chain of trust: KSK, ZSK, DS, and authenticated denial

DNSSEC does not encrypt DNS; it *authenticates* it. Each signed zone publishes `DNSKEY` records and attaches `RRSIG` signatures to its data. Two keys separate concerns [5]:

- The **Zone-Signing Key (ZSK)** signs the zone's records and can be rolled frequently, since nothing outside the zone references it.
- The **Key-Signing Key (KSK)** signs only the zone's `DNSKEY` RRset. The parent zone publishes a `DS` (Delegation Signer) record containing a hash of the child's KSK, binding the child's key into the parent's signed data.

The result is a *chain of trust*: the resolver's hard-coded **trust anchor** (the root KSK, shipped with validating software) validates the root's `DNSKEY`; the root's signed `DS` for `.com` authenticates `.com`'s KSK; and so on down to the `RRSIG` covering the answer itself [5]. A validating resolver sets the `AD` (Authenticated Data) bit only when the entire chain verifies; any failure yields `SERVFAIL` — a deliberately *fail-closed* design [5].

Two subtleties deserve emphasis. First, **denial of existence must also be authenticated**, or an attacker can forge NXDOMAIN responses to deny service selectively. `NSEC` records prove non-existence by naming the next existing name in canonical order — but thereby permit *zone walking*. `NSEC3` (RFC 5155) hashes the names with a salted iterated hash, preserving the proof while defeating trivial enumeration [5]. Second, DNSSEC signs *data*, not the *channel*: a non-validating stub learns nothing unless its upstream sets `AD` honestly — the "last mile" problem that motivates §4.4.

```python
# Simplified DNSSEC validation walk for www.example.com (conceptual)
def validate(answer, rrset_keys, ds_parent, trust_anchor):
    # 1. Verify the answer's RRSIG with the zone's ZSK
    assert verify_rrsig(answer, answer.rrsig, rrset_keys.zsk)
    # 2. Verify the ZSK is endorsed: KSK-signed DNSKEY RRset
    assert verify_rrsig(rrset_keys.dnskey_rrset, ksk_sig, rrset_keys.ksk)
    # 3. Verify the KSK matches the parent's DS record (hash binding)
    assert hash(rrset_keys.ksk) == ds_parent.key_digest
    # 4. Recurse upward until the trust anchor is reached
    return chain_to_anchor(ds_parent, trust_anchor)
```

> **Theorem (DNSSEC soundness):** If the trust anchor is correct, every `DS` and `RRSIG` verifies, and the resolver is fail-closed, then any accepted answer was signed by a key transitively endorsed from the anchor — authenticity of origin, not confidentiality or availability.

The 2018 root KSK rollover — the first ever — revealed how many resolvers held stale trust anchors, and the migration from RSA/SHA-1 toward ECDSA (algorithm 13) and Ed25519 (algorithm 15) trades signature size against validation cost [5].

### 4.3 Cache-poisoning defenses: from Kaminsky to port randomization and bailiwick hardening

**The classical attack.** Pre-2008, a resolver typically sent all queries from a fixed UDP source port. Forging a response required guessing only the 16-bit TXID: 65,536 possibilities. Worse, Kaminsky showed the attacker need not wait for a *real* query for the target name: by querying a *nonexistent* name under the target domain, the attacker forces the resolver to emit a fresh upstream query — then floods forged "referral" responses, each guessing a TXID, each carrying *glue records* redirecting the domain's nameservers to attacker IPs. With many queries in flight, the birthday paradox applies across the cross-product of queries and forgeries, and Kaminsky reported reliable success in as little as ten seconds [8][11].

**The emergency response.** On 8 July 2008, an unprecedented coordinated multi-vendor patch release — organized after a secret March meeting and promoted by US-CERT — shipped the immediate mitigation: **source-port randomization** [9]. The forgery space multiplies: the attacker must now guess the 16-bit TXID *and* the ~16-bit source port, roughly 2³² combinations, and probabilistic model checking confirmed the dramatic increase in expected attack time [10][11].

```text
; Conceptual entropy accounting (off-path adversary)
; Pre-2008:   H = 16 bits (TXID only)            -> ~2^16 guesses
; Post-2008:  H = 16 (TXID) + ~16 (sport)        -> ~2^32 guesses
; Birthday factor with q concurrent queries: effective work ~ 2^H / q
; Defense-in-depth adds: bailiwick checks, 0x20 randomization, RRL, DNSSEC
```

But randomization is *mitigation, not repair*. As noted at the time, the birthday paradox applies to the defender's randomness too: an attacker emitting at √2¹⁶ = 256 times the query volume still reaches ~50% success probability against a 16-bit field — randomization "puts off the dreadful day" rather than ending the threat [9]. The durable fixes are structural:

1. **Bailiwick checking.** A resolver must only accept records *in bailiwick* — within the zone actually queried. Glue for `ns.evil.com` in a referral for `victim.com` is discarded, not cached. This single rule kills the Kaminsky glue-injection vector regardless of entropy [10].
2. **0x20 randomization.** Randomizing the *case* of query-name letters exploits DNS case-insensitivity to smuggle ~1 extra entropy bit per alphabetic character, which the response must echo — cheap entropy with no protocol change.
3. **Response Rate Limiting (RRL).** Authoritative servers cap the rate of identical responses, blunting query-flood elicitation and reflection-amplification abuse.
4. **DNSSEC as the endgame.** A validating resolver rejects forged data cryptographically; entropy becomes irrelevant. DNSSEC is the only defense here that converts a probabilistic barrier into a *computational* one [5].

> **Theorem (Layered forgery resistance):** Let *H* be the guessable entropy (TXID + port + 0x20 bits), *W* the race window before the legitimate response arrives, and *R* the attacker's packet rate. The per-attempt success probability is ≈ *R·W*/2^H (birthday-amplified by concurrent queries); bailiwick rules and DNSSEC validation drive the *utility* of a success to zero even when the probability is nonzero. Security therefore rests on the *conjunction* of probabilistic and structural defenses, not on any single layer.

### 4.4 Encrypted transports: DoT, DoH, DoQ, and ODoH

Cleartext DNS leaks every lookup to every on-path observer — ISPs, Wi-Fi operators, and state-level adversaries. Three standardized encrypted transports address the stub-to-recursive leg [12]:

| Protocol | Spec | Transport | Port | Wire appearance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **DoT** (DNS over TLS) | RFC 7858 [2] | TLS over TCP | 853 | Dedicated port; trivially identifiable, easy to allow or block |
| **DoH** (DNS over HTTPS) | RFC 8484 [3] | HTTPS (HTTP/2) | 443 | Indistinguishable from web traffic to a port-based filter |
| **DoQ** (DNS over QUIC) | RFC 9250 [4] | QUIC (TLS 1.3) | 853/UDP | Multiplexed streams, 0-RTT resumption; ALPN `doq` |

- **DoT** (2016) was first: a clean TLS session on a dedicated port, with Strict and Opportunistic authentication profiles (RFC 8310). Its honesty is its weakness — port 853 is trivially blockable.
- **DoH** (2018) embeds DNS messages in HTTPS, typically `POST`ing wire-format messages to `/dns-query`. Sharing port 443 with the web defeats blanket port blocking, but not SNI- or IP-based blocking of the resolver itself [12].
- **DoQ** (2022) maps DNS directly onto QUIC streams, avoiding HTTP overhead where intermediaries are rare. QUIC's 0-RTT resumption and stream multiplexing eliminate TCP head-of-line blocking, giving DoQ the lowest handshake latency — at the cost of the thinnest deployment footprint [4].
- **ODoH** (Oblivious DoH, RFC 9230) inserts a proxy so that no single entity learns both the client's IP and the query contents — separating *who* from *what* [12].

The central tension is **centralization**: encrypting to a resolver means *one organization* now sees all queries in cleartext. The migration from thousands of ISP resolvers to a handful of global DoH/DoT operators concentrates metadata — and coercion targets — in ways the protocol's designers did not intend but its deployment economics produced.

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

Consolidating the figures above yields a quantitative picture of the modern DNS security posture:

1. **Anycast scale.** Thirteen logical identities, twelve operating organizations, on the order of two thousand physical instances across 130+ countries [6][7]. The root has not suffered a globally visible DDoS outage since anycast became universal — an empirical availability result no 1987 model predicted.
2. **Poisoning resistance.** Pre-2008: ~2¹⁶ forgery space, exploited in ~10 seconds in the Kaminsky demonstration [8]. Post-RFC 5452: ~2³² space; formal probabilistic analysis shows expected attack times pushed beyond plausible race windows, provided ports are *actually* randomized (NAT devices that rewrite source ports can silently collapse the entropy) [10][11]. With bailiwick enforcement, the Kaminsky-class glue attack is structurally eliminated independent of entropy [10].
3. **DNSSEC deployment.** The root is signed; the 2018 root KSK rollover succeeded globally, validating the RFC 5011 trust-anchor rollover machinery. A long tail of non-validating resolvers means DNSSEC's guarantee is only as strong as the validating resolver the client actually uses [5].
4. **Encrypted-transport adoption.** All major browsers and mobile OSs now ship DoH/DoT support; DoQ remains nascent [12]. The encrypted handshake adds roughly one RTT, amortized over connection reuse (and QUIC 0-RTT) — against which resolvers trade anycast proximity, since the largest public DoH operators are themselves anycast.
5. **Theoretical guarantees.** Three stand as theorems rather than measurements: *(a)* DNSSEC provides origin authenticity reducible to signature unforgeability, conditional on a correct trust anchor and fail-closed validation; *(b)* port randomization plus bailiwick checking bounds off-path poisoning success per attempt with zero utility on success; *(c)* encrypted transports provide confidentiality against passive on-path adversaries, reducible to TLS 1.3 security, while offering *no* protection against the resolver operator itself.

> **Theorem (Composition):** Anycast (availability) ∘ DNSSEC (authenticity) ∘ encrypted transport (confidentiality) ∘ anti-poisoning defenses jointly approximate the properties of a centralized authenticated database — but only where each mechanism is deployed and validated. In practice the least-deployed link is DNSSEC validation at the stub.

## 6 Limitations

The DNS ecosystem has unresolved problems we do not paper over:

- **The last mile is unsigned.** DNSSEC validation typically terminates at the recursive resolver; the stub-to-recursive hop relies on the `AD` bit or transport encryption, neither independently verifiable by the stub. A compromised recursive resolver defeats both DNSSEC and DoH for its clients at once.
- **Privacy versus operability.** Encrypted DNS blinds the network defenders — enterprise security teams, malware C2 detection — who relied on cleartext query visibility. The IETF acknowledges this as an unresolved tension, not a solved trade-off [12].
- **Centralization risk.** The economics of DoH favor a few large operators, creating single points of coercion, outage, and invisibly enforceable content filtering at the resolver.
- **Algorithm agility debt.** A future break in a widely deployed DNSSEC signature algorithm would require an emergency rollover across millions of zones, with the 2018 KSK rollover's friction as a lower bound on the difficulty.
- **Anycast's state problem.** As more DNS traffic moves to stateful encrypted transports, anycast's stateless affinity assumptions strain; operators resort to consistent hashing and connection draining — complexity the original design never contemplated.
- **Scope.** Private namespaces (mDNS, split-horizon enterprise DNS), alternative roots, and blockchain-based naming lie outside this analysis, as do the governance questions of root-zone management.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The Domain Name System survived its own success by accretion: anycast smeared thirteen logical servers across thousands of physical instances; DNSSEC bolted a public-key hierarchy onto an unauthenticated protocol; DoT, DoH, and DoQ wrapped the query path in TLS; and the Kaminsky crisis of 2008 converted a 16-bit guessing game into a layered discipline of randomization, bailiwick enforcement, and cryptographic validation. Each layer repaired a real failure, and each introduced a new tension — anycast's opacity, DNSSEC's last-mile gap, encryption's centralization, randomization's merely probabilistic promise.

The through-line is that DNS security is now a *composition* problem. No single RFC secures the system; security emerges — where it emerges — from the conjunction of routing resilience, cryptographic authenticity, transport confidentiality, and cache hygiene, each deployed at the right layer. The open work of the next decade is less about new cryptography than about deployment: getting validation to the stub, managing key and algorithm rollovers without global incidents, and reconciling the privacy encrypted DNS promises with the accountability its concentration threatens.
