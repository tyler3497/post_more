# Differential Dataflow and Timely Dataflow: Partially-Ordered Logical Times, Iteration without Stratification, and Sublinear Incremental Maintenance of Recursive Queries

## Abstract

Classical incremental computation assumes a *totally ordered* notion of time: each input batch is processed after all previous ones quiesce, and recursive queries are maintained by iterating to fixpoint once per batch. This thesis presents **differential dataflow**, which generalizes incremental computation to an arbitrary *partial order* of logical times, and **timely dataflow**, the execution substrate in which timestamps index epochs of input updates and iterations of recursive loops so that distinct phases execute *concurrently* while remaining causally distinguishable. Replacing the difference operator of classical view maintenance with a generalized difference over a discrete partial order yields *sublinear incremental maintenance* of recursive queries — reachability, strongly connected components, Datalog fixpoints — where an update triggers work proportional to the affected region, not the accumulated state. We analyze the **arrangement** abstraction of shared, indexed, lazily-merged multiversion traces, the distributed progress-tracking protocol of timely dataflow, and Abadi and Isard's *could-result-in* semantics, and report empirical results from Naiad and the open-source Rust implementations.

## 1 Introduction

Consider the problem that motivated Naiad [2]: interactive graph analysis on a live social network. A user issues a recursive query — nodes reachable from a seed set — receives an answer in hundreds of milliseconds, then refines it as the graph changes at thousands of edges per second. The query is *recursive*; the input is *streaming*; the latency requirement is *interactive*. In 2013, no system handled all three axes at once. Batch processors had throughput but not latency; stream processors had latency but could not iterate; iterative batch systems iterated only over static snapshots; and incremental systems (DBToaster, materialized view maintenance) updated only *non-recursive* queries [1].

The missing piece was a *model*. Classical incremental view maintenance (IVM) is built on the delta: given an input change ΔI, compute the output change ΔO without recomputing O. But recursion breaks the delta story. To maintain reachability under a single edge insertion, a stratified system must re-run the recursive evaluation to convergence — work proportional to the *state*, not the *change*. Semi-naïve evaluation reduces redundant derivations within one fixpoint computation but does not relate successive fixpoint computations across updates. The dilemma: batch the updates and iterate from scratch, or restrict recursion.

Differential dataflow [1] resolves it by making *iteration itself* a dimension of time. A timestamp is not an integer but an element of a *lattice* — typically a product order combining the epoch (input batch) with the iteration (loop round). An input update arrives at *(e, 0)*; the iterations it induces occur at *(e, 1), (e, 2), …*. Because timestamps are partially ordered, epochs' iterations proceed concurrently: *(1, 5)* and *(2, 3)* are *incomparable*, scheduled in any order or in parallel. The generalized difference operator decomposes computation into *pointwise* differences at each logical time, and an operator applied to differences at time *t* sees only the accumulation of differences at times *s ≤ t*. For monotone operators, a small input change induces small differences, so maintaining the recursive query costs proportionally to the *change* rather than the *state*. Iteration ceases to be a control-flow construct that must be re-executed; it becomes a *data* dimension the difference machinery handles uniformly [1].

This thesis substantiates the claim — *partially ordered time turns iteration into incremental maintenance* — by (i) presenting differentiation on discrete partial orders, (ii) describing timely dataflow [3] and its progress-tracking protocol, (iii) explaining arrangements [5][6], the shared indexed traces that keep state proportional to data, (iv) working through recursive queries as differential programs, (v) reporting empirical results from Naiad and the Rust lineage [2][7], and (vi) formalizing semantics via the *could-result-in* relation [3][4].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Incremental view maintenance and its limits

Materialized view maintenance has a long pedigree: the *delta rules* differentiate relational operators. If *Q = σ(R ⋈ S)* and *R* changes by ΔR, then *ΔQ = σ(ΔR ⋈ S) ∪ σ(ΔR ⋈ ΔS) ∪ σ(R ⋈ ΔS)*. Every relational-algebra operator is differentiable in this sense: its output change is expressible from the input change plus accumulated state. This is the foundation of classical IVM.

Recursion shatters the delta rules. Consider reachability *R⁺ = R ∪ (R⁺ ⋈ R)*. Inserting one edge can connect an entire upstream component to an entire downstream component, changing Θ(n²) pairs, and *no* local delta rule expresses this change. The standard remedy is fixpoint recomputation. Stratified Datalog systems therefore re-evaluate recursively on every update batch (cost ∝ state) or restrict updates to non-recursive predicates. Neither delivers interactive latency on evolving graphs [1].

### 2.2 Iteration in dataflow systems

Naiad's contemporaries handled iteration in two ways. Bulk-synchronous systems (Pregel, Giraph, Spark) execute *supersteps*: all workers synchronize at a global barrier between iterations — simple and correct, but the slowest worker gates all progress, and iteration is bound to a total order. Asynchronous systems (GraphLab) allow uncoordinated iteration but sacrifice determinism and composability: there is no notion of "the result at iteration *i*" for downstream operators to depend on.

Naiad introduced a third way, **timely dataflow** [2][3]: timestamps are first-class, partially ordered, and *loop counters are timestamps*. An iterative computation is a cyclic subgraph; the `iterate` operator stamps each record's loop counter into its timestamp. Progress tracking becomes a distributed protocol over *pointstamps* (location, timestamp pairs) rather than a global barrier. Abadi and Isard's insight is that the *could-result-in* relation — "an event at (ℓ, t) could causally affect an event at (ℓ′, t′)" — is decidable and efficiently trackable, and notifications ("you have now seen all messages at timestamp *t*") can be delivered *locally* once the frontier of unprocessed pointstamps advances past *t* [3].

### 2.3 The Naiad heritage

Differential dataflow was born *inside* Naiad. The CIDR 2013 paper [1] presents "differential computation" as a programming model on the Naiad runtime; the SOSP 2013 paper [2] presents Naiad itself, with timely dataflow as its coordination substrate and differential dataflow as one of several programming models built atop it (alongside streaming queries, iterative machine learning, and interactive graph mining). The layering matters: differential dataflow is a *programming model* (collections, operators, differences); timely dataflow is a *model of execution* (timestamps, notifications, progress tracking). The formal model [3] studies the latter; the information-flow work [4] shows that virtual times, suitably strengthened, yield noninterference-like guarantees reminiscent of security-typed languages — a surprising payoff of the *could-result-in* discipline.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Collections as functions of time

A differential dataflow *collection* *C* is a multiset varying over a discrete partially-ordered set of times *(T, ≤)* — formally, a function:

> *C : D × T → ℤ*

assigning each record *d* and time *t* an integer *multiplicity* (positive for insertions, negative for retractions). The *accumulation* at *t* sums *C(d, s)* over *s ≤ t*, recovering "state as of *t*". The *difference* at *t* is *δC(·, t) = C(·, t) − Σ_{s < t} C(·, s)*: the net change introduced exactly at *t*.

> **Theorem:** (Difference decomposition [1]) For any collection *C* over a discrete partial order, *C(·, t) = Σ_{s ≤ t} δC(·, s)*, and each *δC(·, t)* is finitely supported when only finitely many input updates have occurred.

An operator *Op* is *lifted* to differences: for operators monotone with respect to accumulation, the output difference at *t* depends only on input differences at *s ≤ t*. The computation at each logical time thus sees a *consistent snapshot* — exactly the changes visible at that time — regardless of the physical order in which messages arrive.

### 3.2 Operator differentiation

The classical delta rules generalize. For *join*:

```rust
// Differential join: at time t, consume only differences with timestamps s ≤ t.
// Output difference at t = input differences at t joined against the
// accumulated state of the other side at times ≤ t. [5]
let diff_at_t = diff1_at_t.join(&accum2_at_t)
               .concat(&accum1_at_t.join(&diff2_at_t))
               .concat(&diff1_at_t.join(&diff2_at_t));
```

For *iterate*, the rule is the heart of the contribution. An iterative scope maps a timestamp *t* to *(t, 0), (t, 1), …*: each loop pass increments the iteration coordinate. Because the product order makes *(t, i) < (t, i+1)* while keeping *(t₁, i)* and *(t₂, j)* incomparable for *t₁ ≠ t₂*, the fixpoint computations of distinct input batches are *independent subcomputations* the runtime may interleave arbitrarily. A small change at epoch *e* propagates as differences at *(e, 0)*, inducing differences at *(e, 1), (e, 2), …* — and *only* there. This is sublinear incremental maintenance of recursive queries: work proportional to the number of induced difference-records, which for localized updates is far smaller than the accumulated state [1].

### 3.3 Arrangements: sharing indexed state

A naïve implementation would drown in state: every operator instance retaining every difference at every timestamp. The **arrangement** abstraction [5][6] is the systems answer: an indexed, shared, multiversion trace of a collection. Records are grouped by key, sorted, and stored in *batches* merged on an LSM-tree-like doubling schedule, so each batch participates in O(log *n*) merges and lookups cost O(log *n*). Multiple operators — even multiple dataflows — *share* one arrangement, because each operator's logical view ("accumulation as of frontier *f*") is obtained by seeking the same physical batches with different cursors.

```rust
use differential_dataflow::operators::arrange::ArrangeByKey;

// Arrange edges once; reuse across arbitrarily many joins and dataflows. [5]
let edges = edges_input.to_collection(scope).arrange_by_key();
query.join_core(&edges, |x, q, y| Some((*y, (*x, *q))))
     .join_core(&edges, |y, (x, q), z| Some((*q, (*x, *y, *z))));
```

Arrangements also solve the *import problem*: a collection arranged in one dataflow can be imported into another, or into a nested iterative scope, via `enter`, which re-stamps timestamps with the scope's additional coordinate. The physical trace is untouched; only the logical time interpretation is extended [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Partially ordered time: the product-order construction

The canonical timestamp type is a *product order*. Let *T₁ = (ℕ, ≤)* index input epochs and *T₂ = (ℕ, ≤)* index loop iterations; then *T = T₁ × T₂* with:

> *(e₁, i₁) ≤ (e₂, i₂)  iff  e₁ ≤ e₂ and i₁ ≤ i₂.*

Nested loops nest products: *T₁ × T₂ × T₃* for two iteration levels. The order is only partial: *(1, 5)* and *(2, 0)* are incomparable. Timely dataflow's progress-tracking protocol maintains, per worker, a *frontier* — an antichain of minimal unprocessed timestamps — and a notification for time *t* fires exactly when the frontier advances beyond *t*, i.e., when no message with timestamp *s ≤ t* can still arrive [2][3]. The partial order makes this check *local*: incomparable timestamps need no relative ordering, so epochs proceed without mutual synchronization.

The choice of order is a *programming decision*. Three programmer-selectable orders over *(iteration, epoch)* pairs illustrate the design space [1]:

| Order | Rule | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Product (iterative) | *(i₁, j₁) ≤ (i₂, j₂)* iff *i₁ ≤ i₂ ∧ j₁ ≤ j₂* | Epochs independent; maximal parallelism |
| Lexicographic | *i₁ < i₂ ∨ (i₁ = i₂ ∧ j₁ ≤ j₂)* | New epochs wait for old ones to quiesce |
| Total (scalar) | single counter | Classical stratified evaluation |

The product order is the default because it *maximizes independence*: updates at different epochs never block each other, and within an epoch iterations proceed in order. This is "iteration without stratification": no global barrier separates batch *e* from batch *e+1*; the *data* at *(e+1, 0)* simply does not causally depend on the *fixpoint* at *(e, ∞)*.

### 4.2 Timely dataflow: progress tracking and notifications

Timely dataflow is the execution layer. Each operator is a stateful vertex receiving timestamped messages along edges and may request *notifications*: "call me back when I have received every message with timestamp *t*." The runtime delivers each notification *exactly once*, and only when *safe* — when no future message could bear a timestamp *s ≤ t* [2][3].

Safety is established by **distributed progress tracking**. Every message and held capability is a *pointstamp* (location, timestamp). Workers exchange summaries of outstanding pointstamps; each computes, for every location, the timestamps that could still produce messages. The *could-result-in* relation [3] formalizes this: an event at *(ℓ, t)* could result in one at *(ℓ′, t′)* if the graph admits a path along which timestamps can evolve from *t* to *t′* consistently with operators' declared summaries. The protocol is provably sound: once a worker concludes no outstanding pointstamp could result in a message at *(ℓ, t)*, the notification fires and no such message will ever arrive. Empirically the overhead is small — Naiad sustains sub-millisecond iterations with progress tracking active [2].

### 4.3 Recursion without stratification: reachability and SCC

The canonical demonstration is *incrementally maintained strongly connected components* [1]: arrange the edge relation by source; in an iterative scope, propagate labels (each node's label is the minimum label among successors) to fixpoint with `distinct` keeping differences finite; nodes whose labels stabilize identically form SCCs. The program is a handful of operators — `join`, `map`, `distinct`, `iterate` — and the *same* program handles the initial bulk computation (epoch 0), the iterative fixpoint (loop iterations), and incremental maintenance under edge insertions *and deletions* (subsequent epochs).

Deletions, the bane of classical IVM for recursive queries, are handled by negative multiplicities: retracting an edge introduces a negative difference at *(e, 0)*, and the difference machinery propagates exactly the label changes the retraction induces — no recomputation of unaffected components [1][5].

Datalog evaluation falls out as a special case. A Datalog program's semi-naïve evaluation is an iterative dataflow; differential dataflow maintains it incrementally across *both* rule updates and EDB updates. Stratified negation is supported *within* an epoch (negation applies to the accumulation at the current timestamp), while the partial order prevents the classic pathology of re-stratifying on every update.

### 4.4 Arrangements under the hood: traces, cursors, merging

An arrangement's physical form is a *trace*: immutable indexed batches plus a frontier of unconsolidated updates. Each batch maps *(key, time) → diff*, sorted by key then time. A *cursor* seeks to a key and replays diffs with times *≤ f* for the operator's frontier *f*. Batches merge on a doubling schedule — two similar-sized batches merge into one, consolidating diffs at identical *(key, time)* and discarding zero-sum cancellations. Amortized cost per update is O(log *n*) merges; space is O(*n* log *n*) worst-case but O(*n*) in practice because consolidation annihilates most history [6].

The *sharing* property is what separates a research prototype from a usable system. In the Rust implementation [5], an arrangement is reference-counted; `join_arranged`, `reduce`, `threshold`, and `distinct` all operate against the same trace; a trace can be exported from one timely dataflow and imported into another in the same process. McSherry's arrangements essay [6] demonstrates a shared server maintaining a large dynamic graph once, with many ad-hoc queries attaching at near-zero marginal cost — the dataflow analogue of a database index shared across queries. This makes differential dataflow a credible substrate for *interactive* analytics rather than merely a fast batch engine.

### 4.5 Formal semantics: the could-result-in relation

Abadi and Isard [3] give timely dataflow a mathematical semantics, elevating it from coordination trick to model of computation. The central definition: event *e₁ = (ℓ₁, t₁)* *could result in* event *e₂ = (ℓ₂, t₂)* if a chain of edges and operator behaviors is consistent with the timestamps. The relation is a *preorder* refining the timestamp order; notifications are safe exactly when the frontier has passed all *could-result-in* predecessors of the notification's pointstamp.

Two consequences follow. First, *determinism*: although messages arrive in arbitrary physical order, the semantics of a timely dataflow graph is a function of its inputs — logical times, not interleavings, determine the result. Second, *information flow*: Abadi and Isard [4] read virtual times as *security levels*, and a coherence condition on per-node time mappings yields noninterference-like guarantees — high inputs cannot influence low outputs. The required "modest strengthening" is that operators' timestamp transformations be monotone in the could-result-in preorder. A systems mechanism (progress tracking) thus acquires a clean semantic characterization that pays dividends in an unrelated domain (security).

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness argument for differential iteration

We sketch the proof that differential iteration computes the correct fixpoint for monotone recursive queries, following [1].

> **Theorem:** Let *Q* be a recursive query defined by monotone relational operators, and *C₀, C₁, …* input collections at epochs *0, 1, …*. Let *D* be the differential dataflow evaluation with product-order timestamps. Then for each epoch *e*, the accumulation of the output at timestamps *{(e, i) : i ∈ ℕ}* equals the least fixpoint of *Q* applied to the accumulated input at epoch *e*.

*Proof sketch.* Induct on the partial order. The base case *(e, 0)* holds by construction: differences there are exactly the input change. For the inductive step, assume correctness for all *s < (e, i)*. The operator lifting for monotone operators guarantees the output difference at *(e, i)* equals the change induced by input changes at times *≤ (e, i)* — which, by the induction hypothesis, is precisely the *i*-th iterate of the semi-naïve evaluation. Termination follows because each difference is finitely supported and monotone operators admit no infinite strictly increasing chain of *new* derivations without new input differences. The fixpoint is the least fixpoint by Knaster–Tarski applied to the monotone immediate-consequence operator. ∎

The subtle case is *non-monotone* operators (`distinct`, `threshold`, negation): correctness requires the operator to see the *full accumulation* at times *≤ t* before emitting its difference at *t* — exactly what notifications provide. The operator buffers input differences until its frontier passes *t*, then emits. Timely dataflow's notification mechanism is thus not an optimization but a *correctness requirement* for the difference lifting of non-monotone operators [2][3].

### 5.2 Naiad's empirical results

The SOSP 2013 evaluation [2] reports Naiad outperforming specialized systems in their own domains: streaming queries answered in *hundreds of milliseconds* on continuously updated data; PageRank-style iterations at *sub-millisecond per iteration* on small graphs, enabled by the absence of global barriers; and strongly connected components and reachability over the Twitter follower graph maintained incrementally under a stream of edge updates, with update latency proportional to the *affected region* rather than graph size — the empirical validation of the sublinear-maintenance claim.

The headline number from [1] is that incrementally maintained SCC processes single-edge updates in *milliseconds* where recomputation takes *seconds to minutes* — a gap that widens as the graph grows, because recomputation scales with state while differential maintenance scales with change.

### 5.3 The Rust lineage

The ideas did not remain in a research prototype. McSherry reimplemented timely dataflow and differential dataflow in Rust as open-source crates [7], and the arrangement design matured there [5][6]. Reported properties include single-node streaming-join throughput competitive with hand-tuned C++, memory overhead dominated by arranged traces (O(state) with small constants), and the ability to run the *same* program in batch mode (one epoch), streaming mode (many epochs), and interactive mode (ad-hoc queries against shared arrangements) without modification. The mdbook [5] remains the most accessible technical reference for the trace/cursor/merge design.

## 6 Limitations

**Operator monotonicity and the notification tax.** Non-monotone operators (`distinct`, `threshold`, aggregations with retractions) cannot emit output differences until their input frontier advances past the timestamp in question. In a fine-grained partial order each *(epoch, iteration)* pair is a separate notification point, so these operators serialize on frontier progress. Workloads dominated by `distinct` inside deep iteration can see the notification machinery become the bottleneck [2][5].

**Frontier-size blowup.** The product order's strength — incomparable timestamps — is also a bookkeeping cost. Workers track frontiers as antichains; with many concurrent epochs and deep iterations, antichains grow, and per-message progress-tracking metadata grows with them. Tens of concurrent epochs are manageable [2]; thousands of tiny epochs can inflate coordination state beyond the data itself.

**Compaction and the "infinite history" problem.** Difference collections retain history: the trace physically contains diffs at all times *≤* the current frontier. *Compaction* folds old diffs into the accumulation and discards them, but is safe only when no future query will distinguish the folded times. Choosing *when* to compact is heuristic; compact too eagerly and historical queries break, too lazily and memory grows with the number of epochs rather than the data size [6].

**Learning curve and debuggability.** Partially ordered time is a genuinely difficult programming model. A misplaced `enter` produces results that are *deterministically wrong* — the system is always deterministic [3], so bugs reproduce faithfully but manifest as subtle logical errors rather than crashes. Tooling for visualizing timestamp frontiers remains rudimentary [5].

**When not to use it.** For one-shot batch analytics the arrangement overhead is pure cost — Spark or DuckDB will win. For non-recursive streaming aggregations, Flink's simpler watermarks suffice. Differential dataflow earns its complexity exactly when the workload is *both* incremental *and* iterative; on either axis alone, a specialized system is simpler and often faster [1][2].

## 7 Conclusion

Differential dataflow's contribution can be stated in one sentence: *by generalizing the time domain of incremental computation from a total order to a partial order, iteration becomes just another dimension of change, and recursive queries become incrementally maintainable with work proportional to the update rather than the state* [1]. Timely dataflow [2][3] supplies the execution model — timestamps as first-class logical entities, notifications as the coordination primitive, distributed progress tracking as the safety argument — and arrangements [5][6] supply the systems engineering that keeps the abstraction affordable: shared, indexed, lazily merged traces with O(log *n*) amortized maintenance.

The line from Naiad to the present is unusually direct: the SOSP 2013 best-paper ideas were reimplemented in Rust by their author, documented as a living mdbook, and remain the reference implementation of incremental iterative computation [7]. The formal model [3] and its information-flow reading [4] suggest the ideas have further to travel — virtual time as a unifying abstraction for concurrency, determinism, and security is still underexploited. Open problems are concrete: adaptive compaction policies, cheaper frontier representations for ultra-fine-grained orders, and better tooling for nested partial orders. But the core result stands: *stratification was never a semantic necessity, only a limitation of total time*.
