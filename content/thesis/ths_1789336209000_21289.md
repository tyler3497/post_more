# Statistical Universals of Human Language: Zipf's Law, Heaps' Law, and the Information Theory of Linguistic Efficiency

## Abstract

Human languages, despite their cultural diversity, obey strikingly simple quantitative regularities. The frequency of the *r*-th most frequent word decays approximately as the reciprocal of its rank (**Zipf's law**), vocabulary grows sublinearly with corpus size (**Heaps' law**), and frequent words are systematically shorter (**the law of abbreviation**). This thesis unifies these statistical universals under **information theory**. We review the mathematical forms of the rank–frequency and type–token laws, their Mandelbrot and Herdan generalizations, and the *least-effort* models deriving them from competing speaker and listener pressures. We show how Shannon entropy frames the lexicon as a code trading brevity against ambiguity, and how the optimization framework of Ferrer i Cancho and Solé predicts Zipfian scaling at the critical point of this tradeoff [5]. We present empirical validation across corpora, derivation sketches linking Zipf's and Heaps' laws, generative mechanisms including Simon's preferential attachment and random typing, and a candid assessment of limitations — exponent variability, lemmatization sensitivity, and topical non-stationarity — that keep these laws empirical rather than exact [1][2][6].

## 1 Introduction

Count the words in any large text, rank them by frequency, and a rigid mathematical order emerges: a tiny handful of words — *the*, *of*, *and*, *to* — account for an enormous share of all tokens, while most of the vocabulary lingers in the long tail, many words occurring only once even in million-word corpora [1]. That this distribution follows a precise **power law**, stable across languages, genres, and centuries, is one of the oldest discoveries of quantitative linguistics [2].

Zipf proposed a *principle of least effort*: language is shaped by speakers, who prefer a small cheap vocabulary, and listeners, who prefer a large unambiguous one [1]. Herdan — and later Heaps — found a second universal: distinct word *types* grow as a power of word *tokens* with exponent below one [4][7]. Shannon's 1948 theory supplied the machinery — entropy, redundancy, optimal coding — to treat the lexicon as a code and ask what code a community of speakers and listeners would converge on [8].

Our claim: these are facets of one phenomenon, **linguistic efficiency**. Languages sit near an optimum in the speaker–listener tradeoff, and the power laws are its fingerprints [5]. Section 2 states the laws; Section 3 the methodology; Section 4 the theory; Section 5 the evidence and derivations; Section 6 the limitations; Section 7 the conclusion.

## 2 Background

**2.1 The rank–frequency law.** For a corpus of *N* tokens and *V* types ordered by decreasing frequency with rank *r*:

> **Zipf's law.** *f(r) ∝ r^(−α)*, with *α ≈ 1*.

On a log–log plot, frequencies fall near a straight line of slope *−1*; in the Brown corpus, rank-1 *the* (≈70,000) and rank-2 *of* (≈36,000) match the predicted 2:1 ratio almost exactly [1]. Mandelbrot's refinement fits the low-rank regime better [1]:

> **Mandelbrot's generalization.** *f(r) ∝ (r + β)^(−α)*, *β > 0*,

flattening the curve where pure Zipf's law overestimates the top ranks. Normalization gives *f(r) = C·(r + β)^(−α)*; for *β = 0*, *α > 1*, the constant is the Riemann zeta function *ζ(α)*.

**2.2 The type–token law.** Heaps' law — originally **Herdan's law** (1960) — relates the two size measures of a text [4]:

> **Heaps' law.** *V(n) = K·n^β*,

with *V(n)* distinct types in *n* tokens. For English, *K* is typically between 10 and 100 and *β* between 0.4 and 0.6 [4]. Since *β < 1*, vocabulary growth shows **diminishing returns**. The law survives random shuffling, so it depends on the frequency distribution alone, not word order — the fact exploited to derive it from Zipf's law [4].

**2.3 Information-theoretic preliminaries.** Shannon's **entropy** [8]:

> **Theorem (Shannon source entropy).** For discrete *X* with distribution *p(x)*, *H(X) = −Σₓ p(x) log₂ p(x)* lower-bounds the expected codeword length of any uniquely decodable code for *X*, in bits per symbol.

A steep Zipfian distribution has far lower entropy than a uniform one: language is highly predictable and compressible [8]. Key derived quantities: the **conditional entropy** *H(meaning | word)* (ambiguity from the listener's view) and the **mutual information** between words and meanings.

**2.4 Summary of the laws.**

| Law | Mathematical form | Typical parameters | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zipf's rank–frequency | *f(r) ∝ r^(−α)* | *α ≈ 1* | Few words dominate; long tail of rare words [1] |
| Mandelbrot's correction | *f(r) ∝ (r + β)^(−α)* | *β > 0* small | Better fit at low ranks [1] |
| Heaps–Herdan | *V(n) = K·n^β* | *K ∈ (10, 100)*, *β ∈ (0.4, 0.6)* for English | Sublinear vocabulary growth [4] |
| Meaning–frequency | *μ ∝ f^δ* | *δ ≈ 1/2* | Frequent words carry more meanings [3] |
| Abbreviation | length ↓ as frequency ↑ | negative correlation | Frequent words are short [9] |

## 3 Methodology

Power laws are easy to see and easy to mismeasure. Our methodology combines **corpus measurement**, **statistical fitting**, and **synthetic experimentation**.

**3.1 Corpus construction.** Analyses span single-author texts (*War and Peace*), balanced corpora (Brown), and web-scale collections (Wikipedia), in multiple languages [1][2][4]. A critical choice is **lemmatization**: counting word *forms* versus *lemmas* shifts fitted exponents, though the power-law form survives [2].

**3.2 Fitting.** The classical approach fits a line to *(log r, log f(r))*; it is simple but biased, overweighting the noisy tail. Modern practice uses **maximum likelihood estimation** with likelihood-ratio tests against alternatives (lognormal, stretched exponential); much controversy about Zipf's law traces to fitting methodology rather than data [1]. For Heaps' law, *log V* is regressed on *log n* over nested corpus prefixes [4][6].

**3.3 Synthetic validation.** We generate synthetic corpora from an exact Zipfian distribution and confirm the estimators recover the planted exponent:

```python
import math, random

def sample_zipf(alpha: float, vocab: int) -> int:
    """Inverse-CDF sample of rank r from f(r) proportional to r^-alpha."""
    weights = [r ** -alpha for r in range(1, vocab + 1)]
    total = sum(weights)
    x = random.random() * total
    acc = 0.0
    for r, w in enumerate(weights, start=1):
        acc += w
        if acc >= x:
            return r
    return vocab

def heaps_curve(tokens, block=1000):
    """Vocabulary size after each block of tokens."""
    seen, curve = set(), []
    for i, t in enumerate(tokens, start=1):
        seen.add(t)
        if i % block == 0:
            curve.append((i, len(seen)))
    return curve

tokens = [sample_zipf(1.0, 50000) for _ in range(200_000)]
curve = heaps_curve(tokens)
xs = [math.log(n) for n, _ in curve]
ys = [math.log(v) for _, v in curve]
mx, my = sum(xs) / len(xs), sum(ys) / len(ys)
beta_hat = sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in zip(xs, ys)) / sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x in xs)
print(f"estimated Heaps beta: {beta_hat:.3f}")  # ~ 1/alpha for alpha > 1
```

Word entropy follows directly from the rank–frequency table:

```haskell
-- Shannon entropy (bits) of a rank-frequency distribution
entropy :: [Double] -> Double
entropy freqs =
  let total = sum freqs
      probs = map (/ total) freqs
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Zipf's rank–frequency law and Mandelbrot's correction

The empirical core: function words dominate, and frequencies fall in the harmonic-series pattern — the second word half as frequent as the first, the third a third as frequent — over several orders of magnitude [1]. Mandelbrot derived the shifted form from **optimization**: assigning words to ranks to minimize average cost per unit information, with cost growing in word length, yields exactly *f(r) ∝ (r + β)^(−α)* [1] — the first derivation of Zipf's law from an efficiency principle.

> **Theorem (informal): Mandelbrot's optimal lexicon.** *If the r-th most frequent word has length ℓ(r) and the lexicon minimizes mean length per unit information at fixed entropy rate, the optimal frequencies satisfy f(r) ∝ (r + β)^(−α) for constants α, β set by the cost structure.*

Real curves also show **two scaling regimes**: a shallow one for the core vocabulary and a steeper one for the rare-word tail, with the breakpoint scaling in text length [2]. Lemmatization shifts exponents but preserves the form [2].

### 4.2 Heaps' law and the type–token relationship

Where Zipf's law describes the *shape* of the distribution, Heaps' law describes its *growth*. Two versions exist [4]: the *single-text* version (cumulative vocabulary reading one text front to back; often slightly convex on log–log axes) and the *ensemble* version (many texts of different lengths), where the clean power law holds best [6].

The deepest fact is the law's **derivation from Zipf's law**: under mild exchangeability assumptions, the type–token relation is fixed by the frequency distribution [4]. Shuffle-invariance experiments on *War and Peace* confirm the premise — shuffling changes *K* but leaves *β* nearly untouched [4]. (Full sketch in Section 5.3.) Over historical time, the same diminishing returns govern vocabulary expansion: each new word is invoked less often than the words it competes with [7].

### 4.3 Entropy, least effort, and the information-theoretic lexicon

View the lexicon as a **code**: short codewords for frequent messages minimize speaker effort (the Huffman principle), while a distinct codeword per meaning minimizes listener effort. Natural language compromises — reusing short words for many meanings, reserving long words for rare precise ones.

Ferrer i Cancho and Solé formalized this as **multi-objective optimization** [5]. Map *n* signals to *m* objects; define **speaker cost** as signal entropy *H(S)* and **listener cost** as conditional entropy *H(R | S)*, and minimize *Ω(λ) = λ·H(R | S) + (1 − λ)·H(S)* for *λ* in the open interval (0, 1):

> **Theorem (Ferrer i Cancho & Solé, 2003).** *As λ varies from 0 to 1, the minimizers of Ω(λ) undergo a phase transition. At the critical value λ* ≈ 1/2, the optimal systems exhibit Zipf's law with exponent α ≈ 1 — the exponent observed in natural language [5].*

Zipf's law emerges **at the boundary between speaker-optimal and listener-optimal codes** — where a real communication system, pulled both ways, should sit. The model reproduces the meaning–frequency structure without building the power law in by hand [3][5]. The two costs are estimated from annotated corpora as follows:

```rust
use std::collections::HashMap;

/// Estimate H(S) and H(R|S) from (signal, referent) observations.
fn costs(observations: &[(u32, u32)]) -> (f64, f64) {
    let n = observations.len() as f64;
    let mut sig_counts: HashMap<u32, f64> = HashMap::new();
    let mut joint: HashMap<(u32, u32), f64> = HashMap::new();
    for &(s, r) in observations {
        *sig_counts.entry(s).or_insert(0.0) += 1.0;
        *joint.entry((s, r)).or_insert(0.0) += 1.0;
    }
    let h_s: f64 = sig_counts.values()
        .map(|&c| { let p = c / n; -p * p.log2() }).sum();
    let h_r_given_s: f64 = joint.iter()
        .map(|(&(s, _), &c)| {
            let p_sr = c / n;
            -p_sr * (p_sr / (sig_counts[&s] / n)).log2()
        }).sum();
    (h_s, h_r_given_s) // speaker cost, listener cost
}
```

### 4.4 Zipf's law of abbreviation and the speaker–listener tradeoff

Zipf's third regularity: **more frequent words tend to be shorter** [9]. *The*, *of*, *a*, *I* are among the shortest and most frequent words in English. Short forms for frequent meanings minimize expected utterance length — exactly what an optimal code does [8][9].

Its semantic companion is the **meaning–frequency law**: the number of meanings *μ* grows as *μ ∝ f^δ* with *δ ≈ 1/2* [3]. Frequent words are short *and* polysemous (*run*, *set*, *point*); rare words are long and monosemous. The lexicon spends its brevity budget where it pays most, accepting ambiguity as the price [3]. In laboratory communication games, participants spontaneously shorten frequent signals without instruction — evidence of active least-effort pressure [9].

### 4.5 Generative mechanisms: Simon's urn, random typing, and optimal coding

Three mechanisms generate Zipfian distributions [1]:

1. **Simon's preferential attachment (1955).** New text either introduces a novel word (probability *p*) or copies a previous token proportionally to past frequency — a rich-get-richer urn process with stationary power law *α = 1/(1 − p)*, hence *α ≈ 1* for small innovation rates.
2. **Random typing (Miller, 1957).** A monkey striking keys at random produces approximately Zipfian "words." This **null model** proves Zipf-like curves need no linguistic structure, so the power law alone cannot prove efficiency [1]. But random typing does *not* reproduce the abbreviation or meaning–frequency laws — the signatures of real lexicons [3][9].
3. **Optimal coding.** Mandelbrot's derivation treats the lexicon as the solution to constrained optimization: minimize expected cost at fixed information rate. Simon's urn as a checkable state machine:

```tla
---- MODULE SimonUrn ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets, TLC

CONSTANTS VocabLimit, Steps
VARIABLES tokens, freq

Init == /\ tokens = << >>
        /\ freq = [w \in 1..VocabLimit |-> 0]

NewWord == /\ Len(tokens) < Steps
           /\ \E w \in 1..VocabLimit : freq[w] = 0
              /\ freq' = [freq EXCEPT ![w] = 1]
              /\ tokens' = Append(tokens, w)

CopyWord == /\ Len(tokens) < Steps
             /\ \E w \in 1..VocabLimit : freq[w] > 0
                /\ freq' = [freq EXCEPT ![w] = freq[w] + 1]
                /\ tokens' = Append(tokens, w)

Next == NewWord \/ CopyWord
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<tokens, freq>>
====
```

The mechanisms are not mutually exclusive: preferential attachment describes *how* frequencies evolve; optimal coding explains *why* the system is driven toward the observed exponent [1][5].

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

**5.1 The rank–frequency law across languages.** Universality with quantified variability [1][2]: in Brown, rank-1 *the* (≈70,000) and rank-2 *of* (≈36,000) match the predicted 2:1 ratio; log–log slopes across English texts cluster near *−1.0*; Corral et al. verified the law for word forms and lemmas with a length-dependent double power law [2]; the law holds for Chinese and even Toki Pona, a 120-word artificial language [1].

| Language / corpus | Estimated *α* | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| English (Brown) | ≈ 1.00 | Classic reference corpus |
| English (long novels) | 0.95 – 1.10 | Double power law; crossover at high ranks [2] |
| Spanish (literary) | ≈ 1.00 | Core-vocabulary regime [2] |
| Chinese (news) | 0.90 – 1.05 | Law unaffected by writing system [1] |
| Toki Pona (parallel texts) | ≈ 1.00 | 120-word artificial language [1] |

**5.2 Heaps' law in the wild.** Tracking *V(n)* in *War and Peace*: vocabulary climbs steeply, then bends into the sublinear curve; the shuffled text follows nearly the same curve with the same *β* but different *K* [4]. English parameters (*K* in (10, 100), *β* in (0.4, 0.6)) replicate across corpora [4], and diminishing returns govern two centuries of vocabulary expansion in print [7].

**5.3 Derivation sketch: Heaps' law from Zipf's law.** The theoretical keystone [4]. Draw tokens i.i.d. from *p_r ∝ r^(−α)*, *α > 1*, over an unbounded vocabulary. Rank *r* is absent from *n* tokens with probability *(1 − p_r)^n ≈ exp(−n·p_r)*, so expected vocabulary is

> *V(n) = Σᵣ [1 − (1 − p_r)^n] ≈ Σᵣ [1 − exp(−n·C·r^(−α))].*

The summand transitions from ≈1 to ≈0 around *r** with *n·C·(r**)^(−α) ≈ 1*, i.e. *r** ∝ n^(1/α)*. Ranks far below *r** contribute ≈1 each; far above, negligibly. Hence

> **Result.** *V(n) ∝ n^(1/α)* — Heaps' law with *β = 1/α*.

For *α ≈ 1* this predicts *β ≈ 1*, at the upper edge of observations; finite-vocabulary cutoffs, the Mandelbrot shift, and the two-regime structure close the gap [2][4][6]. The exchangeability premise is exactly what shuffle-invariance confirms [4].

**5.4 The information-theoretic signature.** Three efficiency predictions hold: (1) **abbreviation** — word length correlates negatively with log frequency across languages and modalities [9]; (2) **meaning–frequency** — polysemy follows *μ ∝ f^δ*, *δ ≈ 1/2* [3]; (3) **critical tradeoff** — learners reshape inefficient lexicons toward the speaker–listener optimum [9]. No null model reproduces all three at once; least-effort optimization predicts all three from one principle [1][3][5][9].

## 6 Limitations

- **Exponent variability.** Reported *α* scatters around 1.0 with corpus size, genre, and fitting method [1][2]; whether the true exponent is exactly one [5] remains open.
- **The Mandelbrot fudge.** The shift *β* improves fits but is theoretically underdetermined [1].
- **Lemmatization sensitivity.** Exponents and scaling regimes depend on whether inflected forms are merged [2] — universal in form, not in constant.
- **Topical effects.** Topic mixtures perturb both laws; vocabulary richness fluctuates across documents far more than a homogeneous Zipfian model predicts, and single-text Heaps curves are convex on log–log axes [6].
- **The random-typing objection.** Monkey-typed text is Zipf-like, so the rank–frequency law alone carries little explanatory weight; the theory leans on the abbreviation and meaning–frequency laws, which random typing cannot produce [1][3].
- **Non-stationarity.** Vocabularies cool and expand; parameters from one century need not hold in the next [7].
- **Synthetic-text deviations.** Large-language-model corpora deviate measurably from human Heaps-law parameters — a potential synthetic-text diagnostic [4].

These demote the laws from exact truths to **robust empirical regularities with a principled explanation** — arguably the more interesting status.

## 7 Conclusion

Zipf's rank–frequency law, the Heaps–Herdan type–token law, and the law of abbreviation are jointly intelligible as the signature of an **efficient code**. Shannon's theory supplies the currency (entropy, redundancy, optimal codeword lengths); least effort supplies the economics (speakers minimize production cost, listeners minimize ambiguity); and the optimization models of Mandelbrot and of Ferrer i Cancho and Solé show that power-law statistics with exponents near unity are what this economics predicts at its critical point [1][5][8].

Empirically the account holds across languages, scripts, genres, and centuries, while the derivation of Heaps' law from Zipf's law collapses two universals into one [2][4][7]. The remaining puzzles — the exact exponent, topic structure, machine-text deviations — define the research frontier [6]. What endures is Zipf's insight, sharpened by eight decades of mathematics: beneath the creativity of language lies a quantitative order, the trace of a system optimized, however imperfectly, for the efficient transmission of thought.
