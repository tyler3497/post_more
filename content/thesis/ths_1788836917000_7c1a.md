# Work-Stealing Schedulers in Modern Async Runtimes: Chase-Lev Deques, Tokio's Multi-Threaded Runtime, io_uring Integration, and Latency-Aware Task Placement

## Abstract

This thesis analyzes the work-stealing scheduler as the load-balancing backbone of modern asynchronous runtimes, with the Tokio runtime on Linux as the principal case study. We begin with the classical theory: Blumofe and Leiserson's analysis establishing that randomized work stealing achieves expected running time $T_P = T_1/P + O(T_\infty)$ and $O(P\,T_\infty)$ steal attempts on fully strict computations [1]. We then dissect the engineering that makes the theory practical: the Chase–Lev lock-free work-stealing deque, with its single-owner push/pop discipline at the bottom and concurrent steal discipline at the top, and the memory-ordering obligations (C11 release/acquire, SeqCst fences) identified by Lê et al. [2] that make it correct on weakly ordered hardware. We examine how Tokio's multi-threaded runtime adapts the deque with a bounded local queue, a LIFO slot, a global injector queue, and overflow-shedding policies observable through `RuntimeMetrics` [3][4]. We then study completion-based I/O through io_uring's shared ring buffers [5] and its integration with the scheduler's I/O driver, the tension between thread-per-core affinity and dynamic stealing, and the role of latency-aware (topology-aware) victim selection on NUMA machines. A profiling methodology built on runtime metrics, steal histograms, and controlled benchmarks is presented for diagnosing scheduler pathologies empirically.

## 1. Introduction

Asynchronous runtimes multiplex large numbers of fine-grained tasks onto a smaller pool of operating-system threads. The central scheduling question is how to assign dynamically arriving, short-lived tasks to workers so that (i) no worker idles while work exists elsewhere, (ii) the scheduling overhead per task is a handful of atomic operations, and (iii) tasks that cooperate stay on the same core to exploit cache locality.

Work stealing is the dominant answer. Each worker owns a double-ended queue (deque) of ready tasks; it pushes and pops at the bottom like a stack, while idle workers *steal* from the top of randomly chosen victims' deques. The mechanism was popularized by Cilk, analyzed rigorously by Blumofe and Leiserson [1], and engineered into a practical lock-free deque by Chase and Lev [6]. Two decades of production experience — the Go scheduler, .NET's thread pool, Rayon, crossbeam-deque [7], and Tokio's multi-threaded runtime — have confirmed that the basic design scales, while refining it: bounded queues to avoid allocator pressure, global injector queues to absorb external submissions, LIFO slots to preserve locality, and overflow shedding under saturation [3][4].

Two recent developments force a re-examination. First, Linux's **io_uring** [5] shifts I/O from readiness-based (epoll) to completion-based rings with batching, zero-copy, and even syscall-free submission; a runtime that wastes its scheduling advantages on per-operation syscalls is leaving its largest latency component unoptimized. Second, the end of free core-count scaling and the ubiquity of multi-socket NUMA topologies mean that *random* victim selection — optimal in the theory's uniform-cost model — can be measurably suboptimal when stealing across a NUMA interconnect costs an order of magnitude more than stealing within a socket. This thesis unifies these strands: theory, deque mechanics, Tokio's implementation, io_uring integration, and latency-aware placement, ending with a concrete methodology for profiling and tuning the whole system.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The work/span model

We analyze computations as directed acyclic graphs. $T_1$ is the total work (nodes) and $T_\infty$ the critical-path length (span). On $P$ processors, any greedy scheduler achieves $T_P \le T_1/P + T_\infty$ [1]. Work stealing is *greedy* in the relevant sense: it is the randomized, distributed mechanism by which processors find the work.

> **Theorem (Blumofe–Leiserson):** For a fully strict computation with work $T_1$ and span $T_\infty$, randomized work stealing on $P$ processors executes in expected time $T_P = T_1/P + O(T_\infty)$, with $O(P\,T_\infty)$ steal attempts with high probability [1].

The intuition is geometric: steals occur only when a worker is idle, and each round of $P$ concurrent steals provably makes progress on the critical path (the "delay sequence" argument). Two corollaries matter for runtime designers. First, the number of steal operations is bounded by the span, not the work — scheduling overhead amortizes over the task count. Second, the bound degrades with *contention*: the atomic-access model of [1] assumes concurrent deque accesses serialize, and adversarial queuing inflates constants. This is precisely why deque design and memory ordering matter: theory assumes a cheap-enough primitive; engineering must deliver it.

### 2.2 Chase–Lev deque mechanics

The Chase–Lev deque [6] is a bounded circular buffer with `top` and `bottom` indices and a single-owner discipline:

- `push_bottom`: owner stores the task at `bottom`, then advances `bottom` with a release ordering so stealers observe a fully published entry.
- `pop_bottom`: owner decrements `bottom`, re-reads `top`, and in the contended single-element case resolves the race with the stealer via CAS on `top` (or `bottom`, depending on the variant).
- `steal_top`: a thief reads `top`, reads the task, and advances `top` with a single compare-and-swap; losers of the CAS race simply retry elsewhere.

Correctness hinges on subtle fences. The C11-correct formulation (Lê et al. [2], corrected and machine-checked by later work [8]) requires a SeqCst fence between the `bottom` decrement and the `top` load in `pop_bottom`, and a matching fence in `steal` between the `top` load and `bottom` load. Without these, an owner can read a stale `top`, conclude the deque holds one element, and pop the same task a stealer concurrently removed — a lost task that no scheduler-level logic can recover. A minimal Rust sketch of the discipline:

```rust
// Simplified Chase-Lev discipline (illustrative, not production code).
pub struct ChaseLevDeque<T> {
    buffer: Box<[UnsafeCell<MaybeUninit<T>>]>,
    mask: usize,
    top: AtomicUsize,    // stealer end (CAS)
    bottom: AtomicUsize, // owner end
}

impl<T> ChaseLevDeque<T> {
    /// Owner-only. Release store on `bottom` publishes the slot write.
    pub fn push_bottom(&self, task: T) {
        let b = self.bottom.load(Ordering::Relaxed);
        let t = self.top.load(Ordering::Acquire);
        assert!(b.wrapping_sub(t) < self.buffer.len(), "deque full");
        unsafe { (*self.buffer[b & self.mask].get()).write(MaybeUninit::new(task)) };
        self.bottom.store(b.wrapping_add(1), Ordering::Release);
    }

    /// Thief-side. Single CAS on `top` serializes concurrent stealers.
    pub fn steal_top(&self) -> Option<T> {
        let t = self.top.load(Ordering::Acquire);
        fence(Ordering::SeqCst); // pair with pop_bottom's fence
        let b = self.bottom.load(Ordering::Acquire);
        if t >= b { return None; } // empty (or racing owner)
        let task = unsafe { (*self.buffer[t & self.mask].get()).assume_init_read() };
        if self.top.compare_exchange(t, t.wrapping_add(1),
                Ordering::SeqCst, Ordering::Relaxed).is_ok() {
            Some(task)
        } else {
            mem::forget(task); // lost the race; slot still owned by winner
            None
        }
    }
}
```

The bounded variant trades the original's dynamic growth for fixed capacity: overflow is not grown but *shed* elsewhere (Tokio moves half the local queue to the global injector [4]). This avoids allocator interaction on the hot path at the price of fairness and throughput cliffs under saturation — a measurable pathology we return to in §5.

### 2.3 Fairness: LIFO versus FIFO

Classical Cilk pops and pushes at the bottom (LIFO), which is optimal for cache locality in fork–join computations: the most recently spawned child is the hottest. But LIFO alone admits *unfairness*: an unbounded stream of local pushes can starve old tasks indefinitely. Tokio and the Go scheduler therefore use FIFO local queues for fairness — tasks are dequeued in submission order — plus a single-task **LIFO slot** that bypasses the queue for the just-spawned task, recovering the cache-locality win for the common case [9]. The st3 crate documents this design space explicitly: FIFO stealing with LIFO (or FIFO) worker semantics, and fixed-capacity queues whose overflow a global injector absorbs [9]. The tradeoff is quantifiable: LIFO improves L1/L2 hit rates on fork-heavy workloads; FIFO bounds the maximum age of any queued task, which stabilizes tail latency.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis combines three methods.

1. **Analytic.** We instantiate the Blumofe–Leiserson bounds [1] for async workloads, whose task graphs are *not* fully strict in general (tasks communicate through channels and shared state), and identify where the bounds weaken: steal counts grow with the number of *ready-set reconfigurations* rather than pure span.
2. **Source-level.** We study Tokio's `scheduler::multi_thread` implementation — `worker.rs` (steal loop, searching/idle transitions), `queue.rs` (bounded local queue), and the global injector — together with the `RuntimeMetrics` API surface, which exposes `worker_local_schedule_count`, `worker_overflow_count`, `worker_steal_count`, and per-worker poll-duration histograms [3][4].
3. **Empirical.** We propose a profiling protocol (§5) using runtime metrics, controlled microbenchmarks (fork-heavy, pipeline, I/O-bound), and NUMA-aware victim policies, measuring throughput, p99 task latency, steal success rates, and cross-socket steal fractions.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Chase–Lev steal path under contention

Consider the steady state of $P$ workers each draining local queues. Steals are rare; the hot path is `pop_bottom`, which is pure atomics — no CAS on success in most variants, since the owner alone mutates `bottom`. Contention concentrates in two places: the single-element race (owner `pop` versus stealer `steal`, resolved by one CAS), and many-thief races on a popular victim's `top`, where all but one CAS loser pay for a failed steal.

*Figure 1* illustrates the steal operation: the owner works the bottom end while thieves attempt CAS advances of `top`.

![Chase-Lev deque steal operation](public/thesis/ths_1788836917000_7c1a-0.webp)

Two engineering consequences follow. First, because failed steals cost a CAS plus cache-line transfer of the victim's `top`, runtimes cap *searching* workers: Tokio transitions a worker to "searching" only if fewer than half the workers are already searching [4], which bounds aggregate steal traffic to $O(P)$ rather than $O(P^2)$ cache-line invalidations per round. Second, victim selection uses a per-worker xorshift PRNG (`fastrand_n`) starting from a random index and scanning cyclically [4] — uniform without global coordination, matching the theory's randomized assumption.

### 4.2 Tokio's multi-threaded runtime: injector, LIFO slot, and overflow policy

Tokio's scheduler departs from textbook Chase–Lev in four ways, each visible in `worker.rs` [4] and the metrics API [3]:

1. **Global injector queue.** Tasks spawned from outside any worker (or from blocking threads) land on a global MPMC injector. Workers drain it before stealing. This solves the multi-producer problem Chase–Lev handles poorly [9]: the deque's `push` is single-owner, so external producers need somewhere else to go.
2. **Bounded local queue (default capacity 256) with LIFO slot.** `spawn` from within a worker first tries the single-task LIFO slot, then the local queue; this keeps the just-spawned task hot [9].
3. **Overflow shedding.** When the local queue is full, the worker moves *half* of it to the injector queue (`worker_overflow_count` increments) [3]. This is the fairness valve: an infinite local producer cannot starve peers, at the cost of global-queue contention spikes — visible as correlated overflow counts across workers during bursts.
4. **Park/unpark and searching protocol.** Idle workers park after failing to find work; the `Idle` tracker wakes parked workers when new tasks are injected. The `transition_to_searching` gate keeps at most half the workers in the steal loop [4], a direct implementation of the theory's requirement that steal attempts stay proportional to $P$.

### 4.3 io_uring integration: from readiness to completion

Tokio's I/O driver was built for epoll's *readiness* model: the kernel reports that a socket is readable, the driver wakes the task, the task performs the `read` syscall itself. io_uring [5] replaces this with a *completion* model: the runtime posts a `read` submission queue entry (SQE) into a shared ring, and the kernel executes the operation and posts a completion queue entry (CQE) — batching hundreds of operations into one `io_uring_enter`, with `IORING_SETUP_SQPOLL` enabling zero-syscall submission via a kernel poll thread.

For the scheduler this changes the task lifecycle. In the epoll model, an I/O task's wake path is: driver thread → waker → injector/local queue → worker poll → syscall. In the io_uring model, the completion *is* the data: the task can be resumed with the result already in memory, and the driver can re-submit chained operations (e.g., accept → read → write) without ever returning to a worker. The scheduler implications are:

- **Batching amortizes wakeups.** One CQE harvest can enqueue dozens of ready tasks; pushing them through the injector in a batch halves atomic traffic versus per-completion injection.
- **Poll-mode changes blocking semantics.** With `IOPOLL`/`SQPOLL`, the "I/O driver" is no longer a passive waiter but an active poller; parking a worker while completions sit in the ring adds pure latency, so the idle protocol must poll the CQ ring before parking.
- **Buffer management becomes the bottleneck.** Registered fixed buffers (`IORING_REGISTER_BUFFERS`) remove per-operation mapping cost [5]; the scheduler's task placement should then keep buffer-owning tasks near the ring's completion handling to avoid cross-core cache traffic.

### 4.4 Thread-per-core versus work stealing

Thread-per-core (as in Seastar or io_uring-per-core designs) pins all of a core's work to that core: no steals, no cross-core queue traffic, perfect buffer locality. It pays in *imbalance*: a skewed request distribution leaves cores idle with no recourse, and cross-core communication (sharded services) requires explicit message passing. Work stealing pays in coordination overhead (steal CAS traffic, `top`/`bottom` cache lines bouncing) and buys robustness to skew plus automatic load balance.

The crossover is workload-dependent. For uniform, partitionable request streams on many cores, thread-per-core wins on tail latency. For fork–join, irregular, or bursty workloads — the async norm — work stealing's adaptivity dominates, consistent with the $T_1/P + O(T_\infty)$ guarantee: imbalance is bounded by the span, not by the skew of arrival. A hybrid is emerging in practice: topology-aware stealing (steal within the NUMA node first) approximates thread-per-core locality while retaining the global balance guarantee.

### 4.5 Latency-aware task placement on NUMA

On a two-socket machine, a cross-socket steal costs 2–5× a local steal in interconnect latency and evicts cache lines from the victim's socket. Random victim selection is oblivious to this. Latency-aware placement modifies victim selection with a cost matrix $C(i,j)$ — the expected steal latency from worker $i$ to $j$ — and samples victims proportionally to $1/C(i,j)$, or hierarchically: scan same-core/SMT pairs, then same-socket, then remote socket.

*Figure 3* sketches the NUMA cost structure motivating hierarchical stealing.

![Latency-aware placement on NUMA](public/thesis/ths_1788836917000_7c1a-2.webp)

The theory extends: Blumofe–Leiserson's bound holds for any victim distribution with full support, but the *constants* — the cost of each of the $O(P\,T_\infty)$ steals — shrink when cheap victims are preferred. The risk is systematic starvation of remote work: a strict hierarchy must still sample remote victims with nonzero probability, or a burst of work on one socket stalls while the other socket's workers park. Tokio's uniform-random scan is the safe baseline; production latency-aware designs (e.g., in high-frequency trading runtimes and some Go scheduler experiments) use weighted random with periodic full scans.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

**Proof sketch (steal bound).** Following [1]: define a *delay sequence* as a maximal critical path $u_1 \to \dots \to u_L$ in the augmented DAG $G'$ (with deque edges), interleaved with $R$ steal-attempt rounds partitioned across path segments. Lemma: in each round, the critical instruction is executed or the round contributes a steal that unblocks it. Summing over rounds yields $O(P\,T_\infty)$ steal attempts whp, and expected time $T_P = T_1/P + O(T_\infty)$.

**Profiling methodology.** We instrument the runtime with `RuntimeMetrics` [3] and drive three workload shapes:

| Workload | Task shape | Stresses |
|---|---|---|
| Fork–join fan-out | recursive spawn, join | steal rate, span bound |
| Pipeline/channels | long chains, handoffs | locality, LIFO slot hit rate |
| I/O burst | 10k concurrent reads | injector contention, overflow shedding |

Key metrics and their diagnostic reading:

1. **`worker_steal_count` vs `worker_local_schedule_count`.** A healthy fork–join run shows steals $\ll$ local schedules. Steals approaching local schedules indicate chronic imbalance or too-fine task granularity.
2. **`worker_overflow_count` spikes.** Correlated overflows across workers mark injector saturation: the bounded local queues are shedding half their contents, and the global queue is the bottleneck. Remedy: larger local queues or coarser tasks.
3. **Poll-duration histograms.** A bimodal distribution (many short polls, a fat tail of long polls) implicates blocking tasks hogging workers; `spawn_blocking` should absorb them.
4. **Cross-socket steal fraction.** Sample `/proc` NUMA counters or use perf to attribute `top`-line misses to remote sockets; if $>30\%$ of steals cross sockets, hierarchical victim selection is indicated.

*Figure 4* shows the expected scaling shape: work stealing tracks near-linear until steal traffic and injector contention bend the curve; thread-per-core is flatter but capped by imbalance; a single global queue collapses early.

**Controlled experiment (protocol).** Fix $P \in \{2,4,8,16,32,64\}$. For each workload, run 30 trials of 10s, measuring tasks/s and p99 task latency (spawn-to-completion). Vary: (a) victim policy (uniform vs hierarchical), (b) local queue capacity (64/256/1024), (c) I/O backend (epoll vs io_uring driver). Expected results: hierarchical stealing reduces p99 by 10–25% on NUMA at $P\ge 16$ with negligible throughput change; io_uring reduces p99 I/O latency by 30–50% on burst workloads by eliminating per-operation syscalls [5]; queue capacity shows a U-curve (too small → overflow shedding; too large → fairness loss and cache pressure).

## 6. Limitations

1. **Theory assumes full strictness.** Real async task graphs have channel-mediated dependencies that violate full strictness; the steal bound degrades toward bounds on the number of ready-set changes, which are workload-dependent and harder to measure.
2. **Uniform-cost victim model.** The classical analysis assumes all steals cost the same; NUMA, SMT, and shared-L3 topologies break this, and latency-aware policies lack the clean high-probability bounds of the uniform case.
3. **Metrics are scheduler-internal.** `RuntimeMetrics` reports queue events, not end-to-end task latency; correlating steal statistics with user-visible tail latency requires external instrumentation and careful clock discipline.
4. **io_uring availability and safety.** The completion model requires Linux $\ge$ 5.1 (modern features $\ge$ 5.19), registered buffers complicate memory safety in safe Rust, and `SQPOLL` needs elevated privileges — constraints that keep epoll as the portable default.
5. **Bounded queues and adversarial bursts.** Overflow shedding bounds memory but can serialize the injector under synchronized bursts; no static capacity is optimal across all workloads.

## 7. Conclusion

Work stealing endures because it converts a hard online load-balancing problem into a cheap local protocol with provable bounds: $T_1/P + O(T_\infty)$ expected time with steal attempts proportional to span, not work [1]. The Chase–Lev deque makes the protocol lock-free, but only under exacting memory-ordering discipline [2][8]; Tokio's production design shows how the textbook deque is adapted — bounded queues, LIFO slot, global injector, overflow shedding, searching gates — into a scheduler observable through runtime metrics [3][4]. Two frontiers remain active: completion-based I/O via io_uring [5], which restructures the wake path and rewards batching, and latency-aware victim selection, which repairs the theory's uniform-cost fiction on NUMA hardware. The profiling methodology in §5 gives practitioners the instruments — steal ratios, overflow spikes, poll histograms, cross-socket attribution — to decide, empirically, which frontier matters for their workload.
