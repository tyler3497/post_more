# Score-Based Diffusion Models: Stochastic Differential Equations, Probability-Flow ODEs, and Consistency Distillation

## Abstract

Score-based diffusion models recast generative modeling as the time-reversal of a stochastic differential equation (SDE): a forward Itô process corrupts data into noise, and the reverse-time SDE — whose drift depends only on the *score*, the gradient of the log marginal density — restores it. We develop this framework from first principles, showing how denoising score matching makes the score tractable and deriving the deterministic probability-flow ordinary differential equation (ODE) that shares the reverse SDE's marginals, enabling exact likelihoods, invertible latent encodings, and higher-order sampling. We then analyze the fast-sampling frontier: progressive distillation, which amortizes ODE integration into progressively shallower students, and consistency models, which learn a self-consistent map from every trajectory point to its origin for one-step generation. We compare distillation with training-from-scratch, report state-of-the-art single-step FIDs (1.73 on CIFAR-10), and examine fundamental limits: score error in low-density regions, discretization bias, and the residual gap between few-step and full-trajectory sampling.

## 1. Introduction

The field of deep generative modeling has converged, over the past decade, on a remarkably small set of principles. Adversarial training, variational inference, and autoregressive factorization each dominated distinct eras — yet none of them furnished a single mathematical object in which training, sampling, likelihood computation, and latent manipulation were all instances of the same underlying structure. The score-based diffusion framework of Song, Sohl-Dickstein, Kingma, Kumar, Ermon, and Poole changed this. By formulating noise corruption as a *continuous-time stochastic differential equation*, their 2021 paper unified the two previously distinct families of denoising diffusion probabilistic models and noise-conditioned score networks into a single continuum, and in doing so revealed that generation is simply the time-reversal of a diffusion process [1].

This thesis is an in-depth study of that framework and of the *fast-sampling* revolution it precipitated. We begin with the continuous-time formulation itself: the forward SDE, Anderson's time-reversal theorem, and the reverse SDE whose drift is parameterized by a learned score network. We explain why denoising score matching — training a network to predict the score of Gaussian-perturbed data — is the tractable objective that makes the entire enterprise possible. We then derive the **probability-flow ODE**, the deterministic counterpart of the reverse SDE with identical marginals, enabling exact likelihoods and invertible encodings [1].

But the theory, elegant as it is, carries a computational tax: faithful integration of the reverse SDE or its probability-flow ODE requires hundreds to thousands of sequential network evaluations. The second half of this thesis is devoted to the methods that pay down that tax. We study **progressive distillation**, which iteratively compresses an *N*-step sampler into an *N*/2-step student until single-step generation is reached [2]. We study **consistency models**, which abandon the step-by-step simulation altogether and learn a *consistency function* satisfying the self-consistency property along every probability-flow trajectory [3]. And we examine the modern synthesis — **consistency trajectory models**, which generalize both consistency models and score-based models into a single network capable of arbitrary jumps along the ODE trajectory [4].

Throughout, we state the SDE and ODE dynamics precisely and mark where the theory's assumptions — Lipschitz drifts, square-integrable scores, exact time-reversal — meet the approximations of finite networks and discrete solvers.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Denoising Score Matching

Let $p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})$ denote the unknown data distribution on $\mathbb{R}^d$. The **score** of a density is defined as $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p(\mathbf{x})$. Score matching, introduced by Hyvärinen, proposes to fit a model $s_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ to the true score by minimizing the Fisher divergence

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|s_\theta(\mathbf{x}) - \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})\right\|^2\right].$$

The **denoising** variant of Vincent makes this tractable: perturb the data with Gaussian noise, $p_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}} \mid \mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}; \mathbf{x}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$, and match the score of the *perturbed* distribution $p_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$. A key identity — essentially Tweedie's formula — shows that the denoising objective

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta; \sigma) = \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})}\mathbb{E}_{p_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}} \mid \mathbf{x})}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|s_\theta(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}) - \nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}} \log p_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}} \mid \mathbf{x})\right\|^2\right]$$

is, up to an additive constant, equal to the original objective with $p_\sigma$ as the target. Since $\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}} \log p_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}} \mid \mathbf{x}) = -(\tilde{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{x})/\sigma^2$, the target is analytically known: the network is trained to predict the *noise* that was added, scaled by $-1/\sigma^2$. This is the bridge between score matching and the denoising objectives used in diffusion models.

### 2.2 From Discrete Chains to Continuous Time

Early diffusion models (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Ho et al., 2020) defined the corruption process as a discrete Markov chain $\mathbf{x}_0 \to \mathbf{x}_1 \to \cdots \to \mathbf{x}_T$ with Gaussian transitions. Score networks of the same era (Song & Ermon, 2019) trained a single network at multiple noise scales via annealed Langevin dynamics. The SDE framework subsumes both: any such discrete chain is the Euler–Maruyama discretization of some Itô SDE, and any annealed score network is an estimator of the marginal scores of that SDE's solution [1].

The standard forward SDE is written

$$d\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}, t)\, dt + g(t)\, d\mathbf{w}, \qquad t \in [0, T],$$

where $\mathbf{f}$ is the drift coefficient, $g$ the diffusion coefficient, and $\mathbf{w}$ a standard Wiener process. Two canonical choices dominate practice:

1. **Variance Exploding (VE) SDE:** $\mathbf{f} \equiv \mathbf{0}$, $g(t) = \sqrt{d\sigma^2(t)/dt}$. The process adds noise without contracting the signal; the variance of the marginal explodes as $t \to T$.
2. **Variance Preserving (VP) SDE:** $\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}, t) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)\mathbf{x}$, $g(t) = \sqrt{\beta(t)}$. The process is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process whose stationary distribution is $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$; it is the continuous-time limit of DDPM.

Both are designed so that the terminal marginal $p_T$ is approximately a tractable prior $\pi$ — typically a standard Gaussian — from which sampling is trivial.

### 2.3 Maximum Likelihood and the Weighted Score-Matching Objective

A subtle and important result connects score matching to likelihood. The log-likelihood of a score-based model can be computed through the probability-flow ODE (Section 3.2), but the standard weighted score-matching objective does *not* directly optimize likelihood. Song, Durkan, Murray, and Ermon showed that for a specific choice of weighting function $\lambda(t) = g(t)^2$, the score-matching objective is an *upper bound* on the negative log-likelihood, enabling approximate maximum likelihood training; their models achieved 2.83 bits/dim on CIFAR-10 and 3.76 bits/dim on ImageNet 32×32, matching the best autoregressive models of the era [5].

> **Theorem (Likelihood bound, informal):** *Let $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}(\theta; \lambda)$ be the weighted denoising score-matching objective and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}(\theta)$ the negative log-likelihood of the model. For $\lambda(t) = g(t)^2$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}(\theta; g^2) \geq \mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}(\theta) + C$ for a constant $C$ independent of $\theta$. Minimizing the left-hand side therefore minimizes an upper bound on the right.*

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Reverse-Time SDE

The generative process is the time-reversal of the forward diffusion. Anderson (1982) proved that under mild regularity conditions, the time-reversed process $\bar{\mathbf{x}}_t = \mathbf{x}_{T-t}$ is itself a diffusion, governed by the **reverse-time SDE**

$$d\mathbf{x} = \left[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}, t) - g(t)^2 \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_t(\mathbf{x})\right] dt + g(t)\, d\bar{\mathbf{w}},$$

where time now flows backward from $T$ to $0$ and $\bar{\mathbf{w}}$ is a standard Wiener process in reverse time. The structure is illuminating: the reverse drift is the forward drift *corrected* by a term proportional to the score, $-g(t)^2 \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_t(\mathbf{x})$. Intuitively, the score points uphill on the log-density landscape — toward regions of high data probability — so the reverse process is "noise removal plus a drift toward the data manifold."

In practice, $p_t$ is unknown, so we substitute the learned score network $s_\theta(\mathbf{x}, t) \approx \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_t(\mathbf{x})$ and simulate the *approximate* reverse SDE starting from $\mathbf{x}_T \sim \pi$. If the score estimate were exact and the prior matched $p_T$ exactly, the samples at $t = 0$ would be distributed exactly as $p_{\text{data}}$ [1].

### 3.2 The Probability-Flow ODE

Every SDE has an associated deterministic flow. The Fokker–Planck equation for the reverse SDE's marginals is a continuity equation, $\partial_t p_t + \nabla \cdot (p_t \mathbf{v}) = 0$, for a particular velocity field $\mathbf{v}$. Interpreting that velocity field as the right-hand side of an ODE yields the **probability-flow (PF) ODE**:

$$d\mathbf{x} = \left[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}, t) - \tfrac{1}{2} g(t)^2 \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_t(\mathbf{x})\right] dt.$$

Note the factor of one-half: the PF ODE removes exactly half the stochastic drift, and the remaining half is what diffusion would have contributed — the net effect is a deterministic trajectory whose marginal distributions *coincide* with those of the reverse SDE at every time $t$. This is a theorem, not an approximation [1].

The PF ODE is the workhorse of modern diffusion practice, for three reasons:

| Property | Reverse SDE | Probability-Flow ODE |
|---|---|---|
| Stochasticity | Yes (Wiener term) | No (deterministic) |
| Marginal distributions | $p_t$ | $p_t$ (identical) |
| Likelihood computation | Intractable | Tractable via CNF formula |
| Latent encoding | Non-unique (noise-dependent) | Unique, invertible |
| Solver order | ≤ 1 (Euler–Maruyama) | Arbitrary (Heun, Runge–Kutta) |

Karras et al.

> **Theorem (Probability flow):** *Let $\{\mathbf{x}(t)\}$ solve the reverse-time SDE with exact score, and let $\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}(t)\}$ solve the PF ODE with the same initial condition $\mathbf{x}(T) = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}(T) \sim p_T$. Then for all $t \in [0, T]$, $\mathrm{Law}(\mathbf{x}(t)) = \mathrm{Law}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}(t)) = p_t$.*

The proof follows from the fact that both processes satisfy the same Fokker–Planck equation; uniqueness of the solution transfers the marginals from the SDE to the ODE [1].

### 3.3 Exact Likelihoods via Continuous Normalizing Flows

Because the PF ODE is a deterministic, invertible map $\mathbf{x}_T \mapsto \mathbf{x}_0$, the model is a continuous normalizing flow.

$$\log p_0(\mathbf{x}_0) = \log p_T(\mathbf{x}_T) + \int_0^T \nabla \cdot \tilde{\mathbf{f}}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\, dt,$$

where $\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_\theta$ is the learned PF-ODE drift and the divergence is estimated with the Hutchinson–Skilling trace estimator for efficiency.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Numerical Integration of the PF ODE: From Euler to Heun

The PF ODE in the Karras et al.

$$\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{d\sigma} = \frac{\mathbf{x} - D_\theta(\mathbf{x}; \sigma)}{\sigma}, \qquad \sigma \in [\sigma_{\min}, \sigma_{\max}],$$

where the score is recovered as $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_\sigma(\mathbf{x}) = (D_\theta(\mathbf{x}; \sigma) - \mathbf{x})/\sigma^2$ via Tweedie's formula.

A minimal implementation of the deterministic Heun sampler is instructive:

```python
import torch

def heun_sampler(denoiser, x_T, sigmas):
    """Deterministic 2nd-order Heun integration of the probability-flow ODE.
    sigmas: decreasing noise schedule, sigmas[0] = sigma_max ... sigmas[-1] = 0
    """
    x = x_T
    for i in range(len(sigmas) - 1):
        s_cur, s_next = sigmas[i], sigmas[i + 1]
        # Euler predictor step: dx/dsigma = (x - D(x; sigma)) / sigma
        d_cur = (x - denoiser(x, s_cur)) / s_cur
        x_pred = x + (s_next - s_cur) * d_cur
        if s_next == 0:
            x = x_pred
        else:
            # Heun corrector: average the two derivative evaluations
            d_pred = (x_pred - denoiser(x_pred, s_next)) / s_next
            x = x + (s_next - s_cur) * 0.5 * (d_cur + d_pred)
    return x
```

The corrector halves the local truncation error at the cost of one extra evaluation per step — the decisive advantage of the PF ODE over the reverse SDE, whose strong order is capped at one [6].

### 4.2 Progressive Distillation: Amortizing the ODE Integration

Even 35 evaluations is too many for real-time applications.

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\, t}\left[w(t)\, \big\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\eta(\mathbf{z}_t) - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t)\big\|^2\right],$$

where $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\eta(\mathbf{z}_t)$ is the teacher's *two-step* prediction from noise level $t$ — one DDIM step to an intermediate level $s$, then a second step to the target — and the student must match it in *one* step.

Conceptually, distillation is **amortized numerical integration**: the student network internalizes the vector field that the teacher's solver was integrating step by step.

### 4.3 Consistency Models: Self-Consistency Along Trajectories

**Consistency models** (Song et al., 2023) take a different and more radical view. Rather than distilling a *specific* teacher trajectory, they learn a **consistency function** $\mathbf{f}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$ satisfying the *self-consistency property*:

$$\mathbf{f}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \mathbf{f}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t'}, t') \quad \text{for all } t, t' \in [\epsilon, T] \text{ on the same PF-ODE trajectory},$$

with the boundary condition $\mathbf{f}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_\epsilon, \epsilon) = \mathbf{x}_\epsilon$.

Training comes in two flavors.

Song and Dhariwal's improved techniques for CT identified a subtle flaw in the original formulation — the EMA teacher introduces a bias that the theory did not account for — and replaced it with direct parameterization, Pseudo-Huber losses from robust statistics, and a lognormal noise schedule.

> **Theorem (Consistency implies correctness, informal):** *If a consistency function satisfies exact self-consistency along the PF-ODE trajectories of a score model whose ODE generates $p_{\text{data}}$, and the boundary condition holds, then one-step samples $\mathbf{f}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_T, T)$ are distributed exactly as $p_{\text{data}}$.*

The gap between this theorem and practice is discretization: self-consistency is enforced only at finitely many noise levels, and the learned function interpolates between them [3].

### 4.4 Consistency Trajectory Models and the Generalization Hierarchy

**Consistency trajectory models (CTM)** (Kim et al., 2023) generalize consistency models by learning the map between *any two times* along the trajectory: $G_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, s)$ predicts $\mathbf{x}_s$ for arbitrary $s < t$. Setting $s = 0$ recovers the consistency model; the network also outputs the score, recovering the score-based model. CTM thus unifies all three families and enables *$\gamma$-sampling*: long deterministic jumps interleaved with stochastic corrections. It achieved single-step CIFAR-10 FID of **1.73** and ImageNet-64 FID of **1.92** — state of the art for one-step generation [4].

A related line, **shortcut models**, takes the bootstrapping idea further: instead of distilling the full trajectory in one curriculum of $T$ steps, they train the network to be consistent under *halved* step sizes, requiring only $\log_2 T$ bootstraps, and they recover many-step generation as a non-bootstrapped base case [7].

Three conclusions emerge. First, the path from 1000-step DDPM to 1-step CTM is a *three-order-of-magnitude* cost reduction with no FID loss [2][4][6]. Second, training from scratch (CT) surpassed distillation (CD), confirming the distillation ceiling is real [3]. Third, the two-step regime is disproportionately valuable: one extra evaluation closes most of the remaining gap [3][4].

### 5.2 Convergence Theory

The empirical story is increasingly backed by non-asymptotic theory. Recent analyses provide end-to-end guarantees covering both phases of the pipeline: (i) denoising score matching under gradient descent converges to an $\varepsilon$-accurate score estimator at a rate depending on the data distribution's log-Sobolev constant, and (ii) the sampling error of the discretized reverse process decomposes into score-estimation error, discretization error scaling with the step size, and prior mismatch decaying with $T$. Notably, the theory *derives* the design choices of EDM — the noise schedule, the loss weighting — as approximately optimal rather than merely empirically successful [10].

For consistency models specifically, convergence analyses establish total-variation error bounds for the distilled one-step sampler in terms of the teacher's score error and the discretization granularity, confirming the intuition that consistency training inherits the teacher's errors plus an interpolation penalty [8].

### 5.3 Proof Sketch: Time-Reversal Preserves Marginals

We sketch the central result. Let $p_t$ solve the Fokker–Planck equation of the forward SDE and define the candidate reverse drift $\bar{\mathbf{f}} = \mathbf{f} - g^2 \nabla \log p_t$. Substituting into the reverse-time Fokker–Planck equation and using $\nabla \cdot (p_t \nabla \log p_t) = \Delta p_t$, the diffusion terms cancel, leaving the PDE satisfied by $p_{T-t}$. By uniqueness, the reverse process initialized at $p_T$ has marginals $p_{T-t}$ — it exactly undoes the forward diffusion [1]. The full argument needs care with boundary terms and score integrability — the conditions that fail in low-density regions, where practical score networks struggle [5].

## 6. Limitations

**Score estimation in low-density regions.** Denoising score matching is a regression of the score against Gaussian-perturbed samples; where $p_t$ is small, few training samples land, and the learned score is unreliable. The reverse SDE is most sensitive to score errors precisely where the score is largest — near the data manifold at small $t$ — creating a structural tension that no weighting scheme fully resolves [1][5].

**Discretization bias and the consistency gap.** Progressive distillation compounds the teacher's discretization error across halving rounds; consistency models enforce self-consistency only on a finite grid. The persistent 1–2 point FID gap between one-step and multi-step generation is the measurable footprint of this bias [2][3].

**Likelihood versus perceptual quality.** The weighting $\lambda(t) = g(t)^2$ that optimizes the likelihood bound is *not* the weighting that optimizes FID, and models trained for one objective underperform on the other. Distilled students inherit whichever trade-off the teacher made, and there is no free choice of weighting that is simultaneously optimal for both [5].

**Evaluation and metric gaming.** Training with learned perceptual metrics such as LPIPS biases FID itself, which is why improved CT moved to the Pseudo-Huber loss; cross-paper FID comparisons also suffer from inconsistent Inception weights and sample counts [3].

**The Schrödinger bridge is still approximate.** DSB's iterative proportional fitting converges in theory but each iteration requires training two score networks; in practice one or two iterations are affordable, leaving residual prior mismatch. Exact finite-time transport between arbitrary $p_{\text{data}}$ and $\pi$ remains computationally out of reach [9].

## 7. Conclusion

The arc from score-based SDEs to consistency distillation is, in retrospect, a single story about *what a generative model is*. The SDE framework taught us that generation is time-reversed diffusion and that the score is the sufficient statistic of that reversal [1]. The probability-flow ODE taught us that the stochasticity is optional — the same marginals can be transported deterministically, with exact likelihoods and invertible codes as the reward [1][6]. Distillation taught us that the *integration* of that ODE can be amortized into the weights of a network, first by halving steps and then by collapsing the entire trajectory into a consistency function [2][3][3]. And the trajectory models taught us that these are not competing paradigms but points on a continuum, unified by learning maps between arbitrary times along the flow [4][8].

Open problems remain. The score-estimation bottleneck in low-density regions is fundamental to denoising score matching and may require new objectives; the consistency gap has narrowed but not closed; and the Schrödinger bridge formulation hints at a foundation in which the forward process itself is learned, dissolving the boundary between diffusion models and optimal transport [9]. What is no longer in doubt is the framework's centrality: in five years, score-based diffusion moved from a unifying proposal to the engine behind the highest-fidelity generative systems in existence, and the distillation techniques surveyed here made that engine fast enough to deploy.
