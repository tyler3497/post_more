# Intersection Theory: Chow Rings, the Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch Theorem, and Enumerative Geometry

## Abstract

Intersection theory is the rigorous calculus that turns classical enumerative questions — how many lines meet four given lines in space, how many conics are tangent to five given conics — into computations in the Chow ring of a parameter space. This thesis develops the theory from its foundations: cycles and rational equivalence, the moving lemma, and the deformation to the normal cone that defines Fulton's intersection product on smooth varieties. We construct Chern classes through the splitting principle, compute the Chow rings of projective spaces and Grassmannians, and deploy Schubert calculus to recover the 27 lines on a smooth cubic surface and Chasles' 3264 tangent conics. We prove the projection formula and give a full account of the Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem, relating the Chern character and Todd class through proper pushforward, with the Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch formula as its central corollary. We conclude with the Behrend–Fantechi theory of intrinsic normal cones and virtual fundamental classes, the modern engine of Gromov–Witten and Donaldson–Thomas theory, and we delineate the theory's limitations on singular varieties and beyond rational equivalence.

## 1 Introduction

Enumerative geometry asks how many geometric figures satisfy a given list of incidence conditions. Two distinct lines in the projective plane meet in exactly one point; a line and a conic meet in two. Bézout's theorem generalizes these observations: two plane curves of degrees *d* and *e* with no common component meet in exactly *de* points, counted with multiplicity [1]. But the subject quickly leaves such elementary territory. A smooth cubic surface in three-space contains exactly **27 lines**. There are exactly **3264** smooth conics tangent to five given conics in general position. These numbers fascinated nineteenth-century geometers — Steiner, Chasles, Zeuthen, Schubert — who computed them by brilliant but unrigorous "conservation of number" arguments, deforming configurations until they became tractable and asserting that the count does not change.

Schubert's *Kalkül der abzählenden Geometrie* of 1879 [5] pushed this symbolic calculus to astonishing extremes, tabulating hundreds of millions of quadric surfaces tangent to nine given ones, with no rigorous foundation whatsoever. Hilbert's fifteenth problem demanded that the Schubert calculus be put on solid ground. The modern answer, developed by Fulton and MacPherson and presented systematically in Fulton's *Intersection Theory* [1], is a ring structure on the Chow groups of algebraic cycles modulo rational equivalence, equipped with proper pushforward, flat pullback, Chern classes of vector bundles, and a refined intersection product built from the deformation to the normal cone. Its crown jewel is the Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem, which computes the behavior of coherent sheaves under proper pushforward in terms of characteristic classes.

This thesis develops that theory in full. We begin with cycles and rational equivalence, construct the intersection product, develop Chern classes via the splitting principle, compute the Chow rings that make enumerative geometry possible, prove Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch, and close with the virtual fundamental classes that carry intersection theory into modern enumerative geometry.

## 2 Background

Let *X* be a scheme of finite type over a field *k*; for enumerative applications we usually take *k* algebraically closed, though the foundations work in great generality.

**Cycles.** The group *Z_k(X)* of *k*-dimensional cycles is the free abelian group on the *k*-dimensional irreducible closed subsets of *X*. A *rational equivalence* is generated by divisors of rational functions: for *W ⊂ X* irreducible of dimension *k + 1* and *f* a nonzero rational function on *W*, the divisor *div(f) = Σ ord_V(f)·[V]*, summed over codimension-one subvarieties *V ⊂ W*, is declared to be zero. The **Chow group** is the quotient

> **Definition:** *A_k(X) = Z_k(X) / Rat_k(X)*, where *Rat_k(X)* is the subgroup generated by all *div(f)* as above.

For *X* equidimensional of dimension *n*, one grades by codimension: *A^i(X) = A_{n−i}(X)*. The groups are functorial in two complementary ways [1]:

- **Proper pushforward.** For *f: X → Y* proper, *f_∗: A_k(X) → A_k(Y)* sends *[V]* to *deg(V / f(V))·[f(V)]* when dimensions agree, and to zero otherwise.
- **Flat pullback.** For *f: X → Y* flat of relative dimension *n*, *f^∗: A_k(Y) → A_{k+n}(X)*.

Both operations preserve rational equivalence, which is what makes the theory work. When *X* is proper over *k*, there is a **degree homomorphism** *∫_X: A_0(X) → ℤ* summing coefficients; intersection numbers are degrees of zero-cycles.

The first examples already contain Bézout's theorem:

| Variety | Chow group | Generator |
|---|---|---|
| *A_k(ℙ^n)* | *ℤ* | class of a *k*-plane |
| *A_{n−1}(ℙ^n)* | *ℤ* | hyperplane class *h*, via degree |
| *A_0(X)*, *X* proper | *ℤ* via degree | point class |

A plane curve *C* of degree *d* has class *[C] = d·ℓ* in *A_1(ℙ²)*, where *ℓ* is the line class; two curves of degrees *d*, *e* with no common component satisfy *∫ [C]·[D] = de*. Divisors deserve special mention: on a smooth variety, Cartier divisors modulo linear equivalence form the Picard group, identified with *A_{n−1}(X)*, and intersecting a cycle with a Cartier divisor is defined by restricting the defining equation — the local prototype of every intersection product [1].

## 3 Methodology

The central problem is that two subvarieties need not meet transversely, or even in the expected dimension. Fulton's construction proceeds through several conceptual stages.

**Deformation to the normal cone.** For a closed immersion *X ↪ Y*, there is a flat family *M → ℙ¹* whose general fiber is *Y* and whose special fiber is the **normal cone** *C_X Y*. Flatness yields a **specialization homomorphism** *σ: A_k(Y) → A_k(C_X Y)*: cycles on *Y* are degenerated to cycles on the cone. This is the rigorous form of "conservation of number" — intersection numbers are preserved under the deformation, and the cone is a linear object on which intersections are computable [1].

**Gysin pullback.** If *i: X ↪ Y* is a *regular embedding* of codimension *d* (locally defined by a regular sequence) with normal bundle *N*, specialization followed by intersection with the zero section of *N* gives *i^!: A_k(Y) → A_{k−d}(X)*.

**The intersection product.** On a smooth variety *X*, the diagonal *Δ: X ↪ X × X* is a regular embedding. For classes *α, β*, the product is

> **Definition:** *α · β = Δ^!(α × β) ∈ A_∗(X).*

This makes *A^∗(X)* a commutative graded ring — but commutativity and associativity are deep theorems, not formalities [1].

**The moving lemma.** On a smooth quasi-projective variety, every cycle is rationally equivalent to one meeting any prescribed finite collection of cycles *properly* (in the expected dimension). Hence products can be computed naively after moving cycles into transverse position — the rigorous license for classical arguments [1], [2].

**The projection formula.** For *f: X → Y* proper, the identity

> **Theorem (Projection formula):** *f_∗(f^∗α · β) = α · f_∗β*

holds for *α ∈ A^∗(Y)*, *β ∈ A_∗(X)*. This is the workhorse of enumerative geometry: computations performed upstairs on a parameter space are pushed down to numbers.

**Chern classes from the projective bundle theorem.** For a rank-*r* vector bundle *E → X* with projectivization *π: ℙ(E) → X* and *ξ = c_1(O_{ℙ(E)}(1))*, one has *A_∗(ℙ(E)) ≅ A_∗(X)[ξ]* modulo the single relation *ξ^r − π^∗c_1(E)ξ^{r−1} + ⋯ + (−1)^r π^∗c_r(E) = 0*, which *defines* the Chern classes *c_i(E) ∈ A^i(X)*. The **blow-up formula** similarly describes *A^∗(Bl_Z X)* via the exceptional divisor *E = ℙ(N_{Z/X})*, with relations governed by the Chern classes of the normal bundle [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Chow Rings of Projective Space and Grassmannians

The Chow ring of projective space is the prototype of every enumerative computation:

> **Theorem:** *A^∗(ℙ^n) ≅ ℤ[h] / (h^{n+1})*, where *h = c_1(O(1))* is the hyperplane class, and *∫_{ℙ^n} h^n = 1*.

Bézout's theorem is the statement that *[C]·[D] = de·[pt]* in this ring. Far richer is the Grassmannian *G(1,3)* of lines in *ℙ³*, a smooth fourfold embedded by Plücker coordinates as a quadric in *ℙ⁵*. Fix a flag of linear spaces; the **Schubert varieties** *Ω_{a,b}* parametrize lines satisfying incidence conditions with the flag (for instance, meeting a given line, or contained in a given plane). Their classes *σ_{a,b} ∈ A^{a+b}(G(1,3))*, for *2 ≥ a ≥ b ≥ 0*, form a *ℤ*-basis. The special class *σ_1 = σ_{1,0}* is both the hyperplane class and the class of lines meeting a fixed line. Products are governed by **Pieri's rule**: *σ_λ · σ_r* is the sum of *σ_μ* over partitions *μ* obtained from *λ* by adding a horizontal *r*-strip. Iterating,

*σ_1² = σ_{2,0} + σ_{1,1},  σ_1³ = 2σ_{2,1},  σ_1⁴ = 2σ_{2,2}*,

and since *σ_{2,2}* is the point class, *∫ σ_1⁴ = 2*: exactly two lines meet four general lines in *ℙ³*. **Giambelli's formula** expresses every *σ_λ* as a determinant in the special classes, and **Porteous' formula** computes the class of a degeneracy locus *D_k(φ)* of a bundle map *φ: E → F* as a determinant in the Chern classes of *F − E* — the engine behind counts of singular elements of linear series [1], [2].

### 4.2 Chern Classes and the Splitting Principle

Chern classes are characterized by three axioms: *c_0(E) = 1* and *c_i(E) = 0* for *i > rank(E)*; naturality under pullback; and the **Whitney sum formula** *c(E) = c(E′)·c(E″)* for short exact sequences, together with the normalization *c_1(O_{ℙ^n}(1)) = h*. The decisive computational tool is the **splitting principle**: for any bundle *E* there is a flat morphism *π* (a flag bundle) with *π^∗* injective on Chow groups such that *π^∗E ≅ ⊕ L_i* splits as line bundles. Writing *x_i = c_1(L_i)* for the **Chern roots**, every symmetric polynomial identity in the *x_i* descends to *X*. For example, from the Euler sequence *0 → O → V ⊗ O(1) → T_{ℙ^n} → 0* one reads off *c(T_{ℙ^n}) = (1 + h)^{n+1}* in *ℤ[h]/(h^{n+1})*, whence *∫ c_n(T_{ℙ^n}) = n + 1*, the topological Euler characteristic. The **Segre classes** *s(E) = c(E)^{−1}* appear dually, governing normal cones and blow-ups [1].

### 4.3 The Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch Theorem

For a vector bundle with Chern roots *x_i*, the **Chern character** *ch(E) = Σ e^{x_i} = r + c_1 + (c_1² − 2c_2)/2 + ⋯* is additive on exact sequences, and the **Todd class** *td(E) = ∏ x_i/(1 − e^{−x_i}) = 1 + c_1/2 + (c_1² + c_2)/12 + ⋯* is multiplicative. These correct the failure of the naive pushforward to respect characteristic classes:

> **Theorem (Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch):** Let *f: X → Y* be a proper morphism of smooth varieties. For every coherent sheaf *F* on *X*,
> *ch(f_!F) · td(T_Y) = f_∗(ch(F) · td(T_X))*
> in *A^∗(Y) ⊗ ℚ*, where *f_!* is the alternating sum of higher direct images in *K*-theory.

Taking *Y* to be a point gives the **Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch formula** *χ(X, F) = ∫_X ch(F)·td(T_X)*. For a smooth curve *C* of genus *g* and a line bundle *L* of degree *d*, this recovers the classical Riemann–Roch theorem *h⁰(L) − h¹(L) = d + 1 − g*; for a smooth surface it yields Noether's formula *χ(O_X) = (c_1² + c_2)/12* [1], [2]. The proof factors *f* into a closed immersion followed by a projection, verifying the formula for *ℙ^n → pt* by direct computation and propagating it through deformations — a triumph of the functorial machinery of §3.

### 4.4 Enumerative Geometry: Classical Triumphs

With the machinery in place, the classical numbers fall out as ring computations:

1. **Two lines meet four lines.** *∫_{G(1,3)} σ_1⁴ = 2*, computed above by Pieri's rule [2].
2. **Twenty-seven lines on a smooth cubic surface.** Lines in *ℙ³* contained in a cubic surface *S* are the zeros of a section of the rank-4 bundle *Sym³ S^∨* over *G(1,3)*, where *S* is the tautological subbundle. The class of the zero locus is the top Chern class *c_4(Sym³ S^∨)*, and *∫ c_4 = 27*. For *S* smooth the section is transverse, so the 27 lines are distinct [2].
3. **The 3264 conics.** Chasles introduced the *characteristics* *μ* (conics through a point) and *ν* (conics tangent to a line) on a smooth compactification — the **space of complete conics** — where every condition is a polynomial in *μ, ν*. The characteristic numbers are

| Condition | *μ⁵* | *μ⁴ν* | *μ³ν²* | *μ²ν³* | *μν⁴* | *ν⁵* |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number | 1 | 2 | 4 | 4 | 2 | 1 |

The divisor of conics tangent to a fixed smooth conic has class *2(μ + ν)*, so the number tangent to five general conics is *∫ (2(μ + ν))⁵ = 32·(1 + 10 + 40 + 40 + 10 + 1) = 3264*. Steiner had claimed *7776 = 6⁵* in 1848; Chasles' correction stood for a century before Fulton and MacPherson supplied complete proofs around 1978 [4].

The Schubert calculus scales to still larger numbers [5], [6]:

| Figures | Conditions | Number |
|---|---|---|
| Lines on a general quintic threefold | contained in *X ⊂ ℙ⁴* | 2,875 |
| Conics on a general quintic threefold | contained in *X* | 609,250 |
| Conics in *ℙ³* | tangent to 8 quadrics | 4,407,296 |
| Quadric surfaces in *ℙ³* | tangent to 9 quadrics | 666,841,088 |
| Twisted cubics in *ℙ³* | tangent to 12 quadrics | 5,819,539,783,680 |

Schubert computed the last two in 1879 [5]; the twisted-cubic number was rigorously verified a century later [6]. Remarkably, the 3264 problem is *fully real*: there exist five real conics for which all 3264 tangent conics are real [7].

### 4.5 Virtual Fundamental Classes and Modern Extensions

Classical intersection theory counts only when the parameter space is smooth of the expected dimension. Moduli spaces in modern enumerative geometry — stable maps, stable sheaves — are typically singular and oversized. Behrend and Fantechi resolved this by constructing a **virtual fundamental class** [3]:

- To a Deligne–Mumford stack *X* they attach the **intrinsic normal cone** *C_X*, an Artin stack of pure dimension zero over *X*, built by gluing local normal cones.
- A **perfect obstruction theory** *φ: E^• → L_X* is a morphism in the derived category from a perfect complex concentrated in degrees *−1* and *0*, inducing an isomorphism on *h⁰* and a surjection on *h^{−1}* of the cotangent complex.
- The cone embeds into the vector-bundle stack *h¹/h⁰(E^∨_•)*; intersecting with the zero section yields *[X, φ]^vir = 0^![C_X] ∈ A_{vd}(X)*, where *vd = rank E^•* is the virtual dimension.

> **Theorem (Behrend–Fantechi):** The virtual class is deformation-invariant, coincides with *[X]* when *X* is smooth, and is independent of the chosen global resolution. Integrating cohomology classes against it defines Gromov–Witten invariants; symmetric obstruction theories on Calabi–Yau threefolds define Donaldson–Thomas invariants.

Equivariant localization extends the classical Bott residue formula to this setting, reducing virtual integrals to fixed-point data. Virtual classes are now understood as shadows of derived algebraic geometry, where the moduli space carries a derived structure whose truncation recovers the cone [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We verify the two flagship computations symbolically. First, Pieri's rule on partitions in the *2×2* box gives *σ_1⁴ = 2σ_{2,2}*; second, with *x, y* the Chern roots of *S^∨* on *G(1,3)*, the top Chern class of *Sym³ S^∨* (roots *3x, 2x+y, x+2y, 3y*) reduces via the Chow-ring relations *σ_1³ = 2σ_1σ_{1,1}*, *σ_1²σ_{1,1} = σ_{1,1}² = σ_{2,2}* to *27σ_{2,2}*:

```python
import sympy as sp
from math import comb

# --- (a) Pieri's rule on G(1,3): partitions fitting in a 2x2 box ---
def mul_sigma1(classes):
    out = {}
    for (p1, p2), c in classes.items():
        if p1 < 2:  # add a box to the first row
            out[(p1 + 1, p2)] = out.get((p1 + 1, p2), 0) + c
        if p2 < p1:  # add a box to the second row
            out[(p1, p2 + 1)] = out.get((p1, p2 + 1), 0) + c
    return out

cls = {(0, 0): 1}
for _ in range(4):
    cls = mul_sigma1(cls)
print("sigma_1^4 =", cls)  # {(2, 2): 2}: two lines meet four general lines

# --- (b) 27 lines: top Chern class of Sym^3 of a rank-2 bundle ---
x, y = sp.symbols('x y')
c4 = 9 * x * y * (2 * x + y) * (x + 2 * y)          # product of the four roots
s1, s11, s22 = sp.symbols('s1 s11 s22')             # sigma_1, sigma_{1,1}, point class
assert sp.expand(c4 - (18 * (x + y)**2 * (x * y) + 9 * (x * y)**2)) == 0
c4_schub = 18 * s1**2 * s11 + 9 * s11**2            # via s1 = x+y, s11 = x*y

def reduce_chow(p):                                 # relations in A*(G(1,3))
    p = sp.Poly(p, s1, s11, s22)
    while True:
        terms = dict(p.as_dict()); changed = False; new = {}
        for (i, j, k), c in terms.items():
            if i >= 3:
                new[(i - 2, j + 1, k)] = new.get((i - 2, j + 1, k), 0) + 2 * c
                changed = True                     # s1^3 = 2 s1 s11
            elif i == 2 and j >= 1:
                new[(0, 0, k + 1)] = new.get((0, 0, k + 1), 0) + c
                changed = True                     # s1^2 s11 = point class
            elif j >= 2:
                new[(i, j - 2, k + 1)] = new.get((i, j - 2, k + 1), 0) + c
                changed = True                     # s11^2 = point class
            else:
                new[(i, j, k)] = new.get((i, j, k), 0) + c
        p = sp.Poly(sum(c * s1**i * s11**j * s22**k for (i, j, k), c in new.items()),
                    s1, s11, s22)
        if not changed:
            return p.as_expr()

print("c_4(Sym^3 S*) =", reduce_chow(c4_schub))     # 27*s22

# --- (c) Chasles: 3264 conics tangent to five conics ---
char = {5: 1, 4: 2, 3: 4, 2: 4, 1: 2, 0: 1}         # mu^i nu^{5-i}
total = sum(comb(5, i) * char[i] for i in range(6)) # (mu+nu)^5 = 102
print("(mu+nu)^5 =", total, "-> 32 *", total, "=", 32 * total)
```

The script outputs *σ_1⁴ = {(2,2): 2}*, *c_4 = 27·s22*, and *(μ+ν)⁵ = 102 → 3264*, confirming the three central enumerative identities of §4.4. Behind these computations lies the **conservation of number**: rational equivalence on *ℙ¹* gives *A_0(ℙ¹) ≅ ℤ*, so a family of zero-cycles over *ℙ¹* has constant degree — the count computed in a degenerate special fiber equals the count for general position [1].

## 6 Limitations

The theory's power comes with sharp boundaries:

- **Singular varieties.** Chow's moving lemma fails on singular *X*, and *A^∗(X)* has no ring structure in general. Fulton's **bivariant theory** [1] repairs functoriality by working with operational classes, but explicit computation remains hard.
- **Finer equivalences.** Rational equivalence is the finest adequate relation, but algebraic, homological, and numerical equivalence are coarser and genuinely different: Griffiths exhibited cycles algebraically equivalent to zero but not rationally so, and the standard conjectures predicting homological equals numerical equivalence remain open.
- **Excess intersection.** When cycles meet in larger than the expected dimension, the naive product is wrong; the correction is the top Chern class of the **excess normal bundle** [1].
- **Positive characteristic.** The foundations work, but resolution of singularities and weak factorization are unavailable, and several enumerative arguments require characteristic zero. Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch in any case requires rational coefficients for the Todd denominators.
- **Virtual classes.** A perfect obstruction theory is not intrinsic to *X* — it is extra data — and virtual classes integrate only against the virtual dimension, saying nothing about the actual geometry of oversized components.
- **Computability.** Chow rings are known completely only for special classes — homogeneous spaces, toric varieties, and a handful of moduli spaces. Each new enumerative problem typically demands inventing a new compactification, as the space of complete conics did for the 3264 problem [4].

## 7 Conclusion

Intersection theory transformed enumerative geometry from a collection of ingenious but fragile tricks into a functorial calculus: rational equivalence makes counts deformation-invariant, Chern classes linearize nonlinear conditions, and Schubert calculus turns incidence geometry into combinatorics of partitions. The Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem extends the same philosophy from cycles to sheaves, unifying Riemann–Roch across dimensions. The Behrend–Fantechi virtual class carries the theory into the twenty-first century, underwriting Gromov–Witten and Donaldson–Thomas theory and the mirror-symmetry predictions — such as the 2875 lines on the quintic threefold — that classical methods could never reach. The open frontiers are clear: derived and logarithmic refinements of the virtual class, motivic lifts of intersection numbers, and the standard conjectures that would collapse the tower of equivalence relations into one.
