# The Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture: L-Functions, Selmer Groups, and Heegner Points

## Abstract

The Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, formulated in 1965 from numerical experiments on elliptic curves over the rationals, predicts that the order of vanishing of the Hasse–Weil L-function at s = 1 equals the rank of the Mordell–Weil group, and refines this prediction to an exact formula for the leading Taylor coefficient in terms of the regulator, the real period, Tamagawa numbers, the torsion subgroup, and the order of the Tate–Shafarevich group. This thesis develops the analytic machinery of Hasse–Weil L-functions, the cohomological descent formalism of Selmer groups, and the two great constructive machineries of the theory — Heegner points with the Gross–Zagier formula and Euler systems in the sense of Kolyvagin — and surveys the current frontier: modularity, Iwasawa-theoretic main conjectures of Skinner–Urban, Wei Zhang's work on the indivisibility of Heegner points, and the parity results of Bhargava–Shankar on average ranks. The precise points at which progress stops are identified: the finiteness of the Tate–Shafarevich group in general and the analytic continuation of L-functions beyond modular cases.

##1 Introduction

Few problems in mathematics combine so much concrete computability with such deep inaccessibility as the question of describing the rational points on an elliptic curve. Given a smooth projective curve E over the rational numbers defined by a Weierstrass equation

```
y^2 + a1 x y + a3 y = x^3 + a2 x^2 + a4 x + a6,    ai ∈ Q
```

Mordell's theorem of 1922 asserts that the abelian group E(Q) of rational points is finitely generated. One therefore has a decomposition

```
E(Q) ≅ E(Q)tors ⊕ Z^r
```

where the torsion subgroup E(Q)tors is finite and completely understood — Mazur's torsion theorem of 1978 classifies all fifteen possible torsion structures over Q — while the integer r, the *rank*, measures how many points of infinite order the curve possesses. No provable general algorithm is known that computes r for an arbitrary elliptic curve [4]. The Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture proposes that this purely algebraic invariant is encoded in a purely analytic object: the Hasse–Weil L-function L(E, s) of the curve, evaluated — or rather, vanishing — at the point s = 1.

> Theorem: (Mordell, 1922) For every elliptic curve E defined over Q, the group E(Q) is finitely generated.

The conjecture arose, famously, from machine computation. In the early 1960s, Bryan Birch and Peter Swinnerton-Dyer programmed the EDSAC computer at Cambridge to count, for each elliptic curve E, the numbers N_p = |Ẽ(F_p)| of points on the reduced curve modulo primes p, and to study the product over all primes p less than X of the ratio N_p / p. They observed that this product grows like C_E (log X)^r as X tends to infinity, where r is the observed rank and C_E a constant depending only on the curve [1]. Since N_p = p + 1 − a_p is encoded in the Euler factor of L(E, s) at p, formally "evaluating" the Euler product at s = 1 recovers exactly the product they computed. The analytic hypothesis was thus born: L(E, s), a priori convergent only for Re(s) > 3/2, extends analytically to the whole complex plane, and its vanishing behavior at the center of the critical strip determines the rank.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the analytic theory of Hasse–Weil L-functions and the cohomological descent machinery of Selmer groups and the Tate–Shafarevich group. Section 3 states the conjecture in its weak and strong forms and explains the methodology by which each side of the formula is approached. Section 4 develops the three pillars of modern progress — Heegner points and the Gross–Zagier formula, Kolyvagin's Euler systems, and Iwasawa theory — together with the p-adic refinements. Section 5 records the precise theorems that are now known, and Section 6 the precise obstructions that remain.

##2 Background

### The Hasse–Weil L-function

Let E/Q be an elliptic curve of conductor N. For each prime p not dividing N, reduction modulo p gives an elliptic curve Ẽ over the finite field F_p, and the number of points satisfies

```
N_p = p + 1 − a_p,    with |a_p| ≤ 2√p   (Hasse's bound)
```

For primes p dividing N, the reduction is singular and one defines a_p ∈ {0, ±1} according to the Kodaira type. The Hasse–Weil L-function is the Euler product

```
L(E, s) = ∏_p 1 / (1 − a_p p^{−s} + ε(p) p^{1−2s})
```

where ε(p) = 1 for good primes and 0 for bad primes; the product converges absolutely for Re(s) > 3/2. The completed L-function Λ(E, s) = N^{s/2} (2π)^{−s} Γ(s) L(E, s) is conjectured to satisfy the functional equation Λ(E, s) = w_E Λ(E, 2 − s) with root number w_E ∈ {±1}. Before the year 2001 this analytic continuation was known only in special cases (complex multiplication, some modular cases); it is now a theorem for *every* elliptic curve over Q, a consequence of the modularity theorem of Wiles, Taylor–Wiles, and Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor [4]: every E/Q is a quotient of the modular Jacobian J_0(N), so L(E, s) = L(f, s) for a weight-two newform f of level N, and L(f, s) continues analytically by Hecke's classical theory.

### Selmer groups and descent

The algebraic side is attacked through *descent*. For an integer n ≥ 2, multiplication by n on E gives the exact sequence of Galois modules 0 → E[n] → E → E → 0, whence a connecting homomorphism

```
δ: E(Q) / nE(Q) → H^1(G_Q, E[n])
```

The *n-Selmer group* Sel^(n)(E/Q) is the subgroup of classes in H^1(G_Q, E[n]) that are locally in the image of δ at every place v of Q, i.e. the classes that become rational points over each completion Q_v. Descent computes Sel^(n)(E/Q) explicitly — for n = 2, classically, by solving quartic homogeneous spaces — and one has the fundamental exact sequence

```
0 → E(Q)/nE(Q) → Sel^(n)(E/Q) → Ш(E/Q)[n] → 0
```

where Ш(E/Q), the *Tate–Shafarevich group*, parametrizes principal homogeneous spaces for E that are everywhere locally soluble but have no global rational point. It is the obstruction to the local-to-global principle for curves of genus one, and its conjectural finiteness is the great open problem of the algebraic theory [9].

### The strong conjecture

Let r = rank E(Q), let R_E be the canonical regulator (the covolume of E(Q)/tors in R^r under the Néron–Tate height pairing), let Ω_E be the real period, let c_p be the Tamagawa numbers at bad primes, and let Ш denote the conjecturally finite Tate–Shafarevich group. The *strong* Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer formula asserts

```
L^(r)(E, 1) / r! =  ( Ω_E · R_E · |Ш(E/Q)| · ∏_p c_p ) / |E(Q)_tors|^2
```

where L^(r)(E,1) is the r-th derivative at s = 1. The weak conjecture is the statement ord_{s=1} L(E, s) = r; the strong formula predicts the exact leading coefficient. The presence of |Ш| in the numerator means that the strong conjecture implies the finiteness of Ш, since the left-hand side is a nonzero finite real number.

##3 Methodology

The methodology of the thesis follows the two-track structure forced by the conjecture itself: an analytic track and an algebraic track, with the deep work lying in constructing bridges between them.

1. **Analytic track.** Compute or bound ord_{s=1} L(E, s) using modular symbols, Heegner-point constructions, and nonvanishing theorems. The functional equation gives the parity relation (−1)^{ord} = w_E, so the root number determines the parity of the analytic rank. The decisive analytic instrument for rank one is the Gross–Zagier formula [2], which computes L′(E, 1) as the Néron–Tate height of a Heegner point.
2. **Algebraic track.** Bound the Mordell–Weil rank from above via Selmer groups, and construct rational points from below. The construction technology is *Euler systems*: compatible families of cohomology classes whose explicit reciprocity laws relate them to special values of L-functions. Kolyvagin's Euler system of Heegner points yields the structure theorem for Selmer groups in analytic rank at most one [3]; Kato's Euler system from Beilinson elements handles the p-adic side [10].
3. **Iwasawa-theoretic track.** Study the growth of Selmer groups in towers of number fields. The main conjectures of Iwasawa theory identify characteristic ideals of Selmer modules with p-adic L-functions; Skinner–Urban's proof for GL(2) yields the p-part of the BSD formula for many curves of analytic rank zero [5].
4. **Statistical track.** Average-rank results of Bhargava–Shankar and the parity distribution theorems give unconditional information about ranks of typical curves, constraining what the full conjecture can imply about rank statistics [8].

A key methodological principle is that the rank equality is proved *directionally*: upper bounds on algebraic rank via descent, lower bounds via constructed points, and equality when the two meet. The Gross–Zagier–Kolyvagin combination shows that when L(E, 1) ≠ 0 the algebraic rank is zero, and when ord = 1 the algebraic rank is exactly one — the conjecture's first nontrivial cases.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Heegner points and the Gross–Zagier formula

Let E/Q be of conductor N and let K be an imaginary quadratic field of discriminant D satisfying the Heegner hypothesis: every prime dividing N splits in K. The modular parametrization π: X_0(N) → E sends CM points on the modular curve — points corresponding to elliptic curves with complex multiplication by an order in K — to points on E defined over the ring class fields of K. Tracing down yields the *Heegner point* P_K ∈ E(K), well-defined up to torsion and sign.

> Theorem: (Gross–Zagier, 1986) Let P_K be the Heegner point on E/K. Then
> L′(E/K, 1) = (8π² (φ,φ) / (√|D| · ||ω||²)) · ĥ(P_K),
> where ĥ is the Néron–Tate canonical height and (φ,φ) the Petersson norm of the associated newform [2].

The formula's force is qualitative as much as quantitative: L′(E/K, 1) ≠ 0 *if and only if* P_K is of infinite order. Since L(E/K, s) = L(E, s) L(E^D, s) for the quadratic twist E^D, and the Heegner hypothesis forces the root number of E/K to be −1, one obtains in favorable cases that ord_{s=1} L(E, s) = 1 implies rank E(Q) ≥ 1 with an explicit generator. This is the only known general construction of rational points of infinite order on elliptic curves of analytic rank one — it converts the derivative of an L-function into a point.

A concrete instance: the curve 37a1 (Cremona label), y² + y = x³ − x, has analytic rank one, and the Heegner construction produces the point (0, 0) as generator of E(Q) modulo torsion, with the Gross–Zagier formula relating the height ĥ((0,0)) to L′(E, 1) exactly.

### 4.2 Euler systems and Kolyvagin's theorem

Kolyvagin's insight was to *differentiate* the Heegner point construction. From the basic Heegner point over the ring class field of conductor 1, he produced a whole tower of derived cohomology classes — the *Kolyvagin classes* κ_m ∈ H^1(K, T_p E) — indexed by squarefree products m of auxiliary primes, using norm relations between ring class fields of increasing conductor. These classes satisfy an explicit reciprocity law: the localization of κ_m at a prime dividing m is controlled by the localization of the Heegner point itself. The formalism was later axiomatized by Mazur–Rubin as *Kolyvagin systems* [9].

> Theorem: (Kolyvagin, 1988–1990) Let E/Q be modular with L(E, 1) ≠ 0. Then E(Q) is finite and Ш(E/Q) is finite. If ord_{s=1} L(E, s) = 1, then rank E(Q) = 1 and Ш(E/Q) is finite [3].

The proof's skeleton: assume a Selmer class c is "primitive" in a suitable sense; choose auxiliary primes via Chebotarev density so that the Kolyvagin classes annihilate the dual Selmer group; the reciprocity law forces a contradiction unless the Selmer group is as small as claimed. The argument bounds the Selmer group *from above*, and combined with the Gross–Zagier point (which bounds the rank from below), yields equality. This is the deepest unconditional theorem toward BSD known: the full weak conjecture in analytic ranks 0 and 1, plus finiteness of Ш in those cases.

### 4.3 Iwasawa theory and the main conjectures

Fix an odd prime p. In the cyclotomic Z_p-extension Q_∞ of Q, one studies the Pontryagin dual X of the p-primary Selmer group over the tower — a module over the Iwasawa algebra Λ = Z_p[[Gal(Q_∞/Q)]]. The Iwasawa main conjecture asserts that the characteristic ideal of X equals the ideal generated by the p-adic L-function L_p(E). Skinner and Urban proved the main conjecture for GL(2) under mild hypotheses [5], and the consequences for BSD are striking: for a positive proportion of curves, the p-part of the strong BSD formula holds when the analytic rank is zero, including control of the p-part of |Ш|.

Wei Zhang extended the Euler-system machinery to prove the *indivisibility* of Heegner points: the Heegner point is not divisible by p in the Mordell–Weil group for all but finitely many p, which yields the p-part of BSD in analytic rank one under standard hypotheses [6]. Together with Rubin's earlier proof of the main conjecture for CM elliptic curves via elliptic units [7], these results constitute the p-adic flank of the conjecture: they pin down the exact power of p dividing each side of the strong formula.

### 4.4 Average ranks and the statistical picture

A different, statistical methodology has yielded unconditional theorems about *typical* behavior. Bhargava and Shankar proved, using the geometry of numbers applied to spaces of binary quartic forms, that the average rank of elliptic curves ordered by height is bounded — indeed less than 1 — and that a positive proportion of curves have rank 0 and a positive proportion have rank 1 [8]. Combined with the parity theorem (the root number is equidistributed), this implies that the average *analytic* rank is also bounded, and the celebrated theorem of Bhargava–Shankar–... on the average size of 2-Selmer groups gives that at least a positive proportion of curves satisfy the full BSD conjecture (weak form) via the Gross–Zagier–Kolyvagin results.

| Result | Analytic rank | Algebraic conclusion | Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kolyvagin [3] | 0 | rank 0, Ш finite | Heegner Euler system |
| Kolyvagin [3] | 1 | rank 1, Ш finite | Gross–Zagier + Euler system |
| Skinner–Urban [5] | 0 | p-part of strong BSD | Iwasawa main conjecture |
| Wei Zhang [6] | 1 | p-part of strong BSD | Heegner point indivisibility |
| Bhargava–Shankar [8] | average | avg rank < 1, positive proportion rank 0 and 1 | Geometry of numbers |
| Rubin [7] | 0 (CM case) | p-part of BSD | Elliptic units |

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The computational verification of BSD has reached an extraordinary scale. Using modular symbols to compute L(E, 1) and L′(E, 1) to high precision, two-descent and higher descents to bound Selmer groups, and point searches to exhibit generators, the weak conjecture has been verified for every elliptic curve of conductor below 5000 in analytic ranks 0 and 1, and the strong formula checked numerically to high precision in the overwhelming majority of cases [5, cited work of Stein–Watkins]. The LMFDB (L-functions and Modular Forms Database) now carries BSD invariants — analytic rank, regulator, real and complex periods, Tamagawa product, torsion, and the conjectural order of Ш — for millions of curves, and the agreement of the two sides of the strong formula to dozens of decimal places constitutes the most extensive numerical evidence for any open problem in number theory.

A representative computation, in the style used by verification pipelines, proceeds as follows:

```python
# BSD verification pipeline (schematic, after Stein-Watkins)
from sage.all import EllipticCurve, QQ

def verify_weak_bsd(label):
    E = EllipticCurve(label)          # e.g. '37a1'
    r_an = E.analytic_rank()          # via modular symbols / Dokchitser
    r_lb = E.rank()                   # lower bound: point search
    sel2 = E.selmer_rank()            # upper bound: 2-descent
    assert r_lb <= r_an <= sel2 or (r_an == r_lb == sel2)
    return r_an, r_lb, sel2

# For '37a1': analytic rank 1, generator (0,0), 2-Selmer rank 1 -> verified
```

The proof landscape for the theorems, as opposed to the computations, rests on three completed bridges:

1. *Modularity* [4]: every E/Q is modular, so L(E, s) has analytic continuation and functional equation unconditionally. Without this, even the statement of the conjecture's analytic side would be conditional.
2. *Gross–Zagier–Kolyvagin* [2, 3]: the weak conjecture plus Ш-finiteness in analytic ranks 0 and 1.
3. *Iwasawa main conjectures* [5, 7]: the p-primary part of the strong formula in analytic rank 0 (and, via [6], rank 1) for large classes of curves and primes.

The historically important numerical example is the curve of rank 3 studied by Buhler–Gross–Zagier in 1985, y² + y = x³ − 7x + 6 (conductor 5077), where the regulator, period, and Tamagawa numbers were computed and the strong formula verified to the precision then available — the first serious test of the refined conjecture beyond rank 1.

```haskell
-- The BSD invariant tuple, as a pure data specification
data BSDInvariants = BSD
  { analyticRank :: Int      -- ord_{s=1} L(E,s)
  , regulator    :: Double   -- covolume under Neron-Tate pairing
  , realPeriod   :: Double   -- Omega_E
  , tamagawaProd :: Integer  -- product of local c_p
  , torsionOrder :: Integer  -- |E(Q)_tors|
  , shaOrder     :: Integer  -- conjectural |Sha(E/Q)|
  }
-- strong BSD: L^(r)(E,1)/r! == realPeriod*regulator*shaOrder*tamagawaProd / torsionOrder^2
```

##6 Limitations

The boundary of the known is sharp and well-mapped, and it is worth stating exactly where the machinery fails.

**Analytic rank ≥ 2: no general construction.** There is no known analogue of Heegner points producing points of infinite order when ord_{s=1} L(E, s) ≥ 2. The Gross–Zagier formula computes only the *first* derivative; higher derivatives L^(r)(E, 1) have no known geometric interpretation as heights of algebraic cycles in general. The Beilinson–Bloch conjectures predict such an interpretation via higher Chow groups, but this remains entirely conjectural. Consequently, the implication "analytic rank r implies algebraic rank ≥ r" is open for every r ≥ 2.

**Finiteness of Ш is open in general.** Kolyvagin's theorem gives finiteness only when the analytic rank is at most one. For analytic rank ≥ 2, not a single general finiteness result for Ш(E/Q) is known; indeed, Ш(E/Q)[p] could in principle be infinite for every p. The strong formula's prediction of |Ш| as an integer is therefore conditional on an unproven finiteness hypothesis in all higher-rank cases.

**The p-part of the strong formula is incomplete.** Skinner–Urban and Wei Zhang cover many ordinary primes but exclude, in general, primes of supersingular or additive reduction, primes dividing the degree of the modular parametrization, and the prime 2, where the Iwasawa theory is substantially harder. The 2-primary part of BSD — the most computationally accessible — remains theoretically the least understood.

**Effectivity.** Even where rank 0 or 1 is known, the proofs do not produce algorithms guaranteed to find generators or to certify Ш. The Tate–Shafarevich group can contain elements of arbitrarily large order invisible to any fixed descent, and no theorem bounds the search needed.

**Function-field analogues vs. number fields.** Over function fields of characteristic p, BSD is a theorem (Tate, Milne), because the geometry of surfaces supplies the missing finiteness. The number-field case lacks this geometric crutch; the analogy guides intuition but no proof transfers.

##7 Conclusion

The Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture stands as the central organizing problem of arithmetic geometry: it asserts that two invariants defined in utterly different worlds — the vanishing order of an analytic L-function at its central point, and the rank of a finitely generated abelian group of geometric origin — coincide, and moreover that the leading coefficient of the L-function's Taylor expansion is the exact product of the curve's canonical arithmetic invariants, including the mysterious order of the Tate–Shafarevich group.

What is known is substantial and hard-won: modularity makes the analytic side unconditional [4]; Gross–Zagier and Kolyvagin settle the weak conjecture and Ш-finiteness in analytic ranks zero and one [2, 3]; Iwasawa-theoretic main conjectures pin down p-primary parts of the strong formula [5, 6, 7]; and statistical theorems show the conjecture holds for a positive proportion of all curves [8]. What remains — ranks two and above, general Ш-finiteness, the full strong formula — will likely require a geometric theory of higher derivatives of L-functions that does not yet exist. The conjecture has survived six decades of the deepest tools in number theory; each partial victory has revealed not a path to the summit but the true scale of the mountain.
