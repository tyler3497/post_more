# Ramsey Theory: From the Erdős–Szekeres Monotone Subsequence Theorem to the Hales–Jewett Theorem and Arithmetic Regularity

## Abstract

Ramsey theory studies the principle that complete disorder is impossible: in any sufficiently large structured set, some highly organized subconfiguration must appear, under any partition. This article develops the classical theory from Frank Ramsey's 1930 foundational theorem [1] through the Erdős–Szekeres monotone subsequence theorem [2], Ramsey numbers including the 2023 exponential breakthrough of Campos, Griffiths, Morris, and Sahasrabudhe on diagonal Ramsey numbers [4][5], and the probabilistic lower bounds framing the field's central open problems. We then cross the bridge from graph Ramsey theory to arithmetic Ramsey theory via van der Waerden's theorem, Szemerédi's density theorem, Schur's theorem, and the Folkman–Rado–Sanders theorem. The centerpiece is the Hales–Jewett theorem [3] — the great unifying "combinatorial line" theorem — together with its density version and the polynomial method extensions that culminate in the Green–Tao theorem on arithmetic progressions in the primes [8]. We present the Furstenberg–Katznelson correspondence, the transference principle, explicit quantitative bounds, and a critical discussion of limitations: the primitive-recursive gaps in Hales–Jewett numbers, the tower-function bounds in Szemerédi regularity, and the still-unresolved Erdős–Szekeres convex polygon conjecture.

## 1 Introduction

In 1930, Frank Plumpton Ramsey published "On a Problem of Formal Logic," a paper whose ostensible subject — a decidability fragment of first-order logic — was almost immediately eclipsed by its most famous auxiliary result [1]. Ramsey proved that for any positive integers $s$ and $t$, there exists a smallest integer $R(s,t)$, now called a *Ramsey number*, such that every red–blue coloring of the edges of the complete graph on $R(s,t)$ vertices contains either a red complete subgraph on $s$ vertices or a blue complete subgraph on $t$ vertices. In the language of parties that has become standard: among any $R(s,t)$ people, there are $s$ mutual acquaintances or $t$ mutual strangers. The statement is *total*: it applies to every coloring, however adversarial.

> **Theorem:** (Ramsey, 1930 [1]) For all $s, t \geq 1$ there exists $R(s,t) \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for every 2-coloring of the edges of $K_n$ with $n \geq R(s,t)$, there is a monochromatic copy of $K_s$ in the first color or a monochromatic copy of $K_t$ in the second.

The theorem is paradigmatic of what Graham, Rothschild, and Spencer later named *Ramsey theory*: the systematic study of conditions under which "order must appear" [7]. Yet Ramsey's theorem is only the beginning of a deep and branching landscape. Several decades of research have shown that Ramsey phenomena occur not only in graphs but in arithmetic structures — most famously, van der Waerden's theorem on monochromatic arithmetic progressions, and Szemerédi's theorem, which asserts that every set of integers of positive upper density contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. The pinnacle of the unification is the Hales–Jewett theorem (1963) [3], which simultaneously generalizes van der Waerden's theorem and provides a "combinatorial line" framework rich enough to encode Ramsey-type phenomena across mathematics. From its polynomial generalizations and density forms, modern arithmetic combinatorics reaches the Green–Tao theorem (2008) — the primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions [8] — a result that joins analytic number theory, ergodic theory, and additive combinatorics in a single synthesis.

This article presents a self-contained tour of this architecture, with precise statements, quantitative bounds, proof sketches of the central theorems, and an assessment of the major open frontiers.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Classical Ramsey numbers

The two-color Ramsey number $R(s,t)$ was already studied quantitatively by Erdős and Szekeres in 1935 [2]. Their elementary proof yields the clean upper bound

$$R(s,t) \leq \binom{s+t-2}{s-1},$$

which for the diagonal case $R(k) = R(k,k)$ gives $R(k) \leq \binom{2k-2}{k-1} \leq 4^{k-1}$. For the lower bound, Erdős's 1947 probabilistic argument [6] shows that a uniformly random red–blue coloring of $K_n$ has positive probability of containing no monochromatic $K_k$ when $n < 2^{k/2}$, establishing $R(k) \geq 2^{k/2}$. These two bounds,

$$2^{k/2} \leq R(k) \leq 4^{k},$$

framed the field for nearly a century: the exponential gap between base $2$ and base $4$ became one of combinatorics' most notorious open problems [6].

The only exactly known non-trivial diagonal value is $R(3,3) = 6$, and the celebrated off-diagonal identity $R(3,4) = 9$, $R(3,5) = 14$. Beyond these, only small values are known; $R(5,5)$ remains famously unresolved.

### 2.2 Erdős–Szekeres and geometric Ramsey theory

In "A combinatorial problem in geometry" (1935), Erdős and Szekeres answered Esther Klein's question about convex polygons and simultaneously proved the monotone subsequence theorem that bears their names [2]:

> **Theorem:** (Erdős–Szekeres, 1935 [2]) Every sequence of $(r-1)(s-1)+1$ distinct real numbers contains an increasing subsequence of length $r$ or a decreasing subsequence of length $s$. In particular, every sequence of $n^2+1$ distinct reals contains a monotone subsequence of length $n+1$.

The elegant proof assigns to each position the pair $(a_i, b_i)$ where $a_i$ is the length of the longest increasing subsequence ending there and $b_i$ the longest decreasing one; if $i < j$ and $x_i < x_j$ then $a_j \geq a_i + 1$, and if $x_i > x_j$ then $b_j \geq b_i + 1$, so all pairs are distinct, and the pigeonhole bound follows immediately. This theorem is arguably the first *quantitative* Ramsey result, giving an exact Ramsey number for monotone subsequences, and its proof technique — a labeling argument via pigeonhole — became a template for the entire field.

The same paper launched geometric Ramsey theory: any set of $\binom{2n-4}{n-2}+1$ points in general position in the plane contains $n$ points in convex position, the famous "happy ending" problem. Erdős and Szekeres conjectured the exact value $2^{n-2}+1$; Suk (2016) proved $ES(n) = 2^{n+o(n)}$, essentially settling the exponential rate [2].

### 2.3 The probabilistic method

Erdős's lower bound for $R(k)$ is the founding example of the *probabilistic method*: to show that an object with a property exists, prove that a random object has the property with positive probability [6].

### 2.4 Arithmetic Ramsey theory: van der Waerden, Schur, Szemerédi

Van der Waerden (1927) proved that for every finite coloring of $\mathbb{N}$ and every $k$, there is a monochromatic arithmetic progression of length $k$. Schur (1916) proved that for every finite coloring of $\{1,\dots,S(r)\}$ there is a monochromatic solution to $x+y=z$; in fact Schur's theorem follows from Ramsey's theorem applied to edge-colorings. The Folkman–Rado–Sanders theorem strengthens Schur: for every finite coloring of $\mathbb{N}$ and every $k$, there is a set $\{x_1,\dots,x_k\}$ such that all nonempty subset sums are monochromatic.

The crowning density result is Szemerédi's theorem (1975): every subset of $\mathbb{N}$ of positive upper density contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. Szemerédi proved this directly by combinatorial means; Furstenberg later gave an ergodic-theoretic proof via the correspondence principle, and Gowers gave a quantitative Fourier-analytic proof with bounds of tower type.

## 3 Methodology

The proofs in Ramsey theory cluster around a small number of methodological archetypes, and this article treats them as first-class objects of study:

1. **Induction with recurrence relations.** The classic proof that $R(s,t)$ exists proceeds by the recurrence $R(s,t) \leq R(s-1,t) + R(s,t-1)$, selecting a vertex $v$ and partitioning the remainder into its red neighbors and blue neighbors; one side is large enough to apply induction [1][2].
2. **The probabilistic method and alteration.** Random colorings, the Lovász Local Lemma, and dependent random choice produce lower bounds and Ramsey-type constructions in sparse settings [6].
3. **Density increment and energy increment.** Szemerédi's regularity lemma and the density-increment proof of Roth's theorem argue by contradiction: if a dense set contains no $k$-term progression, some subprogression must exhibit increased density, iterating only boundedly many times.
4. **The correspondence principle.** Furstenberg's correspondence translates combinatorial statements about finite colorings into dynamical statements about measure-preserving systems, unlocking ergodic methods.
5. **The transference principle.** Green and Tao showed that results about dense sets can be transferred to sparse "pseudorandom" sets — such as the primes — via a suitable majorant and Hahn–Banach-type approximation [8].
6. **The Hales–Jewett / polynomial method.** Variable words and combinatorial lines provide a universal alphabet-theoretic encoding of Ramsey configurations, with the polynomial Hales–Jewett theorem (Bergelson–Leibman) extending the method to polynomial patterns [3].

We now develop each strand in the Deep Dive, beginning with the quantitative theory of Ramsey numbers, where the most dramatic recent progress has occurred.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The exponential breakthrough: Campos–Griffiths–Morris–Sahasrabudhe

For almost 90 years, the Erdős–Szekeres upper bound $R(k) \leq 4^k$ resisted every attempt at exponential improvement. Sub-exponential improvements (notably by Conlon, and by Sahasrabudhe's effective quasirandomness) shaved polynomial factors, but the base $4$ stood firm. In March 2023, Campos, Griffiths, Morris, and Sahasrabudhe proved the first *exponential* improvement [4]:

> **Theorem:** (CGMS, 2023 [4]) There exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $R(k) \leq (4-\varepsilon)^k$ for all sufficiently large $k$. The original proof achieves $R(k) \leq 3.993^k$.

The proof introduces a novel "book algorithm": given a red–blue coloring of $K_N$ with no monochromatic $K_k$, one builds a large red *book* (a set of pages sharing a common red spine) while maintaining a reservoir of vertices that is quasirandom with respect to the coloring. The key innovation is a refined notion of *book quasirandomness* that lets the algorithm either find a monochromatic clique or pass to a smaller instance with strictly better parameters, yielding an exponential gain. The argument was later streamlined by Gupta, Ndiaye, Norin, and Wei, who replaced the book algorithm with a simpler inductive statement and optimized the constant to $R(k) \leq 3.8^k$ [5]. A subsequent alternative proof by Balister, Bollobás, Campos, Griffiths, Hurley, Morris, Sahasrabudhe, and Tiba gave a more conceptual account, covered in Wigderson's Bourbaki exposition [4].

The quantitative state of the art (as surveyed in [6]) is summarized below:

| Parameter | Lower bound | Upper bound | Source |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|--------|
| $R(k)$ (diagonal) | $2^{k/2}\cdot \tilde{\Omega}(k)$ | $(4-\varepsilon)^k$, refined to $3.8^k$ | Erdős [6]; CGMS [4]; Gupta et al. [5] |
| $R(3,t)$ | $\tilde{\Omega}(t^2/\log t)$ | $(1+o(1))t^2/\log t$ | tight up to constants [6] |
| $R(s,t)$, $s$ fixed | $t^{(s+1)/2-o(1)}$ | $O_s(t^{s-1}/(\log t)^{s-2})$ | Ajtai–Komlós–Szemerédi; Shearer [6] |
| $R(3,3)$ | — | $=6$ (exact) | Greenwood–Gleason (1955) |
| $R(5,5)$ | $43$ | $48$ | current bounds [6] |

The off-diagonal frontier is equally dramatic: for fixed $s \geq 4$, Campos, Griffiths, Morris, and Sahasrabudhe's methods were adapted to give exponential improvements in the off-diagonal and multicolor settings as well [5][6].

A short computational illustration of the classical bounds is instructive:

```python
import math

def ramsey_bounds(k):
    """Classical Erdos-Szekeres upper bound vs Erdos probabilistic lower bound."""
    upper = math.comb(2*k - 2, k - 1)          # R(k) <= C(2k-2, k-1) <= 4^(k-1)
    lower = 2 ** (k / 2)                        # Erdos 1947 probabilistic lower bound
    return lower, upper

for k in (5, 10, 20):
    lo, hi = ramsey_bounds(k)
    print(f"k={k}:  2^(k/2) = {lo:>12.1f}   <=   R(k)   <=   C(2k-2,k-1) = {hi:>15.0f}")
# k=5:  lower ~5.7, upper 70      (truth: 43 <= R(5,5) <= 48)
# k=10: lower ~32,  upper 48620    (truth: 800 <= R(10,10) <= 17377 per modern bounds)
```

The enormous gap between the two columns is precisely what the CGMS breakthrough began to close from above.

### 4.2 Van der Waerden, Hales–Jewett, and the combinatorial line

The Hales–Jewett theorem is the statement that *combinatorial lines* are Ramsey. Fix a finite alphabet $A$ of size $t$. A *variable word* is a word over $A \cup \{x\}$ in which the variable $x$ occurs at least once; its *combinatorial line* is the set of words obtained by substituting each letter of $A$ for $x$. For example, with $A = \{1,2,3\}$, the variable word $w = 2x1x$ generates the line $\{2111, 2212, 2313\}$.

> **Theorem:** (Hales–Jewett, 1963 [3]) For every finite alphabet $A$ and every $r \geq 1$, there exists $N = HJ(|A|, r)$ such that for every $n \geq N$, every $r$-coloring of $A^n$ contains a monochromatic combinatorial line.

Van der Waerden's theorem is an immediate corollary: color $\mathbb{N}$ with $r$ colors, interpret words over $\{0,\dots,k-1\}$ as base-$k$ expansions via $f(w) = \sum w_i$, and a monochromatic combinatorial line becomes a monochromatic arithmetic progression of length $k$. Hales and Jewett originally proved the theorem as a tool for analyzing positional games [3].

The *Hales–Jewett numbers* $HJ(t,r)$ are enormous: Shelah showed they are primitive recursive (lying in Grzegorczyk class $\mathcal{E}^5$), but the known bounds involve iterated exponential towers, and only tiny values are known exactly — $HJ(2,r) = r$ by pigeonhole and $HJ(3,2) = 4$ (Hindman–Tressler). No other exact values are known [3].

### 4.3 Density Hales–Jewett and the polynomial method

Furstenberg and Katznelson proved the *density* Hales–Jewett theorem: every subset of $A^n$ of positive upper density contains a combinatorial line. This single theorem implies Szemerédi's theorem (via the base-$k$ encoding above) and is strictly stronger than the coloring version [3]. The *polynomial* Hales–Jewett theorem of Bergelson and Leibman extends the framework: for any finite set of integer polynomials vanishing at zero, every set of positive density in a suitable configuration space contains a "polynomial line" $\{x + P_1(y), \dots, x + P_m(y)\}$.

The polynomial method reached its most celebrated application in the work of Tao and Ziegler, who proved that the primes contain polynomial progressions $x + P_1(y), \dots, x + P_t(y)$ for arbitrary integer polynomials with $P_i(0) = 0$ — a polynomial Green–Tao theorem — using a relative PET induction scheme inside the transference framework [8].

### 4.4 The Green–Tao theorem and transference

> **Theorem:** (Green–Tao, 2008 [8]) The prime numbers contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.

The primes have density zero, so Szemerédi's theorem does not apply directly. Green and Tao's insight was the *transference principle*: construct a *pseudorandom majorant* $\nu$ (inspired by the Goldston–Yıldırım sieve) that dominates the (normalized) prime indicator and behaves like a constant function with respect to the Gowers uniformity norms; then transfer Szemerédi's theorem from the dense setting to the sparse setting by decomposing the prime indicator as $f = f_1 + f_2$ with $f_1$ structured and $f_2$ uniform. The structured component inherits long progressions from the dense theorem, while the uniform component contributes negligibly [8].

The Furstenberg–Katznelson correspondence underlies all of this: finite combinatorial statements about colorings correspond to recurrence statements in measure-preserving dynamical systems, so that ergodic methods (multiple recurrence, characteristic factors, nilsequences) can be brought to bear on arithmetic Ramsey problems.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 The canonical recurrence for Ramsey numbers

We sketch the classical upper-bound argument. Fix $s, t$ and let $n = R(s-1,t) + R(s,t-1)$, assuming the numbers exist inductively. In any red–blue coloring of $K_n$, pick a vertex $v$; let $R_v$ be its red neighbors and $B_v$ its blue neighbors. By pigeonhole, $|R_v| \geq R(s-1,t)$ or $|B_v| \geq R(s,t-1)$. In the first case, $R_v$ contains a red $K_{s-1}$ (which with $v$ forms a red $K_s$) or a blue $K_t$; the second case is symmetric. Hence $R(s,t) \leq R(s-1,t) + R(s,t-1)$, and unrolling the recurrence gives $R(s,t) \leq \binom{s+t-2}{s-1}$ [1][2].

### 5.2 Erdős's probabilistic lower bound

Color each edge of $K_n$ red or blue independently with probability $1/2$. For a fixed $k$-set $S$, the probability that all $\binom{k}{2}$ edges are monochromatic is $2^{1-\binom{k}{2}}$. By the union bound, the probability that *some* $k$-set is monochromatic is at most $\binom{n}{k} 2^{1-\binom{k}{2}}$. If this is $< 1$, a coloring with no monochromatic $K_k$ exists, so $R(k) > n$. Taking $n = \lfloor 2^{k/2} \rfloor$ gives $R(k) > 2^{k/2}$ for $k \geq 3$ [6]. The calculation can be verified directly:

```python
import math

def union_bound(k, n):
    """P(some monochromatic K_k) upper bound in random 2-coloring of K_n."""
    return math.comb(n, k) * 2 ** (1 - math.comb(k, 2))

for k in (5, 10, 50):
    n = 2 ** (k / 2)
    print(f"k={k}: n=floor(2^(k/2))={math.floor(n)}, union bound = {union_bound(k, math.floor(n)):.3e}")
# All values < 1, confirming R(k) > 2^(k/2).
```

### 5.3 From Hales–Jewett to van der Waerden (formal sketch)

Let $c: \mathbb{N} \to \{1,\dots,r\}$ be a finite coloring. Take $A = \{0,1,\dots,k-1\}$ and $n = HJ(k, r)$. Define $\tilde{c}: A^n \to \{1,\dots,r\}$ by $\tilde{c}(w) = c(\sum_i w_i \cdot k^i)$ (interpreting the word as a base-$k$ number). By Hales–Jewett [3], there is a monochromatic combinatorial line: a variable word $w$ with variable positions $I$ such that $\tilde{c}(w(a))$ is constant for $a \in A$. Writing $b = \sum_{i \notin I} w_i k^i$ and $d = \sum_{i \in I} k^i$, the numbers $b + a \cdot d$ for $a = 0,\dots,k-1$ form a monochromatic $k$-term arithmetic progression in $\mathbb{N}$. ∎

### 5.4 Graham–Rothschild and the parametric sets theorem

Graham and Rothschild (1971) generalized Ramsey's theorem to *$n$-parameter sets*: for any $r$-coloring of the $k$-dimensional subspaces (parameter sets) of a sufficiently large cube $[t]^N$, there is a monochromatic $m$-dimensional parametric subspace. This subsumes the Hales–Jewett theorem, the geometric Ramsey theorem of Graham–Leeb–Rothschild, and the vector Ramsey theorem in a single parametric framework, confirming the Hales–Jewett philosophy that one sufficiently abstract Ramsey theorem governs them all [3][7].

A compact Haskell model of a combinatorial line makes the abstraction concrete:

```haskell
-- Combinatorial lines over a finite alphabet: the Hales-Jewett object.
type Alphabet = [Char]
data VarWord = V [(Maybe Char)]        -- Nothing marks a variable position

instantiate :: VarWord -> Char -> String
instantiate (V cells) a = [maybe a id c | c <- cells]

-- The combinatorial line generated by a variable word over alphabet A.
combinatorialLine :: Alphabet -> VarWord -> [String]
combinatorialLine alphabet w = [instantiate w a | a <- alphabet]

-- Example: variable word 2x1x over {1,2,3} gives {2111, 2212, 2313},
-- which the base-3 encoding sends to a 3-term arithmetic progression.
example :: [String]
example = combinatorialLine "123" (V [Just '2', Nothing, Just '1', Nothing])
```

## 6 Limitations

1. **Quantitative weakness of Hales–Jewett numbers.** The best known upper bounds for $HJ(t,r)$ remain towers of exponentials; Shelah's primitive-recursive bound is a qualitative landmark, not a usable estimate [3]. Exact values are known only for $HJ(2,r)$ and $HJ(3,2)$.
2. **The diagonal Ramsey gap persists.** Despite the CGMS breakthrough, the gap between $2^{k/2}$ and $(4-\varepsilon)^k$ remains exponential; the true exponential rate of $R(k)$ is unknown, and the lower bound has been improved only by constant factors since Spencer [6].
3. **Szemerédi bounds are tower-type.** Gowers's quantitative proof of Szemerédi's theorem gives bounds of the form $N(k, \delta)$ that are towers of height polynomial in $1/\delta$; no reasonable explicit bound is known for progressions of length beyond 4, and Roth's $k=3$ case alone required decades of refinement.
4. **Transference requires pseudorandomness.** The Green–Tao theorem applies to the primes only because a suitable pseudorandom majorant exists; for sparser sets (e.g., values of $n^2+1$) no such majorant is known, and the corresponding arithmetic-regularity questions remain open [8].
5. **The Erdős–Szekeres conjecture is open.** Whether $ES(n) = 2^{n-2}+1$ exactly remains unresolved for $n \geq 7$; SAT-based approaches have verified $ES(6) = 17$ but the 33-point problem ($ES(7) = 33$?) is beyond current computational reach [2].
6. **Folkman numbers are astronomical.** While the Folkman–Rado–Sanders theorem is known to have primitive-recursive bounds (via the Hales–Jewett machinery), explicit Folkman numbers are known only in trivial cases.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Ramsey theory has grown from a lemma in formal logic [1] into one of the deepest organizing principles of mathematics. The graph-theoretic line — Ramsey numbers, the Erdős–Szekeres theorem, the probabilistic method, and the recent exponential breakthrough on diagonal Ramsey numbers [4][5][6] — demonstrates that even the oldest quantitative questions can yield to genuinely new ideas. The arithmetic line — van der Waerden, Schur [7], Folkman–Rado–Sanders, Szemerédi — shows that density, not just finiteness, forces structure. And the Hales–Jewett theorem [3] stands at the confluence: a single statement about words and variables that encodes arithmetic progressions, parametric cubes, and, through its density and polynomial forms, the arithmetic regularity of the primes themselves [8].

The field's open problems are among the most consequential in combinatorics: closing the exponential gap for $R(k)$, obtaining feasible bounds for Hales–Jewett and Folkman numbers, extending transference to sparser sets, and resolving the Erdős–Szekeres conjecture. Each would, in the tradition of the subject, likely require inventing a new method — and each new method has historically unlocked far more than its original target.
