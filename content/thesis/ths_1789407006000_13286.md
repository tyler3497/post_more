# Complexity and Stability in Ecosystems: May's Paradox, Random-Matrix Ecology, and the Diversity–Stability Debate

## Abstract

The coexistence of vast, species-rich ecosystems with mathematical proofs of their impossibility is theoretical ecology's deepest puzzle. This thesis reconstructs the diversity–stability debate from Elton's early intuition that complexity begets stability [11], through Gardner and Ashby's numerical discovery of a connectance threshold for stability [2], to May's 1972 random-matrix proof that complexity beyond a critical value makes persistence vanishingly improbable [1]. We develop the machinery — community matrices, Lotka–Volterra linearization, and the circular and elliptic laws of random matrix theory — that yields May's criterion, then show how Allesina and Tang refined it with interaction-type structure, proving predator–prey links stabilize while mutualistic and competitive links destabilize [3]. We separate stability from feasibility, introduce trophic coherence as a structural resolution of the paradox [5], and trace the empirical debate from McCann's weak-interaction synthesis [4] to Tilman's decade-long grassland experiments [10]. A from-scratch numerical study reproduces May's threshold and the Allesina–Tang asymmetry, demonstrating that it is structure, not randomness, that explains nature's persistence.

## 1 Introduction

Stand on a coral reef or walk a tropical rainforest and the overwhelming impression is one of exuberant, persistent complexity: thousands of species, woven into food webs of staggering intricacy, persisting for geological timescales. For most of the twentieth century, ecologists took this persistence as evidence for a comforting principle — that *complexity begets stability*. Elton argued that simpler communities were more vulnerable to invasion and collapse [11]; MacArthur contended that more alternative pathways for energy flow buffered a system against perturbation. Diversity was the guarantor of robustness.

In 1972, Robert May detonated this consensus with a one-page letter to *Nature* [1]. Drawing on the theory of large random matrices, he showed that a randomly assembled community becomes *less* likely to be stable as it grows larger, more connected, or more strongly interacting — and that beyond a sharp, calculable threshold, stability is essentially impossible. The result contradicted field experience so directly that it became known as **May's paradox**: if May's mathematics is right, why do complex ecosystems exist at all?

This thesis is a tour of the half-century research program that paradox ignited. Section 2 traces the intellectual background from Elton to Lotka–Volterra dynamics and the first computer experiments of Gardner and Ashby [2]. Section 3 builds the methodological core: community matrices and the random-matrix laws — the circular law and the elliptic law — that turn ecology into spectral geometry. Section 4 deepens the analysis across four fronts: May's criterion itself, the Allesina–Tang interaction-type refinement [3], the distinction between feasibility and stability with trophic coherence as resolution [5], and the empirical diversity–stability debate culminating in Tilman's field experiments [10]. Section 5 presents original numerical experiments reproducing both May's threshold and the interaction-type asymmetry. We conclude with limitations and open problems.

## 2 Background

### From natural history to dynamical systems

The diversity–stability debate predates its mathematics. Charles Elton's 1958 monograph on biological invasions argued that species-poor communities — island faunas, agricultural monocultures — were disproportionately vulnerable to invaders and outbreaks, implying that diversity confers resistance [11]. Robert MacArthur's 1955 analysis of food webs proposed a mechanism: systems with many alternative energy pathways could reroute flow around a disturbance, like a power grid with redundant lines. G. E. Hutchinson's "paradox of the plankton" posed the complementary puzzle of how so many competing species coexist on so few resources. The shared intuition was unmistakable: *more species, more links, more stability*.

### Lotka–Volterra dynamics and the community matrix

The natural mathematical language for these questions is the generalized Lotka–Volterra system:

```python
# Generalized Lotka-Volterra: dN_i/dt = N_i * (r_i + sum_j a_ij N_j)
```

where *N_i* is the density of species *i*, *r_i* its intrinsic growth rate, and *a_ij* the per-capita effect of species *j* on species *i*. Suppose the system possesses a **feasible equilibrium** *N\** with all densities strictly positive. Linearizing around *N\** yields the *community matrix* **M**, whose entries are *M_ij = N\*_i a_ij* for *i ≠ j* and *M_ii = N\*_i a_ii* (self-regulation, typically negative). The equilibrium is *locally asymptotically stable* if and only if every eigenvalue of **M** has negative real part — a classical result of Lyapunov's indirect method. The entire stability question thus collapses to spectral geometry: *where in the complex plane do the eigenvalues of a large ecological matrix lie?*

### Gardner and Ashby: the numerical prophecy

Before May's analysis, Gardner and Ashby performed the first systematic computer experiments on this question [2]. They generated large random linear dynamical systems and measured the probability of stability as a function of *connectance* — the fraction of possible interactions actually present. Their result was striking and almost digital in character: for systems of ten or more components, there exists a critical connectance below which systems are almost certainly stable and above which they are almost certainly unstable, with a precipitous transition between regimes. They had discovered, numerically, the phase transition that May would shortly derive analytically — and their work established the template of the *random community* as ecology's null model.

## 3 Methodology

### The random community matrix ensemble

May's methodological innovation was to treat the community matrix as a draw from a random ensemble [1]. Write **M = −dI + A**, where *d > 0* encodes uniform self-regulation (intraspecific competition) on the diagonal, and the off-diagonal entries of **A** are independent random variables: zero with probability *1 − C* (connectance *C*), and otherwise drawn from a distribution with mean zero and variance *σ²*. The triple *(S, C, σ)* — species richness, connectance, interaction strength — defines May's notion of *complexity*, conventionally summarized by the single parameter *κ = σ√(SC)*.

### The circular law

For such matrices, the eigenvalues of **A** obey Girko's *circular law*: as *S* grows large, the eigenvalues spread approximately uniformly over a disk in the complex plane centered at the origin with radius *σ√(SC)*. Shifting by *−dI* moves this disk leftward; stability requires the entire disk to lie in the left half-plane. The derivation leans on Wigner's semicircle law for symmetric matrices and the Hermitization technique for the asymmetric case, but the ecological content is beautifully simple: *the spectral radius of the interaction matrix grows like the square root of complexity, and self-regulation must outrun it*.

### The elliptic law and correlated pairs

Real ecological interactions come in correlated pairs: if species *j* eats species *i*, then *M_ij* and *M_ji* have opposite signs. Sommers and collaborators showed that introducing a correlation *τ = E(A_ij A_ji) / σ²* between reciprocal entries deforms the eigenvalue disk into an *ellipse* — the *elliptic law* — with real semi-axis *σ√(SC)(1 + τ)* and imaginary semi-axis *σ√(SC)(1 − τ)* [9]. Negative *τ* (predator–prey pairs) flattens the ellipse horizontally, pulling the rightmost eigenvalues leftward; positive *τ* stretches it rightward. This single geometric fact is the engine of the Allesina–Tang refinement.

### Numerical protocol

Throughout this thesis, "stability" means local asymptotic stability of a feasible equilibrium: the maximum real part of the spectrum, *λ_max = max Re λ(M)*, must be negative. Our numerical experiments generate ensembles of community matrices, compute spectra directly, and estimate the probability of stability across parameter sweeps — the same methodology as Gardner and Ashby [2], now with fifty years of additional theory to interpret the results.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 May's criterion and the circular law

From the circular law, the rightmost eigenvalue of **A** sits at approximately *σ√(SC)*. Stability of **M = −dI + A** therefore demands:

> **Theorem: May's stability criterion.** A large random community matrix with *S* species, connectance *C*, interaction-strength variance *σ²*, and self-regulation *d* is stable with probability approaching one if *σ√(SC) < d*, and unstable with probability approaching one if *σ√(SC) > d* [1].

The criterion is a statement about *complexity*: define complexity as *κ = σ√(SC)*. Then stability requires *κ < d*. Every route to greater complexity — more species, denser webs, stronger interactions — pushes the system toward the cliff. The transition sharpens as *S* grows, reproducing Gardner and Ashby's on–off numerical behavior [2] as an analytic fact.

*Figure 1 — May's complexity–stability boundary: the eigenvalue disk of radius σ√(SC) against the stability line at −d (ths_1789407006000_13286-0.webp).*

The paradox is now crisp. Tropical forests and coral reefs sit far beyond any plausible estimate of the critical complexity, yet they persist. May's theorem is not wrong — it is *conditional*: it describes random communities. Nature, the subsequent literature argues, is anything but random.

### 4.2 Allesina and Tang: interaction-type structure

May assumed off-diagonal pairs were independent. But ecological interactions have *sign structure*: predator–prey pairs are *(+, −)*, mutualistic pairs *(+, +)*, competitive pairs *(−, −)*. Allesina and Tang exploited the elliptic law to extend May's result to each case [3]. The correlation *τ* between reciprocal entries is negative for predator–prey pairs and positive for mutualistic and competitive pairs, and the stability criterion becomes *σ√(SC)(1 + τ) < d*:

| Interaction type | Sign pattern | Pair correlation *τ* | Stability criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Random (May) | independent | *τ = 0* | *σ√(SC) < d* |
| Predator–prey | *(+, −)* | *τ < 0* | *σ√(SC)(1 + τ) < d* — easier to satisfy |
| Mutualistic | *(+, +)* | *τ > 0* | *σ√(SC)(1 + τ) < d* — harder to satisfy |
| Competitive | *(−, −)* | *τ > 0* | *σ√(SC)(1 + τ) < d* — harder to satisfy |
| Mixed (Tang et al.) | mixture | *τ* between minus one and one | *σ√(SC)(1 + τ) < d* |

> **Theorem: Allesina–Tang interaction-type criterion.** For community matrices whose interaction pairs share correlation *τ*, stability holds when *σ√(SC)(1 + τ) < d*. Predator–prey interactions (*τ < 0*) are stabilizing and admit arbitrarily large, complex stable networks provided pairs are tightly coupled; mutualistic and competitive interactions (*τ > 0*) are destabilizing [3].

*Figure 2 — The four interaction types and their eigenvalue ellipses in the complex plane (ths_1789407006000_13286-1.webp).*

Two of Allesina and Tang's corollaries are deliciously counterintuitive. First, imposing *realistic food-web structure* on a predator–prey network can *decrease* its probability of stability, because structure dilutes the tight pair coupling that the criterion rewards. Second, a preponderance of *weak* interactions — long celebrated as nature's stabilizer — can likewise reduce stability in their framework, since weak links loosen pair correlations. Their criteria apply to any system of differential equations, making them among the most portable results in theoretical ecology [9].

### 4.3 Feasibility versus stability, and trophic coherence

A subtle point deserves emphasis: May's criterion governs *stability*, the return to equilibrium after small perturbations. It says nothing about *feasibility* — whether a positive equilibrium exists at all. A random community may fail either test, and the two failures have different remedies. Recent work shows that when interaction strength or diversity is pushed past May's threshold, the system does not simply explode: species go extinct until the survivors form a smaller, self-organized community whose statistics match a purely random — but stable — ecosystem [6]. Instability resolves itself through *reorganization*, not apocalypse.

The deepest structural resolution of the paradox comes from *trophic coherence*. Johnson and colleagues observed that real food webs are not random graphs: species fall into trophic levels, and feeding links run overwhelmingly between adjacent levels. They quantified this with an incoherence parameter *q*, the standard deviation of trophic distances across links [5]:

> **Theorem: Trophic coherence and stability.** Trophic coherence predicts linear stability better than size or complexity; a maximally coherent network with constant interaction strengths is always linearly stable; and in coherent network models, stability can *increase* with size and complexity [5].

*Figure 3 — Trophic coherence: a neatly layered food web versus an incoherent tangled one (ths_1789407006000_13286-2.webp).*

Complementary structural features matter too. Empirical community matrices — parameterized from real food webs with biomass-weighted interaction strengths — show *no* complexity–stability relationship once their non-random properties are accounted for: pyramidal interaction-strength structure, trophic interaction topology, predator–prey pair correlations, and leptokurtic (weak-dominated) strength distributions each contribute stabilizing structure that random matrices lack [8]. Modularity and nestedness play analogous roles in mutualistic networks, where nestedness itself can be destabilizing even as it characterizes real pollination webs [3].

### 4.4 The diversity–stability debate: Elton to Tilman

Parallel to the mathematical program ran an empirical one. McCann's landmark 2000 review reframed the debate around *interaction pattern* rather than richness per se [4]: weak trophic interactions dampen consumer–resource oscillations, so the many-weak-few-strong pattern observed in nature is itself a stabilizing feature. The theoretical prediction and the field pattern finally pointed the same way.

The experimental capstone came from Tilman's Cedar Creek grassland experiments. Over a decade of manipulated plant diversity, Tilman, Reich, and Knops showed that ecosystem productivity and its temporal stability *increase* with species richness [10] — the insurance and portfolio effects: asynchronous species responses average out fluctuations, just as diversified portfolios dampen financial volatility. Crucially, this resolves the paradox by distinguishing *levels* of stability: diversity destabilizes individual populations (smaller, more variable abundances) while stabilizing aggregate ecosystem function. The two claims never actually contradicted each other.

*Figure 4 — The diversity–stability debate from Elton to Tilman, with the portfolio-effect curve (ths_1789407006000_13286-3.webp).*

| Era | Claim | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1950s–60s | Diversity stabilizes | Elton's invasion biology [11]; MacArthur's pathway redundancy |
| 1970–72 | Complexity destabilizes | Gardner–Ashby simulations [2]; May's theorem [1] |
| 2000s | Pattern, not richness, matters | McCann's weak-interaction synthesis [4] |
| 2006–present | Diversity stabilizes *function* | Tilman's grassland experiments [10]; portfolio effects |
| 2010s–now | Structure resolves the paradox | Trophic coherence [5]; empirical matrix structure [8]; nonlinear models [7] |

A final twist: the paradox may partly be an artifact of *linearization*. Fully nonlinear models with saturating functional responses show diversity increasing stability outright, because the mathematical singularity at May's tipping point — where linear densities diverge — is removed by saturation [7]. May's paradox, on this view, is a property of the linear approximation, not of ecosystems.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now verify the core claims numerically from scratch. The following Python experiment (i) sweeps May's complexity parameter *κ = σ√(SC)* across the critical value *d = 1* and (ii) compares random, predator–prey-like (*τ = −0.9*), and mutualistic-like (*τ = +0.9*) ensembles at fixed complexity:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

def random_community(S, C, sigma, d, tau=None):
    """Community matrix M = -dI + A with optional pair correlation tau."""
    A = np.zeros((S, S))
    mask = rng.random((S, S)) < C
    if tau is None:
        A[mask] = rng.normal(0, sigma, mask.sum())
    else:
        X = rng.normal(0, sigma, (S, S))
        Y = rng.normal(0, sigma, (S, S))
        V = tau * X + np.sqrt(1 - tau**2) * Y
        A = np.where(mask, (X + V) / np.sqrt(2), 0.0)
    np.fill_diagonal(A, 0.0)
    return -d * np.eye(S) + A

def frac_stable(S, C, sigma, d, tau=None, nrep=300):
    ok = 0
    for _ in range(nrep):
        M = random_community(S, C, sigma, d, tau)
        if np.linalg.eigvals(M).real.max() < 0:
            ok += 1
    return ok / nrep

# (i) May sweep: d = 1, sigma = 0.25, 300 replicates each
for S, C in [(20, 0.2), (40, 0.2), (80, 0.2), (40, 0.4), (80, 0.4)]:
    kappa = 0.25 * np.sqrt(S * C)
    print(f"S={S:3d} C={C:.1f} kappa={kappa:.2f} stable={frac_stable(S, C, 0.25, 1.0):.3f}")

# (ii) Interaction-type asymmetry: S=60, C=0.25, sigma=0.2, d=1
for name, tau in [("random", 0.0), ("predator-prey", -0.9), ("mutualistic", 0.9)]:
    print(f"{name:13s} tau={tau:+.1f} stable={frac_stable(60, 0.25, 0.2, 1.0, tau):.3f}")
```

The measured stability fractions (300 replicates per cell) are:

| Ensemble | *S* | *C* | *σ* | *κ = σ√(SC)* | *τ* | Fraction stable |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| May sweep | 20 | 0.2 | 0.25 | 0.50 | 0.0 | **1.000** |
| May sweep | 40 | 0.2 | 0.25 | 0.71 | 0.0 | **1.000** |
| May sweep | 80 | 0.2 | 0.25 | 1.00 | 0.0 | **0.770** |
| May sweep | 40 | 0.4 | 0.25 | 1.00 | 0.0 | **0.790** |
| May sweep | 80 | 0.4 | 0.25 | 1.41 | 0.0 | **0.000** |
| Random | 60 | 0.25 | 0.20 | 0.77 | 0.0 | **0.997** |
| Predator–prey | 60 | 0.25 | 0.20 | 0.77 | −0.9 | **1.000** |
| Mutualistic | 60 | 0.25 | 0.20 | 0.77 | +0.9 | **0.557** |

Two conclusions follow. First, May's threshold is quantitatively real: stability is certain below *κ = d*, begins to crack exactly at *κ = d*, and vanishes by *κ = 1.41d* — a transition sharp enough to deserve the name *phase transition*. Second, the Allesina–Tang asymmetry is stark: at identical complexity (*κ = 0.77 < d*), predator–prey correlation preserves perfect stability while mutualistic correlation collapses it to little better than a coin flip.

The spectral geometry behind these numbers is visible directly:

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

S, C, sigma, d = 120, 0.15, 0.3, 1.0
M = random_community(S, C, sigma, d)
eig = np.linalg.eigvals(M)
radius = sigma * np.sqrt(S * C)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 6))
ax.scatter(eig.real, eig.imag, s=6, alpha=0.5)
circle = plt.Circle((-d, 0), radius, fill=False, linestyle="--")
ax.add_patch(circle)
ax.axvline(0, color="red", linestyle="--", label="stability boundary")
ax.set_aspect("equal"); ax.set_xlabel("Re lambda"); ax.set_ylabel("Im lambda")
ax.set_title(f"May disk: radius sigma*sqrt(SC) = {radius:.2f}, d = {d}")
plt.savefig("may_spectrum.png", dpi=150)
```

The eigenvalues fill the predicted disk; as *κ* grows past *d*, the disk's right edge crosses the imaginary axis and the equilibrium loses stability. *This is May's paradox rendered as a picture* — and the elliptic-law refinement merely squashes or stretches that disk along the real axis according to interaction type.

## 6 Limitations

This thesis's arguments carry the standard caveats of the random-matrix program, and they are substantial. First, everything here concerns *local* asymptotic stability of a *feasible* equilibrium; it says nothing about global stability, large perturbations, or the existence of the equilibrium itself — and feasibility is often the binding constraint in real communities. Second, eigenvalue analysis of non-normal matrices misses *transient* dynamics: a stable matrix can amplify perturbations enormously before they decay (reactivity), and ecological relevance lives in those transients. Third, the uniform self-regulation *d* is a fiction; real diagonal entries vary with biomass and trophic level, and that row structure alone shifts stability [8]. Fourth, finite-size corrections matter for the modest species counts of real webs. Finally, the entire framework is linearization-bound: as nonlinear analyses show, saturation can dissolve the paradox that linear theory creates [7]. May's criterion is best read not as a law of nature but as a null model — a precisely wrong baseline against which the stabilizing power of real structure can be measured.

## 7 Conclusion

May's paradox has survived fifty years not because it is true of nature, but because it is *productively* false: a razor-sharp null hypothesis that forced ecology to discover what actually stabilizes the living world. The answer, assembled across five decades, is structure at every scale — the sign pattern of interaction pairs [3], the trophic coherence of feeding hierarchies [5], the pyramidal and leptokurtic architecture of real interaction strengths [8], the damping hand of weak links [4], and the statistical averaging of diverse portfolios [10]. Random-matrix ecology began by proving that random ecosystems cannot persist; it matured by showing exactly which departures from randomness let them persist. The open frontiers are feasibility — which equilibria can even exist — and the non-normal, transient, nonlinear dynamics through which real perturbations play out. If there is a single lesson, it is this: *complexity does not stabilize ecosystems; organized complexity does.*
