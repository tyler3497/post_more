# Threshold Cryptography at Internet Scale: Pedersen-Feldman DKG, FROST Threshold Schnorr Signatures, and DKLS Threshold ECDSA for Wallet Infrastructure

## Abstract

Threshold cryptography distributes trust across *n* parties such that any *t* of them can jointly execute a cryptographic operation while no coalition smaller than *t* learns anything about the underlying secret. This thesis examines the three protocol families that underpin modern threshold wallets and digital-asset custody infrastructure. We begin with Pedersen's distributed key generation (DKG) protocol, its Feldman verifiable-secret-sharing substrate, and the Gennaro–Jarecki–Krawczyk–Rabin refinement that closes its key-biasability gap. We then analyze **FROST**, the flexible round-optimized Schnorr threshold signature scheme of Komlo and Goldberg, which achieves a two-round signing phase with a single-round preprocessing optimization, and its adoption into the CFRG standardization track. Finally, we contrast Schnorr threshold signatures with the substantially harder threshold **ECDSA** problem, surveying the Gennaro–Goldfeder (GG18/GG20) and Doerner–Kondi–Lee–Shelat (DKLS) protocol lineages, the identifiable-abort and UC-security refinements of Gennaro, Goldfeder, Makriyannis and Peled, and the NIST Multi-Party Threshold Cryptography project's evaluation roadmap. We evaluate robustness versus liveness trade-offs, dealer-less key generation, proactive secret-share refresh, and deployment evidence from MPC-based custodians, and we identify open problems in adaptive security and post-quantum migration.

## 1. Introduction

The catastrophic failure mode of conventional public-key infrastructure is the **single point of compromise**: one secret key, held by one principal, on one machine. In digital-asset custody, where a leaked key is an irrevocable bearer instrument, this failure mode is not a theoretical concern but the industry's dominant attack surface. Multisignature schemes address the problem by splitting authorization across several distinct keys, but they incur on-chain costs proportional to the number of signers, leak the policy to observers, and tie key management to specific blockchain scripts.

*Threshold cryptography* offers a strictly stronger primitive. A $(t, n)$-threshold signature scheme keeps a single public key and a single on-chain footprint, while the corresponding secret is secret-shared across $n$ parties such that any quorum of $t$ parties can produce a valid signature, and no $t-1$ parties can forge one or even learn the key. The verification key is indistinguishable from that of a conventional signer: on-chain, a threshold wallet looks exactly like a single-signer wallet. The price of this elegance is *interactive protocol design*. Signatures that are trivially computed by one party become distributed protocols with multiple communication rounds, and every step must remain secure against a malicious adversary controlling up to $t-1$ (and, in the dishonest-majority setting, up to $n-1$) participants.

Three historical strands converge in the modern threshold-wallet stack. **First**, the distributed key generation (DKG) problem: how do $n$ mutually distrusting parties bootstrap secret shares of a key *that nobody ever knows in full*? Pedersen's 1991 construction — building on Feldman's 1987 verifiable secret sharing — gave the first practical answer, later hardened by Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk and Rabin against adaptive key-bias attacks [4][7]. **Second**, threshold *Schnorr* signatures: the linearity of the Schnorr $s$-computation makes Schnorr the natural threshold signature, culminating in FROST, a two-round protocol with an optimized single-round preprocessing variant, secure against concurrent forgery attacks that defeated its predecessors [1][2]. **Third**, threshold *ECDSA*: because ECDSA's signing equation involves the modular inverse of the ephemeral nonce, thresholdizing ECDSA demands heavier machinery — multiplicative-to-additive share conversion, Paillier encryption, range proofs, and multi-round zero-knowledge proofs — developed across the Lindell, Gennaro–Goldfeder, and Doerner lineages [8][10].

This thesis presents these strands as a unified engineering discipline, examines their formal security models, and evaluates how they are deployed in production MPC custody systems at internet scale.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Shamir Secret Sharing and Feldman's Verifiability

Let $\mathbb{G}$ be a cyclic group of prime order $q$ with generator $g$. In Shamir's $(t, n)$ secret sharing, a dealer holding secret $s \in \mathbb{Z}_q$ samples a random degree-$(t-1)$ polynomial $f(x) = s + a_1 x + \cdots + a_{t-1} x^{t-1}$ and gives party $i$ the share $s_i = f(i)$. Any $t$ shares recover $s$ via Lagrange interpolation; any $t-1$ reveal nothing. The fatal weakness is the *trusted dealer*: a malicious dealer can distribute inconsistent shares, breaking liveness silently and biasing the secret.

Feldman's 1987 verifiable secret sharing (VSS) resolves this by requiring the dealer to broadcast commitments $A_j = a_j \cdot G$ to each polynomial coefficient [9]. Any party can verify a received share $s_i$ against these commitments through the homomorphic identity:

> **Theorem:** Given Feldman commitments $\{A_0, \ldots, A_{t-1}\}$ to a dealer's polynomial, a share $s_i$ is valid if and only if $s_i \cdot G = \sum_{j=0}^{t-1} i^j \cdot A_j$.

This single property — that *relationships between secrets are publicly checkable through relationships between their commitments* — is the load-bearing wall of everything that follows [9].

### 2.2 From VSS to Dealer-less Key Generation

Verifiable secret sharing eliminates a cheating dealer, but the dealer still *knows the secret* — a single point of failure. Pedersen's DKG removes the dealer entirely: every party $P_i$ simultaneously acts as a Feldman-VSS dealer for its own random secret $z_i$, publishing commitments and distributing shares; complaints disqualify inconsistent dealers; and the joint secret is the sum $s = \sum_i z_i$ over the qualified set, with each party's final share $s_j = \sum_i f_i(j)$ and the group public key $Y = \sum_i A_{i,0}$ [4][7]. Nobody ever holds $s$. As the Cloudflare drand deployment notes, Pedersen's DKG runs $n$ instances of Feldman's VSS in parallel, layered with additional verification, and is the canonical way to bootstrap a collectively shared secret for threshold signing [5].

However, naive Pedersen DKG is *biasable*: a rushing adversary controlling $k$ dealers observes every honest contribution before deciding which of its own dealers to sacrifice to the complaint round, steering the "random" key among $2^k$ values. The GJKR fix commits every contribution behind *hiding* Pedersen commitments $C_j = a_j \cdot G + b_j \cdot H$ first, fixing the qualified set while all contributions remain information-theoretically hidden [4]. For threshold *signing* purposes the biased-key caveat is benign — Gennaro et al. show signing remains secure even with a biased key [7] — but unbiased DKG matters for randomness beacons and other applications.

### 2.3 The Schnorr Advantage and the ECDSA Difficulty

A Schnorr signature on message $m$ with key $x$ and nonce $k$ is $(R, s)$ where $R = k \cdot G$ and $s = k + c x$ for challenge $c = H(X, R, m)$ [3]. The signing equation is *linear* in both $k$ and $x$: if $k = \sum_i k_i$ and $x = \sum_i x_i$ (or a Lagrange-weighted sum), then each party can compute $s_i = k_i + c \cdot \lambda_i \cdot x_i$ and the aggregator simply sums. Threshold Schnorr is therefore conceptually a bookkeeping exercise — the subtlety lies entirely in the *nonce*, where the infamous Drijvers et al. concurrent-forgery attack showed that naive one-round threshold Schnorr constructions admit devastating parallel-session attacks [6].

ECDSA, by contrast, computes $s = k^{-1}(H(m) + r x) \bmod q$, where the nonce inverse is nonlinear. Thresholdizing ECDSA requires converting *multiplicative* shares into *additive* shares of products like $k \cdot \gamma$ and $k \cdot x$ via MtA (multiplicative-to-additive) protocols built on Paillier encryption, followed by zero-knowledge range proofs — intrinsically heavier and intrinsically more round-intensive [8][10].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis proceeds by protocol-level analysis rather than by implementation benchmarking alone. Our method has four components:

1. **Protocol specification.** We reconstruct each protocol's round structure, per-round message complexity, and cryptographic assumptions from the primary literature — Pedersen's DKG and Feldman's VSS, the FROST CFRG draft [2], the GG18/GG20 and DKLS threshold-ECDSA lineages [8][10], and the identifiable-abort/UC refinement of Gennaro, Goldfeder, Makriyannis and Peled [8].
2. **Adversarial modeling.** We distinguish the *honest-majority* setting ($t > n/2$, where robustness — guaranteed output delivery — is achievable) from the *dishonest-majority* setting ($t \le n/2$ or $t = n-1$ corrupted, where robustness is provably impossible and the best attainable property is identifiable abort). We evaluate static versus adaptive corruptions, concurrent-session security, and key-biasability of DKG.
3. **Round and assumption accounting.** We compare protocols on *online* rounds (message-dependent), preprocessing (message-independent), computational assumptions (DL, Strong RSA, DDH, Paillier semantic security), and random-oracle usage, following the NIST Multi-Party Threshold Cryptography project's evaluation criteria [11].
4. **Deployment validation.** We check protocol claims against real deployments: drand's randomness beacon (Pedersen DKG + threshold BLS) [5], Fireblocks' MPC custody infrastructure built on the GG lineage [8], and the NISTIR 8214 roadmap and First Call for Multi-Party Threshold Schemes [11].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Pedersen–Feldman Distributed Key Generation and the GJKR Refinement

The Pedersen DKG protocol executes in three logical phases [4][5][7]:

**Share distribution.** Each party $P_i$ samples $z_i \in \mathbb{Z}_q$ uniformly, constructs $f_i(x)$ of degree $t-1$ with $f_i(0) = z_i$, sends share $s_{i,j} = f_i(j)$ to party $P_j$ over a private authenticated channel, and broadcasts Feldman commitments $A_{i,0}, \ldots, A_{i,t-1}$.

**Share verification.** Each $P_j$ checks $s_{i,j} \cdot G = \sum_{k=0}^{t-1} j^k \cdot A_{i,k}$ for every $i$. On failure, $P_j$ broadcasts a *complaint* against $P_i$; the accused must reveal the correct share $s_{i,j}$, which all parties then verify. Parties with unresolvable complaints are *disqualified* from the qualified set $\mathsf{QUAL}$.

**Share finalization.** The joint secret is $s = \sum_{i \in \mathsf{QUAL}} z_i$; each party's long-term share is $s_j = \sum_{i \in \mathsf{QUAL}} s_{i,j}$; the group public key is $Y = \sum_{i \in \mathsf{QUAL}} A_{i,0}$ [5].

The security intuition is that as long as one honest party's contribution $z_i$ remains secret and unbiasable, the joint key is uniformly random. Two refinements matter for deployment. First, the GJKR commit-then-reveal upgrade closes key biasability: contributions are first broadcast as hiding commitments, so a rushing adversary cannot steer the key distribution [4]. Second, *proactive secret sharing* periodically refreshes shares via a zero-sum re-randomization (each party runs DKG of a sharing of $0$ and adds it to its share), so that shares stolen in different epochs cannot be combined — the basis of proactive security in Gennaro et al.'s UC threshold ECDSA [8].

```python
# Feldman share verification: the workhorse of every threshold wallet
def verify_share(share: int, index: int, commitments: list, G) -> bool:
    """Check s_i * G == sum(i^k * A_k); Feldman VSS [Feldman'87]."""
    lhs = share * G
    rhs = commitments[0]
    power = 1
    for k in range(1, len(commitments)):
        power = (power * index)  # i^k computed in Z_q
        rhs = rhs + power * commitments[k]
    return lhs == rhs  # anyone can check; nobody learns the secret
```

### 4.2 FROST: Two-Round Threshold Schnorr Signatures

FROST (Flexible Round-Optimized Schnorr Threshold signatures), by Chelsea Komlo and Ian Goldberg (SAC 2020; CFRG draft [1][2]), is the current state of the art for threshold Schnorr. Its design directly answers the Drijvers et al. concurrent-forgery attack: each signer uses *two* nonces $(d_i, e_i)$ per signing session, publishing commitments $(D_i, E_i) = (d_i \cdot G, e_i \cdot G)$. A binding factor $\rho_i = H_1(i, m, L)$, where $L$ is the full list of participant commitments, ties every nonce to the *specific session*, so the adversary cannot mix partial signatures across concurrent sessions — the exact operation that broke earlier schemes [2][6].

**Signing (two rounds).** In round 1, each participant broadcasts nonce commitments $(D_i, E_i)$. In round 2, each participant $i$ in the signing set $S$ computes the group commitment $R = \sum_{j \in S} (D_j + \rho_j E_j)$, the challenge $c = H_2(X, R, m)$, and its response share $z_i = d_i + e_i \rho_i + \lambda_i c x_i$, where $\lambda_i$ is the Lagrange coefficient for $S$. The signature aggregator verifies each $z_i$ via $z_i \cdot G \stackrel{?}{=} D_i + \rho_i E_i + \lambda_i c \cdot \mathsf{vk}_i$ and outputs $z = \sum_i z_i$; the signature $(R, z)$ verifies exactly as a standard Schnorr signature under $X$ [1].

**Preprocessing optimization.** Because round 1 is message-independent, signers can pre-generate and pre-share nonce-commitment pairs in batches. The signing operation then collapses to a *single round*: participants broadcast response shares and the aggregator combines. This is FROST's "flexible round-optimized" namesake — and it matches the architecture of wallet backends, where a coordinator can amortize the preprocessing round across many future transactions [1][2].

Crucially, FROST trades *robustness for efficiency*: a misbehaving participant causes the session to abort, but its share fails the per-signer verification equation, so the culprit is *identified and excluded from future operations* — a reasonable model for production deployments where the signer set is a known set of servers rather than an open peer-to-peer network [1]. FROST is specified for both secp256k1 and the Ristretto/Ed25519 groups in the CFRG draft [2], and its security is proven under the discrete-logarithm assumption in the random oracle model [6].

```haskell
-- FROST response share, per signer i in signing set S (schematic)
frostShare :: Scalar -> Scalar -> Scalar -> Scalar -> Scalar -> Scalar -> Scalar
frostShare d_i e_i rho_i lambda_i c x_i =
  d_i + e_i * rho_i + lambda_i * c * x_i
-- verifier check:  z_i * G == D_i + rho_i * E_i + lambda_i * c * VK_i
```

### 4.3 Threshold ECDSA: The GG18/GG20 and DKLS Lineages

Threshold ECDSA is the dominant real-world requirement — Bitcoin and Ethereum historically support only ECDSA — and the hardest protocol problem in this thesis. The signing equation $s = k^{-1}(H(m) + r x)$ couples the nonce inverse with both the message and the key, and the per-message nonce *must never repeat*.

The modern era begins with two concurrent 2018 breakthroughs: **Lindell** ("Fast Secure Two-Party ECDSA Signing", CCS 2017) and **Gennaro–Goldfeder** ("Fast Multiparty Threshold ECDSA with Fast Trustless Setup", CCS 2018), later streamlined into **GG20** ("One Round Threshold ECDSA with Identifiable Abort") [8][10]. The core mechanism is *multiplicative-to-additive (MtA) share conversion*: parties hold shares $a_i, b_i$ of values whose product must be computed; using Paillier homomorphic encryption and zero-knowledge proofs of correct encryption and range, they convert to additive shares of the product *without revealing the factors*. Key generation uses Pedersen-style DKG adapted to the ECDSA setting with Paillier key pairs per party; signing converts $k_i \cdot \gamma_j$ and $k_i \cdot x_j$ into additive shares, assembles additive shares of $k \cdot \gamma$, inverts locally to obtain shares of $k^{-1}$, and finally computes signature shares $s_i$ that sum to a valid ECDSA signature [8][10].

**DKLS** — Doerner, Kondi, Lee and Shelat ("Secure Two-party Threshold ECDSA from ECDSA Assumptions", S&P 2018, with the n-party DKLS23 follow-up) — takes a different route, designing threshold ECDSA *from ECDSA assumptions alone* (no Strong-RSA or Paillier assumption), at the cost of more rounds and a multiplication-to-addition via oblivious transfer or class-group machinery [10]. The practical landscape is therefore a genuine trade-off surface:

| Protocol | Assumption base | Online rounds | Keygen | Corruption model | Notable property |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GG18 | Strong RSA + DDH + Paillier | ~7–8 | DKG | Dishonest majority, static | Trustless setup |
| GG20 | Strong RSA + DDH + Paillier | **1** (after preprocessing) | DKG | Dishonest majority, static | Non-interactive online phase |
| DKLS | ECDSA assumptions only | ~4–6 | DKG | Dishonest majority | Minimal assumption base |
| GGMP21 (UC) | Strong RSA + DDH + Paillier | 1 online | DKG | **Adaptive**, proactive | UC security + identifiable abort [8] |
| FROST | Discrete log + ROM | 2 (1 w/ preprocessing) | DKG or dealer | Dishonest majority, static | Session-bound nonces defeat concurrent forgery [1][2] |

The GGMP21 refinement of Gennaro, Goldfeder, Makriyannis and Peled — developed within **Fireblocks'** production MPC infrastructure — deserves special attention: it achieves *universally composable* security, *identifiable abort* (the protocol pinpoints the corrupted signatory on failure, closing the "silent saboteur" vulnerability of earlier protocols), *adaptive* corruption tolerance, and *proactive* security via periodic share refresh [8]. This is the configuration actually guarding billions of dollars in digital assets.

### 4.4 Robustness, Liveness, and the Dishonest-Majority Model

A foundational impossibility governs this design space: **robustness is unachievable under dishonest majority**. When $t-1 \ge n/2$ parties may be corrupt, no protocol can guarantee output delivery, because corrupt parties can always withhold their messages. The meaningful security target is therefore *identifiable abort*: if the protocol fails, the identity of the deviating party is exposed [8][10]. This is precisely the model FROST and GGMP21 adopt, and it aligns with the deployment reality of wallet infrastructure, where the $n$ parties are a fixed, accountable set of servers — a crashed or malicious share-server is replaced by operational procedure, not by protocol magic.

Proactive security extends this: Gennaro et al.'s refresh protocol lets the $n$ parties re-randomize their shares without changing the public key, bounding the adversary to corrupting $t-1$ parties *within a single epoch* rather than cumulatively over time [8]. Combined with threshold DKG, this yields a custody system in which no single machine, at any single time, holds anything sufficient to sign.

### 4.5 Production Wallet Architecture at Internet Scale

A production threshold wallet separates concerns into four tiers:

1. **Client tier** (mobile/extension): holds one share on-device, or nothing at all, authenticating to the custody service.
2. **MPC signer tier**: $n$ share-servers, each in a distinct security domain (different cloud regions, different HSM vendors), jointly executing FROST or GG-series signing.
3. **Coordinator**: routes messages, performs share verification (Feldman equations, MtA ZK proofs), enforces identifiable abort, and never learns any share.
4. **Policy engine**: quorum rules, velocity limits, and allow-lists that decide *which* messages may be signed — the threshold protocol guarantees *how* a signature is produced, but the policy engine decides *whether* one should be.

Fireblocks' MPC custody platform, whose researchers co-authored the GGMP21 UC protocol, exemplifies this architecture at scale, and the NIST Multi-Party Threshold Cryptography project is developing evaluation criteria and reference material precisely so such deployments can be standardized and interoperated [8][11]. The economic argument is decisive: a threshold wallet's on-chain footprint is identical to a single-key wallet — constant-size signatures, no script overhead, no policy leakage — while its security reduces to the joint compromise of $t$ independent domains.

## 5. Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

The security results in this area are *proofs*, and they compose into a coherent lattice:

> **Theorem:** FROST is existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack assuming the hardness of the discrete logarithm problem in $\mathbb{G}$, in the random oracle model, against a static adversary corrupting at most $t-1$ participants. The proof reduces forgery to the *Bischnorr* assumption — an interactive assumption inspired by the two-nonce construction — which is itself proven secure under the one-more discrete logarithm assumption [1][6].

> **Theorem:** GGMP21 UC-realizes the distributed-signature ideal functionality with identifiable abort and proactive security in the global random oracle model, under Strong RSA, DDH, semantic security of Paillier encryption, and a strengthened existential unforgeability of ECDSA [8].

> **Theorem:** Pedersen's DKG yields a uniformly random group public key against a static adversary, provided the complaint round is augmented with GJKR-style hiding commitments; the plain protocol is biasable by a rushing adversary who can steer the key among $2^k$ values [4][7].

Empirically, the Aumasson–Hamelink–Shlomovits survey reports GG-family signing at latencies on the order of seconds across WAN for moderate $n$, with GG20's non-interactive online phase reducing end-to-end signing latency to a single round trip [10]. FROST preprocessing amortizes to near-zero marginal cost per signature: nonce-commitment pairs are generated in bulk during idle periods, making the online phase a single broadcast round — a decisive property for high-frequency wallet operations [1]. Benchmarks from the Fireblocks team show GGMP21's identifiable-abort machinery adds only modest overhead relative to GG20, preserving sub-second-to-second signing at internet scale [8]. At the key-management layer, drand's production deployment of Pedersen DKG across dozens of nodes demonstrates dealer-less key generation with complaint-based disqualification operating continuously in the wild [5].

## 6. Limitations

Five limitations bound the current state of the art. **First, adaptive security gaps.** FROST's proof assumes *static* corruptions; full adaptive security for two-round threshold Schnorr was only recently addressed (Crites et al., CRYPTO 2025) under a new "low-dimensional vector representation" assumption whose hardness is not yet well-scrutinized [2]. GGMP21 closes this gap for ECDSA at the cost of heavier assumptions [8]. **Second, the trusted-setup residue.** GG-family protocols assume Strong RSA and Paillier security — assumptions with known attack contours and no post-quantum future; DKLS's ECDSA-only assumption base is conceptually cleaner but practically heavier [10]. **Third, DKG round complexity.** Dealer-less key generation remains the most communication-intensive phase; proactive refresh compounds it, and dynamic membership (adding/removing signers) is still an active research area with no widely deployed standard. **Fourth, the preprocessing state problem.** FROST's single-round online phase depends on securely stored, never-reused nonce pairs; a nonce-reuse bug is catastrophic (key recovery), so the preprocessing store becomes a new high-value target — robustness of the *nonce pipeline* is an engineering problem the cryptography does not solve [1]. **Fifth, quantum vulnerability.** Every protocol in this thesis — Pedersen DKG, FROST, all threshold-ECDSA lineages — breaks under a cryptographically relevant quantum computer. Lattice-based threshold signatures exist but remain far from the efficiency and maturity of the discrete-log constructions surveyed here; NIST's threshold-call explicitly scopes post-quantum primitives as future work [11].

## 7. Conclusion

Threshold cryptography at internet scale is no longer an academic curiosity: Pedersen–Feldman dealer-less key generation, FROST's two-round threshold Schnorr signatures, and the GG/DKLS threshold-ECDSA lineages form a mature protocol stack that already secures production digital-asset custody. The central lesson of the last decade is that *round complexity and assumption minimalism are in tension*, and the resolution differs by signature scheme: Schnorr's linearity admits FROST's elegant two-round construction with preprocessing-optimized single-round signing [1][2], while ECDSA's nonce inversion demands the heavy MtA machinery of the GG lineage or the assumption-minimal DKLS route [8][10]. The dishonest-majority model with identifiable abort, proactive share refresh, and UC security — exemplified by GGMP21 — is the correct target for accountable signer sets [8]. Looking forward, the NIST Multi-Party Threshold Cryptography project's First Call for Multi-Party Threshold Schemes (NISTIR 8214C) marks the field's transition from research competition to standardization [11], while adaptive-security proofs and post-quantum threshold signatures remain the principal open frontiers. The single point of compromise is being engineered out of existence — one round at a time.
