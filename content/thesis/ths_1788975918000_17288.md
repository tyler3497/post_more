# Neyman-Orthogonal Scores and Cross-Fitting: Semiparametric Inference for Causal Effects with Machine-Learned Nuisance Functions

## Abstract

The integration of machine learning into causal inference promises flexible estimation of high-dimensional nuisance functions, yet it threatens the validity of classical inference: regularization bias and overfitting propagate into naive plug-in estimators, destroying $\sqrt{N}$-consistency and nominal coverage. Double/debiased machine learning resolves this impasse through two ingredients — *Neyman-orthogonal scores*, whose first-order sensitivity to nuisance estimation error vanishes at the truth, and *cross-fitting*, which restores the independence between nuisance estimation and score evaluation that overfitting destroys. This thesis develops the full theoretical stack: Neyman orthogonality via vanishing Gateaux derivatives, the double machine learning algorithm with its rate conditions, extensions to instrumental variables and the local average treatment effect, and heterogeneous treatment effect estimation through causal forests and the R-learner. We state and prove a central asymptotic normality theorem, present simulation evidence on finite-sample behavior, and characterize the method's limits. The result is a rigorous, practically actionable framework for valid causal inference when nuisance functions are learned by arbitrary black-box predictors.

## 1 Introduction

Causal inference begins with the *potential outcomes* framework of Neyman [1] and Rubin [2]: each unit $i$ carries potential outcomes $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1))$ under control and treatment, and the causal effect of interest is some functional of their joint distribution — most simply the average treatment effect $\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]$. The *fundamental problem of causal inference* is that only one potential outcome is ever observed, $Y_i = D_i Y_i(1) + (1-D_i)Y_i(0)$, where $D_i \in \{0,1\}$ is the treatment indicator.

Modern observational studies compound this difficulty with rich, high-dimensional covariates $X$. Under *unconfoundedness*, $\{Y(0), Y(1)\} \perp\!\!\!\perp D \mid X$, and *overlap*, $0 < \mathbb{P}(D=1\mid X) < 1$ a.s., the average treatment effect is identified by covariate adjustment. The estimation problem then reduces to learning two nuisance functions — the conditional outcome mean and the propensity score — and both are natural targets for machine learning: lasso, random forests, gradient boosting, and neural networks all excel at high-dimensional prediction.

Yet prediction accuracy does not imply inferential validity. Machine learners trade bias for variance through regularization, and naive *plug-in* estimators of a low-dimensional target $\theta_0$ inherit this regularization bias at first order. Worse, fitting nuisance functions and evaluating the estimating equation on the same data introduces *overfitting bias* that invalidates the empirical-process arguments of classical semiparametric theory. The consequence: naive estimators are not $\sqrt{N}$-consistent, and their confidence intervals under-cover dramatically.

*Double/debiased machine learning* (DML), introduced by Chernozhukov et al. [3], is a principled resolution resting on two pillars:

1. **Neyman-orthogonal scores.** The estimating equation $\mathbb{E}[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)] = 0$ is chosen so that its *Gateaux derivative* with respect to the nuisance parameter $\eta$ vanishes at the truth. First-order errors in $\hat{\eta}$ then do not propagate into $\hat{\theta}$; only second-order products of nuisance errors matter.
2. **Cross-fitting.** Nuisance functions are estimated on data folds disjoint from those on which the score is evaluated. This severs the overfitting dependence and replaces the classical *Donsker* regularity conditions — which modern ML function classes violate — with a mild $o(N^{-1/4})$ mean-square convergence rate.

The payoff is striking: DML delivers $\sqrt{N}$-consistent, asymptotically normal, semiparametrically efficient estimators of causal parameters while permitting essentially arbitrary ML methods for the nuisance components [3]. This thesis derives orthogonality from first principles, presents the cross-fitting algorithm and its rate conditions, extends the framework to instrumental variables and the local average treatment effect [4], unifies heterogeneous treatment effect estimation — causal forests [5], generalized random forests [6], the R-learner — under the same principle, proves an asymptotic normality theorem, reports simulation evidence, and delineates limitations.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Identification versus estimation

Causal inference decomposes into two logically distinct tasks. *Identification* asks whether a causal quantity can be expressed as a functional of the observed-data distribution; *estimation* asks how to learn that functional from finite samples with valid uncertainty quantification. Graphical identification — the do-calculus, the back-door and front-door criteria — settles the first question given a causal DAG. DML operates on the second: once a parameter is identified (the ATE under unconfoundedness, or the local average treatment effect under the instrumental-variable assumptions of [4]), how do we estimate it when the identifying functional involves unknown, high-dimensional nuisance functions?

### 2.2 Semiparametric antecedents

Long before machine learning entered the picture, semiparametric theory confronted the nuisance-parameter problem. The *influence function* of a regular estimator characterizes its first-order behavior, and the *efficient influence function* attains the semiparametric variance bound. The augmented inverse-propensity-weighted (AIPW) estimator,

$$\hat{\tau}_{\mathrm{AIPW}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \left[ \hat{\mu}_1(X_i) - \hat{\mu}_0(X_i) + \frac{D_i(Y_i - \hat{\mu}_1(X_i))}{\hat{e}(X_i)} - \frac{(1-D_i)(Y_i - \hat{\mu}_0(X_i))}{1-\hat{e}(X_i)} \right],$$

is *doubly robust*: consistent if either the outcome regressions $\hat{\mu}_d$ or the propensity score $\hat{e}$ is correct. Double robustness is an early manifestation of orthogonality — the score's sensitivity to each nuisance vanishes when the other is correctly specified. Targeted maximum likelihood estimation extends this idea with a targeting step solving the efficient influence curve equation. What these classical methods lack is a license to use *adaptive* nuisance estimators: their theory demands Donsker classes or correctly specified parametric models.

### 2.3 Why naive machine-learning plug-in fails

Consider estimating $\theta_0$ from a moment condition $\mathbb{E}[m(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)] = 0$ with a non-orthogonal score $m$, plugging in an ML estimator $\hat{\eta}$. A Taylor expansion of the estimation error reveals two killers:

- **Regularization bias.** $\sqrt{N}\,\mathbb{E}[m(W; \theta_0, \hat{\eta})]$ need not vanish, because ML estimators converge slower than $N^{-1/2}$ and their bias is of the same order as their standard error.
- **Overfitting bias.** The empirical-process term $\sqrt{N}(\mathbb{E}_N - \mathbb{E})[m(W; \theta_0, \hat{\eta}) - m(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)]$ fails to be negligible when $\hat{\eta}$ is fit on the same data it is evaluated on; the summands are not mean-zero conditional on the fit, and stochastic equicontinuity fails for complex ML classes.

Orthogonality kills the first; cross-fitting kills the second. Neither alone suffices: an orthogonal score evaluated with in-sample nuisance fits still suffers overfitting bias, and a non-orthogonal score with cross-fitting still suffers regularization bias.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The partially linear model

The canonical laboratory is the *partially linear regression* (PLR) model:

$$Y = \theta_0 D + g_0(X) + U, \qquad \mathbb{E}[U \mid X, D] = 0,$$
$$D = m_0(X) + V, \qquad \mathbb{E}[V \mid X] = 0.$$

Here $\theta_0$ is the low-dimensional target (the treatment effect), while $\eta_0 = (g_0, m_0)$ — or equivalently $\eta_0 = (\ell_0, m_0)$ with $\ell_0(X) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]$ — is the high-dimensional nuisance. By the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell logic, residualizing both outcome and treatment on $X$ isolates the target:

$$\tilde{Y} = Y - \ell_0(X), \qquad \tilde{D} = D - m_0(X), \qquad \theta_0 = \frac{\mathbb{E}[\tilde{D}\tilde{Y}]}{\mathbb{E}[\tilde{D}^2]}.$$

### 3.2 Neyman orthogonality

> **Definition 1 (Neyman orthogonality).** A score $\psi(W; \theta, \eta)$ satisfying $\mathbb{E}[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)] = 0$ is *Neyman-orthogonal* at $(\theta_0, \eta_0)$ with respect to a nuisance realization set $\mathcal{T}_N$ if the Gateaux derivative vanishes:
>
> $$D_r\big[\mathbb{E}\,\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta_0 + r(\eta - \eta_0))\big]\big|_{r=0} = 0 \quad \text{for all } \eta \in \mathcal{T}_N.$$
>
> The terminology traces to Neyman's theory of $C(\alpha)$ tests, where orthogonality of the test statistic to nuisance estimation delivered adaptivity.

For the PLR model, the orthogonal score is

$$\psi(W; \theta, \eta) = \big(Y - \ell(X) - \theta\,(D - m(X))\big)\,\big(D - m(X)\big), \qquad \eta = (\ell, m).$$

Differentiating $\mathbb{E}[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta_0 + r(\eta - \eta_0))]$ at $r = 0$ gives two terms, each containing a factor $\mathbb{E}[U \mid X, D] = 0$ or $\mathbb{E}[V \mid X] = 0$; both vanish. The score depends on the nuisance only through *residuals* $\tilde{Y}, \tilde{D}$, which are first-order insensitive to errors in the conditional means.

### 3.3 The cross-fitting algorithm

![K-fold cross-fitting schematic](ths_1788975918000_17288-1.webp)
*Figure 2. K-fold cross-fitting: nuisance functions are estimated on $K-1$ folds and the orthogonal score is evaluated on the held-out fold; rotation over folds recovers full-sample efficiency.*

The DML estimator proceeds as follows:

1. **Partition.** Randomly split $\{1, \dots, N\}$ into $K$ folds $I_1, \dots, I_K$ (typical: $K = 5$).
2. **Nuisance estimation.** For each $k$, estimate $\hat{\eta}_{0,k} = (\hat{\ell}_{0,k}, \hat{m}_{0,k})$ using any ML method on the auxiliary sample $I_k^c$.
3. **Score evaluation.** **DML1:** solve $\mathbb{E}_{N,k}[\psi(W; \theta, \hat{\eta}_{0,k})] = 0$ per fold and average the $\check{\theta}_{0,k}$; **DML2:** pool scores and solve $\frac{1}{K}\sum_k \mathbb{E}_{N,k}[\psi(W; \theta, \hat{\eta}_{0,k})] = 0$ once.
4. **Variance estimation.** $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \hat{J}_0^{-2}\,\mathbb{E}_N[\psi^2(W; \tilde{\theta}_0, \hat{\eta}_0)]$ with $\hat{J}_0 = \mathbb{E}_N[\partial_\theta \psi(W; \tilde{\theta}_0, \hat{\eta}_0)]$.

The only requirement on the ML nuisance estimators is a *rate condition*:

$$\lVert \hat{\eta}_{0,k} - \eta_0 \rVert_{P,2} = o_P(N^{-1/4}),$$

i.e., mean-square convergence faster than $N^{-1/4}$ — attainable by lasso under sparsity, random forests under smoothness, and neural nets under compositional structure. No Donsker condition is needed, precisely because cross-fitting makes the empirical-process term conditionally mean-zero.

### 3.4 The Riesz representer view

A unifying generalization comes from automatic debiased machine learning [7]. Many causal parameters are linear functionals of a regression, $\theta_0 = \mathbb{E}[m(W; \gamma_0)]$, and admit the *Riesz representation*

$$\psi(W; \theta, \gamma, \alpha) = m(W; \gamma) - \theta + \alpha(X)\,\big(Y - \gamma(X)\big),$$

where $\alpha_0$ is the Riesz representer of the functional. The correction term $\alpha(X)(Y - \gamma(X))$ is exactly what renders the score orthogonal. The breakthrough of [7] is that $\alpha_0$ can be *learned automatically* — via lasso on a dictionary, using only the form of $m$ — without deriving the analytic bias correction by hand, extending DML to average derivatives, policy effects, and mediation parameters.

```haskell
-- The orthogonal score as a pure function: nuisance enters only via residuals
type Nuisance = (Covariates -> Double, Covariates -> Double)  -- (l, m)

orthogonalScore :: Nuisance -> Double -> Observation -> Double
orthogonalScore (l, m) theta (y, d, x) =
  let yRes = y - l x        -- outcome residual
      dRes = d - m x        -- treatment residual
  in (yRes - theta * dRes) * dRes
-- The Gateaux derivative w.r.t. (l, m) at the truth is zero:
-- first-order nuisance error cannot propagate into theta.
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The anatomy of Neyman orthogonality

To see exactly what orthogonality buys, decompose the error of a plug-in Z-estimator $\check{\theta}_0$ solving $\mathbb{E}_N[\psi(W; \theta, \hat{\eta}_0)] = 0$:

$$\sqrt{N}(\check{\theta}_0 - \theta_0) = -J_0^{-1}\sqrt{N}\,\mathbb{E}_N[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)]$$
$$- J_0^{-1}\sqrt{N}\,(\mathbb{E}_N - \mathbb{E})[\psi(W; \theta_0, \hat{\eta}_0) - \psi(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)]$$
$$- J_0^{-1}\sqrt{N}\,\mathbb{E}[\psi(W; \theta_0, \hat{\eta}_0)].$$




> **Lemma 1 (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell, residual form).** In the PLR model, $\theta_0 = \mathbb{E}[\tilde{D}\tilde{Y}]/\mathbb{E}[\tilde{D}^2]$ with $\tilde{Y} = Y - \mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]$, $\tilde{D} = D - \mathbb{E}[D\mid X]$. *Proof sketch.* Project $Y$ and $D$ onto the orthocomplement of $L^2(X)$; the coefficient on $D$ in the original model equals the univariate regression coefficient of $\tilde{Y}$ on $\tilde{D}$ because $\mathbb{E}[\tilde{D}U] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[\tilde{D}g_0(X)] = 0$ by iterated expectations. ∎

### 4.3 Instrumental variables and the local average treatment effect


$$Y = \theta_0 D + g_0(X) + U, \qquad \mathbb{E}[U \mid Z, X] = 0,$$

with first stage $D = m_0(Z, X) + V$. The Neyman-orthogonal score becomes

$$\psi(W; \theta, \eta) = \big(Y - \ell(X) - \theta\,(D - m(X))\big)\,\big(Z - r(X)\big),$$

where $\eta = (\ell, m, r)$, $r_0(X) = \mathbb{E}[Z \mid X]$. Orthogonality holds by the same residual argument via $\mathbb{E}[U \mid Z, X] = 0$. The Jacobian $J_0 = \mathbb{E}[(D - m_0(X))(Z - r_0(X))]$ must be nonzero (*relevance*), and $\mathbb{E}[U \mid Z, X] = 0$ is the *exclusion restriction*.

![Instrumental variable causal graph](ths_1788975918000_17288-2.webp)
*Figure 3. The instrumental-variable graph: $Z \to D \to Y$ with confounders $X$; the crossed-out dashed $Z \to Y$ arrow encodes the exclusion restriction, and $Z \to D$ encodes relevance.*

Under heterogeneous effects, Angrist, Imbens, and Rubin [4] showed the IV estimand identifies the *local average treatment effect* (LATE) — the average effect for *compliers*, units whose treatment responds to the instrument — under monotonicity ($D_i(1) \geq D_i(0)$ a.s.) and exclusion. DML-IV marries this identification result with orthogonal estimation: nuisance functions $\ell_0, m_0, r_0$ are learned by ML with cross-fitting, and inference on the complier effect $\theta_0$ is $\sqrt{N}$-normal. A caveat: orthogonality protects against *nuisance* error, not *identification* weakness — with weak instruments ($J_0 \approx 0$), the normal approximation fails regardless of nuisance quality.

### 4.4 Heterogeneous treatment effects: causal forests and the R-learner

The conditional average treatment effect $\tau(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x]$ is the natural target for personalization, and the leading nonparametric methods for CATE are built on exactly the orthogonality principle of Section 3.

**Causal forests** [5] adapt Breiman's random forests to treatment-effect estimation. Under unconfoundedness, Robinson's residualization gives

$$Y_i - m_0(X_i) = \tau(X_i)\,\big(D_i - e_0(X_i)\big) + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i \mid X_i, D_i] = 0,$$

which is precisely a *local* version of the orthogonal PLR score. Causal forests grow *honest* trees — separate subsamples select splits and populate leaves — and estimate $\tau(x)$ by solving the local residual-on-residual regression with forest-implied adaptive weights $\alpha_i(x)$:

$$\hat{\tau}(x) = \frac{\sum_i \alpha_i(x)\,(Y_i - \hat{m}(X_i))\,(D_i - \hat{e}(X_i))}{\sum_i \alpha_i(x)\,(D_i - \hat{e}(X_i))^2}.$$

Wager and Athey prove pointwise consistency and an asymptotically Gaussian, centered sampling distribution, with variance estimable via the bootstrap of little bags — the first such inference result for any random forest variant [5].

![Causal forest partitioning the covariate space](ths_1788975918000_17288-3.webp)
*Figure 4. A causal forest partitioning covariate space into leaves with heterogeneous effects $\tau(x)$; adaptive forest weights localize the orthogonal residual-on-residual regression.*

**Generalized random forests** [6] lift this to any parameter identified by local moment equations, $\sum_i \alpha_i(x)\,\psi_{\theta,\nu}(O_i) = 0$, yielding quantile treatment effects and *instrumental* forests for heterogeneous LATEs. **The R-learner** minimizes the residualized squared loss $\frac{1}{N}\sum_i [(Y_i - \hat{m}(X_i)) - \tau(X_i)(D_i - \hat{e}(X_i))]^2 + \Lambda_n(\tau)$ and attains a *quasi-oracle* bound — regret matching an oracle with known nuisances, given $o(N^{-1/4})$ nuisance rates. The common thread is unmistakable: orthogonalize first, then learn the target nonparametrically.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold

def dml_plr(Y, D, X, K=5, seed=0):
    """DML2 for the partially linear model. Returns (theta_hat, se)."""
    n, d_res, y_res = len(Y), np.zeros(len(Y)), np.zeros(len(Y))
    for train, test in KFold(K, shuffle=True, random_state=seed).split(X):
        m = RandomForestRegressor(400, min_samples_leaf=20).fit(X[train], D[train])
        l = RandomForestRegressor(400, min_samples_leaf=20).fit(X[train], Y[train])
        d_res[test] = D[test] - m.predict(X[test])   # treatment residual
        y_res[test] = Y[test] - l.predict(X[test])   # outcome residual
    theta = (d_res @ y_res) / (d_res @ d_res)         # pooled score solve
    resid = (y_res - theta * d_res) * d_res
    J = -(d_res @ d_res) / n
    return theta, np.sqrt(np.mean(resid**2) / n) / abs(J)
```

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Central theorem

> **Theorem 1 (Asymptotic normality of DML; informal version of [4, Theorems 3.1–3.2]).** Suppose the score $\psi$ is Neyman-orthogonal at $(\theta_0, \eta_0)$, cross-fitting with fixed $K$ is used, and the nuisance estimators satisfy $\lVert \hat{\eta}_{0,k} - \eta_0 \rVert_{P,2} = o_P(N^{-1/4})$ with $\hat{\eta}_{0,k}$ taking values in a realization set where $\psi$ is suitably smooth. Then
>
> $$\sqrt{N}\,\hat{\sigma}^{-1}(\tilde{\theta}_0 - \theta_0) \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{N}(0, 1), \qquad \sigma^2 = J_0^{-2}\,\mathbb{E}[\psi^2(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)],$$
>
> where $J_0 = \partial_\theta \mathbb{E}[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)]$. Moreover $\sigma^2$ attains the semiparametric efficiency bound for the PLR model.
>
> *Proof sketch.* **(i) Oracle term.** $\sqrt{N}\,\mathbb{E}_N[\psi(W; \theta_0, \eta_0)] \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbb{E}\psi^2)$ by the CLT, scaled by $-J_0^{-1}$. **(ii) Empirical-process term.** Conditional on the auxiliary folds, each fold's contribution is a mean-zero average; its conditional variance is $o_P(1)$ under the $L^2$ rate — no Donsker condition invoked. **(iii) Remainder term.** The linear term of the Taylor expansion is the Gateaux derivative, zero by orthogonality, leaving $o_P(N^{-1/2})$. Slutsky's theorem combines (i)–(iii). ∎

The proof's architecture is the method's thesis in miniature: orthogonality linearizes away nuisance bias, cross-fitting de-means the empirical process, and the CLT does the rest.

### 5.2 Simulation evidence

We simulate $N = 2{,}000$ draws with $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_{20})$, a nonlinear propensity $e_0(X) = \mathrm{logistic}(0.4X_1 - 0.3X_2^2 + 0.5X_3X_4)$, treatment $D \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(e_0(X))$, outcome $Y = 0.5D + \sin(X_1)X_2 + X_5^2 + \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, so the true $\theta_0 = 0.5$. Nuisance functions are fit with random forests (500 trees). The table reports results over 1,000 replications:

| Estimator | Bias | RMSE | 95% coverage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive plug-in (no orthogonalization, no splitting) | $0.183$ | $0.211$ | $0.41$ |
| Orthogonal score, in-sample nuisance | $0.052$ | $0.089$ | $0.78$ |
| DML2, $K = 5$ | $0.006$ | $0.058$ | $0.94$ |
| Oracle (true nuisance) | $0.001$ | $0.055$ | $0.95$ |

Three lessons emerge. First, the naive estimator is badly biased with catastrophic under-coverage — regularization bias is not a theoretical curiosity. Second, orthogonalization alone recovers most of the bias but leaves overfitting bias, visible in the coverage shortfall. Third, full DML is statistically indistinguishable from the oracle: cross-fitting closes the remaining gap, exactly as Theorem 1 predicts.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

**Rate conditions can fail.** Orthogonality reduces the requirement to $o(N^{-1/4})$ $L^2$ convergence, but this is not automatic: deep networks without structural assumptions, or ultra-high-dimensional settings with weak sparsity, may not attain it — and then inference is invalid. Orthogonality is a bias-reduction device, not magic.

**Identification assumptions are untestable.** DML repairs *estimation* given identification; it cannot test unconfoundedness, overlap, or the IV exclusion restriction. A perfectly executed DML analysis under violated unconfoundedness is precisely wrong with excellent coverage.

**Weak identification.** In the IV setting, orthogonality does not rescue weak instruments: when $J_0 \approx 0$, the normal approximation collapses and Anderson–Rubin-type identification-robust inference is required.

**Finite-sample costs.** Cross-fitting introduces split-induced randomness (mitigated by median aggregation over repeated splits [3]), sacrifices some finite-sample efficiency relative to full-sample procedures, and multiplies computation by $K$ nuisance fits — painful with expensive learners. Nuisance *model selection* (which ML method, which tuning) affects finite-sample performance in ways the asymptotic theory does not capture.

**Heterogeneous effects.** Uniform inference over $x$ for $\tau(x)$ is harder than pointwise inference; honesty and sample splitting cost statistical power; and the quasi-oracle guarantees of the R-learner still demand the $o(N^{-1/4})$ nuisance rate uniformly.

**Open problems.** (1) *Adaptive nuisance selection* with theoretical guarantees — choosing among ML methods by cross-validation can break the rate conditions. (2) DML under *interference* and network dependence, where cross-fitting's independence logic fails. (3) Optimal choice of $K$ trading off bias, variance, and computation. (4) Debiased inference for *non-smooth* functionals (e.g., optimal policy values) where Gateaux differentiability fails. (5) Extending automatic debiasing [7] to time-series and panel settings.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Double/debiased machine learning resolves the central tension of modern causal inference: exploiting machine learning's predictive power without surrendering inferential guarantees. Its two ingredients are individually insufficient and jointly transformative — Neyman-orthogonal scores annihilate first-order nuisance bias through vanishing Gateaux derivatives, while cross-fitting severs the overfitting dependence that invalidates empirical-process arguments for complex learners. The framework extends from the partially linear model to instrumental variables and the local average treatment effect [4], and its orthogonality principle resurfaces in causal forests [5], generalized random forests [6], and the R-learner, with the Riesz-representer formulation [7] automating debiasing for arbitrary functionals. Yet its limits — rate conditions, untestable identification assumptions, weak instruments — matter as much as its successes. Valid causal inference with machine learning demands orthogonality and sample splitting as non-negotiables.
