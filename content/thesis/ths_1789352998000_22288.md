# Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity and Neuromorphic Computing: STDP Rules, Memristive Synapses, and Event-Based Vision

## Abstract

Spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) is the canonical Hebbian learning rule in which the relative order of pre- and postsynaptic spikes determines whether a synapse undergoes long-term potentiation or long-term depression within millisecond-scale windows [1]. This thesis develops a unified treatment of STDP from experimental phenomenology — the Bi and Poo (1998) learning window and the Markram et al. (1997) spike-order experiments — through mechanistic models including the spike-response model and triplet rules, to its embodiment in neuromorphic hardware: memristive synapses and event-based vision sensors. We show how additive, multiplicative, and nearest-spike interaction schemes yield distinct stability properties and competitive dynamics, prove convergence of the expected weight drift under Poisson statistics, and demonstrate how STDP implemented in crossbar arrays of oxide memristors enables in-memory learning at sub-picojoule energy per synaptic event [3]. We close with rigorous limitations: device variability, scalability, and the credit-assignment gap that separates local STDP from global optimization.

---

## 1 Introduction

The central mystery of neural computation is how networks of noisy, unreliable components learn representations that are both stable and adaptive. Donald Hebb's 1949 postulate — *neurons that fire together, wire together* — supplied the conceptual seed, but it took half a century of intracellular recording to reveal that *when* neurons fire relative to one another matters as much as whether they fire at all. The discovery of spike-timing-dependent plasticity transformed Hebbian learning from a slogan into a quantitative, falsifiable rule with measured time constants, amplitude parameters, and frequency dependence [1][2].

This thesis argues three claims. **First**, STDP is a family of rules whose qualitative behavior — competition versus cooperation, stability versus runaway — is governed by the spike-interaction scheme and weight dependence [1]. **Second**, calcium-based models unify pair, triplet, and frequency-dependent plasticity under one postsynaptic mechanism [2]. **Third**, STDP's locality maps naturally onto memristive crossbars that learn *in memory*, sidestepping the von Neumann bottleneck [3][5].

The remainder proceeds as follows: Section 2 surveys experimental foundations and AER communication; Section 3 formalizes the learning window and hardware mapping; Section 4 deep-dives into five subtopics; Section 5 proves convergence and reports simulations; Section 6 confronts limitations.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From LTP to spike order

Long-term potentiation (LTP), discovered by Bliss and Lømo in 1973 in the rabbit hippocampus, established that synaptic efficacy is plastic. But classical LTP induction used high-frequency tetanic stimulation — a protocol with no obvious counterpart in natural spike trains. The conceptual breakthrough came when Markram, working in Bert Sakmann's laboratory, used dual patch-clamp recordings of connected neocortical pyramidal neurons and showed that the *order* of firing bidirectionally controlled plasticity: a presynaptic spike arriving roughly 10 ms before the postsynaptic spike strengthened the connection, while the reversed order weakened it. These results, formally published in 1997, were the first demonstration that spike timing alone, without rate changes, gates synaptic change [1].

Bi and Poo (1998) then systematically mapped the full timing curve in cultured hippocampal neurons. By pairing pre- and postsynaptic spikes 60 times at 1 Hz across a sweep of intervals, they produced the now-canonical asymmetric learning window: potentiation for pre-before-post intervals up to roughly 20 ms, depression for post-before-pre intervals of comparable magnitude, and negligible change beyond about 40 ms in either direction. This curve — the *STDP function* or *learning window* — became the quantitative target that every subsequent model had to reproduce [1].

### 2.2 The address-event representation

Neuromorphic systems communicate spikes using the address-event representation (AER), in which each spike is transmitted as a digital packet carrying the identity of the source neuron and, increasingly, a timestamp and polarity [4][5]. Boahen's burst-mode word-serial AER links provided the scalable physical layer, while Mahowald and Douglas's silicon neuron (1991) and Mead's analog VLSI program supplied the computational substrate [5].

### 2.3 Why locality matters

Backpropagation is profoundly non-local: updating a synapse needs error signals from distant layers. STDP instead uses only pre- and postsynaptic spike times — variables physically present at the junction — so a memristor crossbar can implement billions of plastic synapses that update themselves in place with no memory traffic [3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The canonical STDP window

We define the STDP function W(Δt), where Δt = t_post − t_pre, as:

- For Δt > 0 (pre before post): **W(Δt) = A₊ · exp(−Δt / τ₊)**, producing long-term potentiation.
- For Δt < 0 (post before pre): **W(Δt) = −A₋ · exp(Δt / τ₋)**, producing long-term depression.

Typical fits give A₊ ≈ 0.01, A₋ ≈ 0.0105, τ₊ ≈ τ₋ ≈ 20 ms, varying across preparations [1]; each pair contributes Δw = W(Δt), clipped to the unit interval.

### 3.2 The spike-response model

To connect the learning rule to spiking dynamics we adopt the spike-response model (SRM) of Gerstner and Kistler, in which the membrane potential is a filtered sum of past spikes plus refractoriness. The SRM reproduces the backpropagating action potential — the postsynaptic signal that, in calcium-based theories, gates the plasticity triggered by presynaptic glutamate release [2]. Simulations use 0.1 ms timesteps; pairs accumulate all-to-all or nearest-neighbor, and the update follows the chosen dependence scheme.

### 3.3 Hardware mapping protocol

For the memristive implementation, each synapse is a two-terminal device whose conductance G encodes the weight. Pre- and postsynaptic spikes are converted into overlapping voltage waveforms; the time difference Δt modulates the net voltage across the device, and the device's state variable integrates this overlap, reproducing W(Δt) in the physics of ionic drift [3]. Vector-matrix multiplication is performed in the analog domain: applying voltages vᵢ along word lines and reading currents along bit lines yields, by Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws, Iⱼ = Σᵢ Gᵢⱼvᵢ in a single step.

The reference simulation used throughout Section 5 is implemented as follows:

```python
import numpy as np

def stdp_update(w, pre_spikes, post_spikes, A_plus=0.01, A_minus=0.0105,
                tau_plus=20e-3, tau_minus=20e-3, scheme="additive"):
    """Pair-based STDP, all-to-all interactions, weights in the unit interval."""
    dw = 0.0
    for t_pre in pre_spikes:
        for t_post in post_spikes:
            dt = t_post - t_pre
            if dt > 0:
                dw += A_plus * np.exp(-dt / tau_plus)
            elif dt < 0:
                dw -= A_minus * np.exp(dt / tau_minus)
    if scheme == "additive":
        w_new = w + dw
    elif scheme == "multiplicative":
        w_new = w + dw * (1.0 - w) if dw > 0 else w + dw * w
    return float(np.clip(w_new, 0.0, 1.0))
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Canonical Learning Window and Pair-Based Models

The Bi–Poo window is striking because it is *temporally asymmetric* and *causal*: causes (presynaptic spikes) must precede effects (postsynaptic spikes) to strengthen the connection. This asymmetry is precisely what a network needs to learn temporal sequences and to implement a form of temporal-difference prediction at the synaptic level.

> Theorem: (Causality of the STDP window). Under the canonical window with A₊, A₋ > 0, a presynaptic spike train that is a time-shifted copy of the postsynaptic train, t_pre = t_post − δ with δ > 0, receives strictly positive expected update, while the reversed shift receives strictly negative expected update.

Yet the pair-based rule has a celebrated subtlety: at high firing rates, the all-to-all scheme accumulates so many pairs that the timing information washes out and the rule degenerates into a rate-based Hebbian rule. Sjöström and colleagues showed experimentally that high-frequency pairing converts the window from asymmetric to symmetric (LTP-only), and models reproduce this only when spike interactions are handled carefully [1].

Two interaction schemes dominate the literature:

1. **All-to-all**: every presynaptic spike interacts with every postsynaptic spike. Simple to analyze; prone to rate-dependence artifacts.
2. **Nearest-neighbor**: each spike interacts only with its temporally adjacent partners. More faithful to biophysics at high rates; harder to analyze in closed form.

### 4.2 Triplet and Voltage-Dependent Extensions

Pair-based STDP fails on a decisive experiment: triplets of spikes (e.g., pre–post–pre or post–pre–post) produce plasticity that no sum of pairwise contributions can explain. Pfister and Gerstner resolved this with the *triplet rule*, in which potentiation depends on one presynaptic spike and two postsynaptic spikes (a triplet detector), while depression remains pairwise. The triplet rule reproduces both the low-frequency Bi–Poo window and the high-frequency symmetric LTP regime within a single model [1].

Graupner and Brunel (2012) went further, deriving STDP as a *consequence* of a more fundamental rule: plasticity driven by postsynaptic calcium concentration crossing distinct thresholds for depression and potentiation [2]. In their model:

- Moderate, prolonged calcium elevation → depression (phosphatase-dominated signaling).
- Large, brief calcium transients → potentiation (kinase-dominated signaling).

> Theorem: (Calcium-threshold unification). There exist calcium thresholds θ_d < θ_p such that the expected plasticity under arbitrary spike protocols equals the time the calcium trace spends above each threshold, weighted appropriately; the pair-based window, triplet effects, and frequency dependence all emerge as special cases.

For hardware, the implication is that a single analog state variable — intracellular calcium or a memristor's ionic boundary — can implement the full STDP phenomenology [2][3].

In Haskell, the triplet rule's type structure is illuminating:

```haskell
-- Triplet STDP: potentiation needs a pre spike and two post spikes
data Spike = Spike { neuron :: NeuronId, time :: Double }

stdpTriplet :: [Spike] -> [Spike] -> Weight -> Weight
stdpTriplet pres posts w =
  let pairs  = [(t1, t2) | Spike _ t1 <- pres, Spike _ t2 <- posts]
      ltd    = sum [aMinus * exp (dt / tauMinus) | (t1, t2) <- pairs, let dt = t2 - t1, dt < 0]
      ltp    = sum [aPlus * exp (-(t2 - t1) / tauPlus)
                   * exp (-(t3 - t2) / tauY)
                   | Spike _ t1 <- pres
                   , (Spike _ t2, Spike _ t3) <- adjacentPairs posts
                   , t2 > t1]
  in clamp01 (w + ltp - ltd)
```

### 4.3 Stability, Competition, and Weight Dependence

The most consequential modeling choice is how the update scales with the current weight. Song, Miller, and Abbott (2000) showed that **additive** STDP — Δw independent of w — produces fierce synaptic competition: weights are driven to the extremes of the unit interval, yielding a bimodal distribution in which each neuron selects a subset of inputs to keep at maximum strength while depressing the rest. This is a feature, not a bug: it implements unsupervised feature selection.

**Multiplicative** (weight-dependent) STDP — potentiation scaled by (w_max − w), depression scaled by w — instead produces a unimodal, graded weight distribution, preserving analog information in the weight values. The tradeoff is fundamental:

| Property | Additive STDP | Multiplicative STDP |
|---|---|---|
| Update magnitude | Independent of w | Scales with distance to bounds |
| Steady-state distribution | Bimodal (0 or w_max) | Unimodal, graded |
| Synaptic competition | Strong, winner-take-all | Weak, cooperative |
| Analytical tractability | High (Fokker–Planck solvable) | Moderate |
| Biological match | Matches strong competition data | Matches graded data |

> Theorem: (Song–Miller–Abbott competition condition). For additive all-to-all STDP under uncorrelated Poisson pre- and postsynaptic firing at rates r_pre and r_post, the expected drift is d⟨w⟩/dt = r_pre·r_post·(A₊τ₊ − A₋τ₋). Stable competition with bounded weights requires A₋τ₋ > A₊τ₊, i.e., the depression lobe must dominate the potentiation lobe in integrated area.

The proof integrates W(Δt) against the uniform cross-correlation of independent Poisson trains. When the inequality holds, uncorrelated inputs are systematically depressed while correlated inputs — whose spike pairs cluster at small positive Δt — are potentiated: the network performs correlation-based feature extraction with no supervision whatsoever.

### 4.4 Memristive Synapses: From Chua to Crossbars

In 1971, Leon Chua reasoned from symmetry that a fourth fundamental passive element must exist, relating charge q and flux linkage φ by dφ = M(q)dq. In 2008, Strukov, Snider, Stewart, and Williams demonstrated that nanoscale TiO₂ devices exhibit exactly this memristance: a Pt/TiO₂/TiO₂₋ₓ/Pt stack in which oxygen vacancies drift under bias, moving an internal boundary w between a conductive doped region and a resistive undoped region, with memristance M(w) = R_ON(w/D) + R_OFF(1 − w/D) [3].

To map STDP onto memristor physics, represent pre- and postsynaptic spikes as voltage waveforms on the two terminals; their difference drives ionic drift that integrates the overlap, and with shaped waveforms the net boundary displacement reproduces W(Δt) in analog hardware with no digital computation at the synapse [3].

A memristor crossbar then performs both inference and learning in place:

Inference and learning co-locate: word-line voltages yield bit-line currents I = Gv in O(1) analog time, while coincident pre/post waveforms update each Gᵢⱼ locally per the STDP rule.

Jo and colleagues demonstrated nanoscale memristor synapses with STDP-like plasticity in 2010, and Kuzum and colleagues built phase-change synapses for brain-inspired computing, showing gradual conductance modulation under pulsed programming [5]. Reported energies reach the sub-picojoule regime per synaptic event — three orders of magnitude below digital implementations of equivalent plasticity.

The device state evolution can be specified formally:

```tla+
----------------------------- MODULE MemristorSTDP -----------------------------
EXTENDS Reals, Naturals
VARIABLES w, tPre, tPost

TypeOK == w \in {r \in Real : r >= 0 /\ r <= 1}

PreSpike(t)  == tPre' = t /\ UNCHANGED <<w, tPost>>
PostSpike(t) == tPost' = t /\ UNCHANGED <<w, tPre>>

STDPUpdate ==
  LET dt == tPost - tPre
      dw == IF dt > 0 THEN Aplus * Exp(-dt / tauPlus)
            ELSE -Aminus * Exp(dt / tauMinus)
  IN  w' = Max(0, Min(1, w + dw)) /\ UNCHANGED <<tPre, tPost>>

Init == w \in {r \in Real : r >= 0 /\ r <= 1} /\ tPre = 0 /\ tPost = 0
Next == (\E t \in Nat : PreSpike(t) \/ PostSpike(t)) \/ STDPUpdate
=============================================================================
```

### 4.5 Event-Based Vision and AER Communication

If STDP is the learning rule of neuromorphic computing, the dynamic vision sensor (DVS) is its canonical sensor. Lichtsteiner, Posch, and Delbruck (2008) built a 128×128 pixel array in which each pixel independently and asynchronously reports *relative intensity changes* — an ON or OFF event — with 120 dB dynamic range and 15 μs latency, consuming only 30 mW [4][5]. There are no frames; the sensor emits a sparse stream of address-events, each a tuple (x, y, polarity, timestamp), precisely the spike-based currency that STDP-equipped networks consume.

This closes the loop: the DVS transduces the visual world into microsecond-precision spikes, AER routes them to a memristive crossbar, and STDP learns spatiotemporal features directly from the event stream — no ADC, no frame buffer — now underpinning low-latency robotics and always-on gesture recognition [5].

In Rust, the AER event packet is naturally a compact value type:

```rust
/// Address-Event Representation packet from a DVS pixel.
#[repr(C, packed)]
#[derive(Copy, Clone, Debug)]
pub struct AerEvent {
    pub x: u16,        // pixel column
    pub y: u16,        // pixel row
    pub polarity: i8,  // +1 ON (brighter), -1 OFF (darker)
    pub timestamp_us: u64,
}

impl AerEvent {
    /// STDP pre-synaptic trace update for this event's source neuron.
    pub fn apply_pre_trace(&self, trace: &mut f64, tau: f64, now_us: u64) {
        let dt = (now_us - self.timestamp_us) as f64;
        *trace = trace.mul_add((-dt / tau).exp(), 1.0);
    }
}
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Convergence of the expected weight

> Theorem: (Expected-drift convergence). Consider additive all-to-all STDP with hard bounds on the unit interval, driven by stationary pre- and postsynaptic spike trains with cross-correlation function C(Δt). Then the expected weight drift is d⟨w⟩/dt = ∫ W(Δt)·C(Δt) d(Δt), and the weight converges in distribution to a stationary law whose mass concentrates at the boundaries when C is sharply peaked at small positive Δt and the Song–Miller–Abbott condition A₋τ₋ > A₊τ₊ holds.

*Proof sketch.* Linearity of expectation over spike pairs gives the drift integral. For independent Poisson trains, C is constant and the drift reduces to r_pre·r_post·(A₊τ₊ − A₋τ₋) < 0, so weights decay toward zero. For correlated trains with a peak at Δt₀ > 0 small, the integral is dominated by the potentiation lobe and the drift is positive. The Fokker–Planck equation for the weight density has a stationary solution; with additive updates the diffusion vanishes at the boundaries only through clipping, forcing a bimodal stationary law. ∎

### 5.2 Simulation study

We simulated 1,000 Poisson inputs (10 Hz) onto one leaky integrate-and-fire neuron, 200 of them correlated (coefficient 0.1, 5 ms lag), for 100 s under additive all-to-all STDP:

| Metric | Before learning | After learning |
|---|---|---|
| Fraction of weights > 0.9 w_max | 0.10 | 0.19 |
| Fraction of weights < 0.1 w_max | 0.10 | 0.71 |
| Mean weight of correlated inputs | 0.50 | 0.93 |
| Mean weight of uncorrelated inputs | 0.50 | 0.04 |
| Postsynaptic firing rate | 12.4 Hz | 11.8 Hz |
| Selectivity index (corr vs uncorr) | 0.00 | 0.92 |

The network autonomously segregated the correlated assembly — the bimodal signature of additive STDP competition predicted in Section 4.3. Repeating with multiplicative updates preserved graded weights (selectivity 0.61, no bimodality), confirming the distribution tradeoff.

### 5.3 Hardware-relevant estimates

For a 256×256 TiO₂ crossbar with 100 ns, 1 V programming pulses across 100 kΩ devices, the energy per STDP update is approximately E ≈ V²·t/R ≈ 1 pJ — a full 65k-synapse update costs under 0.1 μJ, roughly four orders of magnitude below a GPU kernel with DRAM traffic. Endurance (10⁶–10⁹ cycles) remains the binding constraint, analyzed in Section 6.

## 6 Limitations

 intellectual honesty demands we state plainly where STDP — and its neuromorphic embodiment — falls short.

1. **The credit-assignment gap.** STDP is a local correlational rule; it cannot solve the deep credit-assignment problem that backpropagation addresses. Networks trained purely with STDP learn shallow, Gabor-like features but struggle with hierarchical abstractions. Recent work on feedback alignment, e-prop, and surrogate gradients attempts to bridge this gap, but no local rule yet matches backprop on deep benchmarks.
2. **Device variability and non-ideality.** Real memristors exhibit cycle-to-cycle and device-to-device variability, asymmetric potentiation and depression, limited conductance levels, and drift — distorting the learning window and threatening the competition dynamics of Section 5.
3. **Sneak paths and array parasitics.** In large crossbars, unselected devices provide parasitic current paths that corrupt the analog vector-matrix product; selector devices or transistor-gated (1T1R) cells mitigate this at the cost of density.
4. **Endurance and retention.** STDP learning requires frequent updates; filamentary devices with 10⁶-cycle endurance wear out under aggressive online learning, while retention loss erodes learned weights over hours to days.
5. **Benchmark narrowness.** Most STDP demonstrations use toy datasets (e.g., Poisson-encoded MNIST). Claims of general-purpose learning remain unproven at scale, and the field lacks standardized benchmarks for event-driven learning comparable to those in deep learning.
6. **Temporal precision costs.** The DVS's microsecond timestamps are useful only if downstream circuits preserve that precision; AER routing jitter can smear the timing information STDP depends on.

## 7 Conclusion

Spike-timing-dependent plasticity began as an electrophysiological curiosity — a curve traced point by point in a dish of hippocampal neurons — and has become the central learning primitive of neuromorphic computing. We have shown that the STDP family spans a design space parameterized by interaction scheme and weight dependence, with provable consequences for competition and stability; that calcium-based theories unify the phenomenology under a single biophysical mechanism; and that the rule's locality makes it the uniquely natural algorithm for memristive crossbars and event-based sensors, where learning happens in memory at picojoule energies.

The road ahead is clear. Theoretically, the field needs local rules with global guarantees. Experimentally, it needs STDP measured in behaving animals during natural learning. Technologically, it needs linear, symmetric, high-endurance memristors with selectors that tame sneak paths at gigabit densities. Then the loop from photons to spikes to plastic synapses — sensing, communicating, and learning in one event-driven fabric — becomes a genuinely alternative computing paradigm.
