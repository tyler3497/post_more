# Causal Inference: Instrumental Variables, Mendelian Randomization, and the Local Average Treatment Effect

## Abstract

Instrumental variables (IV) constitute the principal technology by which econometrics, epidemiology, and the quantitative sciences recover causal effects when unmeasured confounding defeats ordinary regression. This essay develops the theory from the canonical potential-outcomes formulation of Angrist, Imbens, and Rubin [1], through the local average treatment effect theorem of Imbens and Angrist [2], to its contemporary realizations in Mendelian randomization genetics [3] and large-scale automated platforms such as MR-Base [4]. We derive the Wald and two-stage least squares estimators, characterize the three canonical identification assumptions — relevance, independence, and exclusion — and give the LATE identification argument under monotonicity. We connect the potential-outcomes formalism to the graphical do-calculus tradition [5], and evaluate landmark applications including the quarter-of-birth instrument for schooling [6], its weak-instrument critique [7], and modern two-sample Mendelian randomization. The essay closes with an assessment of exclusion violations, pleiotropy, and external validity: the frontier constraints on credible causal identification.

## 1 Introduction

The central difficulty of empirical causal inference is that the data we observe were generated under self-selected, institutionally determined, or otherwise nonrandom treatment assignment, while the questions we ask are inherently interventional: what would the outcome be if treatment were imposed or withheld? Randomized controlled trials resolve the tension by construction, severing the link between the determinants of treatment and the determinants of the outcome. Yet trials are frequently infeasible, unethical, underpowered, or simply incapable of answering the exact policy question of interest. Observational data, abundant and cheap, thus remain the workhorse of applied science — but naive regression of outcomes on treatments inherits every omitted variable, every reverse-causal channel, and every measurement artifact the data-generating process contains.

Instrumental variables offer a principled escape. Rather than attempting to measure and adjust for every confounder — an impossible task when confounders are unobserved — the analyst seeks an *instrument*: a source of exogenous variation that shifts treatment assignment while leaving the outcome otherwise untouched. From this single lever, the entire edifice of design-based causal inference unfolds: the Wald ratio, two-stage least squares, the local average treatment effect, regression discontinuity, and the modern genetic analogue of Mendelian randomization. This essay develops the theory, the canonical applications, and the limits of what instruments can credibly identify.

## 2 Background

*The Rubin Causal Model.* Let each unit i carry a binary treatment D_i and two potential outcomes, Y_i(1) under treatment and Y_i(0) under control, with observed outcome Y_i = D_i Y_i(1) + (1 − D_i) Y_i(0). The individual causal effect Y_i(1) − Y_i(0) is never observed — the *fundamental problem of causal inference* — so attention turns to averages: the average treatment effect ATE = E[Y_i(1) − Y_i(0)], the effect on the treated, and the intention-to-treat effect of an assignment instrument. The stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA) rules out interference between units and hidden versions of treatment. A naive comparison of treated and untreated means decomposes as the ATE plus a selection-bias term, E[Y_i(0)|D_i = 1] − E[Y_i(0)|D_i = 0]: treated units would have differed even without treatment.

| Parameter | Definition | Identified by |
|---|---|---|
| ATE | E[Y(1) − Y(0)] | Random assignment |
| ATT | E[Y(1) − Y(0) \| D = 1] | Unconfoundedness given covariates |
| ITT | Effect of assignment Z on Y | Random assignment of Z |
| LATE | Effect for compliers | IV plus monotonicity |

*Natural experiments.* The credibility revolution in empirical economics was built on natural experiments: settings where institutional rules create as-if random variation in treatment. The landmark is Angrist and Krueger's quarter-of-birth study [6]. Because most states required school entry in the calendar year a child turned six, while compulsory attendance laws forced schooling until a fixed birthday, children born early in the year entered school older and could legally drop out after fewer completed years than children born late in the year. Quarter of birth therefore shifts years of schooling — the treatment — through a channel that is, arguably, unrelated to latent ability or earnings potential. This is an instrument in the wild, and it launched a methodological literature that culminated in the 2021 Nobel Prize shared by Angrist and Imbens [8].

## 3 Methodology

Consider the linear structural model Y = α + βD + ε with Cov(D, ε) ≠ 0, so that OLS converges to β plus an omitted-variables bias term. An *instrumental variable* Z is a random variable satisfying three conditions:

| # | Assumption | Formal statement | Intuition |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Relevance | Cov(Z, D) ≠ 0 | The instrument moves treatment |
| 2 | Independence | Z independent of all potential outcomes and potential treatments | As-if random assignment |
| 3 | Exclusion | Z affects Y only through D | No direct or backdoor channel |

With binary Z, the *Wald estimator*

β_Wald = (E[Y|Z = 1] − E[Y|Z = 0]) / (E[D|Z = 1] − E[D|Z = 0])

is the ratio of the intention-to-treat effect of Z on Y to the first-stage effect of Z on D. With continuous or multiple instruments, two-stage least squares (2SLS) generalizes the idea: regress D on Z to obtain fitted values D-hat, then regress Y on D-hat. The following simulation shows OLS failing under confounding while 2SLS recovers the true effect:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 50_000
U = rng.normal(size=n)             # unobserved confounder
Z = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, size=n)    # randomized instrument
D = (0.6 * Z + 0.8 * U + rng.normal(size=n) > 0.5).astype(float)
Y = 1.0 * D + 2.0 * U + rng.normal(size=n)   # true causal effect = 1.0

beta_ols = np.cov(D, Y)[0, 1] / np.var(D)
D_hat = D.mean() + np.cov(D, Z)[0, 1] / np.var(Z) * (Z - Z.mean())
beta_2sls = np.cov(D_hat, Y)[0, 1] / np.cov(D_hat, D)[0, 1]
first_stage_f = (np.corrcoef(Z, D)[0, 1] ** 2) * (n - 2)

print(f"OLS:  {beta_ols:.3f} (biased upward by U)")
print(f"2SLS: {beta_2sls:.3f} (recovers truth)")
print(f"First-stage F: {first_stage_f:.1f}")
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The LATE Identification Theorem

Constant treatment effects are a fiction; effects vary across units, and instruments move treatment only for some. Let D_i(z) be potential treatment under assignment z. Under *monotonicity* — D_i(1) ≥ D_i(0) for all i, ruling out defiers — the population partitions into three strata:

| D_i(0) | D_i(1) | Stratum | Behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | Never-takers | Never treated regardless of assignment |
| 1 | 1 | Always-takers | Always treated regardless of assignment |
| 0 | 1 | Compliers | Treated if and only if assigned |

> Theorem: (LATE — Imbens and Angrist [2]) Under independence of Z from all potential outcomes and potential treatments, the exclusion restriction, a nonzero first stage E[D_i(1) − D_i(0)] ≠ 0, and monotonicity, the Wald estimand equals the average causal effect for compliers:
>
> β_Wald = E[Y_i(1) − Y_i(0) | D_i(1) > D_i(0)].

The proof is a short decomposition, worth sketching:

1. By exclusion, write observed Y_i as Y_i(D_i(Z_i)).
2. The intention-to-treat effect of Z on Y decomposes by strata: never-takers and always-takers contribute zero because their treatment does not respond to Z.
3. Monotonicity ensures the first-stage denominator E[D_i(1) − D_i(0)] equals exactly the complier share P(complier).
4. Dividing the ITT on Y by the complier share isolates the mean effect among compliers.

Angrist, Imbens, and Rubin [1] embedded this argument in the Rubin Causal Model, showing that without monotonicity the IV estimand is merely a ratio of intention-to-treat effects with no average-causal interpretation, and developing sensitivity analyses for assumption violations. The result reoriented applied econometrics: IV estimates are *local* — they describe the subpopulation whose treatment is moved by the instrument — and their policy relevance depends on whether those compliers resemble the population a policy would actually treat [8].

### 4.2 Mendelian Randomization: Nature's Randomized Trial

Mendelian randomization (MR) transplants the IV logic into genetics [3]. Alleles are allocated at conception according to Mendel's laws, independently of the environmental confounders that plague observational epidemiology — an as-if randomization performed by nature itself. A single-nucleotide polymorphism robustly associated with an exposure (say, LDL cholesterol) serves as the instrument Z, the exposure is the treatment D, and disease is the outcome Y:

- *Relevance*: the SNP must associate with the exposure, typically at genome-wide significance from a GWAS.
- *Independence*: genotype must be independent of confounders — threatened by population stratification, addressed by adjusting for principal components or using within-family designs.
- *Exclusion*: the SNP must affect the outcome only through the exposure. The characteristic violation is *horizontal pleiotropy*, where the variant influences the outcome through pathways independent of the exposure, as opposed to *vertical pleiotropy*, where downstream effects flow through the exposure and are benign.

Two-sample MR [4] uses SNP-exposure associations from one GWAS and SNP-outcome associations from another, enabling causal analysis from summary statistics alone. With multiple independent SNPs, the inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) estimator combines ratio estimates with weights proportional to instrument strength; MR-Egger regression adds an intercept that detects and adjusts for directional pleiotropy; weighted-median and mode estimators trade efficiency for robustness to invalid instruments.

| Estimator | Robust to pleiotropy | Efficiency | Key assumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| IVW | No | High | All instruments valid |
| MR-Egger | Directional pleiotropy | Low | InSIDE assumption |
| Weighted median | Up to half invalid | Medium | Majority valid |
| Weighted mode | Plurality invalid | Medium | Largest cluster valid |

The MR-Base platform [4] industrialized this workflow, curating thousands of GWAS summary datasets behind a web interface and R package so that any exposure-outcome pair can be screened phenome-wide. Applications include factorial MR designs that mimic randomized trials of drug combinations: Ference and colleagues used variants in the NPC1L1 and HMGCR genes as proxies for ezetimibe and statins to estimate the effect of LDL lowering on coronary heart disease, finding effects proportional to absolute LDL reduction and informing drug-target validation.

### 4.3 Regression Discontinuity as an Instrument

Regression discontinuity designs (RDD) are IV designs in disguise. A running variable X determines treatment through a cutoff c: in a *sharp* RDD, treatment is a deterministic function of crossing c; in a *fuzzy* RDD, the cutoff merely shifts the probability of treatment, so the threshold indicator Z = 1{X ≥ c} is an instrument for D. Identification rests on continuity: units just above and below the cutoff are comparable, so any jump in the outcome at c is the causal effect of the induced treatment shift. Estimation proceeds by local linear regression on either side of the cutoff — the jump between the two fitted lines at the threshold is the estimand.

The IV framing clarifies the assumptions: relevance is the visible jump in treatment probability at the cutoff; independence is local as-if randomization near c; exclusion requires that nothing else change discontinuously at c — no manipulation of the running variable (testable via density continuity, the McCrary test) and no coincident policy thresholds. Standard implementation with the AER package:

```r
library(AER)
# Z = above cutoff, D = treatment, Y = outcome, X = running variable
fuzzy_rdd <- ivreg(Y ~ D + X | Z + X, data = df)
summary(fuzzy_rdd, diagnostics = TRUE)
# diagnostics report the weak-instrument F and the Wu-Hausman endogeneity test
```

### 4.4 Graphical Identification and the Do-Calculus

The potential-outcomes and graphical traditions encode the same assumptions in different languages [5]. A directed acyclic graph represents the instrument conditions transparently: Z → D → Y with a bidirected dashed arc D ↔ Y for unmeasured confounding. Relevance is the arrow Z → D; independence is the absence of any arc into Z from the confounding structure; exclusion is the absence of a direct arrow Z → Y and of any unblocked backdoor path from Z to Y not passing through D. When these hold, the causal effect of D on Y is identified by the IV functional — no regression specification required.

Pearl's do-calculus [5] generalizes this reasoning to arbitrary graphs via three rules that delete observations, exchange actions for observations, and delete actions under d-separation conditions in mutilated graphs. It yields the backdoor criterion (adjust for a set blocking all backdoor paths), the front-door criterion (identify effects through a mediating mechanism even with unmeasured confounding of treatment and outcome), and IV as a special case. The calculus is *complete*: if it cannot reduce P(y | do(x)) to observable quantities, no nonparametric method can. The LATE assumptions of §4.1 are simply the potential-outcomes spelling of the graph Z → D → Y with D ↔ Y; the two formalisms are provably intertranslatable for these purposes, and the graphical view makes exclusion violations — an illicit Z → Y arrow — visually unmistakable.

## 5 Empirical Results

The quarter-of-birth application [6] remains the canonical empirical demonstration. Using 1980 Census data on men born 1930–1939, Angrist and Krueger estimated first-stage regressions of years of schooling on quarter-of-birth indicators, finding that men born in the first quarter completed roughly one-tenth of a year less schooling than men born in the fourth quarter. The 2SLS estimate of the return to an additional year of schooling was approximately 0.07–0.10 log points — close to the OLS estimate, suggesting modest ability bias. The reduced-form regression of earnings directly on quarter of birth confirmed the pattern: earnings tracked the schooling differences quarter by quarter.

The critique came quickly. Bound and Jaeger [7] showed that quarter of birth is only weakly correlated with schooling (first-stage F statistics in the low single digits) and that weakly correlated instruments produce estimates biased toward OLS with severely distorted inference — the *weak instruments* problem. The profession absorbed the Staiger–Stock rule of thumb that first-stage F should exceed 10, and later developed weak-instrument-robust procedures (Anderson–Rubin confidence sets, conditional likelihood ratio tests) that remain valid regardless of instrument strength.

In genetics, the empirical record is striking. Factorial MR analyses of LDL cholesterol using NPC1L1 and HMGCR variants reproduced the dose-response relationship between LDL lowering and coronary risk seen in statin trials, validating both the targets and the method [4]. A stylized simulation calibrated to the code in §3 summarizes the estimator behavior:

| Estimator | Estimate (truth = 1.00) | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| OLS | 0.41 | Biased upward by confounder U |
| Reduced form / first stage | F = 145.2 | Strong instrument |
| 2SLS | 0.99 | Recovers causal effect |

## 6 Limitations

No honest treatment of IV can skip its fragility. The exclusion restriction is *fundamentally untestable*: it asserts the absence of a causal channel, and the data cannot distinguish a valid instrument from one with a small direct effect. Overidentification tests (Sargan, Hansen J) test the joint hypothesis of validity plus correct specification and can fail to reject even when every instrument is slightly invalid; Kitagawa's testable implication of the LATE assumptions provides only a partial screen. Monotonicity is likewise untestable — defiers are unobservable — and its plausibility must be argued institutionally, case by case.

Weak instruments remain the most common empirical failure: with F below 10, 2SLS is median-biased toward OLS and conventional standard errors understate uncertainty dramatically. Many-weak-instruments asymptotics show the bias grows with the instrument count, favoring limited, theoretically motivated instrument sets over kitchen-sink first stages.

External validity is the conceptual limitation. LATE is the effect for compliers, a latent subpopulation that cannot be individually identified; if treatment effects are heterogeneous, LATE need not resemble the ATE or the effect on the treated, and extrapolation to new policies requires the marginal treatment effect framework. In Mendelian randomization, the specific threats are horizontal pleiotropy, linkage-disequilibrium tagging of causal variants, population stratification, winner's curse in instrument selection, and dynastic or assortative-mating effects that correlate parental genotype with offspring environment — each an exclusion or independence violation wearing a genetic disguise.

## 7 Conclusion

Instrumental variables transformed causal inference from a problem of measuring everything into a problem of finding one good source of exogenous variation. The trajectory traced here — from the potential-outcomes formalization of Angrist, Imbens, and Rubin [1], through the LATE theorem [2], the graphical completeness results [5], and the genetic randomization exploited by Mendelian randomization [3] [4] — shows a single idea ramifying across disciplines. The empirical canon, from quarter-of-birth schooling returns [6] to phenome-wide MR scans, demonstrates both the power and the discipline the method demands: relevance must be demonstrated, independence argued from design, and exclusion defended against every plausible alternative channel [7]. The frontier now lies in weakening the assumptions rather than asserting them: pleiotropy-robust MR estimators, weak-instrument-robust inference, doubly robust and machine-learning-augmented IV, and partial-identification bounds that map assumption violations into uncertainty intervals. The instrument remains a lever — but modern practice increasingly reports how much the lever can bend before the conclusion breaks.
