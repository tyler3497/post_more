## Abstract

The actor model reduces concurrent computation to a single primitive — isolated state mutated only in response to asynchronously received messages — and thereby converts fault tolerance from *defensive programming* into *architectural topology*. This thesis traces that idea from Hewitt's 1973 actor formalism [1] and Agha's 1986 formalization [2], through Armstrong's PhD thesis (KTH, 2003), which codified "let it crash" and the OTP supervision tree for nine-nines telecom infrastructure [3], to Akka's exception-matched supervisor strategies [4]. We formalize supervision trees as nested *failure domains* whose restart strategies (one-for-one, one-for-all, rest-for-one) encode sibling state-coupling, and prove that restart-intensity circuit breakers bound fault blast radius and guarantee terminating escalation in finite trees. We contrast OTP's declarative child specs and trap-exit signaling with Akka's imperative deciders [5][6], quantify isolation overhead using Erlang scalability studies [7], and delimit the paradigm: supervision masters *transient* faults and fails on deterministic ones, presupposing validated inputs at trust boundaries and transactional external resources. We argue supervision is a *semantic* property of the computation model: the right to fail must be designed into the topology before business logic.

# Supervision as Semantics: The Actor Model, "Let It Crash", and the Engineering of Fault-Tolerant Distributed Systems from Erlang/OTP to Akka

## Abstract

The actor model reduces concurrent computation to a single primitive — isolated state mutated only in response to asynchronously received messages — and thereby converts fault tolerance from *defensive programming* into *architectural topology*. This thesis traces that idea from Hewitt's 1973 actor formalism [1] and Agha's 1986 formalization [2], through Armstrong's PhD thesis (KTH, 2003), which codified "let it crash" and the OTP supervision tree for nine-nines telecom infrastructure [3], to Akka's exception-matched supervisor strategies [4]. We formalize supervision trees as nested *failure domains* whose restart strategies (one-for-one, one-for-all, rest-for-one) encode sibling state-coupling, and prove that restart-intensity circuit breakers bound fault blast radius and guarantee terminating escalation in finite trees. We contrast OTP's declarative child specs and trap-exit signaling with Akka's imperative deciders [5][6], quantify isolation overhead using Erlang scalability studies [7], and delimit the paradigm: supervision masters *transient* faults and fails on deterministic ones, presupposing validated inputs at trust boundaries and transactional external resources. We argue supervision is a *semantic* property of the computation model: the right to fail must be designed into the topology before business logic.

## 1. Introduction

Every serious concurrent system eventually confronts the same humiliating statistic: software defects are the dominant cause of outages, and defects concentrate precisely in the error-handling code that is least exercised by tests [3]. Defensive programming — try/catch blocks, guard clauses, null checks layered at every layer — does not remove faults; it relocates them into rarely-executed code paths whose correctness cannot be established by observation. Joe Armstrong's radical conclusion, reached over two decades building telephone switches at Ericsson, was to invert the responsibility entirely: *let the failing process die, and let some other process — a supervisor — perform the recovery* [3].

That inversion is only sound under a concurrency model that guarantees **fault isolation**: a crashing component must not corrupt the state of its neighbors. Shared-memory threads fail this requirement — a thread that crashes mid-mutation leaves shared structures uncharacterized, making restart unsafe. The actor model [1][2] provides the isolation: actors own private state, communicate solely through asynchronous messages, and share nothing. The combination — actors for isolation, supervision trees for recovery — underlies systems like Ericsson's AXD301 switches (advertised at 99.9999999% availability) and WhatsApp's millions of concurrent connections per server node.

This thesis examines the actor-plus-supervision architecture as a single, coherent semantic framework. Our contributions are:

1. **A formal treatment of supervision trees** as failure-domain hierarchies with restart strategies as *coupling semantics*, following the OTP specification [5].
2. **A comparative analysis** of Erlang/OTP and Akka supervision mechanics — generic supervisor behavior with `trap_exit` versus per-parent decider functions with directives Resume / Restart / Stop / Escalate [4][6].
3. **Availability results**: a restart-intensity circuit breaker model showing that escalation terminates and blast radius is bounded by tree depth.
4. **An empirical accounting** of isolation overhead (mailbox queuing, process scheduling, supervision bookkeeping) grounded in the Erlang scalability literature [7].
5. **A precise statement of limits**: deterministic faults, external resources, and the "do not program defensively" boundary at trust edges.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The actor model: primitives and guarantees

Hewitt, Bishop, and Steiger (1973) introduced actors as the "universal primitives of concurrent digital computation" [1]. Agha's 1986 monograph gave the canonical formulation: an actor system is a *configuration* of actors, each actor being a triple of a **mailbox** (an unbounded FIFO message queue), a **behavior** (a partial function from messages to actions), and a set of **acquaintances** (addresses it may send messages to) [2]. Upon receiving a message, an actor may only: **send** messages to acquaintances, **create** new actors, or **become** a new behavior for the next message. Two consequences follow that are load-bearing for this thesis:

> **Theorem (Isolation):** Because actors share no memory and interact only through asynchronous message sends, the failure of one actor cannot corrupt the memory state of any other actor. Faults are therefore *confined to the failing actor's own configuration* [2].

> **Theorem (Locality of recovery):** Since an actor's behavior is re-instantiated from its initial definition when recreated, a crashed actor can be replaced by a fresh actor with identical initial behavior, and no other actor's state requires repair.

The model further assumes **fair scheduling** (every message in a mailbox is eventually processed) and **address locality** (an actor may only send to addresses it already knows). Delivery is asynchronous with no ordering guarantees across different senders, which is the source of both the model's scalability and its notorious difficulty for debugging.

| Property | Threads + shared memory | Actors |
|---|---|---|
| State ownership | Shared | Private per actor |
| Interaction | Locks, atomic ops | Asynchronous messages |
| Failure blast radius | Whole address space (potentially) | Single actor |
| Restart safety | Unsafe mid-mutation | Safe from initial behavior |
| Location transparency | None | Natural (address = capability) |

### 2.2 "Let it crash" and the OTP supervision tree

Armstrong's thesis [3] articulates the error-handling philosophy in four slogans (Section 4.3):

1. *Let some other process do the error recovery.*
2. *If you can't do what you want to do, die.*
3. *Let it crash.*
4. *Do not program defensively.*

The reasoning rests on a distinction between *exceptions* (the runtime does not know what to do) and *errors* (the programmer does not know what to do) [3]. If the specification is silent on some case, any code the programmer invents for it is fiction; the correct action is termination with a rich diagnostic, and the *supervisor* — a separate process whose entire job is monitoring — applies the recovery policy.

A supervisor is defined by a *child specification* and two policies:

- **Restart strategy**: `one_for_one` (restart only the crashed child), `one_for_all` (restart all children — coupled states), `rest_for_one` (restart the crashed child and those started after it — startup-ordered dependencies) [5].
- **Restart intensity**: a `{MaxR, MaxT}` circuit breaker — more than `MaxR` restarts within `MaxT` seconds terminates the supervisor itself, *escalating* the fault to its parent [5].

The supervisor links itself to its children and sets `trap_exit`, converting child death signals into messages it can act on [3][8]. Supervisors are themselves supervised, forming a **supervision tree** in which workers are leaves and supervisors are internal nodes [5]. This is the precise mechanism: error *signaling* (a bidirectional link propagating exit reasons) is distinct from error *handling* (the supervisor's strategy).

### 2.3 Akka: supervision as a decider function

Akka transposes the OTP design onto the JVM. In Akka classic, every parent actor is a supervisor of its children; failure information propagates to a **supervisor strategy**, a decider — `PartialFunction[Throwable, Directive]` — mapping exception types to directives: `Resume` (keep actor, keep state, drop the offending message), `Restart` (recreate actor from initial behavior), `Stop` (terminate permanently), `Escalate` (hand the decision to the grandparent) [4]. The default strategy restarts on `Exception`, stops on `ActorInitializationException`/`ActorKilledException`/`DeathPactException`, and escalates anything else.

In Akka Typed, supervision is expressed per-spawn via `Behaviors.supervise(...).onFailure(SupervisorStrategy.restart)` with configurable `maxRestarts` and backoff [4][6]. The philosophical continuity with OTP is exact — *supervision is hierarchical, outside the worker, and crash-driven* — while the mechanics differ: OTP's declarative child specs and trap-exit signaling versus Akka's exception-matched deciders and lifecycle signals.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis proceeds by **formalization and comparative design analysis**, since both runtimes are mature and extensively measured.

- **Formalization (§4.1–§4.2):** Supervision trees as rooted trees of failure domains; restart strategies as coupling relations between siblings; escalation as a bounded counter system with provable termination.
- **Comparative analysis (§4.3):** OTP versus Akka decomposed into four orthogonal mechanisms — *detection*, *signaling*, *decision*, *recovery* — compared mechanism by mechanism against the canonical documentation [4][5][6].
- **Empirical grounding (§5):** Overhead and scalability figures from the Erlang VM literature, notably Trinder et al. [7], plus production reports from telecom and messaging systems.
- **Limit analysis (§6):** The conditions under which supervision degrades, referencing Armstrong's own caveats [3].

The method is deliberately *architectural*: availability is treated as a property of topology and scheduling substrate — measurable in restart budgets and escalation depth — rather than of individual error handlers.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Supervision trees as nested failure domains

Define a supervision tree $T = (V, E, s, b)$ where $V$ is the set of processes, $E$ the parent-child links, $s(v) \in \{\text{one\_for\_one}, \text{one\_for\_all}, \text{rest\_for\_one}\}$ the restart strategy of each supervisor node $v$, and $b(v) = (M_v, T_v)$ its restart budget: at most $M_v$ restarts in any $T_v$-second window. Each leaf is a worker; each internal node is a supervisor that traps exits.

A **failure domain** $D(v)$ is the subtree rooted at $v$. The key insight is that $s(v)$ encodes *state coupling* between siblings:

- `one_for_one` asserts the children are *independent*: a fault in child $c$ carries no information about the consistency of its siblings.
- `one_for_all` asserts *total coupling*: if one child is inconsistent, all siblings' states must be regarded as suspect and rebuilt from initial behaviors.
- `rest_for_one` asserts *startup-ordered coupling*: children started after the crashed one may hold references to its (now invalid) state and must restart.

Mis-specifying the strategy is the classic supervision bug: `one_for_one` over coupled children leaves siblings holding stale references (use-after-restart), while `one_for_all` over independent children amplifies restarts unnecessarily. Strategy selection is a *design* activity, not a configuration tweak.

The restart budget $b(v)$ is a **circuit breaker**. Formally, let $r_1, r_2, \dots$ be restart timestamps of $v$'s subtree. If $\exists\, i$ such that $r_{i+M_v} - r_i < T_v$, then $v$ terminates with the child's exit reason and the fault *escalates* to $\text{parent}(v)$. Escalation continues upward until some ancestor's budget is not exhausted, or the root guardian terminates the application. This guarantees:

> **Theorem (Escalation termination):** For a finite tree with finite budgets, any fault is either absorbed by some ancestor's budget or terminates the root in finite time. Crash loops cannot consume unbounded resources; the system degrades to a *known* terminal state rather than an unknown wedged one.

Supervision never promises to recover from everything — only to never be confused about what happened.

### 4.2 Detection, signaling, decision, recovery

Decomposing any supervision implementation into four mechanisms clarifies where OTP and Akka agree and diverge:

1. **Detection.** OTP uses bidirectional *links* between supervisor and child: when the child terminates abnormally, the runtime delivers an exit signal `{'EXIT', Pid, Reason}` to any linked process with `trap_exit` set [3][5]. Akka classic detects failure when a message handler throws; Akka Typed wraps behaviors in supervision decorators that intercept `onFailure` signals [4][6].

2. **Signaling.** In OTP, the exit *reason* is a first-class value — any Erlang term — so `Reason` can encode structured diagnostics (`{badarith, Stacktrace}`, `{shutdown, ...}`), which the supervisor may pattern-match when deciding. Akka's signal is the thrown `Throwable`, matched by the decider's partial function; unmatched exceptions default to `Escalate` [4].

3. **Decision.** OTP's decision is the child spec + strategy: largely declarative, evaluated per child. Akka's decision is an imperative decider function with access to the supervisor's full state, including the failed child's identity [4]. Akka's four directives are finer-grained than OTP's three strategies: `Resume` has no OTP counterpart, making Akka's decider strictly more expressive for *message-poisoning* scenarios.

4. **Recovery.** OTP restarts the child via its start function with initial arguments; the old mailbox is discarded — *at-most-once* semantics per restart. Akka's `Restart` re-instantiates the actor but, in classic Akka, preserves the mailbox; the *new* behavior must tolerate re-delivered stale messages [4].

These four mechanisms are jointly sufficient for the isolation-plus-recovery pattern, and each runtime's deviations are design choices about *where policy lives* (declarative spec vs. imperative decider), not about the philosophy.

### 4.3 Location transparency and the distributed tree

A supervision tree need not live on one node. Erlang gives every process a globally addressable pid, and supervisors can supervise remote children [5]; Akka Cluster extends supervision via cluster sharding, where shard coordinators act as meta-supervisors rebalancing entity actors [4]. The subtlety: *failure detection* becomes *network failure detection*, and the two are locally indistinguishable. OTP uses node monitors and `net_ticktime` heartbeats; Akka uses Phi-accrual failure detectors. A supervisor that restarts a child it cannot distinguish from a partitioned-but-alive child risks **split-brain duplication** — both ecosystems therefore fence distributed supervision with quorum or leases (e.g., Akka's Split Brain Resolver `keep-majority`), trading availability for consistency exactly at the CAP boundary.

### 4.4 Supervision versus the alternatives

Three competing architectures illuminate supervision by contrast:

- **Defensive in-process handling (try/catch):** Handles faults *where they occur*, requiring every component to be its own recovery expert. The recovery code is the least-tested code (Armstrong's central claim [3]); the blast radius is bounded only by programmer discipline.
- **Retry with backoff (at the caller):** Handles *transient* faults but cannot repair *corrupted callee state*; unbounded retries amplify load during outages (retry storms). Supervision subsumes caller-side retry for callee crashes while adding state repair.
- **Checkpoint/restore:** Preserves more progress than restart-from-initial, but checkpoints are consistency hazards (coordinated checkpointing is expensive; uncoordinated risks domino rollback). Erlang's *intentional* choice to restart from initial behavior is a deliberate simplification: recovery becomes *stateless and therefore composable* [3].

The actor model makes restart-from-initial viable precisely because *per-actor state is small and private*; a thread-based server cannot restart a request handler without losing the shared session state it was mutating. Isolation is what makes the cheap recovery strategy correct.

### 4.5 The mailbox, backpressure, and overload

Supervision recovers crashed actors, but the dominant production failure of actor systems is not crashes — it is **mailbox overflow**: a healthy actor whose arrival rate $\lambda$ exceeds its service rate $\mu$ grows an unbounded queue, exhausting memory. OTP and Akka both treat this as an *overload* problem orthogonal to supervision:

- Erlang/OTP provides `process_info(Pid, message_queue_len)` introspection, selective receive (pattern-matching the mailbox head with priority clauses), and since OTP 21, mailbox-size controls; the standard remedy is a *load-shedding* or *backpressure* layer above the actor [5][7].
- Akka offers bounded mailboxes with configurable `mailbox-push-timeout`, and Akka Streams' reactive-streams backpressure as the principled answer: push-based actor messaging is replaced by pull-based demand signaling at stream boundaries [4].

> **Theorem (Overload is not a supervision fault):** Restarting an overloaded actor does not change $\lambda/\mu$; if $\lambda > \mu$ persists, the restarted actor's mailbox re-grows. Supervision handles *faults*; overload requires *flow control*. Conflating the two is the most common operational error in actor deployments.

Mature actor architectures therefore layer three mechanisms: *supervision* for faults, *backpressure* for overload, and *circuit breakers* at the caller for dependency failure.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Properties

### 5.1 The availability arithmetic

Consider a worker with failure rate $\lambda_f$ (crashes per second) and mean recovery time $R$ (supervisor detection + restart). Under supervision with unlimited budget, steady-state availability is:

$$A = \frac{\text{MTTF}}{\text{MTTF} + \text{MTTR}} = \frac{1/\lambda_f}{1/\lambda_f + R}$$

Supervision's leverage is that $R$ is *milliseconds* (process spawn on BEAM; actor re-instantiation on JVM) while $\lambda_f$ for transient faults — network blips, caught malformed inputs, resource exhaustion that GC resolves — is low. Armstrong's AXD301 experience: faults that would have been outages under defensive handling became sub-second restarts invisible to callers [3].

The circuit breaker modifies this: with budget $(M, T)$, a *deterministic* fault exhausts the budget in $M \cdot R$ seconds and escalates. Total downtime for a deterministic fault is bounded by $\sum_{\text{levels}} M_v R_v$ — linear in tree depth. **Bounded blast radius is a theorem of the budget design, not an empirical hope.**

### 5.2 Isolation overhead

Trinder et al. [7] benchmarked Erlang at scales up to hundreds of thousands of processes, finding per-process footprints of a few hundred bytes and microsecond spawn times on BEAM — the substrate that makes "one process per connection" architectures (WhatsApp's ~2M connections/node) economical. Mailbox queuing adds one queue hop per message versus a function call; the dominant costs are *scheduler* wakeups and *copying* between process heaps (Erlang copies messages; Akka on a single JVM passes references, gaining speed but weakening isolation — a leaked reference reintroduces shared memory).

Supervision bookkeeping — link tables, trap-exit dispatch, restart counters — is $O(1)$ per crash and $O(\text{children})$ per supervisor at startup [5]. The measurable overhead of supervision is therefore *not* per-message; it is the *design* overhead of decomposing work into small restartable units and the strategy-per-supervisor design review, which is skilled labor.

### 5.3 Production evidence

- **Ericsson AXD301** (Erlang/OTP): multi-year field data shows software-error-driven restarts handling faults at rates that would have required manual intervention under defensive designs; advertised availability nine nines [3].
- **WhatsApp** (Erlang): each connection mapped to a supervised process — the architecture only works because per-process overhead is negligible [7].
- **Akka-based trading and IoT platforms**: supervision deciders isolate poisonous messages (`Resume` on deserialization errors, `Restart` on state corruption, `Escalate` on infrastructure faults) [4].

The pattern across all three: *transient faults dominate*, and supervision converts them from incidents into metrics.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Deterministic faults.** A bug that crashes on every input (poison message, corrupt state read at init) defeats any restart policy; the circuit breaker correctly escalates, but recovery then requires human intervention or a deploy. Supervision is a *transient-fault* machine: it contains bugs, it does not fix them [3].

2. **External resources.** Supervision restarts *processes*, not databases or third-party APIs. If the resource itself is the fault (locked row, exhausted pool), restart loops until the budget trips. Transactional outbox patterns and idempotent handlers are required companions, not included in the model.

3. **State loss.** Restart-from-initial discards in-memory state by design. Continuity requires persistence (ETS/Mnesia snapshots, event sourcing) and replay — reintroducing the consistency questions supervision was meant to dodge. There is no free lunch; there is only a well-labeled bill.

4. **Strategy mis-specification.** As §4.1 showed, the restart strategy encodes a coupling claim about sibling state. Wrong claims produce use-after-restart or restart amplification. No runtime checks the claim; it is verified only by design review and chaos testing.

5. **Trust boundaries.** "Do not program defensively" applies *inside* the supervision tree, where crashes are cheap and observable. At the boundary — untrusted network input, external data — validation is mandatory; letting a hostile input crash a worker is fine, but letting it crash a supervisor's entire subtree via `one_for_all` amplification is a denial-of-service vector [3].

6. **Observability.** A system that heals itself can also *hide* its wounds. Without alerting on restart *rates* (not just terminal failures), supervision becomes a bug-concealment device. The circuit breaker budget $(M, T)$ is simultaneously a recovery parameter and the most important SLI in the system.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The actor model and the supervision tree are not two ideas but one: *isolation makes failure cheap, and cheap failure makes recovery an architectural property rather than a coding exercise*. Hewitt gave the model [1], Agha the formalism [2], Armstrong the engineering discipline and the empirical proof at telecom scale [3], and Akka the JVM-idiomatic re-expression [4]. The formal core is small — failure domains, coupling-encoded restart strategies, finite restart budgets with escalation — and its consequences are large: bounded blast radius, terminating escalation, and availability arithmetic dominated by millisecond recovery times.

The honest boundary is equally small: transient faults, not deterministic bugs; processes, not external resources; isolation, not overload. Within it, no competing architecture has demonstrated comparable availability at comparable complexity. The design lesson is a sequencing constraint: *design the failure topology before the business logic*, because the right to fail — isolated, observed, budgeted, and escalated — is the precondition for everything else the system promises.
