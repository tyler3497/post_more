# Solid-State Batteries: Lithium Dendrite Nucleation, Solid Electrolyte Interphases, and Operando Tomography of Ceramic Electrolytes

## Abstract

Solid-state batteries (SSBs) promise a step-change in energy density and safety by replacing flammable liquid electrolytes with ceramic or sulfide conductors and enabling lithium-metal anodes. Yet the field's founding promise — that a stiff ceramic would mechanically *block* lithium dendrites — has proven false in practice: dendrites penetrate even garnets with shear moduli above 50 GPa. This thesis develops a unified mechanistic account of lithium dendrite nucleation and growth in solid electrolytes, integrating fracture mechanics at grain boundaries and pre-existing flaws, the electrochemical stability and passivating behavior of solid electrolyte interphases (SEIs) in oxide versus sulfide systems, and direct evidence from operando synchrotron X-ray tomography of void formation during stripping. We derive the Monroe–Newman stability criterion and show precisely why it fails in polycrystals, formalize the Griffith-type crack-driving pressure of a plating filament, and analyze how stack pressure rescues interfacial contact up to a critical stripping current. The work closes with a quantitative assessment of commercialization barriers through the engineering strategies of QuantumScape, Toyota, and Samsung SDI.

## 1 Introduction

The transition from graphite to lithium metal as the anode is the single largest lever available for raising cell-level specific energy. Pairing lithium metal (3,860 mAh g⁻¹, −3.04 V vs. SHE) with a high-voltage cathode and a non-flammable solid electrolyte defines the technological target of the solid-state battery: specific energies above 400 Wh kg⁻¹ with intrinsic safety against thermal runaway [1][2]. This vision rests on a deceptively simple syllogism — ceramics are hard, lithium is soft, therefore dendrites cannot penetrate — which the experimental record has steadily dismantled.

> **Theorem 1.1 (The ceramic paradox):** *No polycrystalline solid electrolyte investigated to date suppresses lithium dendrite penetration by bulk stiffness alone at current densities relevant to electric-vehicle fast charging, despite shear moduli exceeding the Monroe–Newman threshold by an order of magnitude.*

This thesis is organized around resolving that paradox. Section 2 surveys the three dominant electrolyte families — oxide garnets, sulfide argyrodites, and polymer/ceramic composites — and the interphases they form against lithium. Section 3 develops the coupled electrochemical–mechanical formalism. Section 4 dives into four pillars: (4.1) dendrite nucleation at voids, grain boundaries, and surface flaws; (4.2) interphase chemistry in oxides versus sulfides; (4.3) operando X-ray tomography of voiding and current constriction; and (4.4) stack pressure and critical stripping currents. Section 5 gives quantitative results and formal stability proofs, Section 6 confronts limitations, and Section 7 assesses commercialization through the programs of QuantumScape, Toyota, and Samsung SDI.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Electrolyte families

Three chemistries dominate solid-electrolyte research, each occupying a different corner of the conductivity–stability–processability triangle:

| Family | Example | σ_ion (25 °C) | Shear modulus | Electrochemical stability vs. Li | Processability |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oxide garnet | Li₇La₃Zr₂O₁₂ (LLZO) | 10⁻⁴–10⁻³ S cm⁻¹ | ≈ 55–60 GPa | Thermodynamically stable | Brittle; needs >1,000 °C sintering |
| Sulfide argyrodite | Li₆PS₅Cl | 10⁻³–3×10⁻³ S cm⁻¹ | ≈ 7–10 GPa | Reduces; forms passivating SEI | Soft; cold-pressable |
| Sulfide LGPS-type | Li₁₀GeP₂S₁₂ | up to 1.2×10⁻² S cm⁻¹ | ≈ 15–18 GPa | Reduces below ≈1.7 V; non-passivating | Moisture-sensitive |
| Polymer composite | PEO–LiTFSI + ceramic filler | 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁴ S cm⁻¹ | < 0.1 GPa | Stable with Li | Easy; roll-to-roll |

*Table 1: Representative properties of the three electrolyte families. Conductivity values are room-temperature bulk values for dense pellets [3][4].*

Garnet-type LLZO is the archetypal oxide: cubic-phase stabilization (typically Al or Ga doping) yields 3D lithium percolation, negligible electronic conductivity, and rare thermodynamic stability against lithium metal [1]. Sulfide argyrodites trade stability for conductivity and ductility: they cold-press into dense compacts, but reduce at the lithium interface into products whose passivating quality decides the cell's fate [4][5].

### 2.2 The dendrite problem, restated

In liquid cells, dendrites follow Sand's-time: under diffusion limitation the interfacial salt concentration hits zero at *t_Sand*, after which deposition turns dendritic [6]. Solid electrolytes have no solvent and no classical concentration polarization, yet dendrites still appear — as *metallic filaments growing through ceramic*. Operando microscopy has observed needle and mossy lithium nucleating at LLZO defects at tens of μA cm⁻² [2], and symmetric Li|LLZO|Li cells short near **0.1 mA cm⁻² at 25 °C** [1]. The failure mode is not plastic flow of lithium through ceramic — it is *fracture*.

### 2.3 Interphases: the chemistry that decides everything

A "stable" SSB interface is almost never bare: it is a **solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)** — a nanometer-to-micron reaction layer whose ionic conductivity, electronic conductivity, and mechanical integrity gate every cycle. Two limiting cases:

1. **Self-passivating interphases** (e.g., Li₆PS₅Cl against Li → Li₂S + Li₃P + LiCl) grow to self-limiting thickness because the products are electronically insulating [4].
2. **Non-passivating interphases** (e.g., LGPS, whose products include mixed-conducting phases) grow continuously, raising impedance without bound [4].

LLZO is a third case: intrinsically stable, but its grain boundaries and surfaces are routinely contaminated by Li₂CO₃ from air exposure — ionically resistive, electronically leaky, and a preferred filament highway [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Dendrite nucleation: voids, grain boundaries, and the Griffith mechanism

The pivotal reframing of the last decade: lithium does not *push through* the ceramic lattice — it *wedges open* pre-existing flaws. Porz and co-workers showed lithium plating inside surface defects, pores, and grain-boundary grooves, the filament acting as a hydraulic wedge: plating overpotential converts to mechanical pressure at the flaw tip, driving a Griffith crack through the brittle electrolyte [7]. Atomistic simulations of polycrystalline LLZO confirm the fracture mode explicitly: when a filament approaches a grain boundary parallel to it (incidence angle θ = 0°), symmetric crack opening — **Mode I fracture** — propagates along the boundary, matching cross-sectional SEM of shorted cells [1]. At oblique incidence, mixed-mode fracture with a Mode II shear component appears, and the grain-boundary fracture toughness *K_Ic^GB* is only ≈ 0.33 × *K_Ic* of the bulk — grain boundaries are intrinsically the weakest path [1].

Nucleation follows a hierarchy of preferred sites:

1. **Surface flaws and scratches.** Microscopic nicks provide the toehold for penetration; surface smoothness can matter more than bulk modulus [6].
2. **Grain boundaries.** Operando KPFM shows the Galvani potential dropping preferentially at grain boundaries near the lithium electrode during deposition — concentrating the driving force exactly where fracture toughness is lowest [5].
3. **Voids at the interface.** Any loss of contact — from stripping (see §4.3) or from poor initial wetting — constricts current into the remaining contact points, locally multiplying the current density and seeding filaments.

> **Theorem 4.1 (Griffith nucleation criterion):** *A lithium filament nucleating in a surface flaw of half-length a will propagate unstably when the plating-induced pressure p satisfies p ≥ √(2Eγ / πa), where E is the electrolyte Young's modulus and γ the effective fracture surface energy. Reducing flaw size a by surface finishing raises the nucleation overpotential threshold as a^(−1/2).*

The practical consequence is that **defect engineering dominates material selection**: an ultrathin amorphous Li–La–Zr–O coating that seals surface flaws suppresses filament nucleation and sustains stable polarization up to 3.2 mA cm⁻² in in-plane plating tests, whereas the uncoated pellet soft-shorts from the lowest current density upward [8].

```python
# Griffith nucleation threshold vs flaw size for LLZO (E ~ 150 GPa, gamma ~ 1 J/m^2)
import numpy as np

E, gamma = 150e9, 1.0            # Pa, J/m^2
flaws = np.array([0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0, 10.0]) * 1e-6   # flaw half-length, m
p_crit = np.sqrt(2 * E * gamma / (np.pi * flaws)) / 1e6  # MPa

for a, p in zip(flaws * 1e6, p_crit):
    eta_mV = p * 1e6 * 13e-6 / 96485 * 1e3   # p*V_m/F, V_m(Li)=13 cm^3/mol
    print(f"flaw {a:5.1f} um -> p_crit {p:6.1f} MPa  (~{eta_mV:5.1f} mV overpotential)")
```

The conversion *p = ηF/V̄_m* ties the mechanical threshold to an electrochemical one: a 1 μm flaw in LLZO requires on the order of **hundreds of MPa** — tens of millivolts of plating overpotential — to wedge open, while a 10 μm flaw fails at a few millivolts. Polishing, thin-film coatings, and isostatic pressing are therefore first-order determinants of CCD, not cosmetic steps.

### 4.2 Solid electrolyte interphases: oxides versus sulfides

The SEI in an SSB is not the familiar carbonate mosaic of liquid cells; it is a solid-state decomposition front whose electronic conductivity decides whether the interface self-limits or runs away.

**Oxide garnets — stability with a contaminated skin.** Pristine LLZO is stable against lithium, but air exposure deposits Li₂CO₃/LiOH layers that are ionically blocking and provide heterogeneous nucleation sites. The amorphous LLZO interlayer [8] works by replacing this contamination with a dense, electronically insulating glass that homogenizes current and seals flaws.

**Sulfide argyrodites — passivation by decomposition.** Li₆PS₅Cl reduces at low potential into Li₂S, Li₃P, and LiCl. Because these products are electronic insulators, the decomposition front self-terminates at nanometer thickness — a genuine passivating SEI [4]. Argyrodite cells thus cycle stably at a few MPa with CCDs above 1 mA cm⁻².

**LGPS-type sulfides — the non-passivating extreme.** Li₁₀GeP₂S₁₂ decomposes below ≈1.7 V vs. Li/Li⁺ into phases including Li–Ge alloys that conduct electrons, so the interphase never self-limits: it thickens continuously, consuming electrolyte and isolating the interface [4]. Record ionic conductivity (12 mS cm⁻¹) paired with catastrophic instability — bulk conductivity alone never predicts cell behavior.

```haskell
-- Interphase passivation as a fixed-point problem (Haskell)
data SEI = SEI { thickness :: Double, elecCond :: Double, ionCond :: Double }

-- decomposition rate decays only if products are electronically insulating
grow :: Double -> SEI -> SEI
grow dt s@(SEI x sigmaE sigmaI)
  | sigmaE < 1e-10 = s                                   -- passivated: fixed point
  | otherwise      = SEI (x + rate * dt) sigmaE sigmaI
  where rate = sigmaE * 1e6                              -- electron leakage drives growth

passivates :: SEI -> Bool
passivates = (< 1e-10) . elecCond
-- Li6PS5Cl products: passivates == True ; LGPS products: passivates == False
```

> **Theorem 4.2 (Passivation fixed point):** *An interphase self-limits in thickness if and only if its decomposition products are electronic insulators (σ_e → 0 as x grows); any finite residual electronic conductivity implies unbounded interphase growth and eventual cell death by impedance, regardless of bulk ionic conductivity.*

### 4.3 Operando X-ray tomography: watching voids form

The most decisive evidence for the modern picture comes from **operando synchrotron X-ray computed microtomography**. In the landmark Georgia Tech / Argonne study, symmetric Li|Li₁₀SnP₂S₁₂|Li cells were cycled inside the beamline while 3D reconstructions resolved the lithium/electrolyte interface at micron resolution [3]. The findings rewrote the failure narrative:

- **Voids form during stripping, not plating.** When lithium is stripped faster than creep and diffusion can replenish it, contact is lost in patches — quantified directly by tomography.
- **Voiding drives current constriction.** Remaining contact points carry the full applied current, so *local* current density during the subsequent plating half-cycle far exceeds the nominal value — and it is this constricted current that nucleates dendrites. Failure is a stripping-induced, plating-executed process.
- **The interphase is redox-active**, evolving with state of charge, and global volume changes from partial molar volume mismatches add mechanical load [3].

Complementary work on silicon–sulfide anodes showed the same chemomechanical logic in reverse: delithiation opens shell-like voids around Si particles, but residual electrolyte bridges maintain partial ionic contact — the interface degrades *gracefully* when some compliance exists [9].

The tomography results identify the true control variable: not the plating current alone, but the **stripping current relative to the lithium replenishment rate** — which is set by stack pressure, temperature, and interfacial adhesion.

### 4.4 Stack pressure and the critical stripping current

Lithium metal creeps. Under an applied stack pressure *P*, lithium flows plastically into interfacial voids at a rate set by its creep law, competing against the stripping flux *i/F* that opens them. Kasemchainan and colleagues formalized this as a **critical stripping current** *i\**: below *i\**, creep replenishes contact as fast as stripping removes it; above *i\**, voids accumulate cycle by cycle, contact constricts, and the subsequent plating step dendrites [10].

```rust
// Critical stripping current vs stack pressure (illustrative creep model)
fn critical_stripping_current(pressure_mpa: f64) -> f64 {
    // Kasemchainan et al.: ~0.2 mA/cm^2 at 3 MPa -> ~1.0 mA/cm^2 at 7 MPa
    let (p0, i0, n) = (3.0_f64, 0.2_f64, 1.6_f64); // power-law creep exponent
    i0 * (pressure_mpa / p0).powf(n)
}

fn main() {
    for p in [1.0, 3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 10.0] {
        let i = critical_stripping_current(p);
        let verdict = if i > 1.0 { "EV fast-charge capable" } else { "voiding regime" };
        println!("P = {p:4.1} MPa -> i* = {i:.2} mA/cm^2  [{verdict}]");
    }
}
```

The measured scaling is steep: raising stack pressure from **3 MPa to 7 MPa lifts the critical stripping current from ≈0.2 to ≈1.0 mA cm⁻²** [10]. But pressure is double-edged:

| Pressure regime | Benefit | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| < 1 MPa | — | Immediate voiding; CCD collapses |
| 3–7 MPa | Creep-assisted contact healing; i\* rises steeply | Requires rigid pack engineering |
| > 10 MPa | Maximal contact | Dendrite *extrusion* through cracks; sulfide pellets fracture; pack mass penalty |

*Table 2: Stack pressure trade-offs. Excessive pressure drives lithium into cracks mechanically and fractures brittle pellets [10][1].*

Note the asymmetry: soft sulfides (G ≈ 7–10 GPa) maintain contact at modest pressures, while brittle LLZO (G ≈ 55 GPa) cannot comply — its interface depends on near-perfect initial contact and surface finishing. This is the deep reason sulfide programs (Toyota, Samsung SDI) and ceramic-separator programs (QuantumScape) diverged so sharply in cell architecture.

```tla
---- MODULE StrippingCycle ----
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES contact, voids, cycle
Init == contact = 100 /\ voids = 0 /\ cycle = 0
Strip(i, iStar) ==
    IF i > iStar
    THEN voids' = voids + (i - iStar) /\ contact' = contact - (i - iStar)
    ELSE voids' = 0 /\ contact' = 100   \* creep heals interface
Plate(iLocal, iCrit) ==
    IF iLocal > iCrit THEN voids' = voids  \* dendrite nucleates at constriction
    ELSE UNCHANGED voids
Next == \E i \in 1..10 : Strip(i, 3) /\ Plate(i * 100 \div contact, 5) /\ cycle' = cycle + 1
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<contact, voids, cycle>>
====
```

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 The Monroe–Newman criterion, derived and bounded

Monroe and Newman (2005) performed a linear-stability analysis of a perturbed lithium/electrolyte interface under combined electrochemical and elastic forces [11]. For a sinusoidal perturbation of wavenumber *k*, the growth rate *s(k)* is

*s(k) ∝ −γk² + (electrochemical roughening terms) − G_sep · (elastic stabilization)*

and the interface is stable to all *k* when the separator shear modulus satisfies:

> **Theorem 5.1 (Monroe–Newman):** *Dendrite growth is linearly suppressed when G_separator > 2·G_Li (≈ 6–10 GPa given G_Li ≈ 3–5 GPa). The bound is tight for defect-free, single-crystal, fully dense separators with ideal adhesion.*

**Proof sketch.** The interfacial electrochemical potential contains a pressure term μ = μ₀ + *V̄*σ_n. Advancing a protrusion deforms the separator at elastic cost ∝ G_sep·(strain)², while lithium's own deformation costs ∝ G_Li·(strain)². When G_sep > 2G_Li, the mechanical work of advancing any perturbation exceeds the electrochemical free-energy gain at every wavenumber, so all modes decay. ∎

The criterion's *failure* in practice is equally rigorous: it assumes (i) no pre-existing flaws, (ii) single-crystal isotropy, (iii) perfect adhesion. Violating (i) replaces the stability problem with the Griffith problem of §4.1, where the relevant parameter is fracture toughness *K_Ic* — and grain boundaries carry only ≈ 0.33× the bulk value [1]. **LLZO satisfies Monroe–Newman tenfold and still dendrites**, because real pellets are polycrystalline, flawed, and imperfectly contacted. Necessary, nowhere near sufficient.

### 5.2 Empirical CCD landscape (symmetric Li cells, 25 °C unless noted):

| System | CCD (mA cm⁻²) | Conditions | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bare Li\|LLZO\|Li | ≈ 0.1 | 25 °C; shorts at 0.15 | [1] |
| aLLZO-coated LLZO | > 3.2 | In-plane plating, no stack pressure | [8] |
| Li\|Li₆PS₅Cl\|Li | ≈ 1–2 | 3–5 MPa stack pressure | [4][10] |
| Li\|LGPS\|Li | > 1 (plating) | Interphase grows unboundedly | [4] |

*Table 3: Critical current densities. Coatings and pressure move CCD by more than an order of magnitude; bulk modulus does not predict the ranking [1][2][4][8][10].*

### 5.3 Formal bound on pressure-assisted operation

> **Theorem 5.2 (Pressure–current operating envelope):** *For a lithium-metal solid-state cell with stack pressure P and stripping exponent n, sustained void-free cycling requires i_strip < i\*(P) = i₀(P/P₀)ⁿ and simultaneously i_plate < CCD(P). The feasible region is the intersection of the creep-healing half-plane and the fracture-limited half-plane; outside it, voids accumulate geometrically with cycle number.*

The TLA⁺ specification in §4.4 model-checks the qualitative content: any trace with sustained *i > i\** reaches a state with constricted local current exceeding CCD, so dendrite nucleation becomes *inevitable* rather than merely probable — an empirical observation converted into a liveness property of the cycling protocol.

## 6 Limitations and Future Work

1. **Length-scale gaps.** Atomistic fracture simulations [1] resolve nanometers; tomography [3] resolves microns; cells are centimeters. No multiscale model yet propagates grain-boundary toughness distributions up to cell-level CCD predictions.
2. **Electronic conductivity of electrolytes.** Non-negligible electronic leakage in some garnets may enable *internal* lithium nucleation — dendrites starting inside the ceramic, beyond the Griffith interface picture.
3. **Cathode-side chemomechanics.** High-voltage cathodes impose their own volume-change and interphase burdens; the full cell must satisfy both interfaces simultaneously.
4. **Pressure uniformity at scale.** Maintaining ±10% pressure uniformity across 100+ cm² pouches over thousands of cycles, within pack mass constraints, is an unsolved manufacturing problem.
5. **Sulfide moisture sensitivity.** Argyrodites release H₂S on air exposure, demanding dry-room processing below −40 °C dew point — a cost burden oxides avoid.

## 7 Conclusion

Stiffness does not stop dendrites; **defect management, interphase passivation, and chemomechanical contact maintenance do**. The Monroe–Newman criterion remains the correct *linear-stability* bound for ideal interfaces, but real cells fail by Griffith fracture along grain boundaries, by stripping-induced voiding that constricts current, and by non-passivating interphases that grow without bound. Operando X-ray tomography made these mechanisms visible and therefore engineerable: the critical stripping current, tunable by stack pressure, is now a design variable rather than a mystery.

Commercialization is consequently a manufacturing and architecture contest, not a materials-discovery contest. **QuantumScape** bets on an anode-less architecture with a proprietary ceramic separator and its "Cobra" production process, shipping QSE-5 samples to automotive OEMs — eliminating lithium-foil handling by plating the anode *in situ* on first charge. **Toyota**, pursuing sulfide electrolytes, targets commercial solid-state EVs around 2027–2028, accepting dry-room costs for cold-pressable cells. **Samsung SDI** runs an all-solid-state pilot line with a stated 2027 mass-production target, likewise sulfide-based. All three concede the thesis of this work: the winner will be whoever best controls flaws, interphases, and pressure at automotive scale — not whoever finds the stiffest ceramic.
