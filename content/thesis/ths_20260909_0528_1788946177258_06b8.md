# Oblivious Transfer Extension and Garbled Circuits: IKNP, Half-Gates, and Malicious-Secure Two-Party Computation

## Abstract

Secure two-party computation (2PC) lets two mutually distrustful parties evaluate any function over private inputs, revealing only the output. Yao's garbled circuits [1] have been the dominant constant-round approach since 1986, but practicality was long blocked by oblivious transfer (OT): each evaluator input bit required a public-key OT, and naive garbling cost four ciphertexts per gate. This thesis treats the two breakthroughs that made garbled circuits practical: **oblivious transfer extension** — IKNP [2] and KK13 [3] — which amortizes arbitrarily many OTs over a handful of base OTs using only symmetric cryptography, and the **half-gates** optimization [4], which garbles AND gates with two ciphertexts while keeping XOR gates free. We develop the IKNP matrix protocol and the half-gates construction in full technical detail, trace the ascent to malicious security through actively-secure OT extension [5, 6], authenticated garbling [7], and MASCOT [10], analyze the half-gates lower bound and its circumvention [8], and benchmark modern frameworks — demonstrating million-gate malicious-secure 2PC at seconds-scale latency.

## 1 Introduction

The problem of *secure computation* is among the oldest in modern cryptography. In 1982, Yao posed the millionaires' problem: two parties wish to determine whose fortune is larger without revealing anything else [1]. Four years later he sketched the garbled-circuit protocol — encrypt the truth table of every gate, hand the evaluator exactly the keys corresponding to the true inputs via oblivious transfer, and let the evaluator decrypt gate-by-gate toward the output. Conceptually complete, the construction languished for two decades as impractical: OT required expensive public-key operations per input bit, and the naive garbling required four ciphertexts per gate.

Two independent revolutions changed this calculus. The first, **OT extension** [2], exploited the observation that OT is *complete* for MPC yet symmetric primitives cannot realize OT alone in a black-box sense — but they *can* amplify it. IKNP showed that *k* base OTs (where *k* is the security parameter) suffice to produce *n* effective OTs for arbitrary *n*, at the amortized cost of a few hash evaluations per OT. The second revolution concerned the garbling itself: free-XOR made XOR gates costless, and half-gates [4] reduced AND gates from three ciphertexts to two — a reduction later proven optimal within the natural class of linear garbling schemes, then circumvented by slicing-and-dicing techniques that beat the lower bound [8].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the foundations: Yao's protocol, point-and-permute, free-XOR, and OT. Section 3 develops the core methodology — the IKNP matrix protocol, the KK13 refinement, actively-secure variants [5, 6], and half-gates. Section 4 deep-dives into correlation robustness, the half-gates lower bound and its circumvention, the GMW-versus-garbled-circuit design space, and authenticated garbling [7] with MASCOT [10]. Section 5 presents empirical benchmarks; Sections 6–7 discuss limitations and conclude.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Yao's Garbled Circuits

In Yao's protocol [1], the *garbler* $P_1$ associates each wire $w$ of a Boolean circuit with two random $\kappa$-bit labels $W_w^0, W_w^1$, one per semantic value. For a gate $g$ with input wires $a, b$ and output wire $c$ computing $g: \{0,1\}^2 \to \{0,1\}$, the garbler produces four doubly-encrypted ciphertexts:

$$T_{i,j} = \mathrm{Enc}_{W_a^i, W_b^j}(W_c^{g(i,j)}) \quad i, j \in \{0,1\}$$

and randomly permutes them. The evaluator $P_2$ obtains the labels for $P_1$'s input wires directly, the labels for its *own* input wires via OT, and then decrypts exactly one row per gate, propagating labels forward until the output labels are revealed and decoded. Security against a semi-honest evaluator follows from the semantic security of the encryption: exactly one row decrypts, revealing only the output label. The **point-and-permute** optimization attaches a random select bit $p_w$ to each wire; rows are ordered by permute bits so the evaluator performs exactly one decryption per gate instead of trial-decrypting four rows.

### 2.2 Free-XOR and the Cost Model

Kolesnikov and Schneider observed that if all wire labels satisfy a global relation $W_w^1 = W_w^0 \oplus R$ for a single secret offset $R \in \{0,1\}^\kappa$, then an XOR gate can be evaluated *for free*: the output labels are defined as $W_c^0 = W_a^0 \oplus W_b^0$ and $W_c^1 = W_c^0 \oplus R$, with no ciphertext transmitted and no cryptographic operation at evaluation [4]. Communication is therefore dominated by AND gates, and the entire subsequent literature is an effort to shrink the per-AND-gate cost: 4 ciphertexts (Yao) → 3 (with free-XOR) → 2 (half-gates) → $1.5\kappa + 5$ bits (Rosulek–Roy [8]).

### 2.3 Oblivious Transfer

A 1-out-of-2 OT lets a sender with messages $(x_0, x_1)$ deliver $x_b$ to a receiver holding choice bit $b$, such that the sender learns nothing about $b$ and the receiver learns nothing about $x_{1-b}$. OT is *complete* for secure computation: given OT, any efficiently computable function admits a secure protocol. Public-key OT protocols (e.g., Naor–Pinkas) cost a few modular exponentiations per transfer — acceptable for a handful of OTs, catastrophic for the millions of input labels a large garbled circuit requires. OT extension [2] resolves this by shifting the per-OT cost into the symmetric-key regime.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The IKNP Protocol in Detail

The IKNP construction [2] is best understood as a clever matrix game. Let $\kappa$ be the computational security parameter and suppose the parties want $m$ instances of 1-out-of-2 OT on $\ell$-bit strings.

1. The **receiver** samples a random $m \times \kappa$ binary matrix $T$ and holds choice bits $r = (r_1, \dots, r_m)$. Define $t_j$ as the $j$-th column of $T$ (an $m$-bit string).
2. The **sender** samples a random $\kappa$-bit string $s = (s_1, \dots, s_\kappa)$.
3. The parties execute $\kappa$ *base OTs in the reversed direction*: in the $j$-th base OT, the receiver (acting as sender) offers the pair $(t_j,\, t_j \oplus r)$, and the sender (acting as receiver) inputs choice bit $s_j$, obtaining $q_j = t_j \oplus (r \cdot s_j)$.
4. Assemble the matrix $Q$ whose $j$-th column is $q_j$. For the $i$-th row:
   $$q_i = \begin{cases} t_i & \text{if } r_i = 0 \\ t_i \oplus s & \text{if } r_i = 1 \end{cases}$$
   where $t_i$ is the $i$-th row of $T$.
5. For the $i$-th extended OT, the sender defines its two "messages" via a correlation-robust hash $H$:
   $$y_{i,0} = H(i, q_i), \qquad y_{i,1} = H(i, q_i \oplus s).$$
   The sender masks its real inputs $x_{i,0}, x_{i,1}$ as $(x_{i,0} \oplus y_{i,0},\, x_{i,1} \oplus y_{i,1})$ and transmits both.
6. The receiver, knowing $t_i$ and $r_i$, computes $y_{i, r_i} = H(i, t_i)$ and unmasks $x_{i, r_i}$.

The crucial identity is $q_i \oplus (r_i \cdot s) = t_i$: the sender's two pads are correlated by the secret $s$, while the receiver knows exactly one of them. Security reduces to $H$ being **correlation-robust** — roughly, $H(\cdot, \cdot \oplus s)$ must look random even given oracle access — a notion weaker than a random oracle but still non-standard, discussed further in §4.1.

The amortized cost is $\kappa$ public-key base OTs plus $O(m\kappa)$ bits of communication and $O(m)$ hash evaluations — effectively a few symmetric operations per extended OT [2, 5].

### 3.2 KK13: Short Secrets and 1-out-of-$n$ OT

Kolesnikov and Kumaresan [3] observed that many applications need 1-out-of-$n$ OT (notably private set intersection) with *short* secrets. Their protocol starts from $2\kappa$ 1-out-of-2 base OTs and encodes the receiver's choice using Walsh–Hadamard (WH) codes. The WH code of length $\kappa$ has the property that distinct codewords disagree in exactly $\kappa/2$ positions, which yields 1-out-of-$n$ OTs with communication $O(m \log n)$ rather than the $O(mn)$ of the naive reduction — an asymptotic $O(\log n)$ improvement over IKNP-based approaches for short secrets, and it remains the most efficient semi-honest 1-out-of-$n$ extension known.

### 3.3 Half-Gates: Two Ciphertexts per AND

The half-gates construction [4] decomposes an AND gate $c = a \land b$ into two *half-gates* — AND gates in which the garbler knows one input. Let the global free-XOR offset be $R$, wire labels $W_a^0, W_b^0$, and permute bits $p_a, p_b, p_c$.

**Garbler half-gate.** The garbler knows $a$'s semantic value indirectly through its own choice of the output permute structure; it emits one ciphertext
$$T_G = H(W_a^0) \oplus H(W_a^1) \oplus p_b R,$$
which allows the evaluator — who knows $W_a$ (one of the two labels) — to compute $W_G = H(W_a) \oplus p_a T_G$, a label encoding $a \land p_b$.

**Evaluator half-gate.** Symmetrically, using the evaluator's known label $W_b$:
$$T_E = H(W_b^0) \oplus H(W_b^1) \oplus W_a^0 \oplus p_c R,$$
yielding $W_E = H(W_b) \oplus p_b (T_E \oplus W_a)$, encoding $p_a \land b$.

The final output label is $W_c = W_G \oplus W_E$. The evaluator performs *two* hash evaluations per AND gate instead of one — the price of the bandwidth reduction. Zahur, Rosulek, and Evans proved this optimal: any garbling scheme in the *linear* class (capturing all known practical techniques, where ciphertexts are affine combinations of hash outputs) requires $2\kappa$ bits per AND gate [4].

```python
# Half-gate garbling (simplified; H = fixed-key AES hash)
def garble_and_gate(Wa0, Wb0, pa, pb, pc, R):
    Wa1 = xor(Wa0, R); Wb1 = xor(Wb0, R)
    T_G = xor(H(Wa0), H(Wa1), mul(pb, R))          # garbler half-gate
    T_E = xor(H(Wb0), H(Wb1), Wa0, mul(pc, R))     # evaluator half-gate
    return (T_G, T_E)                              # 2 ciphertexts total

def eval_and_gate(Wa, Wb, pa, pb, T_G, T_E):
    W_G = xor(H(Wa), mul(pa, T_G))
    W_E = xor(H(Wb), mul(pb, xor(T_E, Wa)))
    return xor(W_G, W_E)
```

### 3.4 Actively-Secure OT Extension

Semi-honest OT extension is insufficient against malicious adversaries: a corrupt receiver in IKNP can choose a non-random matrix $T$ to mount *selective-failure* attacks, learning the sender's secret $s$ bit-by-bit from aborts. ALSZ13 [5] added consistency checks to IKNP, giving the first practical maliciously-secure OT extension. KOS15 [6] achieved *optimal* overhead: the sender verifies a random linear combination of the receiver's matrix rows, adding only $O(\kappa)$ communication *independent* of the number of extended OTs — now the standard substrate for malicious-secure MPC preprocessing.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Correlation Robustness and the Random-Permutation Model

The security of IKNP-style extension and half-gates rests on the hash $H$ being *correlation-robust* (for OT) or *circular correlation-robust* (CCR, for garbling) [4]. These notions sit strictly between one-wayness and the random-oracle model: no standard-model construction from one-way functions is known, and practical deployments instantiate $H$ with fixed-key AES (the JustGarble / Bellare-Hoang-Rogaway paradigm), effectively modeling AES as an ideal cipher.

> **Theorem (informal):** If $H$ is circular correlation-robust, then the half-gates garbling scheme satisfies the standard privacy, obliviousness, and authenticity notions for garbling schemes [4].

Recent work has begun to close the foundational gap: Guo et al. showed how to instantiate half-gates in the *standard model* from indistinguishability obfuscation plus one-way functions, via a new notion of *weak* CCR — a feasibility result, but significant given the ongoing NIST effort to standardize garbling. The practical takeaway is unchanged: in the ideal-cipher model, half-gates with AES-NI is both the fastest and the most analyzed choice.

### 4.2 Beating the Lower Bound: Slicing and Dicing

The $2\kappa$ lower bound of [4] applies to *linear* garbling. Rosulek and Roy [8] circumvented it with **slicing and dicing**: wire labels are sliced into halves, and the two halves are processed through separate, carefully correlated sub-protocols. Their scheme garbles an AND gate in $1.5\kappa + 5$ bits while remaining fully free-XOR-compatible — a genuine drop-in replacement for half-gates. The cost is slightly more computation and a more intricate proof, again under CCR-like assumptions. Subsequent work (e.g., Heath's lookup-table garbling) has pushed the constant lower still, but half-gates remains the engineering default because the bandwidth savings of $1.5\kappa$ schemes rarely justify their complexity outside extreme-bandwidth regimes.

| Scheme | AND cost | XOR cost | Assumption | Optimal? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yao (point-and-permute) | $4\kappa$ | $4\kappa$ | PRF | No |
| Free-XOR | $3\kappa$ | $0$ | CCR | No |
| **Half-gates** [4] | **$2\kappa$** | $0$ | CCR | Yes (linear class) |
| Rosulek–Roy [8] | $1.5\kappa+5$ | $0$ | CCR-like | Open |

### 4.3 GMW versus Garbled Circuits: The Round–Bandwidth Tradeoff

The GMW paradigm evaluates the circuit gate-by-gate with interactive multiplication (via OT or Beaver triples), requiring rounds proportional to circuit *depth*; garbled circuits require only a constant number of rounds regardless of depth. OT extension is the hinge on which both swing: GMW consumes one OT per AND gate per evaluation *online*, while Yao's protocol consumes OTs only for the evaluator's *input labels* — the circuit itself is transferred as symmetric ciphertexts.

This yields a clean decision rule used by modern compilers:

- **Low-depth, high-volume circuits** (e.g., AES evaluation, PSI): GMW with OT-based multiplication excels, since per-gate interaction is cheap and parallelism is total.
- **Deep circuits or high-latency networks**: garbled circuits dominate; the entire circuit ships in two rounds.
- **Mixed protocols** (ABY, MOTION): the framework automatically assigns each sub-circuit to the cheaper paradigm, using OT extension as the common preprocessing substrate [5].

Silent-OT techniques such as Ferret [9] push this further: by generating *correlated* OT pseudorandomly from short seeds (a puncturable-PRF/LPN-style expansion), communication per OT drops to a handful of bits, making even GMW-style per-gate OTs affordable at scale.

### 4.4 The Ascent to Malicious Security

Semi-honest garbling is broken by a malicious garbler who garbles a *different* circuit than agreed. The classical countermeasure is **cut-and-choose**: garble $s$ copies, open a random subset for verification, evaluate the rest, and take a majority — at the cost of a statistical factor of $\approx 3s$ in communication.

Wang, Ranellucci, and Katz [7] replaced cut-and-choose with **authenticated garbling**, achieving malicious security with a *single* garbled circuit. The blueprint:

1. **Function-independent preprocessing.** The parties generate *leaky* AND triples (authenticated shares of $(a, b, ab)$) using OT extension [6], then *bucket* and combine them to eliminate leakage — exactly the SPDZ/MASCOT preprocessing paradigm [10], but over $\mathbb{F}_2$.
2. **Function-dependent phase.** The triples are consumed to produce one garbled circuit whose wire labels carry information-theoretic MACs under a global key $\Delta$ held by the evaluator.
3. **Online phase.** The evaluator evaluates as in the semi-honest protocol; any deviation by the garbler is caught by MAC verification with probability $1 - 2^{-\rho}$ for statistical parameter $\rho = 40$.

MASCOT [10] applies the same OT-based authenticated-triple machinery to *arithmetic* circuits over large fields, giving the fastest known maliciously-secure dishonest-majority MPC for arithmetic workloads. Together, [7] and [10] show that OT extension — once merely a performance trick — has become the *universal preprocessing engine* for malicious-secure MPC.

# KOS-style consistency check (simplified): sender verifies receiver's matrix
def kos_check(Q_rows, s, chi):

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Analysis

We summarize published benchmarks from the primary sources, normalized to $\kappa = 128$ and $\rho = 40$ where applicable.

**Half-gates microbenchmarks** [4]. Across AES, SHA, and sorting circuits, half-gates reduced end-to-end time by up to **25%**, bandwidth by up to **33%**, and energy consumption by up to **20%** relative to the best prior free-XOR-compatible scheme — despite doubling the evaluator's hash calls per AND gate, confirming that communication, not computation, is the bottleneck.

**OT extension throughput.** IKNP with ALSZ optimizations [5] generates millions of OTs per second on commodity hardware; the malicious-secure KOS variant [6] adds only small constant overhead. Ferret's silent OT [9] further reduces communication to a few bits per correlated OT.

**Authenticated garbling end-to-end** [7]. The single-circuit authenticated protocol evaluates AES (6,800 AND gates) in the tens of milliseconds LAN setting, within a small factor of semi-honest half-gates — versus the $\approx 40\times$ blowup of naive cut-and-choose at comparable statistical security. MASCOT [10] reports triple generation at rates sufficient for large-scale arithmetic MPC, outperforming prior SHE-based SPDZ preprocessing.

| Workload | Protocol | LAN time (reported) | Communication |
|---|---|---|---|
| AES-128 (6.8k AND) | Half-gates, semi-honest [4] | ~ms | ~170 KB |
| AES-128 | Authenticated garbling [7] | tens of ms | ~1 MB |
| $2^{20}$ OTs | IKNP/ALSZ [5] | ~seconds | ~MBs |
| $2^{20}$ OTs | KOS malicious [6] | ~seconds + small const | +$O(\kappa)$ |

*Formal analysis.* The security theorems compose cleanly: IKNP is secure under correlation-robust hashing [2]; KOS adds malicious security with optimal overhead under the same assumption plus a random oracle for the check [6]; half-gates is private/authentic under CCR [4]; authenticated garbling lifts any such scheme to malicious 2PC with abort [7]. The full stack — KOS OT extension → leaky triples → bucketing → authenticated half-gates — is therefore maliciously secure in the $(\mathcal{F}_{\text{OT}}, \text{RO})$-hybrid model.

## 6 Limitations

**Non-standard assumptions.** Every efficiency result in this thesis — IKNP, KK13, half-gates, KOS, authenticated garbling — relies on correlation-robust or circular correlation-robust hashing, notions with no standard-model instantiation from minimal assumptions. The iO-based standard-model instantiation of half-gates is a feasibility result only; practice continues to bet on AES-as-ideal-cipher. A break of fixed-key AES indifferentiability would ripple through the entire stack.

**Post-quantum exposure.** Both OT extension and garbling are symmetric-key-heavy, but the *base OTs* and correlation-robustness reductions are not quantum-safe in deployed form. Post-quantum garbling remains an open standardization problem.

**Malicious security with fairness/guaranteed output** is not achieved: [7] and [10] provide security *with abort*, and a malicious party can deny the honest party output. Stronger notions (identifiable abort, covert security trade-offs) add further cost.

**Communication floor.** Even $1.5\kappa + 5$ bits per AND [8] is far from the intuition that one bit of functionality should cost $O(1)$ bits; whether $\kappa$-dependence per AND gate is inherent remains open.

**Implementation fragility.** The empirical wins assume AES-NI and constant-time code. Side-channel leakage of the global offset $R$ or the MAC key $\Delta$ is catastrophic — failure modes that software benchmarks understate.

## 7 Conclusion

Oblivious transfer extension and garbled circuits form the central spine of practical secure two-party computation. IKNP [2] taught us to amortize public-key cost into symmetric-key abundance; KK13 [3] and the ALSZ/KOS line [5, 6] refined extension into 1-out-of-$n$, maliciously-secure, and eventually *silent* forms [9]; half-gates [4] compressed garbling to its linear optimum of two ciphertexts per AND gate, with Rosulek–Roy [8] showing the bound itself could be sidestepped; and authenticated garbling [7] together with MASCOT [10] lifted the whole edifice to malicious security at near-semi-honest cost.

The trajectory is unmistakable: each generation converts what was once an asymptotic or public-key expense into a symmetric-key or information-theoretic one, and each conversion is then absorbed into preprocessing. The remaining challenges — standard-model correlation robustness, post-quantum garbling, and closing the gap between the $1.5\kappa$ upper bound and any fundamental lower bound — define the research frontier. For the practitioner, the conclusion is simpler: with OT extension and half-gates, malicious-secure two-party computation of million-gate circuits is no longer a research prototype but an engineering choice.
