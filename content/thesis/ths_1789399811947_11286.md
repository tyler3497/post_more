# The Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture: L-functions of Elliptic Curves, Heegner Points, and Iwasawa Theory

## Abstract

The Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture asserts that the order of vanishing of the Hasse–Weil L-function L(E,s) of an elliptic curve E over the rationals at the central point s=1 equals the rank of the finitely generated Mordell–Weil group E(Q), and that the leading Taylor coefficient encodes the real period, the canonical regulator, the Tamagawa numbers, and the order of the Tate–Shafarevich group. This survey reconstructs the conjecture from its computational origins in the work of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer [1], through the modularity theorem of Wiles, Taylor–Wiles, and Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor [4], the Gross–Zagier formula relating the first derivative of L(E,s) to Néron–Tate heights of Heegner points [2], Kolyvagin's Euler-system machinery proving the rank-zero and rank-one cases [3], the Coates–Wiles theorem [6], and the Iwasawa-theoretic reformulation via p-adic L-functions and the main conjecture proved by Mazur and Wiles [5]. We survey recent progress including Skinner–Urban's divisibility, the work of Bhargava–Skinner–Zhang on positive proportions, p-converse theorems, and BSD over function fields, where the conjecture reduces to finiteness of the Tate–Shafarevich group and follows from the Tate conjecture for elliptic surfaces. Numerical methodology with Sage and Dokchitser's algorithm [9] is exhibited, with concrete curve data.

## 1. Introduction

Few problems in number theory sit so perfectly at the junction of computation, analysis, and algebra as the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture. In the mid-1960s, Bryan Birch and Peter Swinnerton-Dyer, working with one of the earliest electronic computers at Cambridge, tabulated the numbers of points modulo primes on hundreds of elliptic curves and noticed a striking pattern: curves whose reductions mod p had many points, systematically, tended to possess large rational point groups [1]. The pattern was purely experimental at first. Translating the observed growth into the language of L-functions — the standard analytic packaging of point-count data — they conjectured that the order of vanishing of L(E,s) at s=1 is precisely the rank of E(Q) [1]. Six decades later, the conjecture remains open in general, carries a million-dollar Millennium Prize, and has absorbed some of the deepest machinery of twentieth-century arithmetic geometry: modular forms, Galois representations, Heegner points, Euler systems, and Iwasawa theory.

This article develops the conjecture systematically. We begin with the arithmetic of elliptic curves and the definition of the Hasse–Weil L-function, then describe how the modularity theorem supplies the analytic continuation needed to speak of L(E,s) at s=1 at all. The heart of the article is the theorem of Gross–Zagier and Kolyvagin: when the analytic rank is 0 or 1, the conjecture holds, via Heegner points on modular curves and the Euler-system relations they satisfy. We then turn to Iwasawa theory, which studies Selmer groups over infinite p-adic towers and reformulates the refined BSD formula as an equality of ideals — the main conjecture proved by Mazur and Wiles [5]. Along the way we record the status of the conjecture over function fields, where it is intimately tied to the Tate conjecture for surfaces, and we exhibit the computational methods by which millions of curves have been checked numerically.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Elliptic curves and the Mordell–Weil theorem

An elliptic curve over the rationals is a smooth projective curve of genus one with a distinguished rational point, given concretely by a Weierstrass equation

    y^2 + a1 x y + a3 y = x^3 + a2 x^2 + a4 x + a6

with integral coefficients and nonzero discriminant. The rational points E(Q) form a finitely generated abelian group by the theorem of Mordell:

> **Theorem (Mordell):** For an elliptic curve E over Q, the group E(Q) is finitely generated. Hence E(Q) is isomorphic to the direct sum of a finite torsion group E(Q)tors and a free abelian group Z^r, where the integer r = rank E(Q) is called the Mordell–Weil rank, or algebraic rank.

Mazur's theorem completely classifies the possible torsion groups, but the rank remains profoundly mysterious: no algorithm is known that provably computes the rank of an arbitrary elliptic curve, and curves of rank at least 28 are known to exist while the record grows slowly.

### 2.2 The Hasse–Weil L-function

For each prime p of good reduction, let N_p denote the number of points of the reduced curve over the finite field F_p. By Hasse's theorem, N_p = p + 1 - a_p with the trace of Frobenius a_p satisfying the bound abs(a_p) <= 2 sqrt(p). The Hasse–Weil L-function is the Euler product

    L(E,s) = product over p not dividing N_E of 1/(1 - a_p p^{-s} + p^{1-2s}) * product over p dividing N_E of local factors,

convergent for Re(s) > 3/2. The *analytic rank* of E is the order of vanishing r_an(E) = ord_{s=1} L(E,s), once analytic continuation to s=1 is established. The central value problem asks: what does this integer measure?

| 5077a1 | y^2 + y = x^3 - 7x + 6 | 3 | 3 | Numerical; first rank-3 curve found by Mestre |

### 2.3 The refined conjecture

The weak form predicts r_an(E) = rank E(Q). The strong, or refined, form predicts the leading coefficient:

> **Conjecture (Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer, refined):** Let r = rank E(Q). Then
>
>     L^{(r)}(E,1) / r! = Omega_E * Reg(E) * product_p c_p * abs(Sha(E/Q)) / abs(E(Q)tors)^2,
>
> where Omega_E is the real period, Reg(E) the regulator (the covolume of E(Q)/tors under the Néron–Tate height pairing), c_p the Tamagawa numbers measuring local components at bad primes, and Sha(E/Q) the Tate–Shafarevich group.

The group Sha(E/Q) is defined as the kernel of the localization map from the Weil–Châtelet group H^1(Q,E) to the product of the local groups H^1(Q_v,E); it measures the failure of the Hasse principle for principal homogeneous spaces. Its finiteness is itself an open problem and is implied by, but strictly weaker than, the refined formula.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Modularity: making L(E,s) analytic

None of the above can even be stated rigorously without analytic continuation of L(E,s) to s=1. This is supplied by the *modularity theorem*: every elliptic curve E over Q arises from a weight-2 newform. Concretely, there is a surjective morphism from the modular curve X_0(N), where N is the conductor of E, onto E — the modular parametrization — and L(E,s) coincides with the L-function of the associated newform f, which has analytic continuation and a functional equation by Hecke theory [4]. The theorem was proved for semistable curves by Wiles with the assistance of Taylor–Wiles, and in full generality by Breuil, Conrad, Diamond, and Taylor [4].

### 3.2 Heegner points: manufacturing rational points

The proof strategy for analytic rank at most one, due to Gross–Zagier and Kolyvagin, manufactures points rather than searching for them. Let K be an imaginary quadratic field satisfying the *Heegner hypothesis*: every prime dividing the conductor N splits in K. Points on X_0(N) with complex multiplication by the ring of integers of K — Heegner points — are defined over the Hilbert class field of K, and their traces down to K, pushed forward along the modular parametrization phi: X_0(N) -> E, produce points y_K in E(K) called Heegner points [2]. When the analytic rank of E over K is one, these points are nontorsion, and their heights encode L'(E,1).

### 3.3 Kolyvagin's Euler system

From the basic Heegner point, Kolyvagin constructs a full *Euler system*: a compatible family of cohomology classes indexed by squarefree integers, satisfying norm relations mirroring the Euler factors of the L-function. The Euler-system machine then bounds the Selmer group: the classes force the p-Selmer group to be as small as the analytic data predict, yielding finiteness of Sha and equality of ranks when r_an <= 1 [3].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Gross–Zagier formula

The Gross–Zagier formula is the analytic heart of the rank-one theorem [2]:

> **Theorem (Gross–Zagier):** Let E/Q be an elliptic curve of conductor N, and let K be an imaginary quadratic field satisfying the Heegner hypothesis relative to N. Let y_K in E(K) be the associated Heegner point. Then
>
>     L'(E/K, 1) = c(E,K) * <y_K, y_K>,
>
> where the left side is the derivative of the L-function of E base-changed to K, the right side is the Néron–Tate canonical height pairing, and c(E,K) is an explicit nonzero constant depending on the real period and the degree of the modular parametrization.

Consequences are immediate and striking. If r_an(E/K) = 1, then L'(E/K,1) is nonzero, so the height pairing is nonzero, so y_K is a point of infinite order; hence the algebraic rank is at least one [2]. Combined with Kolyvagin's upper bound, equality follows. The formula was a landmark: the first general result connecting derivatives of L-functions to heights of algebraic points, and the prototype for the vast subsequent theory of special-value formulas (Waldspurger, Yuan–Zhang–Zhang, and the p-adic Gross–Zagier formula of Perrin-Riou, later generalized by Disegni [10]).

### 4.2 Kolyvagin's Euler system and the rank-zero and rank-one theorems

Kolyvagin's 1990 work completed the picture [3]:

> **Theorem (Gross–Zagier, Kolyvagin):** If the analytic rank r_an(E/Q) is 0 or 1, then r_an(E/Q) = rank E(Q) and the Tate–Shafarevich group Sha(E/Q) is finite.

The proof proceeds through the Euler system. In rank zero, Kato's Euler system (built from Beilinson–Kato elements in K-theory) provides an independent route: if L(E,1) is nonzero, the Selmer group is finite [3]. For CM curves, Coates and Wiles had already proved the rank-zero case in 1977 using the arithmetic of elliptic units [6]. Kolyvagin's derivative classes — obtained by a combinatorial differentiation process on the Euler system — control the Selmer group one prime at a time, and a careful induction shows that Sha[p^infty] is finite for every p and that the rank matches [3]. Later refinements removed hypotheses: Skinner proved the rank-zero converse direction under mild conditions, and recent p-converse theorems (Burungale–Tian, and others) extend these results using Kato's main conjecture [10].

### 4.3 p-adic L-functions and Iwasawa theory

A complementary viewpoint, initiated by Mazur in the 1970s, studies E over the cyclotomic Z_p-extension Q_infty of Q rather than over Q itself. Mazur and Swinnerton-Dyer constructed a p-adic L-function L_p(E) interpolating the algebraic parts of twisted special values L(E,chi,1) as chi ranges over finite-order characters of Gal(Q_infty/Q) [5]. On the algebraic side, the p-primary Selmer group over Q_infty is a module over the Iwasawa algebra Lambda = Z_p[[T]], and its Pontryagin dual X(E/Q_infty) is conjectured to be Lambda-torsion. The *main conjecture* asserts an equality of ideals:

> **Conjecture (Mazur):** The characteristic ideal of X(E/Q_infty) is generated by the p-adic L-function L_p(E).

This is the elliptic-curve analogue of the classical Iwasawa main conjecture for class groups, proved by Mazur and Wiles [5]. For elliptic curves, the ordinary case was proved by Skinner and Urban via Eisenstein congruences on unitary groups, building on Kato's divisibility; the supersingular case was treated by Kobayashi's plus/minus theory and proved by Wan using Beilinson–Flach elements [8]. A direct consequence: assuming the main conjecture, the p-part of the refined BSD formula holds in analytic rank zero — the order of vanishing of the characteristic ideal at T=0 controls the size of the classical Selmer group [8].

### 4.4 Average ranks and positive proportions

While individual curves of high rank resist proof, spectacular progress has been made on average. Bhargava and Shankar proved that the average rank of elliptic curves over Q is bounded (less than 1), and Bhargava, Skinner, and Zhang combined this with the rank-zero and rank-one theorems to show that a *positive proportion* of elliptic curves satisfy BSD [7]. The argument: a positive proportion of curves have analytic rank 0 (via nonvanishing results for L(E,1) using the methods of Iwaniec, Kowalski, and others), and Kolyvagin's theorem converts analytic rank 0 into the full BSD package for those curves. Related work of Smith on 2^infty-Selmer groups in quadratic twist families proved Goldfeld's conjecture on average for the congruent-number curve, and the even-parity Goldfeld conjecture is now known for that family [10].

### 4.5 BSD over function fields

Over a function field F = F_q(t), the situation is both better and illuminating. Tate showed that BSD for the generic elliptic curve over F_q(t) — in the form that finiteness of Sha is equivalent to the rank conjecture — is equivalent to the Tate conjecture for the associated elliptic surface [7]. Consequently, known cases of the Tate conjecture yield known cases of BSD: for instance, BSD holds for large classes of elliptic curves over function fields, including all isotrivial ones. The function-field setting also admits a proof of the full refined formula via the Artin–Tate formula for the Brauer group of the surface, a geometric analogue with no known number-field counterpart. The contrast sharpens what is missing over Q: a geometric interpretation of Sha as a Brauer group.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

Numerical verification of BSD has been carried out for millions of curves using Cremona's tables and the LMFDB. The standard algorithm, due to Dokchitser, computes L(E,s) and its derivatives at s=1 to high precision via the approximate functional equation, using O(sqrt(N)) Fourier coefficients [9]. The refined formula can then be checked numerically: compute the regulator from a set of generators (found by point search and descent), the real period by numerical integration, Tamagawa numbers from Tate's algorithm, and compare against L^{(r)}(E,1)/r!.

```python
# Sage-style computation of the analytic rank and BSD invariants
E = EllipticCurve('37a1')          # rank-1 curve: y^2 + y = x^3 - x
print(E.rank())                    # 1  (via 2-descent + point search)
print(E.analytic_rank())           # 1  (Dokchitser's algorithm)
L1 = E.lseries().dokchitser(1.0)   # L(E,1) ~ 0  (vanishes: rank 1)
Lp = E.lseries().dokchitser(1.0, prec=53, deriv=1)  # L'(E,1)
Omega = E.period_lattice().omega() # real period
Reg = E.regulator()                # Néron–Tate regulator
Sha_an = E.sha().an_numerical()    # analytic order of Sha ~ 1.0
print("BSD check:", abs(Lp - Omega*Reg*Sha_an) < 1e-6)
```

For the curve 37a1, one finds L'(E,1) = 0.305999..., Omega = 5.986..., Reg = 0.0511..., and the product matches to the computed precision, with analytic Sha equal to 1 [9]. Curves of rank 2, such as 389a1, exhibit L(E,1) = L'(E,1) = 0 with nonzero second derivative, and the refined formula has been verified numerically to high precision for the first several thousand curves. The smallest conductor of a rank-3 curve is 5077, and curves of rank up to 28 have been found by Elkies and others — all consistent with the conjecture, though none of the rank ≥ 2 cases is proved.

## 6. Limitations

The frontier of ignorance is stark. The Gross–Zagier–Kolyvagin theorem covers analytic rank 0 and 1 only; *no* elliptic curve over Q of analytic rank 2 or higher is known to satisfy even the rank part of BSD unconditionally. The obstruction is fundamental: Heegner points detect the first derivative, and no robust construction produces points detecting higher derivatives in general (the theory of Stark–Heegner points of Darmon offers a conjectural p-adic analogue). Finiteness of Sha(E/Q) is open for every curve of analytic rank ≥ 2, and the full refined formula is therefore open there as well. The p-adic methods require ordinarity or restrictive hypotheses at p; supersingular primes, additive reduction, and Eisenstein primes each demand separate, technically formidable treatments [8]. Average-rank results give proportions, not individual curves. And the function-field proofs lean on the Tate conjecture for surfaces, which over number fields has no analogue — the geometric route is closed over Q. Finally, even the rank-zero theorem depends on deep inputs (modularity, nonvanishing theorems, Euler systems), each a major achievement in its own right.

## 7. Conclusion

The Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture predicts a precise identity between the analytic behavior of L(E,s) at s=1 and the arithmetic of rational points: rank equals order of vanishing, and the leading coefficient factors into periods, regulators, Tamagawa numbers, and the order of Sha [1]. Modularity makes the L-function analytic [4]; the Gross–Zagier formula [2] and Kolyvagin's Euler systems [3] prove the conjecture when the analytic rank is 0 or 1; Iwasawa theory recasts the refined formula as a main conjecture over p-adic towers, proved in the ordinary case by Skinner–Urban and extended to supersingular primes by Kobayashi and Wan [5][8]; and over function fields the conjecture is equivalent to the Tate conjecture for elliptic surfaces. Numerical evidence from millions of curves, computed via Dokchitser's algorithm [9], is uniformly consistent. The central open problem — any curve of rank at least two — awaits new ideas, perhaps from higher Stark–Heegner constructions, derived deformation theory, or a genuinely new Euler system. Until then, BSD remains the clearest window we have into the unity of L-functions and Diophantine geometry.
