# Proving Neural Inference Inside Arithmetic Circuits: Quantized Transformer Encodings, Halo2 Lookup Arguments, and Prover Cost Analysis for Zero-Knowledge Machine Learning

## Abstract

Zero-knowledge machine learning inference (zkML) lets a prover convince a verifier that $y = f_\theta(x)$ for a committed model $f_\theta$, without revealing the private input $x$, the proprietary weights $\theta$, or both. This thesis develops the circuit-level foundations of that claim. We formalize neural inference as an arithmetic-circuit relation over a prime field, explain why naive encodings of nonlinearities are intractable, and present quantized fixed-point encodings that map transformer blocks — matrix multiplications, layer normalization, softmax, and GELU — onto Halo2's PLONKish arithmetization with lookup arguments. We reconstruct the ezkl-style compilation pipeline from ONNX to Halo2 circuits, describe weight commitments binding proofs to a parameter vector, and construct recursive aggregation for batched queries. Finally, we give a prover-side cost model grounded in published benchmarks — seconds for MNIST-scale networks, tens of minutes for nanoGPT-scale transformers, sub-second verification throughout — with soundness bounds for the randomized matrix checks that make the approach practical. [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8]

## 1. Introduction

ML inference is increasingly consumed as an opaque service: the client trusts that the claimed model ran faithfully on the submitted data. In regulated domains — credit scoring, biometrics, medical triage — that trust is a liability. Reproducible builds and TEEs help, but yield no *transferable, cryptographically checkable* artifact that a third party, including an on-chain contract, can verify without re-executing the model or seeing private data [3][6].

Zero-knowledge ML inference closes this gap: the prover holds $f_\theta$ and $x$, and produces a succinct proof $\pi$ that $y = f_\theta(x)$ under a public circuit encoding, with selective disclosure of $x$, $\theta$, $y$. Verification is logarithmic or constant in the computation size, and the proof is zero-knowledge over whichever inputs are designated private [1][2].

The central difficulty is *arithmetization*: networks compute over floating-point reals with smooth nonlinearities, while SNARK circuits offer only finite-field addition and multiplication. zkML is therefore a program in lossy but faithful translation — quantizing activations to fixed-point integers, replacing transcendental functions with lookup tables, and laying out constraints for Halo2's PLONKish arithmetization with KZG commitments [4][7][8].

This thesis contributes: **(1)** a formal circuit model of quantized inference with a fixed-point error budget; **(2)** Halo2 lookup arguments for ReLU, sigmoid, GELU, and softmax with a soundness reduction; **(3)** a reconstruction of the ezkl-style pipeline and its trust boundaries [4]; **(4)** weight-commitment and recursive-aggregation constructions for model binding and batched queries; and **(5)** a prover cost model calibrated from 0.12 s (linear regression) to ~15 minutes (13B parameters) [5][6][7].

> **Theorem (informal):** For a quantized network with $m$ MACs and nonlinearities realizable by tables of size at most $2^k$, there is a Halo2 circuit with $O(m \log m)$ constraints proving $y = f_\theta(x)$ in $\tilde{O}(|C|)$ prover time, with $O(\log |C|)$ verification and proof size, under the discrete-logarithm assumption.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 SNARKs and PLONKish arithmetization

A zk-SNARK lets a prover convince a verifier of a constraint system's satisfiability without revealing the witness [1]. Halo2, the proving system behind ezkl, uses a *PLONKish* arithmetization: the circuit is a table with **fixed** columns (constants such as lookup-table entries), **advice** columns (prover-assigned witness), and **instance** columns (public I/O). Constraints are *custom gates* — low-degree polynomial identities over cells, possibly across rotated rows — plus *permutation arguments* copying values between cells and *lookup arguments* constraining advice tuples to a table [8].

Proving has five stages — commit to column polynomials, build the vanishing argument, evaluate at required points, open at those points, compress via the inner-product argument [8]. With KZG, proofs are constant-size and verification fast, at the cost of an SRS and a per-circuit-size trusted setup [5].

### 2.2 The quantization imperative

SNARK circuits support only field addition and multiplication; IEEE-754 semantics are unencodable at practical cost. Every zkML system therefore *quantizes*: floats become integers via $\hat{x} = \mathrm{round}(x \cdot 2^s)$, integer arithmetic runs inside the circuit, rescaling happens at operator boundaries [2][4]. Scale selection, accumulator width, and overflow handling jointly determine fidelity and constraint count; *calibration* on representative inputs lets ezkl pick scales avoiding overflow while preserving accuracy [4].

### 2.3 Nonlinearities: the hard part

Linear layers are sums of products: expensive in constraints but conceptually trivial. The hard operators are ReLU, sigmoid, GELU, softmax, and layer normalization. A degree-7 polynomial GELU approximation accurate to $2^{-16}$ costs several constraints per evaluation, and softmax alone can dominate attention-heavy circuits [7]. The answer is the **lookup argument**: precompute the function on its quantized domain into a fixed table and prove via a subset argument that each (input, output) witness pair belongs to it [8] — a transcendental function for a handful of constraints plus amortized table cost, the most important arithmetization technique in practical zkML [4][7].

### 2.4 The zkML ecosystem

**ezkl** compiles ONNX graphs into Halo2 circuits, proving MNIST-scale models in seconds [2][4]. **Modulus Labs** benchmarked on-chain inference up to 18 million parameters [3]. **zkLLM** targets 13-billion-parameter models with CUDA-accelerated table lookups, reporting ~15 minutes of proving and proofs under 200 KB [6]. **NANOZK** proposes layerwise proving with 24 ms verification per transformer layer [6]. **zkMLaaS** combines im2col lowering with Freivalds-based matrix verification for a reported ~273× lower proof-generation overhead than direct encoding [2]. They differ in proving system and privacy partitioning but share the arithmetization core developed here [1][6].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The proved relation

Let $\mathcal{F} = \{f_\theta : \theta \in \Theta\}$ be a family of architectures with fixed topology, and $\mathsf{cm}_\theta = \mathsf{Commit}(\theta; r)$ a binding weight commitment. The *verifiable inference relation* is

$$\mathcal{R}_{\text{inf}} = \left\{ \big((\mathsf{cm}_\theta, x_{\text{pub}}, y_{\text{pub}}), (\theta, x_{\text{priv}}, r)\big) : \mathsf{Open}(\mathsf{cm}_\theta, \theta, r) = 1 \;\land\; \hat{f}_\theta(x) = y \;\land\; \|f_\theta(x) - y\|_\infty \le \varepsilon \right\}$$

where $\hat{f}_\theta$ is the quantized circuit encoding and $\varepsilon$ the error bound of Section 3.3. Deployments partition publicity differently — public network on private data, private network on public data, or fully public [4] — and zero-knowledge holds over whichever witness components are designated private [1][8].

### 3.2 Fixed-point circuit semantics

Each tensor is an element of $\mathbb{F}_p^k$ read as signed fixed-point with scale $2^s$: $\phi_s(v) = \mathrm{round}(v \cdot 2^s) \bmod p$. For a linear layer, $\hat{z} = \hat{W}\hat{x} + \hat{b}\cdot 2^s$ satisfies $\psi_{2s}(\hat{z}) = Wx + b + \delta$ with $|\delta| \le \tfrac{1}{2}(\|W\|_1+1)2^{-s}$ per coordinate. The circuit then *rescales* by constraining $\hat{z} = q \cdot 2^s + \rho$ with $\rho \in [0, 2^s)$ range-checked — via a lookup or binary decomposition [4][8]. Every fixed-point rounding step is thus a range proof, and lookup arguments earn their keep long before any activation appears.

Accumulator width is the critical parameter: $n$ terms of $b$ bits need $2b + \lceil\log_2 n\rceil$ bits — for $d_{\text{model}} = 1024$, $b = 16$, roughly 42 bits, inside a 254-bit field, but each headroom bit costs range-check constraints. Per-tensor calibration keeps accumulators in range while minimizing precision loss [4][7].

### 3.3 Quantization error budget

> **Theorem (quantization fidelity):** Let $f_\theta$ be an $L$-layer network where each layer is $\Lambda$-Lipschitz in $\ell_\infty$ and each quantized operator adds per-coordinate error at most $\delta$. Then $\|\hat{f}_\theta(x) - f_\theta(x)\|_\infty \le \delta \cdot \frac{\Lambda^L - 1}{\Lambda - 1}$.

*Proof sketch.* If layer $\ell$'s input carries error $e_{\ell-1}$, the layer amplifies it by at most $\Lambda$ and adds $\delta$: $e_\ell \le \Lambda e_{\ell-1} + \delta$. Solving the recurrence gives the bound. ∎

Residual connections and layer normalization keep transformer block Jacobians near 1, so error grows roughly linearly in depth; calibrated 16-bit fixed-point reproduces floating-point argmax decisions with negligible accuracy drop [2][4][7]. The proof attests to $\hat{f}_\theta$, not $f_\theta$ — hence ezkl-style pipelines *calibrate and freeze* the quantized model before synthesis [4].

### 3.4 From ONNX to constraints

The reference compilation pipeline, following ezkl [4], is:

1. **Ingest** — export the model from PyTorch/TensorFlow to ONNX with sample inputs fixing tensor shapes.
2. **Calibrate** — set SRS size $2^K$ and visibility mode; run calibration inputs to select per-tensor scales.
3. **Synthesize** — lower each ONNX operator to a Halo2 *chip*: matmuls to sum-of-products gates, nonlinearities to lookup-table chips, reshapes to permutation-argument wiring.
4. **Witness** — run the quantized model in the clear, recording every intermediate tensor as advice assignments.
5. **Prove/verify** — generate keys, prove, verify in milliseconds, including via an EVM contract [4][5][8].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Quantized circuit encodings of transformer blocks

A transformer block, $\mathrm{Block}(X) = \mathrm{LN}(X + \mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{LN}(X + \mathrm{Attn}(X))))$, arithmetizes in order of increasing difficulty.

**Linear projections.** QKV projections and MLP dense layers are $Y = XW^\top + b$, one row per output element with a custom gate enforcing the rescaled sum of products. The Freivalds optimization replaces the $O(n^3)$ witness check with a randomized one: the circuit checks $A(Br) = Cr$ for a Fiat-Shamir-sampled $r$ — two matrix-vector products at $O(n^2)$ cost [2].

> **Theorem (Freivalds soundness):** If $AB \ne C$ over $\mathbb{F}$, then $\Pr_{r \in \{0,1\}^n}[A(Br) = Cr] \le 1/2$; $t$ repetitions give error at most $2^{-t}$.

*Proof.* With $D = AB - C \ne 0$, some row $d^\top \ne 0$, and $Dr = 0$ needs $d^\top r = 0$ — a nontrivial linear equation satisfied with probability at most $1/2$. ∎

With im2col lowering, this randomization gives the largest reported zkML efficiency gain: ~273× lower proof-generation overhead than direct encoding [2].

**Attention and softmax.** Scores $S = QK^\top/\sqrt{d}$ are another matmul, but $\mathrm{softmax}(S)$ is the circuit's most expensive operator: (i) subtract the row maximum; (ii) table-lookup $\exp$ on the quantized domain; (iii) sum the row; (iv) divide via a *division chip* — prover supplies the quotient, circuit checks $a = q\cdot s + \rho$, $\rho < s$ range-checked [4][7]. Table size $2^k$ must cover the quantized range of shifted scores.

**GELU and layer normalization.** GELU, $x\Phi(x)$, has no useful algebraic simplification — it is a pure table operator sized by the calibrated activation range [7]. Layer normalization needs a mean (linear), a variance (sum of squares, natively quadratic), an *inverse square root* (table plus one Newton refinement checked by the circuit), and an affine rescale [7] — the general pattern for expensive univariate functions: one lookup plus a cheap algebraic check.

| Operator | Native cost | zkML treatment | Cost driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dense / QKV projection | $O(n^2)$ MACs | Sum-of-products gates; Freivalds option | Accumulator range checks |
| Attention scores $QK^\top$ | $O(n^2 d)$ MACs | Same as dense | Sequence length |
| Softmax | Transcendental | Max-subtract + exp table + division chip | Table size $2^k$ |
| GELU | Transcendental | Lookup table | Calibrated input range |
| LayerNorm | Inv-sqrt | Lookup + Newton check | Variance bit-width |
| Residual add | $O(n)$ | Permutation-argument wiring | Negligible |

### 4.2 Halo2 lookup arguments for nonlinearities

Halo2's lookup is a *subset argument*: for a table $S$ with $2^k$ rows of precomputed input/output pairs and prover-assigned advice $A$, the protocol shows every entry of $A$ appears in $S$ [8]. Tuples are compressed with a challenge $\theta$, permuted into $A', S'$, and the grand-product identity

$$Z(\omega X)(A'(X)+\beta)(S'(X)+\gamma) = Z(X)(A(X)+\beta)(S(X)+\gamma)$$

is enforced with boundary conditions and a sorted-run rule; blinded rows give zero-knowledge [8]. It generalizes Plookup: tables may be fixed *or* advice-derived, duplicates allowed, multiple tables composable via tag selectors [8].

For zkML, the table for an activation $\sigma$ is its graph $\{(\hat{v}, \phi_s(\sigma(\psi_s(\hat{v}))))\}$ over the calibrated quantized domain. Three consequences follow. **First, table size governs feasibility**: 16-bit inputs need 65,536 rows ($k=16$); 32-bit domains are impossible, so wide values split into 8-bit limbs looked up separately — and calibration that narrows activation ranges directly shrinks circuits [4][7]. **Second, tables amortize**: one table serves every activation evaluation; the marginal cost is one lookup argument per operator instance [8]. **Third, lookups are the GPU target**: zkLLM's CUDA "tlookup" exists because table-heavy transformer circuits are lookup-bound, and accelerating the grand product is what brings 13B-parameter proving to ~15 minutes [6].

> **Theorem (lookup soundness):** If the Halo2 lookup argument verifies for advice $A$ against table $S$, then except with probability at most $(2^k + q)/|\mathbb{F}|$ over verifier challenges, every value committed in $A$ equals some value in $S$.

*Proof sketch.* The grand-product identity forces $\{A_i+\beta\}$ and $\{A'_i+\beta\}$ to coincide as multisets; the sorted-run constraint forces $A'$ onto runs from $S'$. If some $A_i \notin S$, both hold only if $\beta$ or $\gamma$ hits a pole — probability $\le (2^k+q)/|\mathbb{F}|$ by Schwartz–Zippel. ∎

Concretely: once the verifier accepts, every ReLU, GELU, and softmax output in the witness equals the tabulated function of its input — nonlinear semantics enforced *without any transcendental evaluation inside the circuit*.

### 4.3 ezkl-style verifiable inference pipelines

ezkl is the reference ONNX-to-Halo2 toolchain — a Rust library and CLI with proofs verifiable on-chain, in browsers, or on-device [4]:

```rust
let model = onnx::load("transformer.onnx")?;
let settings = Settings::new()
    .with_scales(calibrate(&model, &sample_inputs)?) // per-tensor fixed-point scales
    .with_visibility(Visibility::PrivateInputs);
let circuit = GraphCircuit::from_settings(&model, &settings)?;
let (pk, vk) = setup(circuit, srs_size = 1 << K)?;   // KZG SRS, log-rows K
let witness = circuit.forward(&quantized_inputs)?;   // advice assignment
let proof = prove(pk, circuit, witness)?;
assert!(verify(vk, proof, &public_inputs));           // ms-scale, incl. EVM
```

Four properties matter. **Calibration is trusted**: scales are chosen by the compiler and the proof attests to the *quantized* model — scale selection must be audited or reproduced [4]. **The SRS is universal per size**: KZG needs $2^K$ SRS elements for log row count $K$; proving time, memory, and key generation all scale with $2^K$ [5][7]. **Visibility is per-tensor**: private inputs with public weights, private weights with public inputs, or fully public [4]. **EVM verification is practical**: constant-size KZG proofs verify at roughly 200,000–600,000 gas, making on-chain verifiable inference economical for high-value decisions [6].

### 4.4 Model weight commitments and recursive proof aggregation

A proof that "$y = f_\theta(x)$ for *some* $\theta$" is worthless if the prover substitutes models per query — the *model-switching attack*. The defense is a **weight commitment**: the owner publishes $\mathsf{cm}_\theta = \mathsf{Commit}(\theta)$ (KZG over weight tensors or a Merkle root over shards), taken as public instance, with advice-encoded weights constrained to open to it — fixed columns for public models, advice columns plus opening check for private ones [4][8]. Model rotation requires a new setup: exactly the audit event governance wants.

For serving many queries, **recursive aggregation** compresses $N$ inference proofs into one aggregate $\Pi$ implying all $N$: **folding** (Nova-style IVC) folds each proof into a running instance with one final SNARK, or **Halo2 recursion** verifies $N$ KZG proofs inside an aggregation circuit — pairing-heavy but constant in the underlying circuit sizes, so amortization wins for large batches. Either way, the aggregate binds every leaf to the same $\mathsf{cm}_\theta$, closing model-switching across the batch, and the on-chain verifier pays one gas check instead of $N$ [6] — the economics behind verifiable AI oracles and auditable biometric gates [2][3].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Measured cost landscape

Published figures trace a consistent curve [2][5][6][7]:

| System / model | Proving time | Verification | Proof size |
|---|---|---|---|
| ezkl, linear regression | 0.12 s | ms | ~KB [6] |
| ezkl, MNIST-scale MLP | seconds | ms | ~KB [2] |
| ezkl, multiclass network | 18.26 s (364.68 s full pipeline) | 0.24 s | ~KB [5] |
| ezkl, nanoGPT (embed 64) | ~5 min | — | — [7] |
| ezkl, nanoGPT (embed 144) | ~20 min | — | — [7] |
| zkLLM, 13B parameters | ~15 min | fast | < 200 KB [6] |
| NANOZK, per transformer layer | — | 24 ms | 5.5 KB/layer [6] |
| zkPyTorch/Expander, 8B | ~150 s/token | — | — [6] |

Three regularities emerge. **Proving is superlinear in model width**: nanoGPT proof generation grows from ~5 minutes at embedding size 64 to ~20 minutes at 144 — roughly quadratic, as expected for $O(n^2 d)$ attention with per-MAC range checks [7]. **The log row count $K$ is the master knob**: proving time and memory scale steeply with the preallocated $2^K$ rows, so right-sizing $K$ is the first optimization [7]. **Verification stays cheap** — milliseconds — which is the entire point: the prover pays minutes, the verifier almost nothing [5][6].

On-chain verification costs ~200,000–600,000 gas (~USD 3–18); a zkML MVP is estimated at 4–6 weeks and USD 40,000–80,000 [6].

### 5.2 End-to-end soundness

> **Theorem (end-to-end soundness):** If (i) KZG is binding, (ii) Halo2 is knowledge-sound, (iii) lookup tables exactly tabulate the quantized nonlinearities, and (iv) $\mathsf{cm}_\theta$ opens to the witness weights, then any PPT prover producing an accepting proof for $(\mathsf{cm}_\theta, x_{\text{pub}}, y_{\text{pub}})$ yields, via the SNARK extractor, a witness with $\hat{f}_\theta(x) = y$, except negligibly.

*Proof sketch.* Knowledge-soundness extracts advice satisfying every gate, lookup, and permutation constraint [8]. Linear gates enforce correct fixed-point matrix arithmetic modulo rescaling range checks; lookup soundness (Section 4.2) forces nonlinearity outputs onto their tables; the permutation argument wires layers consistently — so the advice trace is a genuine forward pass of $\hat{f}_\theta$. The opening check ties the trace's weights to $\mathsf{cm}_\theta$; KZG binding prevents equivocation. The fidelity theorem bounds distance to the float reference. ∎

The assumptions are explicit audit targets — table correctness, calibration honesty, SRS integrity — an improvement over blind trust in a model operator [1][3][4].

### 5.3 Zero-knowledge partitioning

Zero-knowledge follows from the SNARK: the verifier learns only the public instance plus the fact that a witness exists [8]. The subtlety is circuit-shape leakage — architecture, table dimensions, $K$ are public — and model-extraction via many queries, a rate-limiting problem orthogonal to the proof system [1][2].

## 6. Limitations

**Prover cost remains the fundamental barrier.** Minutes per transformer inference — ~5 min for nanoGPT at embedding 64, ~20 min at 144, ~15 min for 13B parameters with GPU acceleration — rules out interactive latency; per-query proving suits only high-value decisions [6][7]. Aggregation amortizes verification, not proving.

**Quantization changes the model.** The proof attests to $\hat{f}_\theta$, whose fidelity bounds do not eliminate float-reference divergence. Adversarial calibration data or distribution shift can move decisions while proofs stay valid [4][7]; mitigations (published calibration sets, deterministic scales, accuracy regressions) are procedural, not cryptographic.

**Trusted setup.** KZG needs an SRS sized to $2^K$; architecture changes altering $K$ need fresh ceremony output or a large universal SRS, plus new keys [5][8]. Transparent alternatives (FRI-based STARKs) remove the setup but pay larger proofs or slower verification here.

**Circuit shape leaks architecture:** dimensions, table sizes, and $K$ are public; padding to uniform sizes multiplies prover cost — a confidentiality/efficiency tension [1][2].

**Incomplete operator coverage.** Top-$k$ sampling, beam search, and data-dependent control flow resist arithmetization; zkML targets scoring and classification, not full autoregressive decoding (~150 s/token for GKR-based 8B) [6].

At 200k–600k gas per verification, low-stakes inferences cannot justify mainnet verification; aggregation and L2 deployment are required [6].

## 7. Conclusion

zkML is a triumph of arithmetization engineering: the primitives — SNARKs, polynomial commitments, lookup arguments — are largely settled, and the frontier is the faithful, economical translation of neural computation into their language. Quantized fixed-point encodings reduce float networks to integer arithmetic with a provable error budget; Halo2 lookup arguments reduce transcendental nonlinearities to subset arguments over precomputed tables; ezkl-style pipelines automate ONNX-to-circuit lowering while exposing calibration and SRS management as explicit trust boundaries; weight commitments close the model-switching attack; and recursive aggregation makes batched verification economical [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8].

Empirically: seconds for MNIST-scale models, tens of seconds for multiclass networks, minutes for nanoGPT-scale transformers, ~15 minutes for 13B parameters with GPU-accelerated lookups — always with millisecond verification and kilobyte proofs [5][6][7]. Freivalds-style checks alone deliver order-of-magnitude savings: the biggest wins come from *not* proving the naive circuit [2].

Whether zkML becomes general infrastructure turns on three directions: **lookup-centric hardware acceleration** (grand products, MSMs on GPUs/ASICs) [6]; **standardized quantization semantics** making calibration reproducible [4][7]; and **aggregation-native serving** with fleets designed around batched proving [2][6]. The deeper significance is architectural: verifiable inference converts "trust our model API" into a checkable claim any party — user, auditor, smart contract — can verify without seeing private inputs or proprietary weights.
