# Speculative Execution Vulnerabilities and Their Mitigations: Spectre and Meltdown Microarchitectures, Retpoline and IBRS Defenses, and Formal Models of Transient Execution

## Abstract

This thesis presents a comprehensive treatment of speculative execution vulnerabilities — the class of transient execution attacks inaugurated by Spectre [1] and Meltdown [2] — and of the mitigations engineered to contain them. We formalize the microarchitectural conditions under which mispredicted or faulting instructions leave secret-dependent residue in shared microarchitectural state, develop a taxonomy distinguishing bounds-check bypass, branch-target injection, return-stack-buffer underflow, speculative store bypass, and rogue data-cache loads [3], and analyze in depth the principal defenses: retpoline return trampolines, indirect-branch-restricted speculation (IBRS and enhanced IBRS), single-thread indirect-branch predictors (STIBP), speculative-store-bypass disable (SSBD), serialization barriers, and kernel page-table isolation (KPTI). We further examine formal frameworks for reasoning about transient execution — speculative non-interference, two-trace symbolic gadget detection in the style of Spectector, and contract-based hardware/software co-design — and survey empirical measurement results across Intel, AMD, and ARM platforms. We conclude with open problems: performance-security co-design, the completeness of deployed mitigations against evolving variants such as Retbleed and Branch History Injection, and the scalability of formal verification.

---

## 1 Introduction

Modern high-performance processors depend on *speculative execution*: the practice of executing instructions before all of their operands and control-flow preconditions are known, discarding the results if the speculation proves incorrect. Branch prediction, out-of-order execution, value prediction, and memory disambiguation collectively contribute the majority of single-thread performance in contemporary cores, and their removal would regress performance by factors commonly estimated at two to five on branch-heavy workloads. The instruction-set architecture (ISA) contract, however, describes only *committed* behavior: instructions that are issued speculatively but subsequently squashed are, by the architectural definition, as if they never executed.

The discovery of Spectre and Meltdown in early 2018 shattered the assumption that squashed instructions are observably inert [1][2]. A transient instruction — one that executes speculatively and is later discarded — can alter *microarchitectural* state: cache occupancy, branch-predictor tables, TLB entries, and port contention. Because that state is shared across security domains and persists beyond the squash, it constitutes a covert channel through which secret data, accessed only during the transient window, can be exfiltrated. The canonical construction has three phases:

1. **Setup.** The attacker mistrains a predictor (pattern history table, branch target buffer, return stack buffer) or arranges a faulting access to privileged memory.
2. **Transient execution.** A mispredicted branch or an out-of-order faulting load executes gadget instructions that encode a secret into microarchitectural state, e.g. `array2[secret * 4096]`.
3. **Transmission.** The attacker measures the residual state — typically via *Flush+Reload* cache timing — to recover the secret byte by byte.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the microarchitectural background: prediction structures, the memory hierarchy as a side channel, and the distinction between the architectural and microarchitectural state. Section 3 describes our methodology: a literature-driven formalization grounded in primary sources [1][2][3][4][5], an analysis of vendor mitigation guidance [4][5], and a survey of detection tooling. Section 4 is the deep dive: the Spectre variant taxonomy, the Meltdown fault-window mechanism, the defense zoo (retpoline, IBRS/eIBRS, STIBP, SSBD, lfence, KPTI), and formal models of transient execution. Section 5 presents empirical and formal results; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

> **Theorem (Transient leakage principle):** Let $M$ be a processor implementing speculative execution with rollback of architectural state only. If a transient instruction sequence $T$ is a function of secret state $s$ and observably perturbs shared microarchitectural state $\mu$, then there exists an adversary procedure $A$ that, by repeatedly triggering $T$ and measuring $\mu$, recovers $s$ with probability approaching $1$ as the number of repetitions grows, provided the measurement channel's error rate is bounded below $1/2$.

The remainder of this work makes this principle concrete, then asks how it can be broken — by hardware, by software, or by proof.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Speculative execution and prediction structures

A modern out-of-order core predicts control flow at several levels. The **pattern history table (PHT)** records the taken/not-taken history of conditional branches and is indexed by branch address (and, on many cores, by global history); the **branch target buffer (BTB)** caches the destinations of indirect branches; the **return stack buffer (RSB)** predicts return addresses from a small hardware call stack; and the **memory disambiguator** speculates that loads do not alias with older in-flight stores. Each structure is trained by past execution — including execution in other privilege levels or even other processes — and each can be deliberately mistrained by an adversary who shares the physical core [1][3].

Crucially, predictor state is *not* rolled back on a pipeline flush. When the branch resolves and the misprediction is detected, architectural registers and memory are restored, but the cache lines touched by transient loads remain cached, and the predictor tables retain whatever the transient path wrote into them. This asymmetry — architectural rollback without microarchitectural rollback — is the root cause of the entire vulnerability class.

### 2.2 The cache as a transmission medium

Transient instructions cannot commit their results to registers or memory, but they can issue *loads*, and loads change cache state. The **Flush+Reload** technique [6] provides the canonical receiver: the attacker flushes a set of shared cache lines (e.g., via `clflush`, or eviction on architectures lacking it), triggers the transient sequence, and then times reloads of each line. A fast reload ($\approx$ tens of cycles) indicates the line was cached by the victim's transient access; a slow reload ($\approx$ hundreds of cycles) indicates it was not. By arranging 256 probe lines — one per possible byte value, spaced by a cache-line stride and multiplied by a large constant such as 4096 to defeat the adjacent-line and stride prefetchers — the attacker converts one transient secret byte into one cache hit, achieving exfiltration rates on the order of kilobytes per second in native code [1].

### 2.3 Threat models and the systematization

Canella et al. systematize transient execution attacks along two axes: the *cause* of transient execution (misprediction vs. fault/assist) and the *domain crossing* being violated [3]. Spectre-type attacks exploit mispredicted control or data flow and read the *victim's own* address space — they break intra-process isolation, sandboxing, and JIT boundaries. Meltdown-type attacks exploit deferred fault handling and read memory the attacker could *never* legally address — they break privilege separation and address-space isolation outright. This distinction determines which mitigations apply: Meltdown is closed by unmapping the kernel (KPTI/KAISER) [2], whereas Spectre requires constraining speculation itself, a fundamentally harder problem [4].

| Attack | Cause of transient window | Secret source | Primary mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spectre-PHT (v1) | Conditional branch mispredict | Victim address space | `lfence`, index masking |
| Spectre-BTB (v2) | Indirect branch target injection | Victim (incl. kernel) | retpoline, IBRS/eIBRS, IBPB |
| Spectre-RSB | Return address mispredict | Victim address space | RSB stuffing, retpoline |
| Spectre-STL (v4) | Speculative store bypass | Victim address space | SSBD, compiler barriers |
| Meltdown (v3) | Deferred privilege-check fault | Kernel / physical memory | KPTI (KAISER) |
| Foreshadow/L1TF (v3a) | Deferred terminal-fault handling | L1D-resident data | L1D flush, PTE inversion |
| MDS (v4-class) | Microarchitectural buffer sampling | Fill buffers, store buffers | VERW / buffer overwrite |

## 3 Methodology

Our investigation proceeds along three complementary lines, mirroring the structure of the primary literature [1][2][3][4][5].

First, **microarchitectural mechanism analysis**: we reconstruct, from the original papers and vendor guidance, the precise pipeline behavior underlying each variant — which predictor is mistrained, what the transient window permits, and which microarchitectural resource serves as the covert channel. Where the literature reports quantitative results (leakage rates, overhead figures), we report them with their experimental context.

Second, **mitigation analysis**: we examine each defense at the level of mechanism rather than marketing — what invariant it establishes, which variants it covers, and where its coverage ends. Retpoline's return-trampoline construction is analyzed instruction by instruction [5]; IBRS/eIBRS and STIBP are analyzed as predictor-mode restrictions [4]; and software barriers are analyzed as speculation fences with precise placement rules.

Third, **formal methods survey**: we review the emerging program-logic and symbolic-execution approaches to transient-execution security — speculative non-interference, the two-trace methodology of Spectector, and contract-based frameworks that apportion responsibility between hardware and compiler. Our criterion throughout is *soundness under the stated model*: a mitigation is only as strong as the microarchitectural assumptions it encodes.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Spectre variant taxonomy: mistraining predictors

**Spectre-PHT (variant 1, bounds-check bypass).** The original and most pervasive variant mistrains the conditional-branch predictor [1]. Consider the canonical gadget:

```c
if (x < array1_size)              // (A) bounds check, trained "taken"
    y = array2[array1[x] * 4096]; // (B) transient out-of-bounds read + encode
```

The attacker first supplies a series of in-bounds values of `x`, training the PHT entry for branch (A) to predict *taken*. The branch condition depends on `array1_size`, which the attacker arranges to be evicted from cache, so resolution stalls for hundreds of cycles. The attacker then supplies a malicious out-of-bounds `x`; the predictor guesses *taken*, and the transient execution of (B) reads `array1[x]` — an arbitrary byte of the victim's address space — and touches `array2[byte * 4096]`, encoding the byte into the cache. When `array1_size` finally arrives, the branch resolves *not taken*, the pipeline flushes, but the cache residue remains for Flush+Reload recovery. Kocher et al. demonstrated this end-to-end in native code and, more alarmingly, from JavaScript in browsers, proving that software sandboxes whose security rests on bounds checks are transiently porous [1][7].

**Spectre-BTB (variant 2, branch-target injection).** Here the attacker poisons the *branch target buffer*: by executing indirect branches at congruent BTB indices (aliasing on low address bits, which are shared across privilege levels on many microarchitectures), the attacker plants a target address of their choosing. When the victim later executes an indirect branch that aliases the poisoned entry, the frontend transiently redirects control to the attacker's gadget — executing with the victim's privileges and register state — before the true target resolves [1]. This is the variant that threatened kernel isolation most directly, because it allows a user-space attacker to steer *kernel* transient execution. Its mitigation story (retpoline, IBRS) is the subject of §4.3.

**Spectre-RSB (return-stack-buffer).** Returns are predicted from the RSB, a small hardware stack of return addresses. On RSB underflow (more returns than calls, or context-switch pollution), some microarchitectures fall back to the BTB — re-exposing branch-target injection through returns. The Retbleed work later showed that return instructions could be systematically coerced into BTB-like prediction, defeating naive retpoline deployments and forcing a migration to retpoline-plus-call-depth-tracking or eIBRS [4].

**Spectre-STL (variant 4, speculative store bypass).** The memory disambiguator may predict that a load does not depend on an older, address-unresolved store, allowing the load to execute early and read a *stale* value. If software performed a store intended to sanitize or redact a value and a subsequent load is speculatively served the pre-store value, the stale secret can be transiently encoded into the cache. Mitigation requires either disabling speculative store bypass in hardware (SSBD) or inserting barriers between the store and the load in security-critical code [4].

### 4.2 Meltdown: the deferred fault and the transient window

Meltdown exploits a different pipeline behavior: on Intel microarchitectures of the era, a load that will fault (e.g., a user-mode load from a kernel-mapped page, where the supervisor bit denies access) was permitted to execute *out of order* and forward its data to dependent micro-operations before the fault was raised at retirement [2]. The permission check was, in effect, deferred to commit time. The attack sequence is:

```c
// rcx = kernel address, rbx = probe array (user-accessible)
mov al, byte [rcx]        // (1) faults, but executes transiently; AL = secret
shl rax, 12               // (2) scale by page size (defeat prefetcher)
mov rbx, qword [rbx+rax]  // (3) encode secret byte into cache
```

Instruction (1) raises a general-protection fault, but only at retirement; meanwhile (2) and (3) execute transiently with the secret value already in `AL`. The fault is handled (or suppressed via signal handling, TSX transaction abort, or speculative fault suppression), and the attacker recovers the byte with Flush+Reload over the probe array. Lipp et al. reported dumping kernel memory at rates up to ~500 KB/s with error rates below 0.1% after error correction, and demonstrated reads across process, VM, and even SGX-enclave boundaries on affected silicon [2].

The enabling condition was an OS design choice with deep roots: for performance, operating systems mapped the entire kernel into *every* process's address space, relying on the supervisor bit for protection. KAISER — originally designed to mitigate KASLR side channels — unmaps the kernel from user page tables (keeping only a minimal trampoline), and its deployment as KPTI (Kernel Page-Table Isolation) closed Meltdown on all major operating systems at a cost of measurable syscall-path overhead [2]. Later Meltdown-class variants (Foreshadow/L1TF, Meltdown-PK, Meltdown-BND) extended the deferred-fault observation to terminal faults, protection keys, and bound-range registers, and were catalogued systematically by Canella et al. [3].

> **Theorem (Fault-deferral leakage):** If a processor forwards the result of a faulting load to dependent operations before the fault is delivered at retirement, and if the faulting address is mapped in the attacker's page tables (regardless of permission bits), then the attacker can construct a transient sequence encoding arbitrary bytes of the faulting page into microarchitectural state. The defense of unmapping sensitive pages (KPTI) removes the necessary precondition.

### 4.3 Defense mechanisms: retpoline, IBRS/eIBRS, STIBP, SSBD, barriers, KPTI

**Retpoline (return trampoline).** Conceived at Google and documented by Intel as a software construct requiring no microcode, retpoline replaces every indirect call and jump with a sequence that performs the control transfer via a *return* instruction whose speculative behavior is constrained [5]. The canonical x86-64 sequence is:

```nasm
call set_up_target        ; pushes return address of "capture_spec" onto stack
capture_spec:
    pause                 ; speculative trap: infinite loop
    jmp capture_spec
set_up_target:
    mov %r11, (%rsp)      ; overwrite return address with real target (in r11)
    ret                   ; architecturally returns to target;
                          ; speculatively, the RSB predicts "capture_spec"
```
## 5 Empirical Analysis and Formal Guarantees

Empirically, the systematization of Canella et al. remains the broadest measurement study: proof-of-concept implementations of each attack class were executed across Intel, AMD, and ARM parts, uncovering six previously overlooked transient-execution effects (Meltdown-PK, Meltdown-BND, and four new Spectre mistraining strategies) and demonstrating that most deployed defenses — including microcode patches — failed to mitigate the full variant space at the time of evaluation [3]. Key quantitative findings from the primary literature include:

- **Leakage bandwidth.** Native-code Spectre-PHT exfiltration on the order of 10 KB/s; JavaScript-based variants roughly an order of magnitude slower but sufficient to read process memory cross-origin before browser mitigations (site isolation, timer coarsening) [1][7]. Meltdown dumping kernel memory at up to ~500 KB/s [2].
- **Mitigation overhead.** KPTI imposed single-digit to low-double-digit percentage overhead on syscall-heavy workloads, largely amortized by PCID support [2]. Retpoline's overhead on the Windows kernel was characterized as near noise-level for most scenarios, versus the substantially higher cost of IBRS toggling on kernel entry [5]. Retbleed mitigations, by contrast, were measured at 13–39% on affected workloads, with mitigations for related "phantom jump" effects exceeding 100% — a vivid illustration of the performance cost of closing speculation channels after the fact [4].
- **Variant half-life.** New variants continued to emerge years after the initial disclosures — Retbleed (2022) defeating retpoline-era assumptions about returns, Branch History Injection (2022) poisoning global history rather than targets, and subsequent work on training-solo and indirect-target-selection effects catalogued in Intel's 2026 guidance [4]. Each new variant re-opened portions of the assumed-closed attack surface.

On the formal side, the guarantees actually achieved are *conditional*: Spectector-style tools prove gadget absence *relative to a bounded speculation window and an abstract cache model*; speculative-load-hardening is verified *assuming the compiler's barrier placement matches the hardware's speculation semantics*; and hardware contracts are verified *against a leakage model*, not against silicon. The gap between the model and the silicon — the predictor behaviors vendors decline to document — is where residual risk lives. The MIT lecture synthesis frames this as the central open architectural question: whether "Architecture 2.0" can define correctness to include the *absence* of secret-dependent microarchitectural observations, rather than merely correct committed state [6].

## 6 Limitations

This treatment has several boundaries that must be stated explicitly.

1. **Model incompleteness.** All formal results surveyed are relative to abstract microarchitectural models. Real predictors (TAGE, perceptron-based, loop predictors) and real memory hierarchies (with prefetchers, fill buffers, and store buffers) exhibit behaviors outside every published model; the MDS class of vulnerabilities — sampling stale data from microarchitectural buffers rather than caches — demonstrated that even the *channel* taxonomy was incomplete [3][4].
2. **Tool scalability.** Symbolic two-trace analysis does not scale to operating-system kernels or browsers; deployed gadget detection relies on unsound heuristics (taint tracking, pattern matching) with both false positives and false negatives [3].
3. **Performance-security tension.** The mitigations with the strongest guarantees (disabling speculation features wholesale, e.g., SSBD or comprehensive `lfence` insertion) carry the highest costs, creating persistent deployment pressure to accept weaker, variant-specific defenses — the exact dynamic that Retbleed and BHI exploited [4].
4. **Scope.** We treat transient execution *confidentiality* violations; transient execution *integrity* effects (e.g., speculative interference with control-flow integrity enforcement) and the interaction of speculation with trusted execution environments beyond the surveyed SGX work are largely out of scope. Side channels not rooted in speculation (power, EM) are excluded by design.

## 7 Conclusion

Spectre and Meltdown revealed that the ISA-level contract — the fiction that only committed instructions matter — is insufficient for security on speculative microarchitectures [1][2]. The industry's response unfolded in three waves: emergency software containment (KPTI, retpoline, barriers) [2][5], hardware predictor controls (IBRS/eIBRS, STIBP, SSBD, IBPB) [4], and an ongoing formal-methods effort to define and verify what secure speculation even means [3]. The systematization literature shows that ad-hoc, variant-at-a-time patching leaves residual attack surface, and that each newly documented predictor behavior re-opens supposedly settled questions [3][4].

The durable lessons are threefold. First, *microarchitectural state is state*: any resource shared across a trust boundary and not rolled back on misspeculation is a potential channel, and security analysis must model it as such. Second, *defense in depth is mandatory but not sufficient*: the mitigation stack in §4.3 closes known variants only under documented hardware assumptions, and undocumented predictor behaviors have repeatedly violated those assumptions. Third, *contracts must be explicit*: until hardware vendors publish complete, checkable leakage contracts and compilers are verified against them, transient-execution security will remain an empirical arms race rather than an engineering discipline. The path from the 2018 disclosures to a principled "Architecture 2.0" [6] — in which processors are correct by construction against speculative leakage — remains the field's defining challenge.
