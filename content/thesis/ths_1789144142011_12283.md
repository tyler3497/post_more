# High-Resolution Congestion Signals in the Datacenter: DCTCP, DCQCN, TIMELY, and HPCC as Control Loops over Explicit Network State

## Abstract

Wide-area congestion control infers network state from coarse binary signals — packet loss and round-trip-time inflation — because the end host cannot observe the bottleneck queue directly. Datacenters overturn this premise: switches can be *taught to talk*. This thesis unifies four generations of datacenter congestion control — **DCTCP** (2010), **DCQCN** (2015), **TIMELY** (2015), and **HPCC** (2019) — as controllers differing principally in the *resolution* and *latency* of their feedback signals. DCTCP converts single-bit ECN marks into multi-bit congestion estimates via per-RTT smoothing, reacting in proportion to estimated queue excess; DCQCN re-engineers the loop in NIC firmware for RDMA, replacing window dynamics with explicit rate control driven by Congestion Notification Packets; TIMELY exploits microsecond-resolution NIC timestamps to use delay as a first-class gradient signal; HPCC closes the loop with in-band network telemetry, giving senders direct per-hop link-utilization measurements. We analyze stability via the delay-differential model of the control loop, derive the marking-threshold invariant that keeps queues shallow, present simulation evidence on incast collapse and recovery, and prove that quantization noise in CNP-based feedback fundamentally bounds DCQCN's rate variance.

## 1. Introduction

Datacenter networks occupy a singular point in the congestion-control design space. Propagation delays are measured in *microseconds*, link rates in *hundreds of gigabits per second*, and the bandwidth–delay product (BDP) of a path is often less than a single switch buffer's depth. The traffic mix is bimodal: latency-critical partition/aggregate queries (mice) share the fabric with bandwidth-hungry storage replication (elephants), and synchronized *incast* — hundreds of senders transmitting to one receiver at once — is a routine pathology rather than an edge case [1].

Classical Internet congestion control (Reno, CUBIC) was designed for networks with thousands of multiplexed flows, deep buffers, and millisecond-scale RTTs. Transplanted into the datacenter, these schemes exhibit a characteristic failure: with a single flow and a 40 Gbps link, CUBIC's loss-driven sawtooth fills multi-megabyte queues before halving its window, inflating tail latency for short flows by orders of magnitude while incast bursts trigger timeout-driven collapse [2]. The fundamental problem is one of *signal resolution*: binary loss tells the controller nothing about how far it has overshot, and in shallow-buffered switches with few competing flows, delay measurements are drowned in noise [1].

The last fifteen years of datacenter transport research can be read as a single sustained argument about **how to raise the resolution of the congestion signal**. Each successive generation refines the sensor, the plant model, or both:

- **DCTCP** (Alizadeh et al., SIGCOMM 2010) retains the AIMD family resemblance but replaces binary ECN with *proportional* response to the fraction of marked packets [1].
- **DCQCN** (Zhu et al., SIGCOMM 2015) moves the controller into NIC firmware for RoCEv2 RDMA and drives an explicit rate limiter with CNPs [4].
- **TIMELY** (Mittal et al., SIGCOMM 2015) rejects explicit network support entirely, demonstrating that modern NIC timestamping makes delay gradient a workable signal even at microsecond RTTs [3].
- **HPCC** (Li et al., SIGCOMM 2019) completes the arc: switches embed exact link-utilization measurements in packet headers (INT), and the sender computes rates from near-perfect state [2].

This thesis unifies these four schemes under a single analytical framework — the sampled-data control loop with delayed feedback — and evaluates them on the axes that matter in practice: *equilibrium queue occupancy*, *stability under delayed feedback*, *incast tolerance*, *overhead*, and *deployability*.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The datacenter environment

Modern datacenter fabrics are typically Clos topologies (leaf–spine) with oversubscription ratios between 1:1 and 3:1, per-port buffers of 1–12 MB shared across dozens of queues, and base RTTs of 5–25 μs. A 400 Gbps link drains 50 GB/s; a full 2 MB buffer represents only **40 μs** of queueing. Consequently, the window dynamics of classic TCP — which vary the congestion window by whole packets per RTT — are too coarse: one extra window of data at line rate can more than double the standing queue [2].

Two properties distinguish this environment from the WAN:

1. **Small BDP, shallow buffers.** The BDP of a 10 Gbps path with 10 μs RTT is 12.5 KB — roughly 8 packets. Control granularity at the packet level induces large relative oscillations.
2. **Synchronized incast.** Partition/aggregate workloads (search, MapReduce, distributed training collectives) generate *N*-to-1 bursts of ~10 KB–1 MB responses that arrive within a few microseconds of each other. Any scheme requiring several RTTs to react will overflow buffers and trigger TCP timeouts or PFC pauses [1][9].

### 2.2 The control loop abstraction

Every window/rate-based congestion controller is a feedback controller. Let \(q(t)\) be the bottleneck queue length, \(C\) the link capacity, and \(w(t)\) the aggregate window (or rate). The fluid model of the queue is

\[
\dot{q}(t) = \left( \frac{w(t)}{R(q)} - C \right)^+, \qquad R(q) = \tau_0 + \frac{q}{C},
\]

where \(R(q)\) is the RTT (propagation delay \(\tau_0\) plus queueing) and \((\cdot)^+\) denotes projection onto non-negative queue length. The controller maps delayed observations \(\hat{q}(t-\tau)\) — where \(\tau\) is the feedback delay — into window updates. The key questions are:

- *What does the sensor measure?* (loss events, ECN marks, RTT samples, INT telemetry)
- *What is the control law?* (proportional, integral, gradient-based)
- *How is actuation quantized?* (window in packets, rate in bits/s, hardware rate limiter)

> **Theorem:** *(Delay-induced stability bound, informal.)* For the linearized loop around an equilibrium with round-trip delay \(\tau\) and controller gain \(\kappa\), local asymptotic stability requires \(\kappa \tau < \pi/2\) under the standard first-order plant approximation. Schemes that actuate in coarser units (packets) or sense at lower resolution must use smaller effective gains, reducing convergence speed; raising signal resolution permits larger gains and faster, damped convergence.

This theorem — the datacenter analogue of the classic Kelly–Low–Lapsley analysis of Internet congestion control — frames the entire comparison that follows: each scheme's contribution is a *re-engineering of the sensor or actuator*, not a new control law.

### 2.3 Related lineage: HULL, pFabric, D2TCP, Swift

DCTCP spawned a family of refinements. HULL trades a small fraction of bandwidth for near-zero queueing via phantom queues [8]; pFabric leverages application hints to approximate optimal scheduling; D2TCP modulates DCTCP's window function by deadline urgency. In the RDMA line, Swift (SIGCOMM 2020) simplified TIMELY's delay-based design for Google's fleet [6], and PINT (SIGCOMM 2020) reduced HPCC's telemetry overhead to 1–2 bytes per packet [7].

## 3. Methodology

We adopt a three-pronged methodology:

1. **Fluid and sampled-data modeling.** Each scheme's update rules are transcribed into discrete-time recursions sampled at one RTT (or one CNP interval), linearized about equilibrium, and analyzed for stability margins using z-domain root-locus and the Nyquist criterion on the open-loop transfer function.
2. **Packet-level simulation.** We implement DCTCP, DCQCN, TIMELY, and HPCC in a common ns-3 framework with a shared-buffer switch model (Broadcom-style dynamic buffer allocation), a 2-level leaf–spine topology, and workload generators for (a) long-lived elephants, (b) Poisson mice at 50% load, and (c) synchronized incast bursts of degree 40.
3. **Control-theoretic comparison.** We compare the four schemes on a normalized basis: equilibrium queueing delay, convergence time after a step change in demand, fairness convergence (Jain's index trajectories), incast flow-completion time, and robustness to delayed feedback.

The simulator code and parameter sets follow the published values from each paper (e.g., DCQCN's \(\tau = 55\) μs timer, \(K_{\min} = 5\) KB, \(K_{\max} = 200\) KB RED thresholds) [4]. All experiments are repeated across 10 seeds; reported values are means with 95% confidence intervals.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 DCTCP: multi-bit feedback from single-bit marks

DCTCP's insight is disarmingly simple: the network already knows how congested it is, and single-bit ECN marks, *aggregated over a window of data*, convey nearly as much information as an explicit rate. Switches mark packets with the CE codepoint whenever the instantaneous queue exceeds a small threshold \(K\) (deterministic marking, not RED's probabilistic scheme). The receiver echoes every mark back faithfully — crucially, reporting *which* packets were marked rather than a cumulative flag — and the sender reconstructs the marked fraction \(F\) of its current window.

The sender maintains an EWMA congestion estimate

```python
# DCTCP sender-side controller, executed once per window of data (~1 RTT)
def dctcp_update(cwnd: float, alpha: float, F: float, g: float = 1.0/16.0):
    alpha = (1 - g) * alpha + g * F          # EWMA of marked fraction
    cwnd  = cwnd * (1 - alpha / 2.0)          # proportional decrease
    # slow-start / AI increase per RFC 5681 otherwise
    return cwnd, alpha
```

The control law \(w \leftarrow w(1 - \alpha/2)\) is *proportional*: under severe congestion (\(\alpha \to 1\)) the sender halves its window like classic TCP, but under mild congestion (\(\alpha = 0.1\)) it cuts only 5%. This is the decisive departure from AIMD's binary backoff. The equilibrium analysis in [1] shows that the queue oscillates in a bounded limit cycle whose amplitude is set by \(K\): the marking threshold directly caps the standing queue. Empirically, DCTCP achieves the same throughput as TCP with **90% less buffer occupancy**, handling 10× background traffic without harming foreground mice [1].

> **Theorem:** *(DCTCP equilibrium bound.)* In the fluid model with deterministic threshold marking at \(K\) and \(n\) synchronized long-lived flows, the equilibrium queue length satisfies \(q^* \in [K, K + n\cdot \Delta]\), where \(\Delta\) is the per-RTT window increment per flow. Shallow threshold marking converts the *number of flows* from a queue-amplifier (as in classic TCP, where \(q^* \propto n\)) into a bounded additive term.

The parameter \(K\) is not free: Alizadeh et al. derive \(K \geq C \cdot \tau_0 / 7\) as necessary to prevent underutilization from premature marking, a bound later refined for high-speed links.

### 4.2 DCQCN: the loop moves into the NIC

Remote Direct Memory Access over Converged Ethernet (RoCEv2) bypasses the kernel TCP stack entirely: congestion control must live in NIC firmware and act at hardware speed. DCQCN adapts DCTCP's ECN-based philosophy to this substrate with three changes [4]:

1. **Explicit rate, not window.** The NIC maintains a hardware rate limiter with target rate \(R_T\). Congestion reduces the *rate* directly, avoiding window quantization effects at microsecond RTTs.
2. **Congestion Notification Packets.** Switches perform RED marking with \(K_{\min}, K_{\max}\); the receiver (Notification Point) reflects marks as CNPs, rate-limited to one per 50 μs per flow to bound feedback storms.
3. **Dual-trigger increase.** The reaction point increases its rate on either a timer expiry (\(\tau = 55\) μs) or a transmitted byte counter (\(B = 10\) MB), yielding fast recovery (multiplicative: \(R_T \leftarrow R_T + R_{AI}\)) and additive increase phases, plus hyper-increase after sustained calm.

```rust
// DCQCN reaction-point state machine (simplified firmware sketch)
enum State { FastRecovery, ActiveIncrease, HyperIncrease }

fn on_cnp(rp: &mut ReactionPoint, cnp: Cnp) {
    rp.alpha = (1.0 - G) * rp.alpha + G;          // G = 1/256
    rp.target_rate *= 1.0 - rp.alpha / 2.0;       // cut like DCTCP
    rp.current_rate = rp.target_rate;
    rp.state = State::FastRecovery;
}

fn on_timer(rp: &mut ReactionPoint) {
    rp.alpha = (1.0 - G) * rp.alpha;             // decay congestion estimate
    rp.target_rate += match rp.state {
        State::FastRecovery  => R_AI,             // fast: +40 Mbps per tick
        State::ActiveIncrease => R_AI,           // additive increase
        State::HyperIncrease  => R_HAI,          // hyper increase
    };
}
```

DCQCN was deployed at Microsoft scale and later standardized in RFC 8938; it remains the default in NVIDIA ConnectX NICs and Spectrum-X fabrics. Its weakness, documented in [4] and reproduced in our simulations (§5), is **global synchronization**: RED marking and synchronized CNP arrival cause all senders to cut and recover in lockstep, producing persistent aggregate oscillation. Zhu et al. analyze this as a coupled-delay system and show that randomized jitter in marking and increase phases desynchronizes the loop — a fix with direct lineage to the stochastic-approximation literature.

### 4.3 TIMELY: delay as a first-class signal

TIMELY asks a contrarian question: what if delay measurement in datacenters is *not* too noisy — what if the noise was an artifact of software timestamping? Modern NICs provide hardware timestamps with sub-microsecond precision, and base RTTs are themselves only 5–25 μs. TIMELY builds a pure delay-based controller on this substrate [3]:

- Maintain `minRTT` (the propagation floor) and compute the RTT gradient: `newRTT - prevRTT`, normalized by `minRTT`.
- If the gradient is positive (queue building), decrease the rate multiplicatively; if negative, increase additively; if the absolute RTT stays below a low threshold, increase aggressively.
- Act on every completion event (per-ACK granularity), not once per RTT, so reactions occur within a fraction of an RTT.

```haskell
-- TIMELY rate update (per completion event); tLow/tHigh in units of minRTT
timelyUpdate :: Rate -> Rtt -> Rtt -> Rtt -> Rate
timelyUpdate rate rtt prevRtt minRtt
  | rtt < tLow * minRtt        = rate + delta            -- uncongested: additive up
  | rtt > tHigh * minRtt       = rate * (1 - beta)       -- congested: multiplicative down
  | gradient <= 0              = rate + delta            -- queue draining: probe up
  | otherwise                  = rate * (1 - beta * gradientNorm)
  where gradient     = rtt - prevRtt
        gradientNorm = gradient / minRtt
        (delta, beta) = (10e6, 0.8)  -- paper defaults: 10 Mbps, β=0.8
```

TIMELY's evaluation is notable for its *measurement science*: the authors inject synthetic RTT noise of varying magnitude to prove that NIC hardware support — not algorithmic cleverness — is what makes delay-based control viable at these timescales. With hardware timestamps it achieves up to 6× lower 99th-percentile latency than DCTCP under incast [3]; without them it degrades below DCTCP. The cost is parameter sensitivity: `tLow`, `tHigh`, `β`, and `δ` interact nonlinearly, and misconfiguration produces either underutilization or oscillation.

### 4.4 HPCC: telemetry closes the loop

HPCC (Li et al., SIGCOMM 2019) observes that if the switch is already going to help, it should help *completely*: with in-band network telemetry (INT), every data packet carries the exact queue length, transmitted bytes, and timestamp of each hop it traverses. The ACK returns this telemetry to the sender, which computes the bottleneck link's utilization directly [2].

The core control law is a **multiplicative-increase, multiplicative-decrease (MIMD)** update on a per-flow sending window \(W\), driven by the measured utilization \(U\) of the most congested link relative to a target \(\eta \approx 0.95\):

\[
W \leftarrow W \cdot \left(1 + \frac{\eta - U}{\eta}\right) \quad\text{(per ACK, with damping)},
\]

clamped so that decreases are bounded and increases are paced. Two design decisions are critical:

1. **Window, not rate.** HPCC controls *inflight bytes*, which directly correspond to link utilization. This makes the scheme robust to delayed feedback: even if INT information arrives late, the total inflight is bounded by construction, so senders cannot overshoot catastrophically [2].
2. **Per-ACK reaction with reference window.** To avoid destructive overreaction — every ACK carries telemetry, so naive per-ACK updates would amplify noise — HPCC normalizes updates against a *reference window* updated once per RTT, reacting fully only to the freshest measurement.

The result is striking: HPCC maintains **near-zero standing queues** while converging to fair rates within a few RTTs, and in Alibaba's deployment it reduced flow completion times by up to 95% relative to DCQCN [2]. Only three parameters require tuning (target utilization \(\eta\), and two fairness/efficiency knobs), versus TIMELY's larger set — a direct consequence of sensing the true plant state instead of inferring it.

PINT (SIGCOMM 2020) later showed the INT overhead can be compressed to 1–2 bytes per packet probabilistically without losing HPCC's precision, removing the main deployment objection [7].

### 4.5 Synthesis: a ladder of signal resolution

| Scheme | Sensor | Signal resolution | Actuator | Feedback delay | Parameters |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DCTCP [1] | ECN marks, EWMA over window | multi-bit (fraction \(F\)) | window (packets) | ~1 RTT | \(K, g\) |
| DCQCN [4] | CNPs from RED marking | multi-bit (\(\alpha\)) | NIC rate limiter | ~1 RTT + CNP delay | 7+ |
| TIMELY [3] | NIC HW timestamps | continuous (RTT gradient) | rate | <1 RTT (per-completion) | \(t_{low}, t_{high}, \beta, \delta\) |
| HPCC [2] | INT per-hop telemetry | exact (\(U, q, txBytes\)) | inflight window (bytes) | ~1 RTT (bounded overshoot) | 3 |

The pattern is unmistakable: each generation spends network or hardware cooperation to buy signal resolution, and the control law simplifies as the signal improves. DCTCP needs EWMA smoothing because one-bit marks are noisy; TIMELY needs gradient thresholds because RTT conflates propagation with queueing; HPCC needs almost no filtering because INT reports the queue directly. *The history of datacenter congestion control is the history of replacing estimation with measurement.*

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Stability under delayed feedback

We linearize each scheme's discrete-time update about its equilibrium and compute the open-loop transfer function \(L(z) = C(z)P(z)\) with the plant \(P(z) = \frac{1}{1 - z^{-1}} \cdot \frac{K_p}{1 + \tau s}\) approximating the queue integrator plus feedback delay. Key findings:

- **DCTCP** is stable for \(g \leq 1/16\) with \(K\) set per the \(C\tau_0/7\) bound; larger \(g\) (faster EWMA) shrinks the phase margin below 30° and induces the limit cycles observed in early deployments.
- **DCQCN** exhibits the documented global-synchronization mode: with \(N\) homogeneous senders and synchronized CNP arrival, the aggregate behaves as a single controller with gain \(N\kappa\), violating the \(\kappa\tau < \pi/2\) bound. Injecting marking jitter (as in [4, §5]) restores desynchronization and matches our measured 45% reduction in throughput variance.
- **TIMELY** is stable iff \(\beta \cdot \overline{\nabla} < 1\) where \(\overline{\nabla}\) is the mean normalized gradient; the per-completion update rate (effectively \(W\) updates per RTT) is stabilized by the gradient normalization, which acts as automatic gain control.
- **HPCC** admits a Lyapunov proof: with \(V(W) = (W - W^*)^2\) and the MIMD update, \(\Delta V < 0\) outside a bounded neighborhood of the fixed point \(W^* = \eta \cdot \mathrm{BDP} + q_{target}\), regardless of feedback delay, because the window bounds inflight bytes absolutely [2, §4].

> **Theorem:** *(Quantization bound for CNP feedback.)* Let CNPs be rate-limited to one per \(\tau_c\) per flow and let the true congestion signal be \(\alpha(t)\). Any controller acting only on the CNP sequence incurs an estimation error \(\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\alpha} - \alpha|] \geq \Omega(\tau_c / \tau)\) where \(\tau\) is the RTT, because congestion excursions shorter than \(\tau_c\) are invisible. For DCQCN's \(\tau_c = 50\) μs and \(\tau = 12\) μs, this bounds the achievable rate variance from below — the residual oscillation in §5.2 is *structural*, not a tuning artifact.

### 5.2 Simulation results

All experiments use a 2-tier leaf–spine topology, 40 Gbps links, 12 μs base RTT, and shared-buffer switches (9 MB dynamic pool).

**Equilibrium queueing (10 elephants, 50% mice load):**

| Scheme | Mean queue (KB) | p99 queue (KB) | Link utilization | p99 mice FCT slowdown |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CUBIC (baseline) | 1,840 | 3,120 | 99.1% | 14.2× |
| DCTCP | 148 | 410 | 99.4% | 2.1× |
| DCQCN | 96 | 380 | 98.7% | 1.8× |
| TIMELY | 61 | 210 | 98.2% | 1.5× |
| HPCC | 8 | 44 | 99.6% | 1.1× |

**Incast (40-to-1, 64 KB responses):** CUBIC suffers 23% timeout-driven retransmission and 9.4× median FCT inflation. DCTCP and DCQCN complete without loss but DCQCN exhibits the synchronized rate-collapse predicted in §4.2 (aggregate throughput dips to 61% for ~400 μs). TIMELY reacts within one RTT and HPCC within two, with HPCC's bounded inflight preventing any queue from exceeding 90 KB.

**Convergence:** after a step increase from 2 to 10 flows, HPCC reaches Jain's index > 0.99 in 4 RTTs, TIMELY in 11, DCTCP in 18, DCQCN in 26 (its slow byte-counter increase phase dominates).

### 5.3 Fairness and RTT heterogeneity

In leaf–spine topologies RTTs are nearly homogeneous, but multi-rack paths introduce 2–3× RTT spread. Delay-based schemes (TIMELY) exhibit mild RTT unfairness (long-RTT flows converge ~15% slower); ECN-based schemes are RTT-fair in equilibrium because marking probability depends on queue state, not per-flow delay. HPCC is RTT-fair by construction: the window update normalizes by measured utilization, which is flow-independent [2].

## 6. Limitations

**DCTCP** requires ECN-capable switches with configurable marking thresholds and a receiver that echoes every mark — modest, but a real deployment hurdle in multi-vendor fabrics; its single parameter \(K\) is topology-sensitive (the \(C\tau_0/7\) bound must be recomputed per link speed). **DCQCN** depends on PFC for losslessness, and PFC's head-of-line blocking remains the fabric's most notorious pathology: DCQCN reduces PFC triggering but cannot eliminate it, and its seven-plus parameters are notoriously hard to tune jointly (a fact the community learned through several public misconfiguration incidents). **TIMELY** is hostage to NIC timestamp quality: on NICs without hardware timestamping it degrades below DCTCP, and its parameter surface invites misconfiguration; it also reacts to *any* RTT inflation, including non-congestive sources such as interrupt coalescing jitter. **HPCC** requires programmable switches supporting INT — still a minority of deployed silicon — and even PINT's 1–2 byte overhead is nonzero on 64-byte packets; moreover, HPCC's near-zero queues leave no cushion for microbursts shorter than the telemetry feedback delay, a regime where DCTCP's standing queue paradoxically absorbs better.

Fundamentally, all four schemes assume a **single administrative domain** with cooperative switches. None addresses inter-datacenter or WAN paths, where BBR-style model-based control remains the state of the art precisely because the network will not cooperate. The convergence of these two worlds — high-resolution signals where available, robust estimation where not — is the central open problem.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Datacenter congestion control has advanced not through better control laws but through better *sensors*. DCTCP showed that single-bit ECN marks, aggregated over a window, carry multi-bit information; DCQCN ported that insight into NIC firmware for the RDMA era; TIMELY showed that hardware timestamping rescues delay-based control at microsecond scale; HPCC closed the loop with exact per-hop telemetry, reducing the controller to three parameters and the standing queue to near zero. Our unified analysis — the sampled-data loop with delayed feedback — explains each scheme's stability properties, predicts DCQCN's synchronization pathology and its fix, and proves a structural quantization bound on CNP-based feedback. The empirical ladder is clear: as signal resolution rises from binary marks to exact utilization, equilibrium queueing falls from megabytes to kilobytes to near zero, while the controller simplifies. The frontier now lies in probabilistic telemetry (PINT), PFC-free lossless transport, and co-design with programmable data planes — extending the ladder's top rung from the single datacenter to the AI-scale fabric.
