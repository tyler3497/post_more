# Compressed Sensing MRI: Sparse Recovery Guarantees, Non-Cartesian k-Space Trajectories, and Deep Unrolled Reconstruction

## Abstract

Magnetic resonance imaging is one of the most clinically valuable yet slowest imaging modalities, with scan times that constrain throughput, patient comfort, and dynamic applications such as cardiac imaging. Compressed sensing (CS) theory, formalized by Candès, Romberg, and Tao [1] and Donoho [2], established that sparse signals can be recovered from highly undersampled linear measurements under incoherence and the restricted isometry property. Lustig, Donoho, and Pauly [3] showed MRI is an almost ideal fit: data are acquired directly in Fourier (k-space), images are compressible in wavelet and finite-difference domains, and variable-density random undersampling produces noise-like incoherent aliasing that nonlinear recovery can remove. This thesis unifies sparse recovery guarantees, the engineering of non-Cartesian k-space trajectories (spiral, radial, variable-density Cartesian) under gradient hardware limits, and the deep unrolled reconstruction paradigm — exemplified by MoDL [4] and learned primal-dual frameworks [5] — which cast classical iterations as trainable networks with explicit data-consistency. We analyze convergence, generalization, and instability modes, survey fastMRI benchmarks [21], and show how theory, sampling physics, and learned solvers jointly define the frontier of accelerated MRI.

## 1 Introduction

Clinical MRI reconstructs images from samples of the spatial Fourier transform of the object's magnetization, acquired sequentially along trajectories in k-space. Because each sample requires gradient encoding, full Nyquist-rate Cartesian sampling is slow: a high-resolution volumetric scan may take tens of minutes. The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem appears to impose a hard floor on the number of measurements required to avoid aliasing — but the theorem assumes only bandlimitedness, a weak prior that ignores the structured sparsity of natural and medical images [2].

Compressed sensing (CS) overturned this reasoning. Its central claim is striking: a signal $x \in \mathbb{C}^n$ that is $s$-sparse (or compressible) in some basis $\Psi$ can be recovered from only $m \ll n$ linear measurements $y = Ax + e$, where $A$ is a suitably incoherent sensing matrix and $e$ is bounded noise, via the tractable convex program

$$\hat{x} = \arg\min_{x}\|\Psi x\|_1 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \|Ax - y\|_2 \le \epsilon.$$

The $\ell_1$ norm serves as the convex surrogate of the combinatorial $\ell_0$ norm, promoting sparsity while remaining solvable by polynomial-time algorithms [1].

> **Definition 1.1 (Sparse and compressible signals).** A signal $x$ is $s$-sparse in basis $\Psi$ if $\|\Psi x\|_0 \le s$. It is *compressible* if its coefficients decay as $|\Psi x|_{(k)} \lesssim k^{-1/p}$ for some $p \le 1$, so that the best $s$-term approximation error decays as $s^{1/2 - 1/p}$.

MRI is almost uniquely suited to this framework. First, measurements are inherently linear in the image: the received signal at k-space location $k$ is $y(k) = \int_{\Omega} \rho(r) e^{-i2\pi k\cdot r}\,dr$, the Fourier transform of the image $\rho$ [3]. Second, MR images are compressible — angiograms are sparse in pixel space, while anatomical images are sparse in wavelet or total-variation domains. Third, the Fourier basis is maximally incoherent with the wavelet basis, so random undersampling produces artifacts that behave like additive noise rather than coherent ghosts [3].

This thesis develops three pillars in depth: (i) the sparse recovery guarantees of CS — RIP, coherence bounds, and noise stability; (ii) the design of non-Cartesian sampling trajectories that realize incoherent measurements under real gradient slew-rate and duty-cycle constraints; and (iii) deep unrolled reconstruction, which inherits the physics of the measurement model while learning data-adaptive priors end-to-end.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 MRI Signal Model

The idealized discrete forward model for multi-coil MRI with $C$ receiver coils is

$$y_c = M F S_c x + e_c, \qquad c = 1,\dots,C,$$

where $x \in \mathbb{C}^n$ is the unknown image, $S_c \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ is the diagonal sensitivity profile of coil $c$, $F \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ is the discrete Fourier transform, $M \in \{0,1\}^{m \times n}$ is the undersampling mask, and $e_c$ is complex Gaussian noise. Concatenating coils gives the compact form $y = Ax + e$ with the forward operator $A = MFS$, where $S$ stacks all coil sensitivities [6]. For non-Cartesian trajectories, $F$ is replaced by a non-uniform fast Fourier transform (NUFFT) operator $F_{\text{nu}}$ that evaluates the Fourier transform at arbitrary k-space points, followed by density compensation [7].

| Component | Symbol | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Image | $x \in \mathbb{C}^n$ | Unknown magnetization image |
| Coil sensitivities | $S_c$ | Spatial modulation from coil array |
| Fourier encoding | $F$ | k-space sampling operator (NUFFT for non-Cartesian) |
| Undersampling mask | $M$ | Selects $m$ of $n$ k-space samples |
| Noise | $e$ | Complex Gaussian, $\|e\|_2 \le \epsilon$ |
| Acceleration factor | $R = n/m$ | Typically 4–12 in practice |

### 2.2 Sparsity in Medical Images

Transform sparsity is the prior that makes CS work. Common sparsifying transforms for MRI include:

- **Pixel domain**: Contrast-enhanced angiograms are directly sparse; most voxels are background [3].
- **Wavelet domain**: Piecewise-smooth anatomical images have compressible Daubechies-4/8 coefficients.
- **Finite differences / total variation**: $\|\nabla x\|_1$ promotes piecewise-constant structure.
- **Temporal transforms**: In dynamic MRI, the $x$-$f$ (spatial–temporal Fourier) representation is sparse for periodic cardiac motion [8].

### 2.3 Classical Accelerated MRI

Two families predated CS and remain complementary. **Parallel imaging** exploits coil redundancy: SENSE unaliases images in the image domain using sensitivity maps, while GRAPPA interpolates missing k-space from autocalibration data [10]. CS and parallel imaging combine multiplicatively: SPIRiT and related methods merge self-consistent k-space interpolation with $\ell_1$ regularization [11]. The combination is standard in clinical CS protocols today.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Compressed Sensing Recovery Problem

The canonical CS-MRI reconstruction solves

$$\hat{x} = \arg\min_x \frac{1}{2}\|Ax - y\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\Psi x\|_1, \tag{1}$$

the unconstrained Lagrangian form, where $\Psi$ is a wavelet or finite-difference operator and $\lambda$ balances data fidelity against sparsity. Lustig et al. [3] popularized the nonlinear conjugate-gradient solution of (1) with wavelet $\ell_1$ and total-variation terms, demonstrating 2–7× accelerations in clinical scans.

> **Theorem 3.1 (Noiseless exact recovery via RIP; Candès–Romberg–Tao [1]).** Let $A$ satisfy the restricted isometry property of order $2s$ with constant $\delta_{2s} < \sqrt{2} - 1$. Then for every $s$-sparse $x$ and measurements $y = Ax$, the solution $\hat{x}$ of $\min\|x\|_1$ s.t. $Ax = y$ equals $x$ exactly.

> **Theorem 3.2 (Stable recovery under noise and compressibility).** Under the same hypothesis with $\delta_{2s} < \sqrt{2}-1$, if $y = Ax + e$ with $\|e\|_2 \le \epsilon$, then $\|\hat{x} - x\|_2 \le C_0 \epsilon + C_1 s^{-1/2}\|x - x_s\|_1$, where $x_s$ is the best $s$-term approximation of $x$ [1]. Error scales linearly with noise and with the tail of the coefficient distribution.

The number of measurements required scales as $m \gtrsim s \log(n/s)$ when $A$ is an $m \times n$ sub-Gaussian random matrix, or $m \gtrsim s\,\mu^2(\Phi,\Psi)\log n$ for randomly subsampled rows of an orthonormal basis $\Phi$ incoherent with the sparsity basis $\Psi$, where the coherence $\mu(\Phi,\Psi) = \sqrt{n}\max_{i,j}|\langle\phi_i,\psi_j\rangle|$ quantifies the worst-case alignment [1]. The Fourier–wavelet pair achieves $\mu = 1$, the maximal incoherence, which is why MRI is the canonical CS application [3].

### 3.2 Sampling Design: Incoherence in Practice

Practical MRI cannot sample arbitrary random k-space points: gradient hardware imposes slew-rate limits, and the signal-to-noise ratio concentrates in central k-space. The standard compromise is **variable-density random undersampling**: fully sample a central calibration region (preserving contrast energy and enabling coil-map estimation), and sample the periphery with a polynomial or Poisson-disc density that decays with $|k|$ [3]. Poisson-disc sampling additionally enforces a minimum separation between samples, yielding uniform incoherence without large gaps [12].

For Cartesian imaging, random phase encodes are selected per frame; for dynamic MRI, different masks per temporal frame make the aliasing incoherent across time. The **acceleration factor** $R$ and the fully-sampled-center fraction are the principal design knobs, trading SNR and artifact suppression.

### 3.3 Reconstruction Algorithms

Classical solvers of (1) fall into three classes:

1. **Proximal first-order methods** — ISTA and FISTA iterate $x^{k+1} = \mathcal{S}_{\lambda/L}\!\left(x^k - \frac{1}{L}A^H(Ax^k - y)\right)$, where $\mathcal{S}$ is soft-thresholding in the $\Psi$ domain; FISTA adds Nesterov momentum for $O(1/k^2)$ convergence [13].
2. **Splitting methods** — ADMM decouples the data-fidelity and sparsity terms via auxiliary variables, admitting closed-form shrinkage steps and flexible extensions to total variation and nuclear-norm penalties.
3. **Greedy and reweighted methods** — orthogonal matching pursuit and iteratively reweighted $\ell_1$ approximate the $\ell_0$ problem more aggressively at higher cost.

```python
def fista_cs_mri(y, A, A_h, Psi, Psi_h, lam, n_iter=200):
    """FISTA for CS-MRI: min_x 0.5||Ax - y||^2 + lam||Psi x||_1."""
    import numpy as np
    x = np.zeros(A.shape[1], dtype=complex)
    z, t = x.copy(), 1.0
    L = estimate_lipschitz(A, A_h)  # largest eigval of A^H A
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        grad = A_h(A(z) - y)
        xp = soft_threshold(Psi(z - grad / L), lam / L)  # in Psi domain
        xp = Psi_h(xp)
        t_new = 0.5 * (1 + np.sqrt(1 + 4 * t * t))
        z = xp + ((t - 1) / t_new) * (xp - x)
        x, t = xp, t_new
    return x
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Non-Cartesian Trajectories: Spiral and Radial Sampling

Cartesian readouts are slow because each readout line requires a phase-encode step. **Non-Cartesian trajectories** — spirals and radial spokes — sample large k-space regions per excitation and offer favorable incoherence:

- **Spiral trajectories** $k(t) = A t e^{i\omega t}$ (Archimedean) cover k-space efficiently with high duty cycle, enabling single-shot and rapid multi-shot acquisitions. They are maximally efficient under slew-rate constraints and naturally oversample the k-space center, providing SNR and motion robustness [14].
- **Radial trajectories** acquire spokes through the origin at golden-angle increments ($\approx 111.25^\circ$), so any contiguous subset of spokes is approximately uniformly distributed — ideal for flexible retrospective temporal resolution in dynamic and real-time imaging [15].

Reconstruction uses the NUFFT: $y = D\,F_{\text{nu}} x$, where $D$ is a density-compensation diagonal weighting that corrects for the non-uniform sample density (e.g., Voronoi or Jackson weighting) [7]. Gridding reconstruction — density-compensated adjoint NUFFT — is fast but exhibits streaking at high undersampling, motivating iterative or learned reconstructions.

*Trade-offs:* non-Cartesian imaging suffers from off-resonance blurring and gradient-delay errors that require field-map or trajectory corrections; its trajectories are hardware-limited, constraining how "random" sampling can be [7, 14].

### 4.2 From Variational Formulations to Unrolled Networks

Classical iterative reconstruction solves a variational problem with a hand-designed prior. **Deep unrolled networks** instead take $K$ iterations of a classical algorithm and treat each iteration as a neural network layer, learning the prior (and sometimes the sampling pattern) from data [4, 5].

The **MoDL** framework [4] unrolls the alternating minimization of

$$\min_x \|Ax - y\|_2^2 + \lambda \|x - \mathcal{D}_\theta(x)\|_2^2,$$

alternating a CNN denoiser $z_k = \mathcal{D}_\theta(x_k)$ (weights shared across iterations) with a data-consistency step $x_{k+1} = (A^HA + \lambda I)^{-1}(A^Hy + \lambda z_k)$ solved by conjugate gradient. Because the forward model $A$ is explicitly embedded, MoDL generalizes across undersampling patterns and resolutions with far fewer parameters than black-box mappings.

**Variational Networks** [16] unroll gradient descent on a learned Fields-of-Experts prior: $x^{k+1} = x^k - \sum_i (K_i^k)^T \Phi_i^{k\prime}(K_i^k x^k) - \lambda^k A^H(Ax^k - y)$, where convolution kernels $K_i^k$ and potential functions $\Phi_i^k$ are learned per stage. Learned primal-dual methods [5] generalize further, learning both primal and dual update operators in a fully data-driven proximal-point scheme.

| Architecture | Unrolled algorithm | Learned components | Data consistency |
|---|---|---|---|
| MoDL [4] | Alternating minimization | Shared CNN denoiser | CG solve, exact |
| Variational Network [16] | Gradient descent | Per-stage filters + potentials | Gradient step |
| Learned Primal-Dual [5] | PDHG | Primal + dual proximal nets | Via learned dual |
| ADMM-Net | ADMM | Shrinkage + transform | Multiplier updates |
| ISTA-Net | ISTA | Thresholds + sparsifying transform | Gradient step |

### 4.3 Convergence and Stability Analysis of Unrolled Schemes

Unrolling preserves algorithmic structure but changes its analysis: a finite-$K$ network is not the limit of an infinite iteration. Recent theory establishes:

- **Fixed-point and nonexpansiveness**: If the learned denoiser is (averaged) nonexpansive — e.g., via spectral normalization or Lipschitz regularization — the unrolled iteration inherits convergence of the underlying proximal algorithm [17].
- **Robustness and instabilities**: Antun et al. demonstrated that learned reconstructions can exhibit severe instabilities: tiny perturbations in image or measurement domain can cause large artifacts or even removal of pathological structures, and networks may hallucinate plausible-looking features absent from the data [18]. This is the central safety concern for clinical deployment.
- **Generalization**: Because unrolled networks embed $A$, they extrapolate to new masks and anatomies better than pure image-to-image networks; nevertheless, distribution shift (different coils, field strengths, pathologies) degrades performance, motivating test-time adaptation and uncertainty quantification [19].

### 4.4 Parallel Imaging Integration and Multi-Coil Models

Modern unrolled networks operate on multi-coil data: the forward operator $A = MFS$ includes estimated sensitivity maps (from ESPIRiT autocalibration), and the network jointly exploits coil redundancy and learned priors [6]. Joint optimization of sampling and reconstruction (J-MoDL) learns the k-space mask alongside network weights, discovering non-intuitive patterns that outperform hand-designed variable-density masks [20]. Calibrationless approaches fold low-rank Hankel-matrix priors (SAKE, LORAKS) into learned frameworks for robustness when calibration data are scarce.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof Sketch: RIP Implies Stable Recovery

We sketch why RIP yields Theorem 3.2. Let $h = \hat{x} - x$ and decompose $h$ over dyadic blocks $T_0, T_1, \dots$ of its largest coefficients. RIP bounds the energy preserved on each block: $(1-\delta_s)\|h_{T_j}\|_2^2 \le \|Ah_{T_j}\|_2^2 \le (1+\delta_s)\|h_{T_j}\|_2^2$. Optimality of $\hat{x}$ gives a cone constraint $\|h_{T_0^c}\|_1 \le \|h_{T_0}\|_1 + 2\|x_{T_0^c}\|_1$. Combining the cone constraint (which controls the tail) with RIP on $T_0 \cup T_1$ (which controls the head) yields $\|h\|_2 \lesssim \epsilon + s^{-1/2}\|x - x_s\|_1$ [1]. The argument is remarkable because it converts a geometric property of $A$ into a deterministic, uniform guarantee over all sparse signals.

### 5.2 Empirical Benchmarks

The **fastMRI** dataset (NYU, 2018–) released thousands of raw multi-coil k-space volumes with standardized 4×/8× Cartesian masks, catalyzing the field [21]. Key empirical findings:

1. At 4× acceleration, classical CS (wavelet + TV via BART) achieves SSIM ≈ 0.85–0.90 on knee data; learned unrolled networks push SSIM above 0.93 with markedly reduced reconstruction time.
2. At 8× acceleration, hand-designed priors degrade visibly (residual aliasing, staircasing); unrolled networks maintain diagnostic quality on most contrasts but exhibit the instability phenomena of [18] under adversarial probing.
3. Non-Cartesian learned reconstructions (spiral/radial) outperform Cartesian at equal scan time for cardiac and speech imaging due to motion robustness and efficient coverage [14, 15].

> **Practical rule of thumb:** acceleration factors of 4–6× are routine with current CS + parallel imaging; 8–12× require learned reconstruction and careful validation against ground truth for the specific anatomy and pathology.

### 5.3 Computational Considerations

Iterative CS reconstruction historically took minutes per slice (dominated by NUFFT and wavelet transforms); GPU-accelerated FISTA/ADMM reduced this to seconds, and trained unrolled networks reconstruct in tens of milliseconds per slice — compatible with scanner-side deployment [4]. Training, however, requires large curated datasets and careful loss design (SSIM + $\ell_1$ mixtures outperform pure $\ell_2$, which oversmooths).

## 6 Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Work

**Theoretical gaps.** RIP is sufficient but far from necessary, and it is NP-hard to verify for a deterministic matrix; guarantees for structured random matrices relevant to MRI (randomly subsampled Fourier with variable density) rely on coherence arguments that are less sharp [1, 12]. The sample-complexity of *learned* unrolled networks — how much data is needed to generalize across scanners — lacks a satisfying theory [17].

**Hardware constraints.** Gradient slew-rate and peripheral-nerve-stimulation limits bound trajectory curvature, so the theoretically optimal incoherent patterns are not physically realizable; co-design of trajectories and reconstructions under explicit hardware models (e.g., PILOT, J-MoDL) is an active frontier [20].

**Safety and trust.** The instability results of [18] show that worst-case perturbations can erase tumors or inject false structure. Certified robustness, uncertainty maps, and prospective clinical reader studies are prerequisites for autonomous deployment. Hallucination risk grows with acceleration factor and must be quantified per application.

**Future directions.** (i) *Generative priors*: diffusion models and score-based priors as plug-and-play denoisers within unrolled iterations, enabling posterior sampling rather than point estimates; (ii) *Quantitative MRI*: joint reconstruction of $T_1/T_2$ parameter maps from undersampled transient-state data (MR fingerprinting) with learned dictionaries; (iii) *Self-supervised learning*: training from undersampled data alone (Noise2Self, SSDU) to escape the ground-truth bottleneck [19]; (iv) *Federated learning* across institutions to capture scanner and population diversity without sharing raw data.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Compressed sensing MRI stands as one of the rare instances where a deep mathematical theory — sparse recovery under incoherence and the restricted isometry property — translated almost directly into clinical technology, accelerating scans by factors of 2–7 while preserving diagnostic fidelity [3]. The engineering of non-Cartesian trajectories realized incoherent measurements within the unforgiving constraints of gradient hardware, and the deep unrolled paradigm now fuses the measurement physics of classical algorithms with the expressive power of learned priors, achieving state-of-the-art quality at millisecond reconstruction times [4, 5]. Yet the guarantees that made CS trustworthy do not automatically transfer to learned solvers: stability, hallucination, and generalization remain open problems demanding the same rigor that the founders of the theory brought to sparse recovery [18]. The next decade of accelerated MRI will be defined not by higher acceleration factors alone, but by *certifiable* reconstruction — methods whose empirical excellence is matched by provable bounds on what they can and cannot invent.
