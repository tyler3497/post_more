# Gravitational-Wave Lensing in the Wave-Optics Regime: Diffraction Integrals, Chromatic Signatures, and Multi-Messenger Cosmology

## Abstract

Gravitational-wave astronomy is approaching the epoch in which lensing of compact-binary signals by intervening mass becomes routine. Unlike electromagnetic radiation, whose optical wavelengths render geometrical optics essentially exact, gravitational waves possess wavelengths comparable to the Schwarzschild radii of abundant lenses, placing a large class of configurations in the wave-optics regime. Here we develop the machinery of wave-optical gravitational lensing: the Kirchhoff diffraction integral for the amplification factor, the dimensionless-frequency taxonomy separating diffractive, interference-dominated, and geometrical-optics limits, and the analytic structure of lens models from point masses to isothermal spheres and NFW halos. Diffraction imprints frequency-dependent amplitude and phase modulations — chromatic signatures detectable even without multiple images — that break degeneracies afflicting geometrical optics, enabling measurements of lens masses, halo profiles, and compact dark matter. We survey empirical searches across LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA catalogs and millihertz-band forecasts for LISA, DECIGO, and the Einstein Telescope, and connect lensed standard sirens to multi-messenger cosmology, where lensing time delays yield independent measurements of the Hubble constant.

## 1 Intro

The first detections of gravitational waves (GWs) by the LIGO and Virgo collaborations inaugurated not merely a new observational window but a new class of probes: *standard sirens*, whose luminosity distances are inferred directly from the waveform without any cosmic distance ladder [5]. As catalogs have grown past a hundred compact-binary mergers, the question has shifted from *whether* gravitational waves will be lensed to *how often* and *in which regime*. Galaxy-scale and cluster-scale lenses will produce multiple, delayed GW images amenable to geometrical optics, but the longer wavelengths of GWs — from meters to megameters across the ground-based and space-based bands — guarantee that a vast population of stellar-mass and halo-scale lenses act as *diffractive* elements whose imprint on the waveform is wave-optical rather than ray-optical [1].

This wave-optics lensing of GWs is conceptually distinct from anything in electromagnetic lensing. For light, diffraction matters only for radio wavelengths and caustic crossings; for GWs, diffraction is the *typical* case over enormous ranges of the lens-mass–frequency parameter space [2, 3]. The consequence is a rich phenomenology:

- **Chromatic amplification**: the amplification factor $F(f)$ is a complex, frequency-dependent function, so the observed waveform carries amplitude *and* phase modulations whose frequency dependence encodes lens structure [4].
- **Degeneracy breaking**: interference fringes probe the lens potential beyond what image positions and magnifications constrain, resolving ambiguities (e.g., the mass-sheet degeneracy) that are exact symmetries of geometrical optics [7].
- **Single-image sensitivity**: diffractive modulation is detectable even when the source lies outside all caustics and no multiple images exist, enormously enlarging the effective lensing cross-section [2].

In this thesis we formalize these ideas. Section 2 reviews the wave equation in perturbed Friedmann–Robertson–Walker backgrounds and derives the diffraction integral. Section 3 sets out the methodology of numerical evaluation, the dimensionless-frequency regime taxonomy, and the principal lens models. Section 4 develops the deep structure: the point-mass solution in terms of hypergeometric functions, extended halo models, the Fresnel-scale hierarchy and its cosmological meaning, multi-lens and microlensing interference, and the application to time-delay cosmography. Section 5 turns to empirical results — searches in the GWTC catalogs, agnostic detection statistics, and third-generation forecasts. Section 6 confronts limitations, and Section 7 concludes with the multi-messenger outlook.

> **Theorem: (Wave-optics completeness of the diffraction integral).** *Under the thin-lens and small-angle approximations, the frequency-domain amplification factor $F(f)$ given by the Kirchhoff diffraction integral [Eq. (3.1)] reduces, in the high-frequency limit $w \to \infty$, to the sum over stationary points of the Fermat potential, recovering geometrical optics; in the low-frequency limit $w \to 0$, it reduces to a smooth, frequency-approaching-unity factor with no discrete images [1]. Hence the integral subsumes both classical regimes as special cases [9].*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Waves in a perturbed universe

In the weak-field limit the background spacetime is the perturbed FRW metric

$$
ds^2 = -(1+2U)c^2dt^2 + a^2(t)(1-2U)\,d\mathbf{r}^2,
$$

with $U \ll 1$ the lensing potential and $a(t)$ the scale factor. GWs on this background are tensor perturbations $h_{\mu\nu}$; within the eikonal approximation one writes $h_{\mu\nu} = \phi\, e_{\mu\nu}$, where the polarization tensor $e_{\mu\nu}$ is taken as constant through the lensing event, an excellent approximation because lensing potentials are weak and deflection angles small [1]. The scalar amplitude $\phi$ in the frequency domain satisfies, to leading order,

$$
(c^2\nabla^2 + \omega^2)\,\tilde{\phi} = 4\omega^2 c^{-2} U\,\tilde{\phi},
\tag{2.1}
$$

with $\omega = 2\pi f$ [4]. This Helmholtz-type equation is formally identical to the wave equation of scalar optics with an effective refractive index $n \approx 1 - 2U/c^2$, which is why the machinery of classical diffraction theory transfers directly [9].

### 2.2 The geometrical-optics limit and its failures

When the GW wavelength $\lambda$ is much smaller than the Schwarzschild radius $R_S$ of the lens, stationary-phase evaluation of the wave field yields the familiar discrete images: each image $j$ contributes a magnification $\mu_j$, a time delay $T_j$, and a Morse phase $n_j \in \{0, \tfrac{1}{2}, 1\}$ according to its type (minimum, saddle, maximum) [1]. The total amplification is then

$$
F_{\rm GO}(f) = \sum_j |\mu_j|^{1/2}\, e^{2\pi i f T_j - i\pi n_j},
\tag{2.2}
$$

producing beating between images separated in time. For GWs this limit fails conspicuously whenever

$$
\lambda \gtrsim R_S \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad M_L \lesssim 300\,M_\odot\left(\frac{f}{100\,{\rm Hz}}\right)^{-1},
\tag{2.3}
$$

a condition satisfied by stellar-mass and halo-substructure lenses across the ground-based band, and by halo masses $10^5$–$10^9\,M_\odot$ in the LISA millihertz band [8]. Near caustics, geometrical optics predicts divergent intensities that wave optics regularizes through diffraction [9].

### 2.3 The dimensionless frequency

All of wave-optics lensing is organized by a single dimensionless number,

$$
w = \frac{8\pi G M_{Lz} f}{c^3} \approx 0.84 \left(\frac{M_{Lz}}{100\,M_\odot}\right)\left(\frac{f}{100\,{\rm Hz}}\right),
\tag{2.4}
$$

where $M_{Lz} = M_L(1+z_d)$ is the redshifted lens mass. Roughly: $w \ll 1$ is the *diffraction-dominated* regime (the wave barely notices the lens); $w \sim 1$ is the *transition* regime with maximally informative chromatic structure; and $w \gg 1$ is the *geometrical-optics* regime with interference fringes between discrete images [1, 3]. This taxonomy governs every detection strategy discussed in Section 5.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The diffraction integral

Under the thin-lens and small-angle approximations, the ratio of lensed to unlensed GW amplitude is [1, 4]

$$
F(f, \mathbf{y}) = \frac{1+z_d}{c}\,\frac{D_s}{D_d D_{ds}}\,\xi_0^2\,\frac{f}{i}\int d^2\mathbf{x}\,
\exp\big[2\pi i f\, t_d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})\big],
\tag{3.1}
$$

where $\mathbf{x} = \boldsymbol{\xi}/\xi_0$ and $\mathbf{y} = \boldsymbol{\eta}\,D_d/\xi_0 D_s$ are dimensionless image- and source-plane coordinates, $D_d, D_{ds}, D_s$ are angular-diameter distances, $\xi_0$ is a normalization scale, and $t_d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})$ is the Fermat time-delay surface: the sum of a geometrical path-length term and the Shapiro gravitational delay. Numerically, (3.1) is a highly oscillatory double integral; modern work evaluates it by Picard–Lefschetz thimble methods, asymptotic expansion hybrids, and zero-crossing quadrature, with convergence studies showing that naive grids fail catastrophically at $w \gg 1$ [3].

### 3.2 Canonical lens models

The phenomenology depends on the projected potential through $t_d$. Three workhorse models suffice for most applications:

| Lens model | Density profile | Use case | $w$-behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Point mass** | $M_L\,\delta^{(3)}$ | Stars, compact DM | Closed form via $_2F_1$; strong fringes at $w\sim 1$ |
| **SIS** (singular isothermal sphere) | $\rho \propto \sigma_v^2/r^2$ | Galaxies, halos | Central caustic; fringe damping at large $y$ |
| **NFW** (Navarro–Frenk–White) | $\rho \propto (r/r_s)^{-1}(1+r/r_s)^{-2}$ | DM subhalos | Finite-core corrections distinguish SIDM/FDM at low $w$ [8] |

For the point-mass lens the diffraction integral reduces to a one-dimensional Bessel integral,

$$
F(w,y) = e^{i w y^2/2}\, e^{\pi w/4}\,\Gamma\!\left(1-\frac{iw}{2}\right) {}_2F_1\!\left(\frac{iw}{2}, 1; 1; -\frac{y^2}{2} - \frac{y^2 w}{4}\,i\right),
\tag{3.2}
$$

which exhibits its most pronounced oscillations at $w \sim 1$–$10$ and small impact parameter $y$ [1, 3].

### 3.3 Numerical pipeline

A canonical detection pipeline proceeds in four stages:

1. **Template bank construction**: unlensed waveforms (e.g., IMRPhenomXPHM) multiplied by a grid of $F(w,y)$ evaluations, or injected via reduced-order stochastic-diffraction (ROSD) bases built from singular-value decompositions of stellar-field simulations [3].
2. **Residual analysis**: fit the best unlensed template; compute the frequency-domain residual $r(f) = \tilde{h}_{\rm data}(f)/\tilde{h}_{\rm template}(f) - 1$.
3. **Agnostic detection**: dynamic-programming search for coherent amplitude/phase modulations in $r(f)$ without assuming a lens model [2].
4. **Bayesian parameter estimation**: nested sampling over $(M_{Lz}, y, \text{profile})$ with the diffraction integral evaluated per likelihood call, increasingly accelerated by machine-learned emulators [3].

The following Python sketch computes the point-mass amplification factor via adaptive quadrature, the workhorse inner loop of every such pipeline:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad
from scipy.special import j0

def amplification_pointmass(w, y, xmax=50.0, phi_m=None):
    """Complex amplification factor F(w, y) for a point-mass lens.

    w : dimensionless frequency 8*pi*G*MLz*f / c**3
    y : dimensionless impact parameter
    """
    if phi_m is None:  # minimum Fermat phase for point mass
        phi_m = 0.5 * (y - np.sqrt(y*y + 4.0))**2 \
                - np.log(0.5*(y + np.sqrt(y*y + 4.0)))
    pref = -1j * w * np.exp(1j*w*y*y/2.0 + 1j*w*phi_m)
    def re_integrand(x): return x * j0(w*x*y) * np.cos(w*(x*x/2 - x + phi_m))
    def im_integrand(x): return x * j0(w*x*y) * np.sin(w*(x*x/2 - x + phi_m))
    re = quad(re_integrand, 0.0, xmax, limit=400, epsabs=1e-9)[0]
    im = quad(im_integrand, 0.0, xmax, limit=400, epsabs=1e-9)[0]
    return pref * (re + 1j*im)

ws = np.logspace(-2, 2, 400)
for y in (0.3, 1.0, 3.0):
    F = np.array([amplification_pointmass(w, y) for w in ws])
    print(f"y={y}: max |F|={np.abs(F).max():.3f}, "
          f"max phase dev={np.degrees(np.angle(F)).max():.1f} deg")
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Interference fringes and the beating spectrum

In the transition regime $w \sim 1$, the two dominant stationary paths — the unlensed "through" wave and the deflected wave — interfere. The resulting $|F(w)|$ is not monotonic but displays fringes whose spacing in frequency is set by the lens mass: $\Delta f \sim (4GM_{Lz}/c^3)^{-1}$. Because a chirping binary sweeps *through* this fringe pattern as $f(t)$ rises, the time-domain waveform acquires quasi-periodic amplitude modulation — a *beating* envelope superimposed on the chirp [1, 2]. The fringe visibility peaks for aligned configurations ($y \lesssim 1$); for large $y$ the secondary path is exponentially suppressed and only diffractive softening of the amplitude remains.

A purely functional encoding of the same physics clarifies the structure. The amplification factor is the Fourier-like transform of the Fermat-delay kernel; in Haskell one may express the regime dispatch exactly:

```haskell
-- Wave-optics lensing regime dispatch on dimensionless frequency w
data Regime = Diffraction | Transition | GeometricOptics deriving (Show, Eq)

regimeOf :: Double -> Regime
regimeOf w | w < 0.1  = Diffraction       -- smooth, |F| -> 1
           | w < 10.0 = Transition        -- fringes, chromatic
           | otherwise = GeometricOptics  -- discrete images, GO sum

-- Toy fringe spectrum for a point-mass lens at small y:
-- the oscillatory kernel exp(i w x^2/2) J_0(w x y)
fringeKernel :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
fringeKernel w x y = cos (w * x * x / 2 - w * x) * besselJ0 (w * x * y)
  where besselJ0 = error "link against a Bessel implementation"

massFromFringeSpacing :: Double -> Double
massFromFringeSpacing dfHz =
  -- M_Lz [solar masses] from observed fringe spacing Delta f
  2.0e4 / dfHz   -- ~ c^3 / (4 G Delta f), in M_sun
```

### 4.2 Extended halos: SIS, NFW, and the Fresnel microscope

Extended lenses enrich the signature because the Fermat surface develops caustic structure. For the SIS lens, a fold caustic produces the characteristic $\mu \propto (\text{distance})^{-1/2}$ divergence that wave optics truncates into a finite Airy-like pattern. For NFW subhalos, finite-core and truncation effects imprint themselves specifically at low $w$, distinguishing cold dark matter from fuzzy dark matter or self-interacting dark matter *without* resolving any image [8]. The waves are insensitive to structure below the Fresnel scale $r_F \propto f^{-1/2}$, so the *chirping* nature of the source is decisive: as frequency rises, $r_F$ shrinks and the amplification factor scans progressively finer lens structure — a genuine microscope whose resolving power improves over the inspiral [3, 10]. Formally, the Born-approximation relation connects amplitude/phase fluctuations to the matter power spectrum $P(k)$, enabling statistical inference of substructure abundance from ensembles of events [4].

### 4.3 Microlensing fields: stars inside macrolenses

Strongly lensed GWs do not travel through empty space: the stellar fields of the macrolens galaxy diffract each macro-image. Near a critical curve the effective Einstein radius is magnified, and microlensing by stars becomes not merely possible but *unavoidable* for highly magnified events [3]. The full description requires evaluating (3.1) for thousands of microlenses — a problem attacked by reduced-order stochastic-diffraction (ROSD) models, which compress stellar-field wave-optics distortions into an SVD basis with stochastic coefficients usable directly as Bayesian priors [3]. Frequency-dependent microlensing also leaks into the inferred in-plane spin components of the binary, a potential contaminant of spin-population studies that must be modeled rather than ignored [6].

### 4.4 Multi-messenger cosmology: lensed standard sirens

The cosmological prize is time-delay cosmography with *lensed* standard sirens. A multiply imaged GW event supplies, from the waveform alone, the luminosity distance $D_L$ (a standard-siren measurement, as demonstrated by GW170817 [5]); the arrival-time differences between images, combined with a lens model constrained by an electromagnetic counterpart or host-galaxy identification, yield the time-delay distance $D_{\Delta t}$, which is inversely proportional to $H_0$. Wave optics sharpens this program in two ways:

1. **Fringe cosmography**: in the transition regime, the fringe spacing measures $M_{Lz} = M_L(1+z_d)$ directly from the waveform, breaking the mass–distance degeneracy that plagues purely geometrical-optics lens modeling [2, 7].
2. **Morse-phase confirmation**: the $-\pi/2$ phase shift of saddle-point (Type II) images is a robust prediction of wave propagation, and its detection in the waveform independently confirms the strong-lensing hypothesis before any EM follow-up [10].

For LISA-band supermassive black hole binaries, lensing of both the GW and the AGN counterpart by the same structure enables a fully self-calibrated distance ladder — the lens simultaneously magnifies the siren and distorts the host light, with the GW fringes providing an independent mass normalization [8, 10].

A Rust implementation of the standard-siren distance estimator at the heart of such analyses is compact:

```rust
/// Luminosity distance [Mpc] from a standard-siren GW amplitude.
/// h0: observed strain amplitude; m_chirp: chirp mass [M_sun];
/// f: frequency [Hz]; iota: inclination angle [rad].
pub fn luminosity_distance(h0: f64, m_chirp: f64, f: f64, iota: f64) -> f64 {
    const G: f64 = 6.67430e-11;
    const C: f64 = 2.99792458e8;
    const MSUN: f64 = 1.98847e30;
    const MPC: f64 = 3.085677581e22;
    let m = m_chirp * MSUN;
    let h_pref = 4.0 * G * m / (C * C);
    // Leading-order quadrupole: h0 ~ (G M / c^2 d_L) * (pi G M f / c^3)^{2/3} * ang(iota)
    let ang = (1.0 + iota.cos().powi(2)) / 2.0;
    let numer = h_pref * (std::f64::consts::PI * G * m * f / C.powi(3)).powf(2.0 / 3.0) * ang;
    numer / h0 / MPC
}

        assert!((d - 40.0).abs() < 60.0);
    }
}
```

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 What the catalogs say (and do not)

No unambiguous gravitational-wave lensing detection exists as of the GWTC-3 catalog. The strongest model-independent statement comes from the first chromatic microlensing search over the 72 events of GWTC-3, which found no statistically significant microlensing signal [6]. This null result is informative: it already constrains the abundance of compact dark-matter objects in the $10^2$–$10^3\,M_\odot$ range probed by diffractive imprints at LIGO frequencies, since such objects would produce detectable fringe modulations in high-SNR events [2].

Several candidate lensed pairs have been reported — most notably repeated analyses of high-mass events such as GW231123 — but each is consistent with unlensed explanations once trials factors and population priors are accounted for [9]. The consensus forecast is that detections are essentially inevitable in the fifth observing run (O5), with $\mathcal{O}(10^3$–$10^5)$ events per year expected from third-generation detectors, among which tens to hundreds will carry identifiable wave-optics signatures [3].

### 5.2 Agnostic detection and machine learning

Because real lenses are messy — stellar fields, substructure, elliptical macrolenses — template-agnostic methods matter. Dynamic-programming searches for coherent frequency-domain modulations in residuals can recover diffraction imprints from pseudo-Jaffe lenses aligned to within the Einstein radius for sources out to $z_s \sim 0.2$–$0.3$ with fully upgraded aLIGO/Virgo, and out to $z_s \sim 2$–$4$ with third-generation instruments [2]. More recently, frequency-domain lensing feature-extraction networks trained on point-mass, SIS, CIS, and NFW amplification curves distinguish lens *profiles* in the DECIGO band with average $F_1 \approx 0.81$, where $w \lesssim 1$ makes wave-optics features most discriminative; the same task degrades in the Einstein Telescope band precisely because $w \gg 1$ pushes events toward geometrical optics and washes out the fringes [3, 10].

### 5.3 Millihertz forecasts

LISA's $10^{-4}$–$1\,$Hz band is the natural home of wave-optics lensing: $w \sim 1$ corresponds to $M_{Lz} \sim 8 \times 10^6\,M_\odot\,(10^{-3}\,{\rm Hz}/f)^{-1}$, i.e., exactly the dark-matter subhalo mass scale [8]. Massive black hole binaries in LISA will therefore be diffracted by the low-mass halo population that is invisible to every other probe — a direct window on the small-scale matter power spectrum and on the core/cusp structure of halos [8, 10].

## 6 Limitations

1. **The lens-model inverse problem is ill-posed.** Many potentials produce nearly identical $F(f)$ at the SNR of real events; profile discrimination requires $w \sim 1$ alignment *and* high SNR, a rare conjunction [10].
2. **Waveform systematics masquerade as lensing.** Eccentricity, precession, higher modes, and calibration errors all produce frequency-dependent amplitude/phase structure. The claimed lensing signal must survive marginalization over these effects — the lesson of every retracted candidate [6, 9].
3. **The thin-lens approximation breaks in hierarchical triples**, where the source orbits a nearby massive black hole and the lens cannot be projected onto a single plane; fully three-dimensional wave-optics propagation is then required and is an active frontier [9].
4. **Polarization is assumed frozen.** Spin–curvature coupling of the tensor field through strong-field regions is neglected in the scalar treatment; it is expected to be negligible for the weak potentials considered but is unproven for near-caustic, near-horizon trajectories [1].
5. **Cosmological inference needs the counterpart.** Without an EM identification of the lens and host, time-delay cosmography inherits the lens-model degeneracies of the optical tradition; the GW fringes mitigate but do not eliminate them [7].

## 7 Conclusion

Wave-optics lensing transforms gravitational waves from point probes into *interferometers with astronomical baselines*: the wave itself interrogates the lens potential at every point of the Fermat surface simultaneously, and the resulting diffraction fringes encode mass, structure, and distance in a single waveform. The formalism — the diffraction integral and its dimensionless-frequency taxonomy — is mature; the detection enterprise is just beginning, with null results in GWTC-3 already carving into compact-dark-matter parameter space and third-generation detectors promising a harvest of diffracted events. The deepest prize is multi-messenger: a lensed binary neutron star, seen in both GW fringes and electromagnetic light, would calibrate the cosmic distance scale through pure gravitational physics — a standard siren behind a gravitational telescope. When that event arrives, the fringes in its waveform will be among the most information-dense oscillations ever recorded, carrying, in a few seconds of strain data, the mass of an invisible lens and the expansion rate of the universe.
