# Submodular Function Maximization: The Greedy (1−1/e) Guarantee, the Multilinear Extension, and Continuous Greedy under Matroid and Knapsack Constraints

## Abstract

Monotone submodular set functions model diminishing returns and arise in sensor placement, document summarization, influence maximization, and facility location. This article gives a self-contained, proof-driven account of maximizing such functions under cardinality, matroid, and knapsack constraints. We prove the classical Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher theorem: the greedy algorithm is a (1−1/e)-approximation under a cardinality constraint, via the recurrence lemma, and exhibit the weighted-coverage instance on which the analysis is tight. We then develop the multilinear extension — the expectation of the function under independent rounding — and its concavity along nonnegative directions, which underpins the continuous greedy algorithm: an ordinary differential equation evolving inside the matroid polytope whose trajectory integrates a fractional solution of value at least (1−1/e) times optimal, rounded losslessly by pipage or swap rounding. We cover the knapsack case via partial enumeration, curvature refinements, and recent combinatorial alternatives, and close with the value-oracle and complexity-theoretic lower bounds that certify the constant as optimal, together with the method's practical and computational limitations.

## 1 Intro

Few inequalities in combinatorial optimization have repaid their proof effort as handsomely as the bound

$$f(S_k) \ge \left(1 - \left(1 - \tfrac{1}{k}\right)^k\right) \mathrm{OPT} \ge \left(1 - \tfrac{1}{e}\right)\mathrm{OPT},$$

which certifies that the simplest imaginable heuristic — *repeatedly take the element with the largest immediate gain* — recovers at least 63% of the optimum of any monotone submodular function under a cardinality constraint [1]. The result, due to Nemhauser, Wolsey, and Fisher (1978), launched submodular optimization as a discipline and remains the workhorse behind deployed systems for sensor placement, extractive summarization, viral marketing, and feature selection.

The constant $1 - 1/e$ is not an artifact of a loose analysis. It is *tight*: no polynomial-query algorithm in the value-oracle model, and no polynomial-time algorithm at all unless P equals NP, can do better [4]. For three decades the greedy algorithm's guarantee stood essentially unmatched for richer constraints — a general matroid admits only a $1/2$-approximation by greedy [3] — until Calinescu, Chekuri, Pál, and Vondrák introduced the **continuous greedy** algorithm, lifting the optimal constant to arbitrary matroid constraints via the **multilinear extension** and pipage rounding [4].

This article develops the full arc:

1. the definitions and the greedy analysis under a cardinality constraint, with the tight example;
2. the multilinear extension and the geometry that makes continuous optimization of a discrete function possible;
3. continuous greedy as a differential equation inside the matroid polytope, plus lossless rounding;
4. knapsack constraints, curvature refinements, and modern combinatorial alternatives;
5. lower bounds, empirical behavior, and limitations.

## 2 Background

### Submodularity

Let $N$ be a finite ground set with $|N| = n$, and let $f: 2^N \to \mathbb{R}_+$ be a set function. For $S \subseteq N$ and $e \in N$, the **marginal gain** of $e$ with respect to $S$ is

$$\Delta(e \mid S) = f(S \cup \{e\}) - f(S).$$

> **Definition (Submodularity).** $f$ is *submodular* if for all $A, B \subseteq N$,
> $$f(A) + f(B) \ge f(A \cup B) + f(A \cap B),$$
> equivalently, if $\Delta(e \mid A) \ge \Delta(e \mid B)$ whenever $A \subseteq B$ and $e \notin B$: marginal gains diminish as the context grows.

We assume throughout that $f$ is **monotone** ($\Delta(e \mid S) \ge 0$ for all $S, e$), **normalized** ($f(\emptyset) = 0$), and nonnegative. Canonical examples include *coverage functions* (the total weight of elements covered by a family of sets), *facility location*, *rank functions of matroids*, and *influence spread* in social networks [2].

### Constraints

We study $\max_{S \in \mathcal{I}} f(S)$ for several families $\mathcal{I}$ of feasible sets:

- **Cardinality (uniform matroid):** $\mathcal{I} = \{S : |S| \le k\}$.
- **Matroid:** $\mathcal{I}$ satisfies the hereditary property and the exchange axiom; a *base* is a maximal independent set. The feasible region's convex hull is the **matroid polytope** $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}) = \{x \ge 0 : x(S) \le r(S) \text{ for all } S\}$, where $r$ is the rank function.
- **Knapsack:** each $e$ has cost $c_e \ge 0$ and $\mathcal{I} = \{S : \sum_{e \in S} c_e \le B\}$.

Access to $f$ is through a **value oracle**: a black box returning $f(S)$ for any queried set $S$. All guarantees are stated in terms of oracle-query complexity [4].

### Two extensions to the continuum

The **Lovász extension** linearly interpolates $f$ along sorted coordinates; it is convex exactly when $f$ is submodular and is the right tool for *minimization*. For *maximization* the operative object is the **multilinear extension**:

$$F(x) = \mathbb{E}[f(R(x))],$$

where $R(x) \subseteq N$ includes each element $e$ independently with probability $x_e$, for $x$ in the unit hypercube. $F$ agrees with $f$ on the vertices of the cube, is smooth, and — crucially — is *concave along any nonnegative direction*: all mixed second partials satisfy $\partial^2 F / \partial x_e \partial x_{e'} \le 0$ [2][4]. This one-sided concavity is what lets a greedy process climb it.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The greedy algorithm and its analysis

The algorithm is disarmingly simple:

```python
def greedy(f, ground_set, k):
    S = set()
    for _ in range(k):
        # pick the element with the largest marginal gain
        e = max((e for e in ground_set - S),
                key=lambda e: f(S | {e}) - f(S))
        S.add(e)
    return S
```

> **Theorem (Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher, 1978).** Let $f$ be monotone submodular and let $S_k$ be the set produced by greedy after $k$ steps. Then $f(S_k) \ge (1 - (1-1/k)^k)\,\mathrm{OPT} \ge (1 - 1/e)\,\mathrm{OPT}$ [1].

*Proof.* Let $C$ be an optimal set, $f(C) = \mathrm{OPT}$, $|C| \le k$, and write $S_i$ for the greedy set after $i$ steps. By submodularity and monotonicity,

$$\mathrm{OPT} - f(S_i) \le f(C \cup S_i) - f(S_i) \le \sum_{e \in C \setminus S_i} \Delta(e \mid S_i) \le k \cdot \max_{e} \Delta(e \mid S_i).$$

Greedy's choice gives $\max_e \Delta(e \mid S_i) = f(S_{i+1}) - f(S_i)$, so with the deficit $\delta_i = \mathrm{OPT} - f(S_i)$,

$$\delta_{i+1} \le \left(1 - \tfrac{1}{k}\right)\delta_i.$$

Unrolling, $\delta_k \le (1-1/k)^k\,\mathrm{OPT}$, and since $(1-1/k)^k < 1/e$, the claim follows. ∎

The proof's engine is the **recurrence lemma**: each step closes at least a $1/k$ fraction of the remaining gap. Everything else in this article is, in a precise sense, a generalization of this one inequality to fractional and constrained settings.

### 3.2 From greedy to continuous greedy

For a matroid constraint, discrete greedy stalls at a $1/2$-approximation [3]. The breakthrough of Calinescu et al. is to run greedy *in continuous time on the multilinear extension* [4]:

1. Start at $y(0) = \mathbf{0}$.
2. At each instant $t$, move in the feasible direction of steepest ascent:
   $$\frac{dy}{dt} = v^\star(y), \qquad v^\star(y) = \arg\max_{v \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})} \langle v, \nabla F(y) \rangle,$$
   where $\nabla F(y)_e = \mathbb{E}[\Delta(e \mid R(y))]$ is estimated by sampling.
3. Stop at $t = 1$; the point $y(1)$ lies in the matroid polytope.

The key estimate mirrors the recurrence lemma. Let $x^\star$ be the characteristic vector of an optimal integral solution. Because $F$ is concave along the nonnegative direction $x^\star - y$,

$$\left\langle x^\star, \nabla F(y) \right\rangle \ge F(y + (x^\star - y)) - F(y) \ge \mathrm{OPT} - F(y),$$

where the last step uses that $F$ dominates $f$ on the cube. Since $v^\star(y)$ maximizes the inner product over the polytope containing $x^\star$,

$$\frac{d}{dt} F(y(t)) = \langle v^\star(y), \nabla F(y) \rangle \ge \mathrm{OPT} - F(y(t)).$$

Solving $\phi'(t) \ge \mathrm{OPT} - \phi(t)$ with $\phi(0) = 0$ yields $\phi(1) \ge (1-1/e)\,\mathrm{OPT}$ — the same differential shadow of the discrete recurrence.

### 3.3 Rounding the fractional solution

A fractional point must be converted to an independent set without losing value. **Pipage rounding** (Ageev–Sviridenko) does this for a single matroid: while $y$ is fractional, find two fractional coordinates and transfer mass between them along the tight rank constraint; submodularity of the underlying structure guarantees the multilinear objective does not decrease, and the process terminates at a vertex — an independent set — with $\mathbb{E}[f(S)] \ge F(y)$ [4]. **Swap rounding** achieves the same via matroid exchange operations. Either way, the fractional $(1-1/e)$ guarantee survives rounding in expectation.

### 3.4 The knapsack constraint

Under a knapsack budget, plain greedy can fail arbitrarily (a single expensive, high-value item defeats it). Sviridenko's fix is **partial enumeration**: guess the (at most three) most valuable items of the optimum by brute force, then run a density-ordered greedy ($\Delta(e \mid S)/c_e$) on the remainder. The combination attains $(1-1/e)$, and the continuous-greedy framework extends to knapsack polytopes as well [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The recurrence lemma, line by line

The inequality $\mathrm{OPT} - f(S_i) \le \sum_{e \in C \setminus S_i} \Delta(e \mid S_i)$ deserves a second look, because it is the only place submodularity enters. Telescoping,

$$f(C \cup S_i) - f(S_i) = \sum_{j} \Delta(c_j \mid S_i \cup \{c_1, \dots, c_{j-1}\}) \le \sum_{j} \Delta(c_j \mid S_i),$$

where the inequality *is* diminishing returns. Monotonicity gives $f(C \cup S_i) \ge f(C) = \mathrm{OPT}$. Note what is *not* needed: no curvature bound, no structure on $C$ beyond $|C| \le k$. The argument is purely local — which is why it ports, almost verbatim, to the gradient estimate of continuous greedy.

Why does discrete greedy degrade to $1/2$ under a general matroid [3]? The step "at most $k$ terms in the sum" breaks: the optimum may be a base of size $r$ while greedy's feasible choices shrink differently, and the exchange axiom only guarantees a *pairwise* replacement argument, halving the constant.

### 4.2 Geometry of the multilinear extension

$F(x) = \sum_{S \subseteq N} f(S) \prod_{e \in S} x_e \prod_{e \notin S}(1 - x_e)$ is a multilinear polynomial — linear in each variable separately. Three facts do all the work:

- **Vertex agreement:** $F(\mathbf{1}_S) = f(S)$, so the discrete problem embeds exactly.
- **Gradient as expected marginal gain:** $\partial F/\partial x_e = \mathbb{E}[\Delta(e \mid R(x))]$, estimable with $O(1)$ samples per coordinate to any desired accuracy.
- **Nonpositive mixed partials:** $\partial^2 F/\partial x_e \partial x_{e'} \le 0$ for $e \ne e'$, equivalent to submodularity, yielding concavity along nonnegative directions.

Contrast the Lovász extension, which is *convex* for submodular $f$ and therefore useless for maximization — a maximum of a convex function over a polytope sits at a vertex but greedy ascent on it has no guarantee. The multilinear extension's one-sided concavity is precisely the analytic shadow of diminishing returns, and it is what makes "follow the gradient inside the polytope" a principled algorithm rather than a heuristic.

### 4.3 Continuous greedy as an ordinary differential equation

Discretize time into steps of size $\delta$: $y \leftarrow y + \delta \cdot v^\star(y)$. As $\delta \to 0$ this is the ODE of Section 3.2. Two observations clarify the method:

1. **It is greedy in function space.** At each instant, $v^\star(y)$ is the *linear* maximizer over the matroid polytope — exactly the matroid greedy algorithm applied to the weight vector $\nabla F(y)$. The algorithm is thus "greedy" at every infinitesimal step, but in the polytope rather than the ground set.
2. **The matroid polytope is what makes the gradient step feasible.** Maximizing a linear function over $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})$ is solvable by the classic matroid greedy algorithm; for a general polytope this step would itself be hard. The choice of feasible region and the choice of extension are deeply coupled.

The differential inequality $\phi'(t) + \phi(t) \ge \mathrm{OPT}$ integrates to $\phi(1) \ge (1 - e^{-1})\mathrm{OPT}$ — compare the discrete unrolling $\delta_k \le (1-1/k)^k \mathrm{OPT}$. Same recurrence, same constant; the continuum merely removes the integrality friction.

### 4.4 Rounding without regret

Pipage rounding exploits a structural fact: for a matroid rank constraint tight at the current fractional point, mass can be shifted between two fractional variables $y_e, y_{e'}$ while staying feasible, and $F$ restricted to this line is *convex* (mixed partials nonpositive plus multilinearity make the univariate restriction convex along the feasible direction). Hence one endpoint is at least as good, and an endpoint has one fewer fractional coordinate. Iterating reaches a vertex — a genuine independent set — with no loss in expectation [4]. Swap rounding rephrases this as a sequence of matroid basis exchanges, each preserving the expected value. The practical upshot: the hard analytic work buys a fractional guarantee, and combinatorics cashes it out for free.

### 4.5 Knapsack constraints, curvature, and the frontier

**Knapsack.** Beyond partial enumeration, the knapsack polytope $\{x : \sum c_e x_e \le B\}$ admits a continuous-greedy treatment with a modified stopping/rounding scheme, and the generalized assignment problem reduces to submodular maximization over a partition matroid, yielding a $(1-1/e-o(1))$-approximation that simplifies earlier special-purpose algorithms [4].

**Curvature.** When $f$ is close to linear, greedy does better. The *curvature* $c = 1 - \min_e \Delta(e \mid N \setminus \{e\}) / \Delta(e \mid \emptyset)$ measures deviation from linearity ($c = 0$ is linear, $c = 1$ is fully curved); Vondrák showed continuous greedy attains $(1 - e^{-c})/c$, and no better ratio is possible in the value-oracle model [5].

**Modern alternatives.** Filmus and Ward derandomized the whole pipeline: greedy followed by local search on a *non-oblivious* potential function (a reweighting of $f$ related to Padé approximants of $e^x$) achieves $1-1/e$ combinatorially, with no extension and no rounding [5]. Very recently, a Poisson-process-based method matches the tight bound with a remarkably simple stochastic process and few single-element swaps [6]. The constant $1-1/e$ keeps reappearing; the proofs keep getting shorter.

| Algorithm | Constraint | Ratio | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discrete greedy [1] | Cardinality | $1-1/e$ (tight) | Deterministic, $O(nk)$ queries |
| Lazy greedy (Minoux) | Cardinality | $1-1/e$ (same output) | Far fewer queries in practice |
| Discrete greedy [3] | Matroid | $1/2$ (tight for greedy) | Deterministic |
| Continuous greedy + pipage [4] | Matroid | $1-1/e$ (optimal) | Randomized, sampling-based |
| Filmus–Ward [5] | Matroid | $1-1/e$ (optimal) | Combinatorial, non-oblivious local search |
| Poisson process [6] | Matroid | $1-1/e$ (optimal) | Simple stochastic process |
| Enumeration + density greedy | Knapsack | $1-1/e$ | Deterministic |

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### Tightness of the greedy analysis

The $(1-1/e)$ bound cannot be improved for greedy, even under a cardinality constraint. Consider the following weighted-coverage instance, the canonical extremal example. Partition the universe into $k$ groups $E_1, \dots, E_k$, each of total weight $k$. The optimal sets $O_j$ each cover one full group, for $\mathrm{OPT} = k^2$. Define $k$ further sets $G_1, \dots, G_k$ where $G_t$ covers, inside *each* group, fresh weight exactly $(1-1/k)^{t-1}$ — possible by splitting each group's weight into geometrically shrinking pieces plus a leftover sliver covered only by the $O_j$'s.

At step $t$, with adversarial tie-breaking, greedy picks $G_t$: its marginal gain is $k(1-1/k)^{t-1}$, which dominates every remaining $G_s$ ($s > t$) and ties the best remaining $O_j$. Greedy's total is therefore

$$\sum_{t=0}^{k-1} k\left(1-\tfrac{1}{k}\right)^t = k^2\left(1 - \left(1-\tfrac{1}{k}\right)^k\right),$$

so the ratio $f(S_k)/\mathrm{OPT} = 1 - (1-1/k)^k \to 1 - 1/e$ as $k \to \infty$ [1]. The analysis of Section 3.1 is exact.

### Complexity-theoretic optimality

Tightness for *greedy* does not imply tightness for *all algorithms* — but both hold here:

- **Value-oracle model:** Nemhauser and Wolsey (1978) proved that any algorithm achieving better than $(1-1/e)$ needs exponentially many value queries [4].
- **Explicit instances:** Feige (1998) reduced from max-$k$-cover to show that beating $(1-1/e)$ in polynomial time would imply P equals NP [4].

### Empirical behavior

Worst-case instances like the one above are adversarially constructed; on real data greedy dramatically over-performs its certificate. Across sensor placement, document summarization, and facility-location benchmarks, greedy solutions routinely land far above the 63% floor — practitioners treat the bound as a safety net, not a prediction. The engineering wins are elsewhere: **lazy greedy** (Minoux 1978) exploits submodularity to maintain upper bounds on marginal gains in a priority queue, typically cutting oracle queries by orders of magnitude while producing *identical* output [1]; and stochastic variants trade a small $\epsilon$ in the ratio for near-linear query complexity. Continuous greedy, by contrast, remains expensive — gradient sampling plus rounding dominates runtime — which is exactly the gap the newer combinatorial algorithms [5][6] aim to close.

## 6 Limitations

- **Oracle costs are real.** The value-oracle model counts queries, not wall-clock time; evaluating $f$ (e.g., simulating influence spread) can dominate, and continuous greedy multiplies this by sampling and discretization overhead.
- **Monotonicity is load-bearing.** For *non-monotone* submodular maximization, greedy can fail catastrophically; the right tools are the double-greedy $1/2$-approximation for the unconstrained case and measured variants of continuous greedy for constrained cases [3].
- **Richer constraints degrade the constant.** Intersections of matroids, $p$-systems, and multiple knapsacks admit only weaker guarantees, and some are provably hard to approximate well.
- **Rounding is constraint-specific.** Pipage and swap rounding lean on matroid exchange properties; each new polytope needs its own rounding scheme, and none is known for several natural constraint families.
- **Verification lags.** The cardinality-case greedy bound has been machine-checked in a proof assistant [7], but the continuous-greedy pipeline — sampling, ODE discretization, rounding — remains unverified, and its probabilistic claims resist current formal methods.
- **Adaptivity.** Continuous greedy is inherently sequential; low-adaptivity variants exist but with weaker constants, limiting massive parallelism.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The story of submodular maximization is the story of a single inequality — the recurrence $\delta_{i+1} \le (1-1/k)\delta_i$ — climbing a ladder of abstraction. Discretely, it gives the Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher greedy guarantee, tight on a weighted-coverage instance and optimal under standard complexity assumptions. Lifted to the multilinear extension, the same recurrence becomes a differential inequality inside the matroid polytope, and continuous greedy plus pipage rounding extends the optimal $1-1/e$ constant from cardinality to arbitrary matroids and knapsacks. Curvature refines the constant; non-oblivious local search and Poisson-process methods re-derive it combinatorially; lower bounds certify that no one will ever beat it. Nearly fifty years on, $1-1/e$ remains both the ceiling and — remarkably often in practice — a floor far below what the simplest algorithm actually achieves.
