# Nested Dissection Ordering and Supernodal Sparse Cholesky Factorization: Graph Separators, Elimination Trees, Symbolic Factorization, and Multifrontal Methods for Million-Variable SPD Systems

## Abstract

This thesis presents a unified treatment of nested dissection ordering and supernodal sparse Cholesky factorization for symmetric positive definite (SPD) linear systems *Ax = b* with millions of unknowns. We develop the graph-theoretic foundations: vertex separators, the Lipton–Tarjan planar separator theorem, and recursive separator hierarchies that yield *O(N^{3/2})* work and *O(N log N)* fill bounds on two-dimensional grids [1][2][3]. We formalize the elimination tree as the structural backbone of sparse Cholesky, enabling symbolic factorization, supernode amalgamation, and dependency analysis for parallelism [4]. We then derive the multifrontal method as a reorganization of Cholesky into dense partial factorizations on frontal matrices with extend-add assembly [5][6]. A supernodal numeric phase exploits Level-3 BLAS to reach a large fraction of peak flop rates on modern processors [7]. Empirical evaluation on Laplacian and elasticity discretizations shows nested dissection outperforming minimum-degree orderings asymptotically, while supernodal multifrontal codes dominate in wall-clock time [8][9]. We close with limitations in three dimensions, indefinite systems, and memory scaling, and chart directions toward task-based runtime scheduling.

---

## 1. Introduction

The numerical solution of large sparse symmetric positive definite (SPD) linear systems is among the most consequential computational kernels in science and engineering. Discretizations of elliptic partial differential equations, structural mechanics models, interior-point optimization subproblems, and Gaussian process regression all reduce, at their core, to solving *Ax = b* where *A ∈ ℝ^{n×n}* is SPD and sparse. For *n* in the millions, dense *O(n³)* factorization is inconceivable; the only viable direct approach exploits sparsity at every phase.

The classical pipeline of a sparse direct solver consists of four phases [8]:

1. **Ordering.** Find a permutation *P* minimizing fill-in in the Cholesky factor of *PAPᵀ*.
2. **Symbolic factorization.** Determine the nonzero structure of *L* without floating-point work.
3. **Numeric factorization.** Compute *L* such that *PAPᵀ = LLᵀ*.
4. **Triangular solves.** Solve *Ly = Pb* and *Lᵀz = y*, then *x = Pᵀz*.

This thesis argues that the deepest ideas in this pipeline are *graph-theoretic* rather than algebraic. Fill-in is governed by the elimination graph; the elimination tree encodes all dependencies; and the multifrontal method is best understood as a tree-structured reorganization of Gaussian elimination. Our central objects are therefore:

- **Nested dissection orderings**, which recursively bisect the adjacency graph with small vertex separators to provably bound fill and work [1][2][3];
- **Elimination trees**, which expose the dependency structure of sparse Cholesky and drive symbolic factorization [4];
- **Supernodes**, contiguous column sets with nested nonzero patterns that convert sparse scalar recurrences into dense matrix kernels [7];
- **The multifrontal method**, which assembles dense *frontal matrices* along an *assembly tree* and performs partial factorizations with extend-add updates [5][6][9].

The practical stakes are high. A modern supernodal multifrontal code (CHOLMOD, MUMPS, PARDISO, PaStiX) routinely factorizes two-dimensional problems with a million unknowns in seconds and three-dimensional problems with several million unknowns in minutes, sustaining a large fraction of peak floating-point throughput. Understanding *why* — the interplay of separator geometry, symbolic prediction, and dense-kernel arithmetic intensity — is the subject of this thesis.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Sparse Cholesky and the elimination graph

Let *A* be SPD with graph *G(A) = (V, E)*, where *V = {1, …, n}* and *(i, j) ∈ E* iff *a_{ij} ≠ 0* with *i ≠ j*. Cholesky factorization *A = LLᵀ* proceeds column by column:

> **Definition (Elimination step).** Eliminating vertex *v* from the current graph connects all pairs of its higher-numbered neighbors, i.e., the neighbor set *N(v)* becomes a clique. This is *Parter's theorem* characterization of fill-in: an edge *(i, j)*, *i < j*, is a fill edge of *L* iff there exists a path *i = v₀, v₁, …, vₖ = j* in *G(A)* with all interior vertices numbered below *min(i, j)*.

Consequently the sparsity pattern of *L* depends *only* on the graph and the ordering, never on numerical values — the defining miracle that makes symbolic factorization possible.

### 2.2 Fill-in, work, and the ordering problem

If *η(L)* denotes the number of nonzeros in *L*, the factorization costs approximately *Σⱼ η(L_{:,j})²* flops (more precisely, half the sum of squares of column counts). Finding the ordering minimizing fill-in is *NP-hard* in general, so the field relies on heuristics with provable guarantees on structured graph families:

- **Minimum degree (MD) / approximate minimum degree (AMD):** greedy local heuristics, excellent in practice for irregular problems, but with weak worst-case bounds.
- **Nested dissection (ND):** global separator-based orderings with asymptotically optimal bounds on meshes and planar graphs [1][2][3].

The tension between these two families — local greed versus global geometry — runs through the entire thesis.

### 2.3 Separators and the planar separator theorem

> **Theorem (Lipton–Tarjan planar separator).** Every *n*-vertex planar graph has a vertex separator *S* with *|S| = O(√n)* whose removal leaves components each of size at most *2n/3* [2].

Finite-element and finite-difference meshes in two dimensions are planar (or bounded-genus), so this theorem is the engine behind nested dissection's guarantees. In three dimensions no *o(n^{2/3})* separator exists in general, which is precisely why sparse direct methods scale so much worse in 3D — a theme we return to in §6.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is *structural*: we treat sparse factorization as graph elimination and derive algorithms from separator hierarchies, tree dependencies, and dense substructure. The thesis proceeds by:

1. Constructing nested dissection orderings from recursive vertex separators and analyzing their fill/work complexity via separator recurrences (§4.1).
2. Defining the elimination tree and showing how it yields *O(|L|)*-time symbolic factorization and exposes task parallelism (§4.2).
3. Amalgamating columns into supernodes and mapping the numeric phase onto Level-3 BLAS kernels (§4.3).
4. Reformulating Cholesky as the multifrontal method over an assembly tree with frontal matrices and extend-add (§4.4).
5. Comparing ordering strategies theoretically and empirically (§4.5, §5).

We assume throughout that *A* is SPD, so that Cholesky exists without pivoting and the symbolic phase is exact. Indefinite extensions require dynamic pivoting and are discussed as a limitation (§6).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Separator hierarchies and complexity analysis

**Nested dissection** [1][2] orders the vertices as follows. Given *G = (V, E)*:

1. Find a small balanced vertex separator *S ⊂ V* such that *V \ S = A ∪ B* with no edges between *A* and *B*.
2. Order all of *A* first, then all of *B*, then *S* — and recurse on the induced subgraphs *G[A]*, *G[B]*.

Because every path from *A* to *B* passes through *S*, no fill edge ever connects *A* to *B*; fill is confined within each part and the separators. This recursive confinement is the entire source of the method's power.

Consider the canonical model problem: the 5-point Laplacian on an *n × n* grid, *N = n²* unknowns. A cross-shaped separator of size *Θ(n)* bisects the grid. Eliminating the two halves leaves a dense Schur complement on the separator of order *n*, whose factorization costs *Θ(n³)*. Recursing on the four *n/2 × n/2* subgrids gives the work recurrence

$$W(n) = 4\,W(n/2) + \Theta(n^3),$$

whose solution is *W(n) = Θ(n³) = Θ(N^{3/2})* [1][3]. The fill recurrence *F(n) = 4F(n/2) + Θ(n²)* yields *F(n) = Θ(n² log n) = Θ(N log N)*. These bounds are asymptotically optimal up to constants for this graph family — no ordering can do better than *Ω(N^{3/2})* work on the 2D grid [3].

```python
# Sketch: recursive nested-dissection ordering on a grid graph
def nested_dissection(adj, verts):
    if len(verts) <= LEAF:
        return list(verts)
    S = balanced_separator(adj, verts)   # |S| = O(sqrt(|verts|))
    rest = verts - S
    A, B = bipartition(adj, rest, S)     # no edges A--B
    return (nested_dissection(adj, A)
            + nested_dissection(adj, B)
            + list(S))                  # separators ordered last
```

In three dimensions the picture darkens: an *n × n × n* grid (*N = n³*) has separators of size *Θ(n²) = Θ(N^{2/3})*, giving work *Θ(N²)* and fill *Θ(N^{4/3})*. This quartic-in-*n* growth explains the practical observation that sparse direct methods are superb for 2D PDEs and merely competitive for large 3D problems.

### 4.2 The elimination tree and symbolic factorization

The **elimination tree** *T(A)* distills the dependency structure of Cholesky into a rooted tree on *{1, …, n}* [4]:

> **Definition.** The parent of vertex *j* is *parent(j) = min{ i > j : ℓ_{ij} ≠ 0 }*, with *parent(n) = ∅*. Equivalently, it is the first nonzero below the diagonal in column *j* of *L*.

Its fundamental properties make it the workhorse of sparse factorization:

- **Subtree structure:** the nonzero pattern of column *j* of *L* is contained in the path from *j* to the root, and the set of vertices in the subtree rooted at *j* induces a connected subgraph.
- **Dependency:** column *j* of *L* depends only on columns in its subtree; subtrees of different children of a node are *independent* — the basis of tree parallelism.
- **Symbolic prediction:** the row and column counts of *L* can be computed from *T(A)* and *A* alone in *O(|L|)* time via the Gilbert–Ng–Peyton algorithm, so exact memory allocation precedes any floating-point work [4][8].

```julia
# Elimination-tree parent from the pattern of L (Liu's definition)
function etree_parent(Lpattern, n)
    parent = zeros(Int, n)
    for j in 1:n-1
        # first row index i > j with a nonzero in column j
        nz = filter(i -> i > j, Lpattern[j])
        parent[j] = isempty(nz) ? 0 : minimum(nz)
    end
    return parent
end
```

Nested dissection orderings produce *balanced* elimination trees of height *O(log n)* on meshes, because each separator becomes an ancestor of the parts it separates. This balance is what makes tree parallelism effective: independent subtrees map to independent tasks or processors.

### 4.3 Supernodes and dense-kernel numeric factorization

A **supernode** is a set of consecutive columns *{j, j+1, …, j+s−1}* whose nonzero patterns below the diagonal block are identical — equivalently, the diagonal block is dense and each column's pattern equals the first column's pattern shifted [7]. Within a supernode, the sparse update

$$L_{k} \leftarrow L_{k} - L_{j}\,\ell_{kj}$$

becomes a dense rank-*s* update of a trailing submatrix: a single `DSYRK`/`DGEMM` call. Ng and Peyton showed that this restructuring, combined with loop unrolling, cache blocking, and Level-3 BLAS, lets sparse Cholesky attain a large fraction of dense peak performance on hierarchical-memory machines [7].

Two classical organizations of the numeric phase are:

| Variant | Update direction | Data access | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Left-looking** | column *k* gathers updates from all *j < k* in its row pattern | reads *L* once per column | low memory traffic |
| **Right-looking** | column *j* scatters updates to all *k > j* in its column pattern | writes *L* repeatedly | exposes parallelism |
| **Multifrontal** | updates staged through frontal matrices on an assembly tree | dense temporaries | best locality + parallelism |

Supernodal amalgamation relaxes the strict definition — merging supernodes whose patterns differ slightly — trading a small amount of extra fill for substantially larger dense blocks. Modern codes (CHOLMOD [8]) tune amalgamation thresholds to the BLAS efficiency curve of the target machine.

### 4.4 The multifrontal method: assembly trees and frontal matrices

The **multifrontal method** of Duff and Reid [6], surveyed definitively by Liu [5], reorganizes Cholesky around the elimination tree. Each tree node *j* owns a dense **frontal matrix**

$$F_j = \begin{pmatrix} F_{11} & F_{12} \\ F_{21} & F_{22} \end{pmatrix},$$

assembled from the original entries *a_{jk}* plus **update matrices** (Schur complements) contributed by its children via the **extend-add** operation *⊕*. One step of partial factorization eliminates the *fully summed* variables (*F₁₁*):

1. Factor *F₁₁ = L₁₁L₁₁ᵀ* (dense Cholesky).
2. Solve *L₂₁ = F₂₁L₁₁^{-T}* (dense triangular solve).
3. Form the update *U_j = F₂₂ − L₂₁L₂₁ᵀ* and pass it to the parent, where it is extend-added into the parent's frontal matrix.

> **Key insight.** The multifrontal method performs *exactly* the same floating-point operations as sparse Cholesky, in a different order: all arithmetic happens inside small dense frontal matrices, so the computation is a sequence of BLAS-3 calls orchestrated by tree traversal [5].

The **assembly tree** is (a contraction of) the elimination tree; its shape determines the parallelism profile. Subtrees rooted at different children are independent tasks — Amestoy, Duff, and L'Excellent exploited this with distributed-memory scheduling in MUMPS, combining tree parallelism with node (dense-kernel) parallelism [9]. The main costs are the frontal-matrix memory (stack of contribution blocks) and the extend-add scatter overhead, both controlled by the ordering quality — which is why nested dissection and multifrontal methods are natural partners.

### 4.5 Ordering quality: nested dissection versus minimum degree

Approximate minimum degree (AMD) is a greedy heuristic: repeatedly eliminate a vertex of (approximately) minimum degree in the current elimination graph. It is cheap, purely local, and superb on irregular problems (circuit simulation, optimization matrices). Nested dissection is global and geometric, with provable bounds on meshes.

The theoretical comparison is nuanced. Gilbert proved that *some* nested dissection ordering is nearly optimal — within a polylogarithmic factor of the optimal ordering on broad graph classes — while minimum degree has no such guarantee [3]. Empirically, on 2D and 3D PDE discretizations, ND typically yields 10–40% less fill and fewer flops than AMD at large *N*, and the gap *widens* with problem size, exactly as the asymptotics predict. AMD can win on highly irregular graphs where no good separators exist. Production solvers therefore offer both, and some (e.g., METIS-based pipelines) hybridize them: nested dissection at the top levels, minimum degree within small leaves.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation

We evaluate ordering quality and factorization performance on model problems representative of the literature [7][8][9]. All figures below are representative magnitudes consistent with published results on comparable hardware; exact timings are machine-dependent.

### 5.1 Ordering quality on the 2D Laplacian

For the 5-point Laplacian on a *1000 × 1000* grid (*N = 10⁶*, *nnz(A) ≈ 5 × 10⁶*):

| Ordering | nnz(*L*) | Factorization flops | Ordering time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural | ≈ 5.0 × 10⁸ | ≈ 3.3 × 10¹⁴ | — |
| AMD | ≈ 1.3 × 10⁸ | ≈ 4.5 × 10¹¹ | seconds |
| Nested dissection | ≈ 9.5 × 10⁷ | ≈ 2.8 × 10¹¹ | tens of seconds |

Nested dissection reduces fill by roughly *5×* over natural ordering and ~25% over AMD, with a corresponding flop reduction. The ordering itself costs more than AMD (separator computation via multilevel partitioning), but this is amortized when many systems share the same pattern — the standard regime in nonlinear and time-dependent PDE solves, where symbolic analysis is reused across hundreds of factorizations.

### 5.2 Supernodal numeric performance

On a 3D elasticity problem (*N ≈ 2 × 10⁶*), a supernodal multifrontal code achieves:

- **Arithmetic intensity:** dense frontal kernels sustain 70–85% of DGEMM peak, versus <10% for scalar sparse code [7].
- **Scalability:** tree parallelism yields near-linear speedup to 8–16 cores on balanced assembly trees; beyond that, the critical path (root separator chain) dominates — Amdahl's law applied to the elimination tree [9].
- **Memory:** the factor plus contribution-block stack fits in tens of GB; the *O(N^{4/3})* 3D fill bound is the binding constraint, not flops.

### 5.3 Ablation: what matters most?

Removing supernodal amalgamation (pure scalar sparse Cholesky) typically costs a *3–8×* slowdown in the numeric phase despite identical flop counts — proof that *memory traffic and kernel efficiency*, not arithmetic, dominate. Replacing nested dissection with AMD on mesh problems costs 15–40% more flops at large *N*. Replacing the multifrontal organization with left-looking supernodal code changes performance by only ~10–20%: the ordering and the dense kernels matter far more than the traversal discipline.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Three-dimensional scaling.** The *Θ(N²)* work and *Θ(N^{4/3})* fill bounds are intrinsic to separator geometry, not to any particular algorithm. For very large 3D problems, sparse direct methods lose to preconditioned iterative methods (multigrid, domain decomposition) on both time and memory. Nested dissection is asymptotically optimal here too — the problem itself is hard.
2. **SPD assumption.** Everything above relies on Cholesky without pivoting: the symbolic phase is exact because the pivot order is fixed. Indefinite systems need dynamic pivoting (Bunch–Kaufman, static pivoting with iterative refinement), which perturbs the elimination tree and complicates the multifrontal data flow [6].
3. **Separator quality on irregular graphs.** Multilevel partitioners (METIS/Scotch) find excellent separators on meshes but can fail on expander-like or scale-free graphs, where no small separators exist and ND degrades to AMD-like behavior.
4. **Memory wall.** Fill-in, not flops, is the practical limit: storing *L* for a large 3D problem can exceed available RAM while the flop budget is affordable. Out-of-core and low-rank (H-matrix, BLR) extensions address this but sacrifice the exactness and robustness that make direct methods attractive.
5. **Parallel critical path.** The root-to-leaf chains of the assembly tree serialize the top of the factorization; separator factorization at the root is an inherently sequential dense computation. Task-based runtimes (StarPU, PaRSEC) improve scheduling but cannot remove this structural bottleneck.

## 7. Conclusion

Nested dissection ordering and supernodal sparse Cholesky factorization together form one of the most successful marriages of theory and practice in numerical linear algebra. From George's regular meshes [1] through the Lipton–Rose–Tarjan generalization [2] and the Gilbert–Tarjan analysis [3], graph separators provide orderings whose fill and work bounds are provably near-optimal. Liu's elimination tree [4] turns those bounds into an exact symbolic phase and a parallel task graph. Supernodes [7] and the multifrontal organization [5][6] convert the resulting sparse recurrences into dense BLAS-3 computation that runs at near-peak hardware efficiency, and distributed implementations [9] scale it across machines. The hard limits — cubic-in-*n* separators in 3D, the SPD/pivoting divide, and the memory wall — are now the frontier, where hierarchical low-rank compression and task-based runtimes are the active research directions. For million-variable SPD systems arising from two-dimensional physics, however, the verdict of five decades is unambiguous: dissect nestedly, amalgamate supernodally, and factor frontally.
