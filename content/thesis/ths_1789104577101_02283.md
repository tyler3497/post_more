# Secure Multiparty Computation: Garbled Circuits, the GMW Protocol, and the SPDZ Preprocessing Model

## Abstract

Secure multiparty computation (MPC) enables $n$ mutually distrusting parties holding private inputs $x_1,\dots,x_n$ to jointly evaluate an agreed function $f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ while revealing nothing beyond the prescribed output. This thesis presents a unified treatment of the three canonical dishonest-majority paradigms that define modern practical MPC: **Yao's garbled circuits**, which encode Boolean circuits as doubly-encrypted truth tables evaluated in constant rounds; the **Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson (GMW) protocol**, which evaluates circuits gate-by-gate over XOR secret shares with oblivious transfer as its sole interaction primitive; and the **SPDZ preprocessing model**, which splits maliciously secure arithmetic computation into an expensive input-independent offline phase producing authenticated multiplication triples and a cheap online phase costing only two field openings per multiplication. We formalize each core primitive — the garbled gate, the 1-out-of-4 OT AND evaluation, and Beaver-triple randomization over MAC-authenticated additive shares — prove their correctness and security reductions, compare asymptotic communication and round complexity, and characterize the preprocessing–online trade-off that makes SPDZ the dominant architecture for malicious-security MPC over large arithmetic circuits.

## 1. Introduction

Secure function evaluation was posed by Yao in 1982 [11] — two millionaires comparing fortunes without disclosure — and matured through Yao's garbled-circuit technique (FOCS 1986 [1]) and the Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson completeness theorem: *any* efficiently computable function admits a secure protocol assuming oblivious transfer [2].

Two orthogonal design axes emerged. The **circuit axis** distinguishes Boolean circuits (natural for garbled circuits and GMW) from arithmetic circuits over finite fields (natural for secret-sharing protocols). The **adversary axis** distinguishes the *honest-majority* setting, where protocols such as BGW and CCD exploit information-theoretic secret sharing with $t < n/2$ corruptions, from the *dishonest-majority* setting, where up to $n-1$ parties may collude and information-theoretic security is impossible without setup — cryptographic assumptions become mandatory.

Dishonest-majority MPC was long considered prohibitively expensive. The **SPDZ protocol** of Damgård, Pastro, Smart, and Zakarias [5] changed this by importing Beaver's *preprocessing model* [4]: push all expensive cryptography into an input-independent offline phase that manufactures correlated randomness, leaving the online phase essentially information-theoretic.

Our contributions: a self-contained formalization of garbled circuits in the Bellare–Hoang–Rogaway framework [3] with its key optimizations; a complete derivation of GMW's AND-gate evaluation through pairwise 1-out-of-4 oblivious transfer [2]; a rigorous exposition of SPDZ's authenticated secret sharing, information-theoretic MAC algebra, and somewhat-homomorphic-encryption-based offline phase [5], including the MASCOT [6] and Overdrive [7] successors; and a unified asymptotic comparison showing Beaver triples as the common substrate of all three paradigms.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The MPC Ideal Functionality

MPC security is stated in the *real/ideal paradigm*: a protocol $\pi$ realizes $\mathcal{F}$ if for every real adversary $\mathcal{A}$ an ideal-world simulator $\mathcal{S}$ produces an indistinguishable view. Adversaries are *semi-honest* (follow the protocol, attempt to infer more) or *malicious* (deviate arbitrarily), and corruption is *static* or *adaptive*.

> **Theorem:** *(Kilian, 1988 — OT-completeness).* Oblivious transfer is complete for secure computation: any functionality securely realizable at all is realizable in the OT-hybrid model [12].

This theorem is the fulcrum of the entire thesis: GMW assumes OT per AND gate, garbled circuits use OT for input transfer, and SPDZ's offline phase reduces triple generation to OT (MASCOT [6]) or somewhat homomorphic encryption [5].

### 2.2 Cryptographic Building Blocks

**Oblivious transfer (OT).** In 1-out-of-2 OT, a sender holds $(m_0, m_1)$ and a receiver holds a choice bit $b$; the receiver learns $m_b$ only, while the sender learns nothing about $b$ [12]. *OT extension* amortizes many OTs from a few base OTs, making OT-per-gate practical [8].

**Additive secret sharing.** A value $x \in \mathbb{F}$ is shared as $x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$ where each party $P_i$ holds $x_i$ uniform subject to the constraint. Linear operations are *local*: $\langle z \rangle = \langle x \rangle + \langle y \rangle$ requires no communication. Multiplication, however, is not local — this single fact motivates the entire preprocessing literature [9].

### 2.3 Circuit Models and Cost Metrics

Protocols are compared on **round complexity**, **communication per gate**, and **computational cost** (symmetric vs. public-key operations). For Boolean protocols the relevant gate count is AND gates, since XOR gates can be made free [10].

| Metric | Garbled circuits | GMW | SPDZ (online) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Circuit domain | Boolean | Boolean | Arithmetic over $\mathbb{F}_p$ |
| Rounds | $O(1)$ | $O(\text{depth})$ | $O(\text{depth})$ |
| Comm. per AND/mult | $O(\kappa)$ (2 ciphertexts, half-gates) | $O(n^2\kappa)$ (OTs) | $O(n)$ field elements (2 openings) |
| Adversary | Semi-honest (malicious w/ cut-and-choose) | Semi-honest (malicious w/ compilers) | Malicious, $n-1$ corruptions |
| Setup/preprocessing | None (OT extension) | None | Heavy: triples + MACs |

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Yao's Garbled Circuits

The garbler assigns to each wire $w$ two random $\kappa$-bit *labels* $W_w^0, W_w^1$ encoding the Boolean values. For a gate $g$ with input wires $a, b$ and output wire $c$ computing $g: \{0,1\}^2 \to \{0,1\}$, the garbler produces a *garbled table* of four doubly-encrypted entries:

$$T_g = \left\{\ \mathrm{Enc}_{W_a^i, W_b^j}\left(W_c^{\,g(i,j)}\right)\ \right\}_{i,j \in \{0,1\}}$$

permuted randomly (the *point-and-permute* optimization adds a public permute bit to each label so the evaluator decrypts exactly one row).







```python
import os
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.ciphers import Cipher, algorithms, modes

def garble_and_gate(wa0, wa1, wb0, wb1, wc0, wc1):
    """Return 4-row garbled table for an AND gate (point-and-permute omitted)."""
    table = []
    for i, wa in enumerate([wa0, wa1]):
        for j, wb in enumerate([wb0, wb1]):
            out = wc1 if (i & j) else wc0          # AND truth table
            key = bytes(x ^ y for x, y in zip(wa, wb))
            nonce = os.urandom(12)
            ct = Cipher(algorithms.AES(key), modes.GCM(nonce)).encryptor()
            table.append(nonce + ct.update(out) + ct.finalize() + ct.tag)
    return table  # evaluator decrypts exactly one row
```

### 3.2 The GMW Protocol

GMW evaluates the same Boolean circuit, but instead of encrypting truth tables, the parties maintain an invariant: for every wire $w$ carrying value $v$, the parties hold XOR-shares $v_1,\dots,v_n$ with $v = \bigoplus_i v_i$ [2].

- **Input sharing:** party $P_i$ with input bit $x$ samples uniform $x_1,\dots,x_n$ with $\bigoplus x_i = x$ and distributes them.
- **XOR/NOT gates:** evaluated *locally* — each party XORs (or flips, for exactly one designated party) its shares.
- **AND gates:** require interaction — each party pair uses one **1-out-of-4 OT** to obtain shares of the cross terms $x_i y_j \oplus x_j y_i$ (full derivation in Section 4.2) [2].

> **Theorem:** *(GMW completeness).* Assuming semi-honest two-party OT, for every $n$-input functionality $\mathcal{F}$ there exists an $n$-party protocol that $(n-1)$-securely computes $\mathcal{F}$ against semi-honest adversaries, in the OT-hybrid model [2].

The price is round complexity: the protocol needs one OT round per AND-gate *layer*, i.e., $O(\text{depth})$ rounds. Communication is $O(n^2)$ OTs per AND gate — cheap on LAN, punishing on WAN.

### 3.3 The SPDZ Preprocessing Model

SPDZ targets **arithmetic circuits** over a large prime field $\mathbb{F}_p$ with **malicious security against $n-1$ corruptions** — the strongest standard notion short of guaranteed output delivery (impossible with dishonest majority). Its architecture splits computation into two phases [5]:

**Representations.** Values are additively shared with information-theoretic authentication. A global MAC key $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_p$ is itself secret-shared as $\alpha = \sum_i \alpha_i$, and each shared value $x$ carries a MAC share $\gamma(x)_i$ such that $\sum_i \gamma(x)_i = \alpha \cdot x$. No party knows $\alpha$; the representation is written $\langle x \rangle$.

The MAC algebra is linear, so additions and scalar multiplications are computed locally on shares [5].

**Online phase.** Given preprocessed **Beaver triples** — random authenticated sharings $\langle a \rangle, \langle b \rangle, \langle c \rangle$ with $c = a \cdot b$ — multiplication of $\langle x \rangle, \langle y \rangle$ proceeds exactly as in the semi-honest case:

1. Open $d = x - a$ and $e = y - b$ (two reconstructions — the *only* communication).
2. Each party computes $\langle z \rangle_i = d \cdot e \cdot [i=1] + d \cdot \langle b \rangle_i + e \cdot \langle a \rangle_i + \langle c \rangle_i$.

Correctness is immediate: $z = (x-a)(y-b) + (x-a)b + (y-b)a + ab = xy$ [4][9]. Crucially, $d$ and $e$ are uniformly random masks, so opening them leaks nothing about $x, y$.

**MAC check.** Before output, the parties run a batched verification: for all opened values $v_1,\dots,v_m$ they sample public random coefficients $r_j$, commit to $t_i = \sum_j r_j (\gamma(v_j)_i - \alpha_i v_j)$, then open and check $\sum_i t_i = 0$. Any additive error injected into a value survives with probability at most $1/|\mathbb{F}_p|$ over the choice of $r_j$ — negligible for 128-bit fields — and the commit-then-open ordering defeats rushing adversaries [5]. On failure the protocol aborts rather than outputting a wrong value — security *with abort*, the strongest notion achievable under dishonest majority.

**Offline phase.** Generating authenticated triples without any party learning $a, b, c$ is the hard problem. The original SPDZ protocol uses **somewhat homomorphic encryption** (BGV-style): parties encrypt their shares, homomorphically compute encryptions of $a \cdot b$, and attach zero-knowledge proofs of plaintext knowledge to enforce malicious behavior [5]. Successors improved this dramatically: **MASCOT** generates triples from oblivious transfer with malicious security via OT extension [6], and **Overdrive** accelerates the SHE-based approach with better packing [7].

```rust
/// Authenticated additive share in the SPDZ online phase (simplified).
struct SpdzShare {
    value_share: u64,  // x_i,  additive share of x
    mac_share: u64,    // gamma(x)_i, additive share of alpha * x
}

impl SpdzShare {
    /// Linear ops are local: no communication.
    fn add(&self, other: &SpdzShare, p: u64) -> SpdzShare {
        SpdzShare {
            value_share: (self.value_share + other.value_share) % p,
            mac_share: (self.mac_share + other.mac_share) % p,
        }
    }
    fn mul_const(&self, c: u64, p: u64) -> SpdzShare {
        SpdzShare {
            value_share: (self.value_share * c) % p,
            mac_share: (self.mac_share * c) % p,
        }
    }
}
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Garbled Gate as a Cryptographic Compiler

A garbled gate is best understood not as encryption of data but as *encryption of control flow*: the gate's truth table is public, the wire values are secret, and the garbled table is a cryptographic compiler that binds the two. The BHR security reduction is instructive: the privacy simulator, given only $f$ and $y = f(x)$, garbles a fake circuit whose tables all encrypt the true output labels, programming PRF responses so the evaluator's decryption succeeds exactly on the active row. Holding one label per wire, the evaluator cannot distinguish inactive rows from random strings under CPA security [3].

### 4.2 GMW's AND Gate: OT as a Multiplication Primitive

The GMW AND-gate derivation deserves to be written in full, because it exposes oblivious transfer *as* a multiplication protocol. For $z = x \land y$ with shares $x = \bigoplus_i x_i$, $y = \bigoplus_i y_i$:

$$z = \left(\bigoplus_i x_i\right)\left(\bigoplus_j y_j\right) = \bigoplus_i x_i y_i \;\oplus\; \bigoplus_{i<j}\left(x_i y_j \oplus x_j y_i\right)$$

Each party computes $x_i y_i$ locally. For each pair $i < j$, the parties need additive shares of $x_i y_j \oplus x_j y_i$ where $P_i$ knows $(x_i, y_i)$ and $P_j$ knows $(x_j, y_j)$. This is precisely a 1-out-of-4 OT: $P_i$ samples $z_{ij} \xleftarrow{\$} \{0,1\}$ and inputs the four messages

$$m_{a,b} = z_{ij} \oplus (x_i \cdot b) \oplus (a \cdot y_i) \qquad a,b \in \{0,1\}$$

$P_j$ chooses $(a,b) = (x_j, y_j)$ and receives $z_{ij} \oplus x_i y_j \oplus x_j y_i$; $P_i$ keeps $z_{ij}$. Their XOR is the desired cross term, and $P_j$ learns nothing about $(x_i, y_i)$ beyond it, by OT security. Summing all $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs plus local terms yields shares of $z$ [2]. Note the parallel with Beaver triples (Section 4.4): both techniques *linearize* multiplication by masking true inputs with correlated randomness — GMW's correlation is the OT itself, consumed interactively per gate; SPDZ's is the triple, consumed non-interactively from a preprocessed pool.

### 4.3 SPDZ Authentication: The MAC Algebra

The SPDZ MAC scheme's elegance lies in the fact that authentication is *homomorphic over the same linear operations the protocol needs*. Represent a sharing as $\langle x \rangle = \{(x_i, \gamma(x)_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ with $\sum_i \gamma(x)_i = \alpha \sum_i x_i$. Addition and scalar multiplication preserve the relation locally. The only operation that breaks it — multiplication — is handled by Beaver's trick, which itself uses only openings (public reconstructions) and linear operations, hence preserves authenticability [5].

The MAC check protocol is the subtle core. Naively verifying every opened value would double communication; instead SPDZ batches: for all publicly opened values $v_1,\dots,v_m$ with MAC shares $\gamma(v_j)_i$, the parties sample public random coefficients $r_j$, commit to $t_i = \sum_j r_j (\gamma(v_j)_i - \alpha_i v_j)$, open the commitments, and check $\sum_i t_i = 0$. A standard Schwartz–Zippel-style argument shows that any additive error $\delta_j$ injected into some $v_j$ survives the check with probability at most $1/|\mathbb{F}_p|$ over the choice of $r_j$ — negligible for 128-bit fields. The commit-then-open ordering is essential: it prevents a rushing adversary from choosing errors after seeing the coefficients [5].

Triple generation must produce $\langle a \rangle, \langle b \rangle, \langle c \rangle$ with $c = ab$ while leaking nothing about $a, b$. The original SHE-based construction has parties publish encryptions of their shares under a distributed BGV key, homomorphically compute the cross terms, and decrypt distributively, with zero-knowledge proofs of plaintext knowledge enforcing honest behavior [5]. MASCOT replaces the public-key machinery with maliciously secure OT extension [6].

### 4.4 Beaver Triples: The Universal Substrate

Donald Beaver's 1991 observation — that a random triple $(a, b, c=ab)$ *randomizes* multiplication — is the most reused idea in MPC [4]. Its power is threefold: **it decouples interaction from inputs** (the triple is generated before inputs are known); **it is scheme-agnostic** (the same algebra works over XOR-shares, additive field shares, and Shamir shares); and **it composes** (triples are consumed independently per gate, so the online phase parallelizes across each circuit layer).

The following table summarizes the asymptotic cost of one multiplication/AND gate across paradigms, for $n$ parties and security parameter $\kappa$:

| Protocol | Rounds per layer | Communication per gate | Crypto per gate | Adversary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yao (half-gates) | $O(1)$ total | $2\kappa$ bits | Symmetric | Semi-honest |
| GMW | $O(1)$ | $O(n^2\kappa)$ bits (OTs) | OT extension | Semi-honest |
| BMR (multiparty garbling) | $O(1)$ | $O(n^2\kappa)$ bits | Symmetric + OT | Malicious |
| SPDZ online | $O(1)$ | $2$ field elements opened | None (info-theoretic) | Malicious, $n-1$ |
| SPDZ offline (MASCOT) | $O(1)$ amortized | $O(\kappa)$ bits per triple | OT extension | Malicious, $n-1$ |

The table's message is stark: *the online phase of SPDZ is essentially free* — two openings per multiplication, no cryptography — because the offline phase has already paid. Garbled circuits pay symmetric-key work per gate but need only constant rounds; GMW pays per-gate OTs but no preprocessing. The choice is an engineering trade-off over circuit shape, network latency, and trust model [8][11].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Security Theorems

> **Theorem:** *(SPDZ security, Damgård et al.).* The SPDZ protocol securely realizes any arithmetic circuit functionality over $\mathbb{F}_p$ against a static malicious adversary corrupting up to $n-1$ parties, with abort, in the preprocessing-hybrid model. The distinguishing advantage of any PPT environment is bounded by $q/|\mathbb{F}_p| + \mathrm{negl}(\kappa)$, where $q$ is the number of MAC checks [5].

*Proof sketch.* The simulator extracts the adversary's inputs from the authenticated input-sharing phase, forwards them to the ideal functionality, and simulates the online phase with random triples: since $d = x - a$ and $e = y - b$ are uniform for uniform $a, b$, the simulation is perfect. Additive tampering is caught by the MAC check except with probability $1/|\mathbb{F}_p|$, and the offline zero-knowledge proofs reject malformed ciphertexts [5].

> **Theorem:** *(GMW, Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson).* In the OT-hybrid model, the GMW protocol $(n-1)$-securely computes any polynomial-size circuit against semi-honest adversaries. The simulator replaces each honest party's shares with uniform bits and each OT with a simulated transcript; indistinguishability follows from OT security and the uniformity of XOR-shares [2].

> **Theorem:** *(Beaver triple correctness).* Let $\langle x \rangle, \langle y \rangle$ be additive sharings and $(\langle a \rangle, \langle b \rangle, \langle c \rangle)$ a triple with $c = ab$. Opening $d = x - a$, $e = y - b$ and setting $\langle z \rangle_i = de\cdot[i{=}1] + d\langle b \rangle_i + e\langle a \rangle_i + \langle c \rangle_i$ yields a sharing of $z = xy$, and the joint distribution of $(d, e)$ is independent of $(x, y)$ [4][9].

### 5.2 Performance Landscape

The MP-SPDZ framework [8] provides the definitive empirical comparison, implementing all major paradigms in one codebase. Its published benchmarks establish the qualitative landscape that theory predicts:

- **SPDZ online phase** achieves throughput in the *thousands of field multiplications per second* on commodity LAN, with per-multiplication communication of exactly two field-element openings — the information-theoretic minimum for this sharing scheme [5][8].
- **Preprocessing dominates cost**: MASCOT-based triple generation runs orders of magnitude slower than the online phase, confirming the offline/online split as a deliberate *latency–throughput* trade-off — expensive setup amortized over many cheap evaluations [6].
- **Garbled circuits** remain unmatched for low-depth Boolean computations over WAN: constant rounds make latency the binding constraint, and half-gate garbling keeps per-AND communication at $2\kappa$ bits [3].
- **GMW with OT extension** is competitive on LAN for shallow circuits but degrades linearly with circuit depth due to $O(\text{depth})$ round complexity [2].

A recurring empirical finding across implementations is that *XOR is free but AND is not*: circuits must be optimized for multiplicative complexity (AND count for Boolean, multiplication count for arithmetic), not total gate count — a lesson that reshapes how MPC-friendly algorithms are designed [10].

## 6. Limitations

**The preprocessing bottleneck.** SPDZ's offline phase exceeds the online phase by orders of magnitude; for one-shot computations it cannot be amortized, and total cost may exceed maliciously secure garbled circuits with cut-and-choose. Function-dependent preprocessing (reusing correlated randomness across related computations) remains an active research area [6][7].

**No guaranteed output delivery.** With a dishonest majority, a malicious party can always abort after learning the output — fairness and guaranteed output delivery are *provably impossible* without honest majority. SPDZ provides security *with abort* (and identifiable abort in some variants); applications requiring fairness must either assume honest majority or accept this limitation [5].

**Circuit blowup for non-algebraic operations.** Arithmetic circuits over $\mathbb{F}_p$ express additions and multiplications natively, but comparisons, bit decompositions, and fixed-point truncation require expensive conversions (e.g., bit-decomposition subcircuits or daBits/edaBits in MP-SPDZ). Mixed computations often need *protocol conversion* between arithmetic and Boolean domains, adding conversion overhead [8].

**GMW's round complexity.** $O(\text{depth})$ rounds make GMW unsuitable for deep circuits over high-latency networks; each AND layer is a network round-trip. Constant-round alternatives (BMR-style multiparty garbling) exist but inherit garbled circuits' per-gate ciphertext costs scaled by $n$ [2].

**Covert vs. malicious, static vs. adaptive.** The theorems above assume *static* corruption; adaptive security requires significantly heavier machinery (non-committing encryption, UC-secure OT) that most implementations lack. Covert security — cheating detected with some probability — offers a cheaper middle ground at the cost of a weaker threat model [5].

**Side channels and implementation pitfalls.** The proofs assume idealized primitives; deployments must additionally handle timing side channels in OT extension, secure erasure of preprocessing material (triples are key material — reuse or leakage breaks everything), and authenticated, session-bound transcripts to prevent cross-protocol attacks [11].

## 7. Conclusion

They are three projections of a single design principle: **secure computation = randomized encoding + trivial evaluation**. Yao randomizes the circuit itself, replacing semantic wire values with random labels; GMW randomizes the *shares*, replacing wire values with uniform XOR-masks and using OT to multiply them; SPDZ randomizes *multiplication*, replacing each product with a masked reconstruction against preprocessed triples.

The practical consequence is the modern MPC stack: an offline phase manufacturing authenticated correlated randomness (via SHE [5], OT [6], or hybrids [7]), and an online phase that is essentially distributed arithmetic. Open problems remain — concretely efficient maliciously secure constant-round protocols for deep circuits, function-dependent preprocessing with formal composability, and closing the gap between covert and full malicious security at acceptable cost. But the trajectory since 1986 is unambiguous: each generation pushes more cryptography into preprocessing, driving the marginal cost of a secure multiplication toward the information-theoretic floor.
