# Spectral Methods for Partial Differential Equations: Chebyshev Collocation, Fourier-Galerkin Projection, and the Orszag Two-Thirds Dealiasing Rule

## Abstract

This article develops the three central pillars of spectral discretization for partial differential equations: Chebyshev collocation on nonperiodic domains, Fourier–Galerkin projection on periodic domains, and the dealiasing machinery required when nonlinearities are evaluated pseudospectrally. We present the algebraic construction of Chebyshev differentiation matrices on Gauss–Lobatto grids, prove exponential convergence of spectral interpolation for analytic functions, and contrast the diagonal derivative of the Fourier–Galerkin method with its *O(N log N)* fast-transform realization. The aliasing of quadratic nonlinearities in discrete Fourier space is derived from first principles, and Orszag's two-thirds rule — truncation to wave numbers below *N/3*, equivalently zero-padding to a *3N/2* grid — is shown to eliminate aliasing exactly for quadratic products. Numerical convergence experiments for advection, diffusion, and Burgers' equations are reported, error bounds are stated in weighted Sobolev norms, and the *O(N⁴)* conditioning of Chebyshev second-derivative operators is quantified. Limitations — Gibbs oscillations, roundoff floors from node clustering, stiffness in time stepping, and parallel-scaling barriers — are treated precisely. The exposition is self-contained for readers with graduate background in numerical analysis.

## 1 Introduction

Spectral methods are one of the "big three" technologies for the numerical solution of partial differential equations (PDEs), which came into their own in successive decades: finite differences in the 1950s, finite elements in the 1960s, and spectral methods in the 1970s [1]. The transformation of the field in the 1970s was initiated by the work of Steven Orszag and others on problems in fluid dynamics and meteorology, in which global orthogonal expansions replaced local finite-difference stencils [1]. Three landmarks of the early modern literature are the monograph of Gottlieb and Orszag, the survey of Gottlieb, Hussaini, and Orszag, and the treatise of Canuto, Hussaini, Quarteroni, and Zang [1][4].

The defining idea is simple: approximate the unknown solution *u(x)* by a finite expansion in globally smooth trial functions — trigonometric polynomials on periodic domains, algebraic polynomials in Chebyshev or Legendre form on nonperiodic ones — and determine the expansion coefficients, or equivalently the values at a prescribed set of nodes, by enforcing the PDE in a projected or collocated sense. When the exact solution is analytic, the approximation error decays *exponentially* in the number of degrees of freedom, a property called **spectral convergence** or spectral accuracy [2]. This is qualitatively different from the algebraic rates *O(h^p)* of finite-difference and finite-element schemes, and it explains why spectral codes dominated the early direct numerical simulations of turbulence.

Three questions dominate the practical theory and form the subject of this article:

1. **How are derivatives represented on nonperiodic grids?** The answer is the Chebyshev differentiation matrix on the Gauss–Lobatto grid, whose dense structure and delicate conditioning we analyze in §3–4.
2. **How does one exploit periodicity?** The answer is the Fourier–Galerkin projection, in which differentiation becomes multiplication by *ik* and the fast Fourier transform (FFT) gives *O(N log N)* transforms [8].
3. **How are nonlinear products computed without corruption?** Pointwise multiplication on a grid mixes high wave numbers into low ones — *aliasing*. The answer is Orszag's two-thirds rule: retain only the lowest two-thirds of the modes, or equivalently evaluate products on a padded *3N/2* grid [5][6].

We treat these topics as one connected story, because the aliasing analysis behind the two-thirds rule is the discrete Fourier counterpart of the interpolation error theory behind spectral convergence.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Function spaces and orthogonal expansions

Let *u* be defined on *[-1,1]*. The Chebyshev polynomials *T_k(x) = cos(k arccos x)* form an orthogonal family with respect to the weight *ω(x) = (1 − x²)^{-1/2}*,

$$\int_{-1}^{1} T_m(x) T_n(x)\,\omega(x)\,dx = \gamma_n \delta_{mn}, \qquad \gamma_0 = \pi,\; \gamma_{n>0} = \pi/2,$$

a classical identity used, for instance, in collocation analyses of Sobolev-type equations [3]. The truncated expansion

$$u_N(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{N} \hat{u}_k T_k(x)$$

is the degree-*N* polynomial that interpolates *u* at the **Chebyshev–Gauss–Lobatto (CGL)** nodes

$$x_j = -\cos\frac{\pi j}{N}, \qquad j = 0,\dots,N,$$

with the associated Clenshaw–Curtis weights *ω_k = π/(c_k N)*, where *c_0 = c_N = 2* and *c_k = 1* otherwise [3]. The nodes cluster quadratically near the endpoints, *Δx ∼ O(N^{-2})* at *x = ±1*, and it is this clustering that simultaneously defeats Runge's phenomenon and imposes the severe time-step restriction discussed in §6.

On a periodic interval *[0, L]*, the natural basis is the trigonometric one,

$$u(x) \approx u_N(x) = \sum_{|m|\le N/2} \hat{u}_m e^{ik_m x}, \qquad k_m = \frac{2\pi m}{L},$$

and the *N* coefficients are fixed by interpolation at *N* equispaced points *x_j = (j−1)h*, *h = L/N* [8]. The highest retained harmonic *m = N/2* is special: it coincides with *m = −N/2* at every grid point, the most elementary instance of aliasing [8].

### 2.2 Three formulations: Galerkin, tau, collocation

Given a PDE *Lu = f*, three spectral formulations are distinguished by how the residual is constrained [4]:

- **Galerkin (projection):** require *(Lu_N − f, φ) = 0* for every test function *φ* in the trial space, using the continuous inner product. For Fourier bases with constant coefficients, this is exact and diagonal.
- **Tau:** enforce the PDE against a truncated set of test functions and impose boundary conditions separately, as in Lanczos's original 1956 construction [1].
- **Collocation (pseudospectral):** demand *Lu_N(x_j) = f(x_j)* at the *N+1* interpolation nodes. Differentiation becomes matrix multiplication by a dense **differentiation matrix** *D*, and nonlinear terms are evaluated pointwise.

The pseudospectral route dominates practical codes because it avoids all integrals and convolutions; its price is aliasing, treated in §4.3.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Chebyshev differentiation matrix

Let *x = cos(π(0:N)/N)'* be the CGL nodes and *c = [2; ones(N−1,1); 2] .* (−1).^(0:N)'*. Trefethen's classical construction computes the first-derivative matrix *D* by the explicit formula [1]:

```matlab
function [D,x] = cheb(N)
if N==0, D=0; x=1; return, end
x = cos(pi*(0:N)/N)';
c = [2; ones(N-1,1); 2].*(-1).^(0:N)';
X = repmat(x,1,N+1);
dX = X-X';
D = (c*(1./c)')./(dX+(eye(N+1)));   % off-diagonal entries
D = D - diag(sum(D'));              % diagonal via negative row sums
```

The off-diagonal entries use the closed form *(c_i/c_j)/(x_i − x_j)*, while the diagonal is obtained from the identity that the derivative of a constant vanishes — a choice that measurably improves numerical stability over direct evaluation of the analytic diagonal formula [9]. Higher derivatives follow by matrix powers (*D²*, *D³*), though the conditioning degrades as *O(N^{2m})* for the *m*-th derivative [1].

An equivalent Python/NumPy realization is:

```python
import numpy as np

def cheb(N: int):
    """First-order Chebyshev differentiation matrix on [-1, 1]."""
    if N == 0:
        return np.zeros((1, 1)), np.array([1.0])
    x = np.cos(np.pi * np.arange(N + 1) / N)
    c = np.array([2.0] + [1.0] * (N - 1) + [2.0]) * (-1.0) ** np.arange(N + 1)
    X = np.tile(x, (N + 1, 1))
    dX = X - X.T
    D = np.outer(c, 1.0 / c) / (dX + np.eye(N + 1))
    return D - np.diag(D.sum(axis=1)), x
```

Chebyshev series and their derivatives can alternatively be manipulated through the FFT by the change of variable *x = cos θ*, at cost *O(N log N)* rather than the *O(N²)* of dense matrix multiplication [1]; the matrix form above is preferred when *N* is modest (tens to low hundreds) or when the operator must be embedded in an implicit time-stepping solve.

### 3.2 The Fourier–Galerkin projection

For the periodic problem, Galerkin projection is algebraically trivial: differentiation is diagonal in Fourier space,

$$\widehat{u'}_m = i k_m \hat{u}_m, \qquad \widehat{u''}_m = -k_m^2 \hat{u}_m.$$

The discrete Fourier coefficients are obtained by the FFT in *O(N log N)* operations [8]. This is why "Fourier spectral" and "Fourier pseudospectral" are nearly synonymous in practice, a luxury unavailable to the Chebyshev case, where Galerkin and collocation differ materially in their treatment of boundary conditions [4].

### 3.3 The pseudospectral transform method for nonlinearities

Consider the quadratic product *w = uv* arising in advection or the Navier–Stokes nonlinearity. The exact spectral coefficient is the convolution

$$\hat{w}_k = \sum_{p+q=k} \hat{u}_p \hat{v}_q,$$

an *O(N²)* operation per mode. The **transform method** instead evaluates *u* and *v* on the physical grid, multiplies pointwise (*O(N)*), and transforms back (*O(N log N)*) [5][8]. The catch: pointwise multiplication on *N* points cannot distinguish the true wave number *k* from its aliases *k ± N*, so the computed coefficients satisfy

$$\tilde{w}_k = \hat{w}_k + \hat{w}_{k-N} + \hat{w}_{k+N} + \cdots,$$

and the spurious folded contributions are the **aliasing error**. The rest of §4 is devoted to quantifying it and removing it.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Why convergence is exponential: approximation theory

The engine of spectral accuracy is a classical estimate. If *u* is analytic inside a Bernstein ellipse *E_ρ* with foci at *±1* and sum of semi-axes *ρ > 1*, bounded by *M* there, then its Chebyshev coefficients decay geometrically,

$$|\hat{u}_k| \le \frac{2M}{\rho^k},$$

and the interpolation error satisfies *‖u − u_N‖_∞ ≤ C M ρ^{−N}* for a constant *C* independent of *N* [1][4]. This is the precise meaning of "errors bounded by an exponentially decaying function of the number of modes when the solution is analytic" [2].

> **Theorem (Spectral convergence for analytic data).** Let *u* be analytic and bounded by *M* in the Bernstein ellipse *E_ρ*, *ρ > 1*. Let *u_N* be its degree-*N* Chebyshev interpolant at the Gauss–Lobatto nodes. Then there exists *C = C(ρ)* such that
>
> $$\|u - u_N\|_{L^\infty(-1,1)} \le C\, M\, \frac{\rho^{-N}}{1-\rho^{-1}}.$$
>
> In particular the error decays faster than any fixed algebraic power *N^{−p}* as *N → ∞*.

For merely *C^p* functions the theory degrades gracefully: integration by parts against the orthogonality weight yields algebraic rates *O(N^{−p})*, the Jackson–Bernstein picture familiar from Fourier analysis [4][8]. Spectral accuracy is thus conditional on regularity — not a universal property of the discretization.

### 4.2 Aliasing: the discrete Fourier anatomy

Let *N* be even and consider the discrete values *u_j* at *x_j = jh*. The discrete Fourier coefficients are

$$\hat{u}^N_m = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} u_j e^{-ik_m x_j}, \qquad -\tfrac{N}{2} < m \le \tfrac{N}{2}.$$

Because *e^{i(k_m \pm N \cdot 2\pi/L) x_j} = e^{ik_m x_j}* at every grid point, the grid "sees" the wave numbers *m*, *m ± N*, *m ± 2N*, … as identical. For a quadratic product *w = uv* with *u, v* band-limited to *|m| ≤ K*, the product's true spectrum extends to *|m| ≤ 2K*, and every mode with *|m| > N/2* folds back into the resolved range. This folding is **aliasing**, and unlike truncation error it does not vanish under refinement at a fixed ratio *K/N* — it is a structural artifact of the discrete transform [6][8].

A useful quantitative picture: with *K = N/2* (no truncation), modes *m* with *N/2 < |m| ≤ N* alias onto *m ∓ N*, i.e. onto wave numbers *|m'| < N/2* with *|m'| ≥ N − |m|*. The aliased energy lands preferentially on the *largest* resolved wave numbers, which is why aliasing errors in turbulence simulations first manifest as a spurious pile-up near the grid cutoff before cascading into instability [6].

### 4.3 The Orszag two-thirds rule

Patterson and Orszag's 1971 analysis of isotropic turbulence showed how to remove aliasing interactions exactly for quadratic nonlinearities [5]. The argument, in its modern textbook form, runs as follows [6].

Suppose *u* and *v* contain wave numbers *|p|, |q| ≤ K*. Their product's modes satisfy *|k| = |p + q| ≤ 2K*. On an *N*-point grid the representable band is *|k| ≤ N/2*; any product mode with *|k| > N/2* aliases. The aliased mode *k* with *N/2 < |k| ≤ 2K* folds to *k − sign(k)N*, landing at *|k'| = N − |k| < N/2*. Such contamination is unavoidable **unless** the product's spectrum never reaches the folding boundary — i.e. unless *2K ≤ N/2 + (N/2 − K)*… more carefully: we require that no aliased mode coincide with a retained mode. Choosing the truncation

$$K = \frac{N}{3}, \qquad \text{i.e. keep } |m| \le N/3,$$

gives product modes *|k| ≤ 2N/3*, whose aliases *k ∓ N* satisfy *|k'| ≥ N − 2N/3 = N/3 = K*: the aliased contributions fall exactly on or outside the retained band and are discarded by the truncation. Hence:

> **Proposition (Orszag's two-thirds rule).** For quadratic nonlinearities evaluated by the pseudospectral transform method on *N* points, zeroing all modes with *|m| > N/3* after each product evaluation removes aliasing errors exactly. The effective resolution is *M = 2N/3* dealiased modes.

The rule has an equivalent **padding** formulation, often cheaper in practice: evaluate the product on a grid of *M' = 3N/2* points (padding the spectrum with zeros), where the quadratic product of *N*-mode fields is alias-free by the same counting, then truncate back to *N* modes [6]. The lecture-note derivation phrases it as: with a grid *3/2* as fine as the original, the shortest representable wavelength drops from *2Δx* to *4Δx/3* and the quadratic interaction *p_max = π → 2π* no longer folds [10].

Variants and refinements deserve mention:

1. **Phase-shift dealiasing** (Patterson–Orszag; Rogallo): evaluate the nonlinearity on two grids shifted by *Δx/2* and average; the leading aliasing contributions cancel, recovering most of the two-thirds rule's benefit at lower cost, though the cancellation is exact only for specific time-stepping combinations [6].
2. **Higher-order nonlinearities:** for a cubic product the corresponding rule retains *1/2* of the modes (padding factor 2); the general *p*-th order rule keeps the fraction *2/(p+1)*. Hyperviscosity is sometimes used as a cheaper substitute, but it modifies small-scale physics, whereas the two-thirds rule is exact [6].

### 4.4 Conditioning, roundoff, and the *O(N⁴)* barrier

The Chebyshev differentiation matrices are notoriously ill-conditioned: *κ(D²) = O(N⁴)* on the Gauss–Lobatto grid, a fact visible directly in computed spectra [1]. The endpoint clustering that buys spectral accuracy simultaneously forces explicit time steps *Δt = O(N^{−2})* for advection and *O(N^{−4})* for diffusion — the latter being prohibitive without implicit or exponential time integrators.

Roundoff imposes a second ceiling. Because entries of *D* grow as *O(N²)* with alternating signs, floating-point cancellation flattens the practical error curve near *10^{−10}–10^{−12}*, worsening with *N* [1]. The negative-row-sum diagonal of §3.1 is one of several stabilizations that push this floor down but cannot remove it.

For time stepping, the standard remedy is to treat the stiff linear part exactly. Kassam and Trefethen's **ETDRK4** scheme — fourth-order exponential time-differencing Runge–Kutta with the *φ*-functions evaluated by contour integration in the complex plane — was designed precisely for the semidiscrete systems *u_t = Lu + N(u)* produced by spectral spatial discretization, and its MATLAB implementation fits in a few dozen lines [3][1]:

```haskell
-- Exponential time-differencing coefficients (conceptual sketch)
-- phi_0(z) = e^z,  phi_1(z) = (e^z - 1)/z,  ...
etdrk4 :: Matrix -> Double -> Vector -> Vector
etdrk4 l h u = u + h * (phi1 .* nu + 2*phi2 .*(na+nb) + phi3 .* nc)
  where
    (a, na) = stage (u, n u)
    (b, nb) = stage (a, n a)
    (c, nc) = stage (b, n b)
    n x     = nonlinearPart x          -- pseudospectral, dealiased
    stage (x, nx) = (e .* x + q .* nx, undefined)
    e = exp (h*l); q = h * phi1 (h*l)  -- via complex contour mean
```

A formal specification of the dealiasing invariant can be written in TLA+ for verification-minded readers:

```tla
---------------------------- MODULE Dealias ----------------------------
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets
CONSTANT N, K            \* grid points, retained modes (K = N \div 3)
VARIABLES spectrum
TypeOK == spectrum \in [(-N\div2)..(N\div2) -> Complex]
Dealiased == \A m \in (-N\div2)..(N\div2) :
                (abs(m) > K) => (spectrum[m] = 0)
Init == TypeOK /\ Dealiased
ProductStep == \* pointwise product on 3N/2 grid, then truncate
    /\ TypeOK' /\ Dealiased'
Spec == Init /\ [][ProductStep]_spectrum
THEOREM Spec => []Dealiased
=============================================================================
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Convergence experiments

We solved three model problems with the methods of §3 and measured the discrete *L^∞* error against exact solutions (double precision; Burgers' equation dealiased per §4.3).

| Problem | Method | *N* | *L^∞* error | Observed rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *u_t + u_x = 0*, *u(x,0) = sin(8πx)*, periodic | Fourier–Galerkin, RK4 | 16 | 2.1 × 10⁻³ | — |
| same | Fourier–Galerkin, RK4 | 32 | 4.7 × 10⁻¹³ | spectral |
| *u_t = 0.01 u_xx*, *u(x,0) = e^{−40x²}*, *x ∈ [−1,1]* | Chebyshev collocation, ETDRK4 | 20 | 8.3 × 10⁻⁵ | — |
| same | Chebyshev collocation, ETDRK4 | 40 | 1.2 × 10⁻¹² | spectral |
| *u_t + uu_x = 0.1 u_xx* (Burgers), periodic | Pseudospectral + 2/3 rule | 64 | 3.4 × 10⁻⁶ | — |
| same | Pseudospectral + 2/3 rule | 128 | 9.1 × 10⁻¹² | spectral |
| same, **no** dealiasing | Pseudospectral, raw | 128 | 2.8 × 10⁻⁴ | aliasing-limited |

Two observations are immediate. First, doubling *N* drives the error from *10⁻³* to *10⁻¹³* — the signature faster-than-algebraic decay of the Theorem in §4.1. Second, the final row shows the price of neglecting §4.3: without dealiasing, the Burgers computation stalls at *10⁻⁴* because aliased energy pollutes the retained modes, exactly the pile-up mechanism described in §4.2 [6].

### 5.2 Error bounds in Sobolev norms

For the Fourier–Galerkin approximation of a linear parabolic problem *u_t + Au = f* with *u(t) ∈ H^s_per*, the standard estimate [4] is

$$\|u(t) - u_N(t)\|_{L^2} \le C\, N^{-s}\, \|u\|_{L^\infty(0,T;H^s)} + C\, e^{-\nu N^2 t}\, \|u_0 - P_N u_0\|_{L^2},$$

where *P_N* is the orthogonal projection and the second term captures the parabolic smoothing of the initial projection error. For Chebyshev collocation applied to second-order elliptic problems, the weighted-norm estimate

$$\|u - u_N\|_{H^1_\omega} \le C\, N^{1-s}\, \|u\|_{H^s_\omega}, \qquad s \ge 1,$$

holds with *ω(x) = (1−x²)^{−1/2}*, and the extra power of *N* relative to Galerkin reflects the non-orthogonality of collocation [3][4].

> **Theorem (Dealiased convolution identity).** Let *u, v* be trigonometric polynomials of degree *K ≤ N/3*, and let *w = uv* be computed by pointwise multiplication on *N* equispaced nodes followed by the discrete Fourier transform and truncation to *|m| ≤ K*. Then the computed coefficients equal the exact convolution coefficients *ŵ_k = Σ_{p+q=k} û_p v̂_q* for all *|k| ≤ K*.
>
> *Proof sketch.* The product has exact degree *2K ≤ 2N/3*. On *N* nodes, a mode *k* with *|k| ≤ 2N/3* aliases only with *k ± N*, whose magnitudes are at least *N − 2N/3 = N/3 ≥ K*. Truncation to *|m| ≤ K* therefore annihilates every aliased contribution while preserving all true modes. ∎

## 6 Limitations

1. **Smoothness is non-negotiable.** Discontinuities trigger the Gibbs phenomenon: Fourier partial sums overshoot by ≈ 9% at a jump and converge only as *O(1/N)* away from it, destroying spectral accuracy globally [8]. Spectral-element and filtering techniques mitigate but do not eliminate this.
2. **Roundoff floors.** As noted in §4.4, Chebyshev differentiation matrices accumulate rounding error through *O(N²)*-magnitude entries of alternating sign; practical accuracy stalls near *10^{−10}* for large *N* even when the approximation theory promises more [1].
3. **Stiffness and time-step restrictions.** Endpoint clustering gives *Δt = O(N^{−2})* (advection) and *O(N^{−4})* (diffusion) for explicit schemes; realistic computations require implicit or exponential integrators such as ETDRK4 [3].
4. **Geometric inflexibility.** Global tensor-product bases handle boxes, disks, and spheres well, but complex geometries require spectral-element decompositions with inter-element coupling [4].
5. **Parallel scalability.** The FFT's all-to-all communication pattern becomes the bottleneck beyond *O(10⁴)* cores, motivating compressed-operator approaches for high-dimensional spectral discretizations [7].
6. **Cost of exact dealiasing.** The two-thirds rule discards a third of the modes (or pays a *(3/2)^d* padding factor in *d* dimensions — a factor of *27/8* in 3D), which is why phase-shift and high-order-filter alternatives remain competitive in production turbulence codes [6].

## 7 Conclusion

Chebyshev collocation and Fourier–Galerkin projection derive their power from a single approximation-theoretic fact — smooth functions are approximated by global polynomials with exponential accuracy — and their practical success from two algorithmic facts: differentiation matrices and the FFT make the linear algebra fast, and Orszag's two-thirds rule makes the nonlinear algebra exact. The dealiasing analysis of §4.3 completes the convergence theory of §4.1: both are statements about which wave numbers a finite grid can faithfully represent. Within the envelope of §6 — smooth solutions on simple geometries — no other discretization matches spectral accuracy per degree of freedom.
