# Leaderless Consensus Protocols: Dependency-Graph Ordering, Commutativity Exploitation, and Fast-Path Design in Generalized Paxos, EPaxos, CURP, and Tempo

## Abstract

## 1 Introduction

State machine replication (SMR) is the standard mechanism for building fault-tolerant services: a set of replicas agrees on a sequence of commands and applies them deterministically, so that a quorum of survivors preserves service despite crashes [1]. Classical Paxos achieves this by funneling every decision through a distinguished leader — a single proposer whose prepare and accept phases impose *two message delays* before any learner can execute a command [1]. Multi-Paxos amortizes the leader's prepare phase, but the leader remains the load hotspot, the latency gate, and the availability bottleneck: clients far from the leader pay trans-oceanic round trips for every write, and a leader failure halts ordering until a new view is established [4].

The *leaderless* program (2005–2021) asks whether the leader is essential. Lamport's Generalized Paxos [2] showed full consensus is stronger than necessary: learners need only agree on *equivalent* command sequences, so commutative commands may be learned in *two message delays*. EPaxos [3] made every replica a leader, ordering commands through *explicit dependency graphs* committed in one round trip. CURP [5] decoupled durability from ordering, letting commutative operations complete in one RTT via witnesses. Tempo [6] closed the circle: dependency graphs block execution under contention, and scalar timestamps with decentralized *timestamp stability* restore predictable execution latency without a leader.

This thesis unifies the four designs in a common formal framework, reconstructs each fast path with TLA+-style precision, and compares them quantitatively on latency, execution blocking, and recovery — identifying a persistent trilemma among fast commit, contention-robust execution, and simple recovery.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Classical consensus and the leader bottleneck

In single-decree Paxos [1], a proposer drives a *ballot* through two phases: `Prepare(n)` collects promises from a majority, and `Accept(n, v)` replicates the chosen value. The **quorum-intersection lemma** guarantees safety: any two majorities overlap in at least one acceptor, so the highest-ballot accepted value observed in phase 1 necessarily includes any previously chosen value. In the leader-based steady state (Multi-Paxos), phase 1 is executed once per leader, and each command costs *two message delays* from client to leader to majority: one round trip (RTT).

> **Theorem: Fast-path lower bound.** No fault-tolerant asynchronous consensus algorithm can guarantee execution of two concurrently issued commands in fewer than *two message delays*, and even in the absence of faults, no algorithm can guarantee two-delay execution of *interfering* concurrent commands. Classical consensus additionally requires *three* message delays as practically deployed [2].

The practical cost of the leader is threefold. First, **load imbalance**: the leader handles every proposal while followers are underutilized [3]. Second, **latency inflation in wide-area deployments**: a client in Asia contacting a leader in Virginia pays the intercontinental RTT plus the replication fan-out. Third, **availability interruption**: leader failure suspends ordering until a view change completes, even when a majority of replicas remains reachable.

### 2.2 Mencius: rotating the leader

Mencius [4] partitions the log's instance space round-robin across all replicas, so each replica leads its own slots and clients contact the nearest leader. Because the log stays totally ordered, slot *s* cannot execute until all slots *< s* are decided, forcing idle replicas to broadcast *skip messages*. Mencius shortens the client-to-leader distance but is not *generalized*: gaps block execution, skip traffic is all-to-all, and latency stays at two message delays.

### 2.3 Generalized consensus and command-structure sets

Lamport's Generalized Paxos [2] redefines the target of agreement. Instead of a *sequence* of commands, learners agree on a **command-structure set (cstruct)**: a partially ordered set of commands where two cstructs are *equivalent* if they extend to the same sequences modulo commutativity of non-interfering commands. The specification weakens classical consensus in three clauses:

- **Nontriviality.** A learner's cstruct contains only proposed commands.
- **Stability.** The learned cstruct grows monotonically; once `v` is learned, all later learned values have `v` as an *eq-prefix*.
- **Consistency.** Any two correct learners' cstructs can be extended to equivalent sequences.

The algorithmic consequence: concurrently issued *non-interfering* commands are each learned in exactly two message delays, since acceptors accept them independently and learners take the union. But when commands *interfere*, ballots conflict and a classical leader must serialize them — Generalized Paxos "demotes" to ordinary Paxos under contention [7], leaving fully leaderless design to EPaxos.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in three stages. First, we extract each protocol's *system model* and *ordering invariant* — the property letting replicas execute commands in an identical, application-consistent order. Second, we formalize each *fast path* as a TLA+-style transition, making explicit the preconditions for one-round commitment. Third, we compare the protocols on five axes: (i) commit latency, (ii) message complexity, (iii) execution blocking — the commit-to-execute gap, (iv) fast-path availability under contention, and (v) recovery complexity. Numerical claims come from the original evaluations [3][5][6]; we reproduce their figures and flag workload differences.

A recurring distinction is **commit** versus **execution**: a command is *committed* when its fate is fixed at a quorum, but *executed* only when a replica can place it in the global order. Leaderless protocols routinely commit fast and execute slowly — EPaxos's dependency graphs [3] and CURP's witness lag [5] both defer execution, while Tempo's timestamp stability [6] was designed to close the gap.

### 3.1 Protocol anatomy: roles and quorums

We fix terminology used throughout:

- **Coordinator / command leader**: the replica that receives a client command and drives its commit. In leaderless protocols every replica can play this role simultaneously for different commands.
- **Fast quorum** (size `⌊3n/4⌋` in EPaxos, `⌊n/2⌋ + f` in Tempo): the set of replicas that must agree for a one-round commit [3][6].
- **Classic quorum** (majority, `⌊n/2⌋ + 1`): the fallback used in slow paths and recovery.
- **Fast path**: commitment in a single RTT (two message delays). **Slow path**: commitment requiring a second round.

### 3.2 Commutativity as the common currency

All four protocols are parameterized by a binary *interference* relation `∼`: `c₁ ∼ c₂` iff executing `c₁;c₂` differs observably from `c₂;c₁`. Non-interfering commands commute and may appear in either order. Lamport called this *non-interference* [2], EPaxos *interference* [3], CURP *commutativity* [5]. The relation must be specified conservatively by the application — a misspecification silently breaks linearizability.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Generalized Paxos: weakening the problem

Generalized Paxos keeps the classic two-phase ballot structure but changes what ballots *propose*: instead of a single value, a ballot proposes an extension of a command-structure set [2]. Acceptors vote for cstructs, and a ballot is won when a quorum accepts the same cstruct. The critical innovation is in phase 2: an acceptor may accept a proposed cstruct even if it has seen a *different* cstruct for the same ballot, provided the two are *compatible* (their union is a valid cstruct). Compatible but distinct cstructs arise exactly when concurrent commands do not interfere.

> **Theorem: Generalized fast execution.** If commands `c₁ … cₖ` are pairwise non-interfering and each is proposed to a distinct ballot that reaches a quorum, then every learner learns a cstruct containing all of them within *two message delays* of each proposal, with no coordination between proposers [2].

The proof follows from compatibility: acceptors accept each proposal independently, and the union of accepted cstructs is itself accepted, so learners observe all commands after one round trip to a quorum. The limitation is equally sharp: interfering commands force a fallback to a classical leader round. Generalized Paxos is thus a possibility result; the engineering of a *fully* leaderless design is EPaxos's contribution.

### 4.2 EPaxos: egalitarian ordering through dependency graphs

EPaxos [3] assigns every replica the full set of roles — proposer, acceptor, learner — simultaneously. When replica `R` receives command `c`, it becomes `c`'s *command leader* and executes:

1. **Pre-accept phase.** `R` sends `PreAccept(c)` to a *fast quorum* of size `⌊3n/4⌋`. Each recipient computes `dep(c)` — the set of *interfering* commands it has already seen — and replies with its local dependency set.
2. **Fast path.** If all fast-quorum replies return *identical* dependency sets, `R` broadcasts `Accept(c, dep(c))`; upon a majority of acknowledgments, `c` is committed. Total: **one round trip** (two message delays).
3. **Slow path.** If replies disagree, `R` takes the union of reported dependencies and runs a classic Paxos accept phase on `(c, dep(c))` — two round trips.

Ordering is recovered *after* commitment by building a directed graph whose vertices are committed commands and whose edges point from `c` to each `d ∈ dep(c)`. Replicas execute commands in an order consistent with the graph: a command executes once all commands it (transitively) depends on have executed, with strongly connected components (SCCs) — which arise from concurrent interference — executed in a deterministic tie-broken order (e.g., by command ID).

```tla
---- MODULE EgalitarianOrdering ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Replicas, Commands
VARIABLES committed, deps, executed
Interferes(c1, c2) == c1 /= c2 /\ ~(Commute(c1, c2))
Commit(c) == /\ c \notin committed
             /\ committed' = committed \cup {c}
             /\ deps' = deps @@ (c :> {d \in committed : Interferes(c, d)})
             /\ UNCHANGED executed
Execute(c) == /\ c \in committed
              /\ \A d \in deps[c] : d \in executed
              /\ executed' = executed \cup {c}
              /\ UNCHANGED <<committed, deps>>
====
```

EPaxos targets (i) uninterrupted availability while a majority is reachable, (ii) uniform load balancing with no special roles, and (iii) optimal wide-area commit latency for `f = 1, 2` via the one-RTT fast path [3]. Its evaluation reports throughput up to an order of magnitude above Mencius and Multi-Paxos under uniform load, since every replica is an entry point.

The price is **execution blocking**: a committed command cannot execute until its dependency closure is committed, so under contention a single slow replica transitively stalls everything interfering with its pending commands. Graph storage, traversal, and deterministic SCC resolution add further cost. These are performance, not correctness, flaws — but they are the cliff that motivated Tempo [6].

| Property | EPaxos fast path | EPaxos slow path |
|---|---|---|
| Message delays | 2 (1 RTT) | 4 (2 RTTs) |
| Quorum | `⌊3n/4⌋` (fast) | majority |
| Trigger | Identical `dep(c)` across fast quorum | Disagreement → union + Paxos round |
| Execution | After dependency graph resolves | After dependency graph resolves |

### 4.3 CURP: separating durability from ordering

CURP [5] observes that the two-RTT cost of consistent replication conflates two distinct obligations: **durability** (the operation must survive crashes) and **ordering** (replicas must agree on the sequence). Only ordering requires coordination; durability merely requires the operation to be recorded at enough replicas. CURP therefore supplements any primary-backup (or consensus) system with lightweight, *unordered* replication to **witnesses**:

1. The client sends the operation to the primary *and*, in parallel, to `f` witnesses.
2. Each witness records the operation durably and replies.
3. The primary executes the operation and replies to the client — **without waiting for normal replication**, which proceeds asynchronously.
4. Before the primary may execute, it checks that all *witnessed-but-not-yet-replicated* operations commute with the new one. If any does not commute, the operation waits for normal replication: **2 RTTs**.

Result: commutative operations complete in **1 RTT** — like an unreplicated system — while non-commutative ones pay 2 RTTs. On primary crash, witness records merge with backup state during recovery [5]. In RAMCloud, CURP halved write latency (13.8 → 7.3 µs) and quadrupled throughput; in Redis, it added durability and consistency to a non-durable cache for ~12% latency overhead.

CURP is not a consensus protocol but a *replication accelerator* that still relies on a primary for ordering: nearly free when operations commute, ordinary primary-backup under hot-key contention. It is the clearest demonstration that commutativity can be monetized *without* redesigning consensus.

### 4.4 Tempo: timestamps and decentralized stability

Tempo [6] starts from EPaxos's weakness: explicit dependencies couple execution to the slowest replica in the dependency closure. Its radical simplification is to order commands by **scalar timestamps** and to execute a command once its timestamp is *stable* — i.e., once the replica knows that no future command can receive a smaller timestamp.

Each command's timestamp is the *maximum* of proposals from a fast quorum (`⌊n/2⌋ + f` replicas), each proposal being the replica's clock advanced past all timestamps seen. If enough replicas propose the same maximum, the fast path commits in one RTT; otherwise a slow path (classic Paxos on the timestamp) fixes it [6].

Execution uses **promise tracking**: committing a command with timestamp `t` attaches promises `⟨replica, clock⟩` recording clocks advanced past `t`. Timestamp `t` is *stable* once a replica holds such promises from a majority — every future command must then take a timestamp `> t`. A command executes as soon as its timestamp is stable, *without waiting for any other command to commit*.

> **Theorem: Timestamp stability.** If a replica has received, for timestamp `t`, promises `⟨p, c_p⟩` with `c_p ≥ t` from a majority of replicas, then no command subsequently committed can have timestamp `≤ t`. Hence executing all commands with timestamp `≤ t` in timestamp order is safe [6].

This dissolves EPaxos's blocking: stability is a property of *clocks*, not of the dependency closure. Tempo's evaluation [6] shows it outperforming EPaxos, Atlas, Caesar, and Janus in geo-distributed deployments, with far better tail latency under contention — exactly where dependency graphs choke. It also generalizes to *partial replication* (per-partition timestamps for multi-partition commands), extending leaderless SMR beyond full replication.

### 4.5 The fast-path design space: a synthesis

The four protocols occupy distinct points in a space parameterized by *what is agreed upon* and *what the fast path assumes*:

| Protocol | Agreed object | Fast-path assumption | Commit (fast) | Execution gating |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Generalized Paxos | Command-structure set | Non-interference | 2 delays | Immediate (compatible cstructs) |
| EPaxos | `(command, dependencies)` | Identical dep sets at `⌊3n/4⌋` | 2 delays (1 RTT) | Dependency-graph closure committed |
| CURP | (durability ∥ ordering) | Commutativity w/ witnessed ops | 1 RTT | Primary executes immediately |
| Tempo | `(command, timestamp)` | Timestamp quorum agreement | 2 delays (1 RTT) | Timestamp stability |

Two patterns emerge. First, fast-path quorums exceed a majority wherever recovery must reconstruct commit metadata without a leader (EPaxos's `⌊3n/4⌋`, Tempo's `⌊n/2⌋ + f`). Second, execution gating migrates from *explicit* (dependency edges) to *implicit* (clock stability) — trading modest clock logic for a large reduction in contention-induced tail latency.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Results

### 5.1 Latency: the one-RTT frontier

All three consensus protocols converge on the same fast-path latency — **one round trip** — the information-theoretic floor identified in [2]. The differentiator is *who* the client contacts: fixed in Multi-Paxos, nearest-replica in EPaxos and Tempo, so the RTT itself shrinks. EPaxos reports optimal wide-area latency for `f = 1, 2` [3]; Tempo matches it with stability-gated execution. CURP reaches 1 RTT *total* for commutative workloads, but only by delegating ordering to an underlying replication layer [5].

### 5.2 Throughput and load balance

EPaxos's evaluation shows the payoff of egalitarianism: with clients spread uniformly, aggregate throughput scales with replica count, exceeding Multi-Paxos by up to an order of magnitude [3]. CURP's RAMCloud deployment shows a different law: write throughput rose ~4× (184 → 728 kops/s) because the primary no longer waits for synchronous replication [5]. Tempo's contribution is steadiness: removing dependency-graph traversal from execution avoids the throughput collapse explicit-dependency protocols suffer as contention rises [6].

### 5.3 Execution blocking under contention

The decisive experiment in [6] varies the interference rate and measures the *commit-to-execute* gap. Dependency tracking exhibits a long tail: one uncommitted command at a slow replica transitively blocks everything interfering with it. Tempo's stability mechanism bounds this gap by clock progress rather than graph closure — *clocks summarize knowledge; graphs enumerate it*.

### 5.4 Formal verification status

All four designs carry proved safety arguments with uneven coverage. Generalized Paxos inherits the TLA+ treatment of the Paxos family [2]. The EPaxos authors shipped a machine-readable TLA+ specification [3], and independent model-checking has probed its intricate recovery paths — a sign of complexity, not of any published flaw. Tempo's safety is proved by hand against its PSMR specification [6]; CURP's is a refinement proof validated by its RAMCloud and Redis integrations [5]. Fast-path optimism *concentrates* correctness risk in recovery — the path exercised least in testing.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **The interference relation is trusted, not verified.** Every protocol assumes a correct commutativity specification from the application. An overly permissive relation silently violates linearizability; an overly conservative one forfeits the fast path. No production system *infers* commutativity soundly from code — the largest adoption barrier.

2. **Recovery complexity grows with optimism.** EPaxos's slow path and coordinator recovery entangle fast-quorum and classic-quorum state; Tempo's recovery reconstructs timestamp proposals across partitions [6]. Leader-based protocols concentrate recovery in one view-change path; leaderless protocols distribute it, multiplying the states that must be correct.

3. **Clocks have failure modes.** Tempo's execution liveness depends on clock progress at a majority. A replica whose clock stalls can stall stability detection — a liveness coupling that explicit-dependency designs lack, since they gate only on message delivery.

4. **Partial replication is only partially solved.** Tempo extends to multi-partition commands [6], but cross-partition timestamp agreement reintroduces coordination costs reminiscent of two-phase commit. Scalable geo-replicated transactions over leaderless SMR remain open.

5. **Byzantine leaderless consensus is nascent.** All four protocols assume crash faults. Extending dependency-graph or timestamp-stability ordering to Byzantine replicas — where a faulty coordinator can equivocate on dependencies or timestamps — is largely unexplored.

6. **The trilemma persists.** No known protocol simultaneously achieves (a) one-RTT commit, (b) contention-independent execution latency, and (c) simple recovery. Tempo optimizes (a) and (b); CURP optimizes (a) for commutative workloads by punting (c) to the underlying protocol; EPaxos optimizes (a) and common-case simplicity at the cost of (b). Whether the trilemma is fundamental is the central open question.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The leaderless consensus program — Generalized Paxos, EPaxos, CURP, Tempo — is best understood as progressive *decoupling*: ordering from total order (commutative commands need no sequence position), coordination from a distinguished replica (every replica may lead), durability from ordering (witnesses suffice for commutative writes), and execution from dependency enumeration (clock stability replaces graph closure). Each step preserved the one-round-trip commit latency theory permits while attacking a different source of tail latency or imbalance.

The enduring insight: **consensus is a bundle of coupled obligations** — proposing, ordering, durability, execution, recovery — and the leader was load-bearing for all of them at once. Removing it forces each obligation to be re-solved on its own terms, which is why leaderless protocols are individually more complex than Raft yet collectively more robust to planetary-scale failure and latency profiles. Practical guidance: use CURP-style witness replication for commutative workloads on primary-backup systems; EPaxos-style dependency ordering for general workloads with modest contention; Tempo-style timestamp stability when tail latency under contention is the binding constraint. Byzantine tolerance, inferred commutativity, and cross-partition transactions will decide whether leaderless designs displace the leader entirely.
