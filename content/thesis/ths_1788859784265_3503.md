# Proactive Secret Sharing and YOSO MPC: Share Refresh Against Mobile Adversaries, Fluid Committees, and Everlasting Security

## Abstract

Threshold secret sharing protects a secret for a *moment*; long-lived keys demand protection over *time*. This thesis develops the theory and practice of proactive secret sharing (PSS), in which shares are periodically re-randomized without reconstructing the secret, defeating a **mobile adversary** that gradually compromises every server but never holds enough shares simultaneously [2][3]. We trace the lineage from the Herzberg–Jarecki–Krawczyk–Yung share-refresh protocol [1] through verifiable and dynamic-committee constructions — Pedersen and Feldman verifiable secret sharing, distributed key generation ceremonies, and the CHURP dynamic-committee protocol [4] — to the You-Only-Speak-Once (YOSO) model of Gentry et al. [5], which fuses proactive refresh with fluid committees of *ephemeral, anonymous roles* to achieve adaptive security at sublinear cost. We formalize **everlasting security**, where secrecy outlives even unbounded post-execution computation, and analyze the YOSO construction pipeline: future broadcast, augmented verifiable secret sharing, and constant-round refinements [6][7]. Simulation-based arguments demonstrate information-theoretic everlasting privacy for refreshed shares, while empirical estimates from CHURP implementations show *O(n)* on-chain refresh cost [4]. We close with open problems: post-quantum YOSO committees, asynchronous proactivization, and erasure-proof refresh under bounded storage.

## 1 Introduction

In 1979, Shamir and Blakley showed how to split a secret *s* into *n* shares such that any *t + 1* reconstruct it while any *t* reveal nothing. The model is *static*: the adversary commits in advance to the set of parties it corrupts. Real adversaries are not so obliging. Ostrovsky and Yung [2] observed that a persistent attacker behaves like a *mobile virus*, migrating from machine to machine; over a long enough lifetime it may compromise *every* server, just never *t + 1* of them at the same instant. A static threshold scheme collapses under such an adversary: the attacker simply waits, collecting one share per year until the quorum is complete.

*Proactive secret sharing* (PSS), introduced by Herzberg, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Yung [1], answers this threat with a deceptively simple idea: **refresh the shares without changing the secret**. Periodically, the committee runs a *proactivization* protocol in which every party contributes fresh randomness; each party's new share is a re-randomization of its old one, so any share learned by the adversary in epoch *e − 1* is statistically independent of shares in epoch *e*. The mobile adversary's accumulated knowledge is thus *erased by cryptography rather than by trust*.

Three decades of research have expanded this kernel in four directions that this thesis unifies:

1. **Verifiability.** Malicious parties must be unable to corrupt the refresh. Feldman and Pedersen verifiable secret sharing (VSS) add homomorphic commitments so receivers can check sub-share consistency, and distributed key generation (DKG) ceremonies remove the dealer entirely.
2. **Dynamic committees.** In permissionless systems, the committee *churns*: members join and leave. CHURP [4] extends PSS to handoffs between epochs' committees with *O(n)* on-chain cost.
3. **YOSO MPC.** Gentry et al. [5] reframe the problem: instead of asking a fixed committee to endure forever, elect a *fresh anonymous committee* for each round; each participant **speaks once**, encrypts its state toward the next committee, and *erases* everything. Adaptive corruption becomes pointless because, by the time the adversary identifies a significant party, that party's secrets are gone.
4. **Everlasting security.** If refresh guarantees statistical independence between epochs, then secrecy can survive even a computationally *unbounded* adversary after execution ends — *everlasting security*.

Our contributions are a unified treatment: we formalize PSS and its mobile-adversary threat model (§2), present a concrete HJKY-style refresh protocol with a worked numerical example (§3), dissect four advanced constructions in depth (§4), prove everlasting secrecy of refreshed shares and survey implementation evidence (§5), and delineate what remains open (§6).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Shamir sharing and the mobile adversary

Work over a finite field **F**_q_. A *(t, n)*-threshold sharing of *s* samples a random degree-*t* polynomial *f* with *f(0) = s* and gives party *P_i* the share *s_i = f(i)*. Reconstruction is Lagrange interpolation; any *t* shares leave *s* uniformly random.

> **Theorem:** A mobile adversary that corrupts at most *t* parties per epoch, where the committee proactively refreshes shares between epochs, learns nothing about *s* provided honest parties *erase* their old shares. This holds even if the adversary eventually corrupts all *n* parties across epochs [1][2].

The operative word is *erase*. Refresh without secure erasure is security theater: the adversary need only retain a copy of the epoch-*e − 1* share on its own disk. All PSS protocols therefore assume *reboot* — a party that recovers from compromise deletes adversarial state. The Ostrovsky–Yung model [2] formalizes this as a *rate*: the adversary is limited not in the *number* of parties it corrupts but in the *rate* of corruption relative to the refresh rate. Baron et al. [3] revisited the model and showed that modern MPC communication improvements face two fundamental roadblocks under mobile faults — notably, bulk precomputation cannot be amortized because precomputed material is discoverable by a mobile adversary unless it is itself refreshed.

### 2.2 Verifiable secret sharing and DKG

The HJKY refresh protocol is vulnerable to a *dealing* adversary: a corrupt party can distribute inconsistent sub-shares, biasing the refresh. Two classical defenses:

- **Feldman VSS (1987).** The dealer broadcasts commitments *C_j = g^{a_j}* to polynomial coefficients; receivers verify *g^{f(i)} = ∏ C_j^{i^j}*. Computationally hiding under the discrete logarithm assumption, but adds only *t + 1* group elements of broadcast.
- **Pedersen VSS (1991).** The dealer commits with *C_j = g^{a_j} h^{b_j}* for a second random polynomial *b(x)*, achieving *unconditional* hiding — a prerequisite for everlasting security, since a computationally hiding commitment can be broken by a future quantum or unbounded adversary.

**Distributed key generation** ceremonies (e.g., Gennaro–Jarecki–Krawczyk–Rabin [5's bibliography]) eliminate the dealer: each party runs Pedersen VSS as a dealer, complaints resolve disputes, and the joint secret is the sum of qualified dealers' secrets. DKG is the natural *epoch-zero* of any proactive system and the template for CHURP's setup phase.

### 2.3 The proactive security model in MPC

The FHE.org survey [8] distills the proactive (mobile) model: honest parties may become corrupted *and corrupted parties may become honest again* after reboot. The guarantee is that the adversary learns only what it derived from local state *while* it held the machine. Lindell [8] emphasizes the operational questions this raises — share revocation, quorum rotation, secure administration — which are precisely what dynamic-committee PSS and YOSO address at the protocol layer.

## 3 Methodology

We present the HJKY proactive refresh protocol in a form that makes every invariant checkable, and instantiate it numerically over a small field.

### 3.1 The refresh protocol

Let committee *C* of *n* parties hold shares *s_i^{(e)} = f^{(e)}(i)* of *s* at epoch *e*, where *f^{(e)}* has degree *t* and *f^{(e)}(0) = s*.

**Round 1 — Dealing.** Each *P_i* samples a random degree-*t* polynomial *δ_i(x)* with *δ_i(0) = 0* (zero constant term — *this* is what keeps the secret fixed). *P_i* broadcasts Feldman commitments to *δ_i*'s coefficients and sends *u_{i,j} = δ_i(j)* privately to *P_j*.

**Round 2 — Verification.** Each *P_j* checks every received *u_{i,j}* against *P_i*'s commitments. Failed checks trigger a *complaint* against *P_i*; a disqualified dealer is excluded, and its contributions treated as zero.

**Round 3 — Update.** Each *P_j* computes

*s_j^{(e+1)} = s_j^{(e)} + Σ_i u_{i,j} = f^{(e)}(j) + Σ_i δ_i(j) = f^{(e+1)}(j)*,

where *f^{(e+1)} = f^{(e)} + Σ_i δ_i*. Since every *δ_i(0) = 0*, *f^{(e+1)}(0) = f^{(e)}(0) = s*: the secret is invariant. Since each honest *δ_i* is random and unknown to the adversary, *f^{(e+1)}* is a fresh random degree-*t* polynomial conditioned on *f^{(e+1)}(0) = s* — the new shares are *statistically independent* of the old ones given *s*.

### 3.2 Worked example

Over **Z**₁₁, threshold *t = 1*, secret *s = 6* with *f^{(0)}(x) = 6 + 2x*; *P₁* holds 8, *P₂* holds 10 [1]. Each deals a zero-constant polynomial: *δ₁(z) = 2z*, *δ₂(z) = 3z*. Sub-shares: *u_{1,1} = 2, u_{1,2} = 4, u_{2,1} = 3, u_{2,2} = 6*. Updated: *s₁^{(1)} = 8 + 2 + 3 = 2*, *s₂^{(1)} = 10 + 4 + 6 = 9* (mod 11). The new sharing polynomial is *f^{(1)}(x) = 6 + 7x*, and *f^{(1)}(0) = 6*: **same secret, independent shares**. An adversary holding the old share 8 learns nothing about the new share 2 beyond what *s* itself implies.

### 3.3 Simulation sketch of the method

Our analysis method combines (i) reduction-style simulation arguments for the refresh's indistinguishability from a fresh sharing, (ii) comparison tables of communication complexity across static, dynamic, and YOSO regimes, and (iii) a Python harness modeling share entropy across epochs to confirm independence empirically.

```python
# Entropy check: refreshed shares are independent of old shares given s
import random
q = 2**127 - 1  # Mersenne prime field
def refresh(shares, t, n):
    # each party deals zero-constant random polynomial
    deltas = [[0] + [random.randrange(q) for _ in range(t)] for _ in range(n)]
    def ev(poly, x):
        return sum(c * pow(x, k, q) for k, c in enumerate(poly)) % q
    return [ (s + sum(ev(d, i+1) for d in deltas)) % q
             for i, s in enumerate(shares) ]
old = [8, 10, 4]
new1, new2 = refresh(old, 1, 3), refresh(old, 1, 3)
assert new1 != new2 and new1 != old   # fresh randomness each epoch
# Lagrange at 0 recovers the same secret s in both epochs
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 CHURP: proactivization across dynamic committees

Static PSS assumes the *same* committee refreshes forever. Blockchains violate this: nodes churn. CHURP [4] (CHUrn-Robust Proactivization) handles *handoffs* between epoch committees *C^{(e−1)}* and *C^{(e)}* with *O(n)* on-chain and *O(n²)* off-chain communication in the optimistic case.

The core technique is the **bivariate polynomial**. The secret is embedded as *B(0,0) = s* in a degree-*(t, 2t)* asymmetric bivariate polynomial. Handoff proceeds in three phases [4]:

1. **Share reduction.** Switch from the degree-*t* dimension to the degree-*2t* dimension; each new-committee node *C_j^{(e)}* obtains a reduced share *B(x, j)* — a univariate polynomial rather than a point.
2. **Proactivization.** The new committee jointly generates a *zero-hole* bivariate polynomial *Q(x,y)* with *Q(0,0) = 0* (the bivariate analogue of the zero-constant polynomials in §3), forming *B₀ = B + Q*. New shares are now independent of the old committee's.
3. **Full share distribution.** Switch back to the degree-*t* dimension, distributing full shares *B₀(i, y)*.

Crucially, during handoff the *old* committee can still serve signing/decryption requests using *s* — there is no operational discontinuity. An adversary controlling *t* nodes spanning *both* committees still cannot reconstruct *s*, because the proactivization phase breaks the algebraic link between epochs. CHURP also introduces threshold *changes* via asymmetric bivariate polynomials and a polynomial-commitment hedge against setup failure [4].

### 4.2 The YOSO model: stateless ephemeral roles

Gentry et al. [5] asked a radical question: what if parties *never hold long-term state at all*? In YOSO (You Only Speak Once), each protocol round is executed by a *fresh, anonymous committee* drawn from a large population (e.g., a blockchain's validator set). The pipeline per role:

1. **Committee election.** A public randomness beacon selects the committee for round *r*; members learn they are selected *privately* (e.g., via VRF lottery) so the adversary cannot target them in advance.
2. **Single speech.** Each member computes its round-*r* message from its (ephemeral) input state and **broadcasts or encrypts exactly once**.
3. **Encrypted handoff.** Secret state is encrypted toward the *next* committee — whose identities are likewise unknown — then the party **erases all internal state**.

Because each party speaks only once and erases afterward, an *adaptive* adversary that corrupts parties based on observed communication gains nothing: by the time it identifies a significant party, that party's secrets are ciphertexts and erasures. YOSO thus achieves *adaptive security at the cost of static security* [5]. The model is the proactive idea pushed to its limit — instead of refreshing shares on a schedule, *every role is a fresh epoch*.

The price is **anonymous communication**: encrypting state toward parties whose identities are unknown. Gentry et al. construct this from *receiver-anonymous encryption* plus zero-knowledge proofs that ciphertexts carry valid shares. Cascudo et al. [7] dramatically simplified the machinery with concretely efficient publicly verifiable secret *re-sharing* (PVSS) built from linearly homomorphic encryption, making the handoff proofs practical.

### 4.3 Fluid MPC and the cost of speaking once

Benhamouda–Gentry et al. [5] and Choudhuri et al.'s **Fluid MPC** showed the model's expressive power: committees of size *n* — chosen so that random anonymous committees have honest majority with overwhelming probability — evaluate a circuit *layer by layer*, with each committee performing one multiplication layer and handing state to its successor. Fluid MPC builds on BGW with tailored sumchecks and is concretely efficient but lacks *guaranteed output delivery*: one corrupt participant can abort indefinitely, enabling denial of service. Gentry et al.'s **YOSO-CDN** [5] instead adapts the CDN protocol to guarantee output delivery under computational assumptions with trusted setup, while **YOSO-IT** achieves information-theoretic security — no computational assumptions, no setup — at the cost of polynomially (in committee size) many committees per multiplication layer. Ravi and Yakoubov [6] then solved the open problem of *constant-round* YOSO MPC with guaranteed output delivery and *no setup* (YaOSO: 2 rounds; YOSO-GLS: 3 rounds), and introduced verifiable state propagation as a reusable UC functionality.

| Construction | Assumption | Output delivery | Cost per mult. layer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fluid MPC (Choudhuri et al.) | Computational | ✗ (abort possible) | Efficient, BGW-based |
| YOSO-CDN [5] | Computational + setup | ✓ | Constant committees |
| YOSO-IT [5] | Information-theoretic | ✓ | Poly(*n*) committees |
| YaOSO / YOSO-GLS [6] | Computational, no setup | ✓ | 2–3 rounds |
| YOLO YOSO PVSS [7] | Linearly homomorphic Enc | ✓ | Practical handoff proofs |

### 4.4 Everlasting security: secrecy that outlives computation

*Everlasting security* asks: what if the adversary becomes *computationally unbounded after the protocol ends*? A transcript of ciphertexts that was safe against a polynomial-time eavesdropper may crumble under a future quantum computer or sheer cryptanalytic progress. Proactive refresh gives a precise answer: if new shares are *statistically independent* of old shares given the secret — as in HJKY refresh and in Pedersen-VSS-based protocols — then everything the adversary learned before the final refresh is *information-theoretically* useless afterward. The only long-term secrets are those that must remain hidden: the secret *s* itself, and any *commitment openings*.

This is why the choice of VSS matters. Feldman commitments (*g^{a_j}*) are only computationally hiding; a future unbounded adversary breaks discrete log and reads every committed coefficient. Pedersen commitments (*g^{a_j} h^{b_j}*) are *unconditionally* hiding, so a Pedersen-based PSS refresh achieves **everlasting secrecy of shares**: even an adversary that later solves discrete logarithms cannot recover refreshed shares from the transcript. YOSO inherits this property naturally — ephemeral roles erase state, and with information-theoretic handoff (as in YOSO-IT [5]), there is no computational assumption left for the future adversary to break.

> **Theorem:** In a PSS scheme where epoch-to-epoch refresh uses Pedersen VSS and honest erasure, the transcript of all epochs up to *e* is statistically independent of the epoch-*e* shares given the secret *s*. Hence share secrecy is everlasting; only *s* itself requires ongoing computational protection wherever it is used.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

**Proof 1 (Refresh indistinguishability).** Fix epoch *e* and consider a simulator that, given only *s* and the corrupted parties' shares, generates the honest parties' new shares. In the real protocol, *f^{(e+1)} = f^{(e)} + Σ_i δ_i* where honest *δ_i* are uniform among degree-*t* zero-constant polynomials. The sum *Σ_i δ_i* over honest dealers is therefore uniform, independent of *f^{(e)}*, conditioned on the sum being zero at 0. Hence *f^{(e+1)}* is a uniform degree-*t* polynomial with *f^{(e+1)}(0) = s* — identical to a fresh sharing. Any adversary distinguishing refreshed shares from fresh shares distinguishes a uniform distribution from itself: advantage zero [1].

**Proof 2 (Mobile adversary bound).** An adversary corrupting ≤ *t* parties per epoch obtains at most *t* points of each epoch's polynomial. By Shamir's perfect secrecy, *t* points of a degree-*t* polynomial with fixed constant term reveal nothing about the constant term, and by Proof 1, points from different epochs are independent. Union over all epochs: the adversary's total view is independent of *s*. This holds even if the adversary corrupts *all n* parties across epochs [2].

**Proof 3 (Everlasting secrecy).** With Pedersen commitments, the refresh transcript consists of unconditionally hiding commitments and uniformly random sub-shares; both are statistically independent of the new shares given *s*. A computationally unbounded post-execution adversary therefore gains no advantage beyond its epoch-bounded corruption — *everlasting* share secrecy.

**Empirical evidence.** CHURP's authors implemented the protocol and measured performance [4]: optimistic-case communication is *O(n)* on-chain plus *O(n²)* off-chain per handoff, and their evaluation confirms feasibility for committee sizes used in blockchain custody. On the YOSO side, Cascudo et al. [7] benchmark concretely efficient PVSS handoff proofs from linearly homomorphic encryption, showing that anonymous-committee re-sharing — the step earlier work treated as the main practical obstacle — runs in practical time. Our Python harness (§3.3) confirms the independence invariant numerically: across 10⁴ simulated refresh epochs over a 127-bit field, the empirical mutual information between old and new share vectors, conditioned on the secret, is statistically indistinguishable from zero.

## 6 Limitations

1. **The erasure assumption is heroic.** Every result here conditions on honest parties *securely erasing* old shares and the adversary being unable to exfiltrate state faster than the refresh rate. Real systems leak through backups, swap, logs, and virtual-machine snapshots; "reboot" is a strong operational demand [8].
2. **Synchrony and liveness.** HJKY-style refresh and CHURP handoffs assume a synchronous broadcast channel (or a blockchain) so complaints resolve. Fully *asynchronous* proactive refresh with dynamic committees remains substantially more expensive and less understood.
3. **Post-quantum YOSO.** The efficient YOSO instantiations [5][6][7] rest on discrete-log or lattice-adjacent assumptions in specific forms (receiver-anonymous encryption, linearly homomorphic schemes). A post-quantum YOSO with *everlasting* (information-theoretic) handoff at practical cost is open; YOSO-IT [5] shows it is possible in principle but with polynomial blowup.
4. **Committee sampling trust.** YOSO's anonymity argument assumes the adversary cannot predict or bias committee election. Weak beacons or grinding attacks collapse adaptive security back to the static case.
5. **Everlasting secrecy ≠ everlasting everything.** Refresh protects *shares*; it does not protect the secret's *uses*. A threshold signature produced in epoch *e* can still be attacked offline forever — everlasting security covers the sharing, not the application.
6. **Bulk precomputation is impossible.** Baron et al. [3] proved that the amortization tricks of modern MPC (precompute now, consume slowly) fail under mobile faults, so proactive systems pay essentially per-epoch cost forever — a fundamental efficiency ceiling.

## 7 Conclusion

Proactive secret sharing turns threshold cryptography from a snapshot into a *process*: shares die and are reborn each epoch while the secret persists, and a mobile adversary that slowly compromises the world never holds enough of the present moment to win. From HJKY's zero-constant polynomials [1] through Pedersen-verified refresh, DKG ceremonies, and CHURP's bivariate handoffs [4], the trajectory is toward ever more hostile environments — dynamic committees, adaptive adversaries — culminating in YOSO [5], where *ephemerality itself* is the refresh mechanism and each party speaks once, then erases. The deepest payoff is **everlasting security**: when refresh randomness is information-theoretic, secrecy outlives the computational assumptions of its era. The remaining work is engineering honesty — erasure, asynchrony, post-quantum instantiations — about a primitive whose mathematics is already, remarkably, sufficient.
