# Accountable Finality in Proof-of-Stake: Casper FFG, Justification and Finalization, Slashing Conditions, and Long-Range Attack Resistance

## Abstract

Finality — the irrevocable commitment of a distributed ledger to a prefix of its history — separates settlement-grade consensus from probabilistic convergence. This thesis examines **Casper the Friendly Finality Gadget (FFG)**, the checkpoint-based overlay supplying Byzantine finality to Ethereum's proof-of-stake beacon chain. We formalize *supermajority links*, *justification*, and *finalization*, and present the two slashing conditions whose violation renders equivocation cryptographically attributable. We reconstruct the proofs of **accountable safety** (conflicting finalized checkpoints imply at least one-third of stake provably misbehaved) and **plausible liveness** (the inactivity leak guarantees finality can always resume), and situate FFG within **Gasper**'s integration with the LMD-GHOST fork-choice rule [2]. We analyze **long-range attacks** — posterior corruption and secret-key leakage — and show how **weak subjectivity**, checkpointing, and Bitcoin-anchored designs respond to an impossibility result for purely internal defenses [4][5]. The **ebb-and-flow** availability–finality dilemma and the bouncing attack are reviewed [3]. We close with limitations: plausible liveness is non-constructive, the inactivity leak trades safety for liveness under partitions, and accountable safety prices only slashable misbehavior.

## 1 Introduction

A blockchain is, at bottom, a distributed log whose readers must agree on an ever-growing *prefix*. In proof-of-work systems such as Bitcoin, agreement is asymptotic: the probability that a block at depth *k* is displaced decays exponentially in *k*, but it is never zero. For applications that settle value — exchanges crediting deposits, bridges releasing collateral, rollups posting commitments — probabilistic finality is an unsatisfying foundation. **Economic finality** asks a sharper question: *how much would it cost an adversary to revert a confirmed block?* If the answer is a concrete, attributable quantity of staked capital, then finality becomes an enforceable economic guarantee rather than a statistical hope.

Casper the Friendly Finality Gadget, introduced by Buterin and Griffith [1], was the first widely deployed answer. Casper FFG is deliberately *not* a complete consensus protocol; it is an **overlay** — a finality gadget — that can be composed with an arbitrary block-proposal mechanism, whether proof-of-work or proof-of-stake. Validators post signed votes on *checkpoints* (epoch-boundary blocks); when a supermajority (≥ 2/3 of stake) of votes forms certain patterns, checkpoints become *justified* and then *finalized*. Finalization is backed by **slashing conditions**: any validator whose votes violate two simple rules can be cryptographically convicted and lose its stake. The headline theorem, **accountable safety**, states that two conflicting checkpoints cannot both be finalized unless at least one-third of the total stake provably violated a slashing condition — so misbehavior is not merely expensive, it is *attributable*.

Ethereum's beacon chain instantiates FFG at planetary scale within **Gasper**, the combination of Casper FFG and the LMD-GHOST fork-choice rule [2]. This thesis reconstructs the gadget's mechanics from first principles, proves its core theorems, analyzes the **long-range attacks** that threaten every PoS design and the **weak subjectivity** doctrine that contains them, and critically evaluates what accountable safety does and does not buy.

> **Thesis roadmap.** Section 2 situates FFG among BFT replication, Casper CBC, and the nothing-at-stake problem. Section 3 formalizes the checkpoint model, supermajority links, justification, finalization, and slashing. Section 4 deep-dives into the justification pipeline, the accountable-safety proof, liveness and the inactivity leak, long-range attacks and weak subjectivity, and the ebb-and-flow dilemma. Section 5 surveys formal verifications and empirical parameters of the live beacon chain. Section 6 states limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From BFT state-machine replication to finality gadgets

Classical Byzantine fault-tolerant (BFT) state-machine replication — PBFT [Castro–Liskov], HotStuff, Tendermint — decides a total order of commands through multi-phase voting (prepare/commit) among a *fixed, known* validator set, tolerating *f < n/3* Byzantine faults in partial synchrony. Safety is absolute: once a value commits, no conflicting value can ever commit. Finality gadgets transpose this idea into the blockchain setting, where the validator set is *dynamic* (stake enters and exits) and the underlying chain grows by an independent proposal mechanism.

Casper exists in two lineages. **Casper CBC** (correct-by-construction, Zamfir) derives safety from first principles of message-passing and "latest-message" reasoning. **Casper FFG** (Buterin–Griffith) is the pragmatic sibling: it takes an existing chain and *overlays* checkpoint voting, explicitly targeting the Ethereum roadmap [1]. FFG's design philosophy is minimalism — the gadget must be simple enough to be proved correct on paper and simple enough to survive contact with a live, adversarial network.

### 2.2 The nothing-at-stake problem and why finality gadgets matter

In proof-of-stake, block production is computationally free. A validator asked to extend one of two competing forks loses nothing by extending *both*: there is no marginal cost to equivocation. This is the **nothing-at-stake** problem, and it defeats naive longest-chain PoS: an adversary can cheaply maintain arbitrarily many forks, and latecomers cannot distinguish the "real" history from simulated alternatives. Slashing changes the calculus by attaching a cost to *attesting*, not just to producing blocks. FFG's slashing conditions convert the cheap talk of attestations into cryptographically binding commitments: once a validator votes *s → t*, that vote is evidence that constrains all of its future votes, and contradictions are self-incriminating.

### 2.3 Checkpoints, epochs, and the validator model

FFG discretizes time into **epochs**, each containing a fixed number of slots (32 slots of 12 seconds in the beacon chain, so an epoch lasts 6.4 minutes). The **checkpoint** of epoch *e* is the first block of that epoch; checkpoints form a tree mirroring the block tree, and *h(B)* denotes a checkpoint's height in that tree. Validators are identified by public keys with associated stake (deposits); the total stake is *D*. A **vote** (attestation) is a signed tuple

$$\langle \nu, s, t, h(s), h(t) \rangle$$

meaning validator *ν* votes for the **checkpoint edge** *s → t*, where *s* is the source (a justified checkpoint *ν* has seen) and *t* is the target (a descendant checkpoint). Honest validators cast exactly one such vote per epoch, targeting the current epoch's checkpoint from the highest justified checkpoint in their view [1][2].

## 3 Methodology: The Formal Framework

We work in the standard partially synchronous model: messages among honest validators are delivered within an unknown bound *Δ* after an unknown global stabilization time (GST); before GST, the adversary controls delivery arbitrarily. The validator set may be dynamic, but safety arguments are stated per fixed total deposit *D* over the epochs in question, with validator-set changes handled by the beacon chain's activation/exit queues.

### 3.1 Supermajority links, justification, finalization

The entire gadget is built from one counting primitive.

> **Definition 1 (Supermajority link).** An ordered checkpoint pair *(a, b)* is a **supermajority link**, written *a →ᴶ b*, if validators controlling at least *2D/3* of total stake have published votes with source *a* and target *b*. Supermajority links may *skip* checkpoints: *h(b) > h(a) + 1* is permitted.

Justification and finalization are then defined inductively over a validator's *view* *G* (the set of messages it has observed), which is why the sets *J(G)* and *F(G)* are view-dependent:

> **Definition 2 (Justified checkpoint).** The genesis checkpoint *r* is justified. A checkpoint *c ≠ r* is **justified** in view *G* if there exists a supermajority link *c′ →ᴶ c* in *G* with *c′* justified. The chain of links back to genesis witnesses justification.

> **Definition 3 (Finalized checkpoint).** A checkpoint *c* is **finalized** in view *G* if *c* is justified and there exists a supermajority link *c →ᴶ c′* with *h(c′) = h(c) + 1* — a supermajority link to a *direct child* checkpoint.

The child must be a direct child (no skipping) for finalization, though justification links may skip. In the beacon chain this yields the familiar rule that finality typically lands two epochs after justification: epoch *e−2*'s checkpoint finalizes when epoch *e*'s checkpoint is justified on top of it (the *k*-finalization rules of `process_justification_and_finalization` generalize this to delays of up to four epochs under asynchrony) [2].

| Concept | Formal condition | Stake threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Supermajority link *a →ᴶ b* | ≥ 2/3 of stake voted *a → b* | 2D/3 |
| Justified checkpoint *c* | genesis, or ∃ justified *a*: *a →ᴶ c* | inductive |
| Finalized checkpoint *c* | justified *c* and ∃ *c →ᴶ c′*, *h(c′) = h(c)+1* | inductive |

### 3.2 The slashing conditions

Two votes by the same validator are *slashable* — constituting self-incriminating proof of misbehavior — if either condition holds. Let the votes be *α₁ = ⟨ν, s₁, t₁, h(s₁), h(t₁)⟩* and *α₂ = ⟨ν, s₂, t₂, h(s₂), h(t₂)⟩*:

> **Slashing Condition I (no double vote).** *h(t₁) = h(t₂)* with *α₁ ≠ α₂*. *A validator must not publish two distinct votes for the same target height.* This is the PoS analogue of equivocation.

> **Slashing Condition II (no surround vote).** *h(s₁) < h(s₂) < h(t₂) < h(t₁)*. *A validator must not vote within the span of its other votes* — the second vote's interval is strictly nested inside the first's.

These are Buterin's "two Casper commandments" [1]. Any observer who collects both offending votes can submit them as evidence; the protocol then destroys the offender's deposit (a small finder's fee rewards the whistleblower — 1/512 of the slashed balance in the beacon chain). Crucially, an honest validator following the protocol *cannot* violate either condition: casting exactly one vote per epoch, always from the highest justified checkpoint seen, never double-votes and never surrounds, because source heights are non-decreasing across epochs while target heights strictly increase.

In TLA⁺-style pseudocode, the safety monitor is simply:

```tla
--------------------------- MODULE CasperSlashing ---------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLE votes \* set of [validator |-> v, source |-> s, target |-> t]

Slashable(v1, v2) ==
    /\ v1.validator = v2.validator
    /\ v1 /= v2
    /\ \/ v1.target = v2.target                          \* Condition I
       \/ /\ v1.source < v2.source
          /\ v2.source < v2.target
          /\ v2.target < v1.target                        \* Condition II

TypeInvariant == \A v1, v2 \in votes : ~Slashable(v1, v2)
=============================================================================
```

Any execution violating `TypeInvariant` identifies a concrete culprit set — the *accountability* in accountable safety.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The justification/finalization pipeline in Gasper

Casper FFG as originally specified [1] is view-abstract; **Gasper** [2] operationalizes it atop the beacon chain's slot/epoch cadence with the LMD-GHOST fork-choice rule. Each epoch, every validator attests once: the *source* is the highest justified checkpoint in its view, the *target* is the epoch-boundary checkpoint of the chain it considers canonical. Aggregation of these attestations into supermajority links is exactly the justification computation.

Finalization in the live protocol follows the *finalization rules* evaluated at each epoch boundary over the last four justified checkpoints. Let *bits[i]* record whether the *i*-th most recent epoch boundary was justified and let *old_prev_justified_checkpoint* track the previously justified checkpoint. The four rules finalize the previously justified checkpoint when justification bits form patterns such as *(1,1,1)* over three consecutive epochs (the common two-epoch-delay case) or sparser patterns like *(1,0,0,1)* under delay [2]. The consequence: under synchrony, a checkpoint is justified in its own epoch and finalized two epochs later — **≈ 12.8 minutes** after proposal — and under asynchrony finalization degrades gracefully rather than failing outright.

```python
# Simplified justification/finalization state machine (beacon-chain flavor)
def process_epoch(state):
    # attestation aggregation -> supermajority links
    for checkpoint in epoch_boundary_checkpoints(state):
        supporting = sum(v.balance for v in validators
                         if v.vote == (state.justified, checkpoint))
        if supporting * 3 >= 2 * total_active_balance(state):
            state.justified = checkpoint          # Definition 2
            state.justification_bits.push(True)
    # k-finalization rules over the last 4 bits
    bits = state.justification_bits[-4:]
    if bits matches (1,1,1):      finalize(state.prev_justified)
    elif bits matches (1,0,0,1):  finalize(state.prev_justified)  # delayed case
```

### 4.2 Accountable safety: the proof

> **Theorem (Accountable Safety) [1].** Two conflicting checkpoints — checkpoints on different branches, neither an ancestor of the other — cannot both be finalized unless validators controlling at least *D/3* of total stake violated a slashing condition.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose conflicting checkpoints *A* (epoch *e_A*) and *B* (epoch *e_B*) are both finalized. Finalization yields supermajority links *A →ᴶ A′* and *B →ᴶ B′* to direct children, plus, by induction on Definition 2, chains of justified checkpoints from genesis to each. Assume *e_A ≥ e_B*.

- **Case *e_A = e_B*.** Both children sit at the same height. Two supermajority quorums at one height intersect in ≥ *D/3* of stake; those validators published two distinct votes with *h(t₁) = h(t₂)* — Condition I violated.
- **Case *e_A > e_B*.** Walk *A*'s justification chain down to the first checkpoint at height ≤ *e_B*. Either it lands exactly on *e_B*, reducing to the previous case, or it *straddles* it: there exist justified *Aᵢ, Aᵢ₊₁* with *h(Aᵢ₊₁) < e_B < h(Aᵢ)*. But *B →ᴶ B′* spans exactly *e_B → e_B+1*, which lies strictly inside *Aᵢ₊₁ →ᴶ Aᵢ*. The ≥ *D/3* validators in the quorum intersection thus published votes with *h(s₁) < h(s₂) < h(t₂) < h(t₁)* — Condition II violated. ∎

The proof's force lies in its *constructive identification*: the intersection argument names the offenders, so the protocol can slash them. Accountable safety is strictly stronger than classical BFT safety — it does not merely bound the adversary at *f < n/3*; it *prices* any safety violation at ≥ *D/3* of stake destroyed, with cryptographic receipts.

### 4.3 Plausible liveness and the inactivity leak

> **Theorem (Plausible Liveness) [1].** Regardless of past events, it is always possible for validators controlling *2D/3* of stake to finalize a new checkpoint — provided new blocks can be proposed.

Plausible liveness is deliberately weak: it asserts the *existence* of a finalizing future, not that the protocol finds it. The mechanism that makes liveness plausible under validator apathy or network partition is the **inactivity leak**. If four consecutive epochs pass without finalization (`MIN_EPOCHS_TO_INACTIVITY_PENALTY = 4`), validators that fail to attest begin losing stake, with per-epoch penalties growing linearly in the number of non-finalized epochs — so cumulative losses grow *quadratically*:

| Epochs since finality | 4 | 5 | 6 | 8 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leaked this epoch (relative units) | 0→onset | 5 | 6 | 8 | 12 |
| Cumulative leak | 0 | 5 | 11 | 26 | 68 |

Active validators' relative share therefore climbs back above 2/3 even if the offline set never returns; finality resumes, and the chain "heals" by economically ejecting the inactive. This is elegant — but it is also the protocol's most honest admission: under a deep, lasting partition, FFG *sacrifices safety* (both sides can finalize conflicting checkpoints once each bleeds the other below 1/3) to recover liveness. The inactivity leak converts a liveness failure into a safety failure rather than stalling forever — a deliberate choice, since a permanently stalled chain is useless, whereas a split chain at least lets each partition's users transact. Clients must therefore treat *finality during a leak* with suspicion, which is precisely why weak subjectivity (Section 4.4) is load-bearing.

### 4.4 Long-range attacks, posterior corruption, and weak subjectivity

Finality gadgets defend the *head* of the chain; **long-range attacks** target its *tail*. In a **posterior-corruption** (long-range secret-key-leakage, LRSL) attack, the adversary acquires — by bribery, theft, or purchase — the signing keys of validators who *once* held a supermajority of stake but have since exited or sold it [5].

Casper FFG's original paper [1] already proposed the structural defense: **checkpoints cannot be revised once finalized**, so a long-range fork must branch *before* the latest finalized checkpoint — and clients simply refuse chains that do. But this only works if the client *knows* a recent finalized checkpoint.

Two research directions sharpen this picture. First, **forward-secure and puncturable signatures** aim to make old keys useless even if leaked: a validator's key evolves each epoch and old epochs' signing capability is destroyed, so posterior corruption cannot rewrite history [5].

### 4.5 Ebb-and-flow: the availability–finality dilemma and the bouncing attack

Neu, Tas, and Tse [3] gave the cleanest conceptual frame for what FFG is *for*: the **availability–finality dilemma**. A *dynamically available* protocol (longest-chain style) keeps growing under partitions but offers no finality; a *BFT-style* protocol offers finality but stalls when participation drops.

The same paper exposed how delicate the composition is. In the **bouncing attack**, an adversary with a small stake withholds its FFG votes, then releases them late to flip the "highest justified checkpoint" that the fork-choice rule respects — bouncing honest validators back and forth between two chains indefinitely, so that *no checkpoint ever finalizes* and the available ledger itself becomes unsafe.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Mechanized verification

Casper's paper proofs have been machine-checked. Palmskog et al. formalized accountable safety and plausible liveness in the **Coq** proof assistant atop a ToyChain blockchain model adapted to the beacon chain's data structures. A separate effort modeled Gasper's justification/finalization rules in **Dafny** against the consensus-specs fork-choice code, mechanically proving the key lemmas on justified and finalized blocks in the block tree [6]. These verifications target exactly the quorum-counting and epoch-arithmetic seams where off-by-one errors hide.

### 5.2 Beacon-chain parameters at scale

| Parameter | Value | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Slots per epoch | 32 | checkpoint cadence |
| Slot time | 12 s | attestation granularity |
| Epoch duration | 6.4 min | one checkpoint per epoch |
| Typical justification→finalization latency | 2 epochs ≈ 12.8 min | economic settlement time |
| Finality delay bound | ≤ 4 epochs | *k*-finalization rules |
| Inactivity leak onset | 4 epochs w/o finality | liveness recovery |
| Whistleblower reward | 1/512 of slashed balance | slashing incentive |
| Proposer reward quotient | 1/8 of included rewards | attestation inclusion |

With 2/3 of stake honest and synchrony [7], the beacon chain justifies every epoch-boundary checkpoint and finalizes with the standard two-epoch lag; the inactivity leak has never triggered on mainnet, which is itself empirical evidence for the protocol's liveness under real-world conditions. The cost of reverting a finalized checkpoint is bounded below by one-third of total staked ETH — tens of billions of dollars — *plus* the attacker's stake is identifiably destroyed, which is what makes the guarantee *accountable* rather than merely expensive.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Plausible liveness is non-constructive.** The theorem guarantees a finalizing future *exists*; it does not bound how long the inactivity leak takes to restore a 2/3 supermajority under adversarial conditions, nor does it protect against the bouncing attack [3], which stalls finalization without violating any slashing condition.
2. **The inactivity leak trades safety for liveness.** Under a sustained partition, both sides bleed the absent validators and finalize conflicting checkpoints. Accountable safety's *D/3* bound assumes the slashing evidence is *observable*; across a partition, each side observes only its own reality. Recovery is social, not cryptographic.
3. **Weak subjectivity is a trust assumption, not a theorem.** Everything about long-range-attack resistance rests on clients obtaining fresh checkpoints out-of-band [4][5]. The protocol is secure only for participants who never fall behind by more than the weak subjectivity period — a constraint with no analogue in proof-of-work.
4. **Dynamic validator sets strain the model.** The paper proofs fix total deposit *D*; the beacon chain's activation/exit queues, churn limits, and effective-balance updates mean the "2/3 of stake" is a moving target. Gasper [2] extends the analysis to dynamic sets, but the gap between the idealized model and the implementation's balance accounting remains the likeliest hiding place for bugs — which is why the Dafny/Coq efforts target exactly this seam.
5. **Accountability is slashable-only.** Slashing punishes attributable *protocol* violations. It does not punish censorship, MEV extraction, or strategic vote timing that stays within the letter of the two commandments. Economic finality is not economic *fairness*.

## 7 Conclusion

Casper FFG's enduring contribution is a decomposition: *finality* need not be a property of the block-production mechanism at all. By overlaying checkpoint voting with two crisp slashing conditions on any chain, FFG converts the cheap talk of proof-of-stake attestations into binding, self-incriminating commitments, and proves that any safety violation costs at least a third of stake — with the culprits named in the evidence.

Yet the full picture is humbler than the headline theorem. Long-range attacks show that finality at the head means little without weak subjectivity at the tail [4][5]; the bouncing attack shows that composing a gadget with a fork-choice rule creates emergent vulnerabilities neither component has alone [3]; and the inactivity leak shows that ultimate liveness is purchased with a safety escape hatch.
