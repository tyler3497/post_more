# Secure Multiparty Computation with Dishonest Majority: the SPDZ Protocol, the Preprocessing Model, and Authenticated Secret Sharing

## Abstract

This thesis presents a self-contained technical treatment of the SPDZ family of protocols for secure multiparty computation (MPC) in the *dishonest-majority* setting, where any subset of up to n minus 1 parties may behave arbitrarily. We develop the protocol's two pillars in full detail: the information-theoretic online phase built on authenticated additive secret sharing with a global message-authentication-code (MAC) key, and the preprocessing model in which correlated randomness — authenticated Beaver multiplication triples — is generated by heavy cryptography before any input is known. We reconstruct the original somewhat-homomorphic-encryption preprocessing of Damgård, Pastro, Smart, and Zakarias [1], the oblivious-transfer-based MASCOT triple factory of Keller, Orsini, and Scholl [2], the covertly secure SPDZ-2 variant [3], and the extension to arithmetic modulo a power of two in SPDZ2k [4]. Along the way we give exact security arguments for the MAC check, complexity analyses, and a comparative evaluation of preprocessing backends drawn from the MP-SPDZ framework [5]. The thesis closes with limitations, open problems, and the protocol's role in deployed privacy-preserving systems.

##1 Introduction

Secure multiparty computation asks a deceptively simple question: can *n* mutually distrustful parties compute a joint function of their private inputs while revealing nothing beyond the output, even when all but one of them collude maliciously? The classical feasibility results of Yao [6], Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson, and Ben-Or, Goldwasser, and Wigderson established that any function can be computed securely, but the adversarial model matters enormously. Protocols secure against a *semi-honest* adversary — one that follows the protocol while trying to learn extra information — can be fast. Protocols secure against a *malicious* adversary that may deviate arbitrarily, in the *dishonest-majority* setting where the adversary may corrupt up to *n* minus 1 of *n* parties, pay a steep price: there is no honest majority to outvote a cheater, no guaranteed output delivery, and every value must be cryptographically authenticated.

> **Theorem:** In the dishonest-majority setting with a malicious adversary, any protocol computing a function *f* with abort-based security must authenticate every wire value against additive tampering, because the adversary controls enough parties to reconstruct any unauthenticated sharing. [1]

The SPDZ protocol — named after its authors' initials in the paper by Damgård, Pastro, Smart, and Zakarias at CRYPTO 2012 [1], pronounced "Speedz" — resolved the efficiency dilemma by *splitting the computation into two phases*. A data-independent **preprocessing** (offline) phase uses expensive public-key cryptography to manufacture *authenticated multiplication triples*, independent of the parties' inputs. A data-dependent **online** phase then evaluates the arithmetic circuit using only cheap information-theoretic operations: additions are local, multiplications consume one triple each, and all authentication is verified by a single batched MAC check at the end — malicious security at essentially the cost of semi-honest secret sharing.

##2 Background

### Secret sharing and the dishonest majority

An *(n, n)*-threshold additive secret sharing of a value *x* over a finite field *F* distributes random shares *x_1, ..., x_n* with sum equal to *x*; any *n* minus 1 shares reveal nothing. Unlike Shamir sharing, additive sharing tolerates an arbitrary corruption threshold: even *n* minus 1 colluding parties learn nothing about *x* beyond their own shares. This makes additive sharing the natural substrate for dishonest-majority MPC, at the cost that *reconstruction requires all parties* and there is no redundancy to detect cheating. Authentication must therefore be layered on top.

### Beaver's multiplication triples

Beaver [6] showed that multiplication can be reduced to the consumption of *correlated randomness*. A multiplication triple is a triple of sharings *(a, b, c)* with *c = a · b*, generated independently of the inputs. To multiply secret-shared *x* and *y*, the parties open the masked values *ε = x minus a* and *δ = y minus b* and compute shares of *xy* as *c + ε·b + δ·a + ε·δ*. The masks blind the true values information-theoretically, so the online multiplication costs only the opening of two values. The catch — and the entire content of maliciously secure MPC — is that the triples themselves, and the opened values, must be *authenticated*: a corrupt party that adds an error to its share of *c* shifts the product undetectably unless MACs bind every share.

### Somewhat homomorphic encryption

A somewhat homomorphic encryption (SHE) scheme supports addition of ciphertexts and a bounded number of multiplications — enough for one multiplication depth, which is exactly what triple generation needs. The original SPDZ preprocessing encrypts each party's random contributions and homomorphically forms encryptions of *a*, *b*, and the masked product *a·b* [1].

### Oblivious transfer and OT extension

Oblivious transfer (OT) is the workhorse of the OT-based preprocessing line. In a 1-out-of-2 OT, a sender holds two strings and a receiver learns exactly one, chosen by a secret bit, while the sender learns nothing about the choice. OT *extension* [7] bootstraps a small number of base OTs into millions of OTs using only symmetric cryptography, making OT-based triple generation concretely efficient. MASCOT exploits an *arithmetic* view of OT: each OT instance contributes one bit-level product, and consistency checks aggregate these into authenticated field triples at a cost below six times the semi-honest baseline [2].

### The UC framework and security with abort

Security is formalized in the universal composability (UC) framework against a static malicious adversary corrupting any *n* minus 1 parties. In the dishonest-majority setting, *guaranteed output delivery* and *fairness* are impossible in general; SPDZ achieves *security with abort*: either all honest parties receive the correct output, or the protocol aborts with cheating detected. The ideal functionality *F_ABORT* captures this, and the MAC check is the mechanism that forces any deviation to cause abort except with probability bounded by the field size [1].

| Protocol | Preprocessing assumption | Online cost per mult. | Security |
|---|---|---|---|
| SPDZ (DPSZ12) [1] | SHE + ZK proofs | 2 field elements per party | Malicious, abort |
| SPDZ-2 (covert) [3] | SHE + ZK proofs | 2 field elements per party | Covert |
| MASCOT (KOS16) [2] | OT extension | 2 field elements per party | Malicious, abort |
| SPDZ2k [4] | SHE over cyclotomic rings | 2 ring elements per party | Malicious, abort |

##3 Methodology

Our methodology is *protocol reconstruction with proof sketches*: we present each protocol in the *F-hybrid* model, state the exact ideal functionalities for triple generation and MAC checking, and argue security by reduction to the underlying primitive. We adopt the following notation throughout, consistent with the SPDZ literature [1], [2]:

- *F_p* denotes the prime field; *p* is chosen large enough that *1/p* is negligible.
- A value *x* shared additively is written *(x_1, ..., x_n)*; angle-bracket notation *⟨x⟩* denotes the *authenticated* sharing defined below.
- The global MAC key *α* is itself secret-shared as *(α_1, ..., α_n)* and never reconstructed.
- *⟦x⟧* denotes a triple component sharing; *(a, b, c)* with *c = ab* denotes one authenticated multiplication triple.

An **authenticated share** of *x* consists of the additive share *x_i* together with a MAC share *m_i*, where the MAC values satisfy *Σ_i m_i = α · x*. We write *⟨x⟩ = ((x_1, m_1), ..., (x_n, m_n))*. The linearity of the MAC is the engine of the whole protocol: both the sharing and the MAC are linear in *x*, so additions and scalar multiplications are performed locally without communication, and the MAC relation is preserved automatically [1].

##4 Deep Dive

### The MAC algebra and authenticated secret sharing

The central algebraic object is the *information-theoretic MAC* under a global key. Let *α* be uniform in *F_p*, shared as *(α_1, ..., α_n)*, with no party ever learning *α*. To authenticate a secret-shared value *x = Σ_i x_i*, the parties hold MAC shares *m_i* with *Σ_i m_i = α·x*. We denote this *⟨x⟩*. The scheme is information-theoretic: an adversary that does not know *α* cannot forge a consistent MAC on a modified share except by guessing *α*, which succeeds with probability at most *1/p* per attempt [1].

Linear operations are free. Given *⟨x⟩* and *⟨y⟩*, each party locally computes *(x_i + y_i, m_i^x + m_i^y)*, yielding *⟨x + y⟩* because the MAC relation is linear:

> **Theorem:** For authenticated sharings *⟨x⟩, ⟨y⟩* under the same global key *α*, the local computation *⟨x⟩ + ⟨y⟩* yields a valid authenticated sharing of *x + y*, and *c·⟨x⟩* yields a valid authenticated sharing of *c·x* for any public constant *c*. No communication is required. [1]

Multiplication, however, is not linear: the product of two sharings has degree two in the shares. This is precisely where Beaver triples enter, and where the preprocessing model earns its keep. The online phase never multiplies two secret values directly; it always consumes a pre-authenticated triple.

```python
# Authenticated sharing: local linear operations (no communication)
class AuthShare:
    def __init__(self, x_i, m_i):
        self.x_i = x_i      # additive share of x
        self.m_i = m_i      # MAC share: sum(m) == alpha * x
    def __add__(self, other):
        return AuthShare(self.x_i + other.x_i, self.m_i + other.m_i)
    def __mul__(self, c):   # public constant c
        return AuthShare(c * self.x_i, c * self.m_i)
    # Multiplication of two AuthShares is NOT local:
    # it requires a Beaver triple from preprocessing.
```

### The online phase: Beaver multiplication under MACs

Suppose the parties hold *⟨x⟩*, *⟨y⟩*, and an authenticated triple *(⟨a⟩, ⟨b⟩, ⟨c⟩)* with *c = a·b*. The online multiplication proceeds as follows [1]:

1. **Mask and open.** Compute *⟨ε⟩ = ⟨x⟩ minus ⟨a⟩* and *⟨δ⟩ = ⟨y⟩ minus ⟨b⟩*; partially open both by broadcasting the *x*-shares only (the MAC shares stay secret for now). Every party learns *ε* and *δ*, which are uniformly random masks of *x* and *y*.
2. **Local recombination.** Each party computes its share of the product as *z_i = c_i + ε·b_i + δ·a_i*, plus *ε·δ* added by (convention) party *P_1*. The MAC shares combine identically: *μ_i = m_i^c + ε·m_i^b + δ·m_i^a*, with *ε·δ·α_1* folded in by *P_1* using its key share.
3. **Deferred verification.** The parties do *not* check MACs yet. They accumulate every opened value and every MAC share into the state of the final MAC check.

The communication cost is exactly two field elements broadcast per party per multiplication gate — the openings of *ε* and *δ* — matching the semi-honest baseline. All authentication is deferred to a single batched check, which is the protocol's decisive efficiency win.

> **Theorem:** If the triple satisfies *c = a·b* and all MACs are valid, the online multiplication outputs a valid authenticated sharing *⟨xy⟩*. If any opened value or share was tampered with, the final MAC check rejects except with probability at most *2/p* over the verifier's random linear combination. [1]

The **MAC check** itself is a small masterpiece of amortization. After the circuit evaluation, the parties hold a list of opened values *a_1, ..., a_t* (all *ε*, *δ*, and output shares ever broadcast) and corresponding MAC shares. They sample a public random vector *r* (via coin tossing or a random oracle), compute the linear combination *a = Σ_j r_j · a_j* and each party's combined MAC share *σ_i = Σ_j r_j · m_i^{(j)}*, then commit to *σ_i* before opening *α_i*. Verification checks *Σ_i σ_i = a · Σ_i α_i*. A cheating adversary that introduced an additive error *Δ* into any opened value must satisfy *Σ_i σ_i^adv = (a + Δ)·α*, i.e., must guess *α·Δ* — information-theoretically impossible beyond the *2/p* bound [1].

```haskell
-- MAC check: batched verification of all opened values
-- Parties hold opened values [a_j], MAC shares [m_i_j], key shares alpha_i
macCheck :: [Field] -> [[Field]] -> [Field] -> Bool
macCheck opened macShares alphaShares =
    let r      = publicRandomVector (length opened)  -- sampled after all openings
        a      = sum (zipWith (*) r opened)          -- random linear combination
        sigma  = map (sum . zipWith (*) r) macShares -- per-party combined MAC
        alpha  = sum alphaShares                     -- key reconstructed only here
    in sum sigma == a * alpha   -- commit sigma before opening alpha
```

### The preprocessing model I: SHE-based triple generation

The original SPDZ preprocessing [1] manufactures authenticated triples using somewhat homomorphic encryption with a *distributed* secret key. After a setup that shares the MAC key *α* and publishes encryptions of the key shares, each triple is built as follows:

1. Each party *P_i* samples random *a_i, b_i, r_i* and broadcasts encryptions, with zero-knowledge proofs of plaintext knowledge (*Π_ZKPoPK*) binding them to the ciphertexts.
2. Using homomorphism, all parties locally compute an encryption of *a = Σ a_i*, *b = Σ b_i*, and the masked product *a·b + r* where *r = Σ r_i*.
3. The parties run a distributed decryption of *a·b + r* — the mask *r* blinds the true product — and *P_1* sets *c_1 = (a·b + r) minus r_1* while others set *c_i = minus r_i*, yielding additive shares of *c = a·b* that no party could bias.
4. MACs are formed homomorphically: the encryption of *α* times the encryption of each share value, re-randomized per party, produces encryptions of the MAC shares, which are then distributedly decrypted to the MAC-share holders.

The zero-knowledge proofs are load-bearing: without them, a malicious party could submit an encryption of a non-field element or correlate its contribution with another party's, breaking the uniformity of the triple [1].

```rust
// Preprocessing (SHE): homomorphic triple assembly, per party P_i
fn she_triple_round(ct_a: &Ct, ct_b: &Ct, ct_r: &Ct, ct_alpha: &Ct) -> Ct {
    // Enc(a*b + r): one homomorphic multiplication depth, then mask
    let ct_ab = ct_a.hom_mul(ct_b);          // uses the single mult level
    let ct_masked = ct_ab.hom_add(ct_r);     // blind before joint decryption
    ct_masked
}
// After distributed decryption of (a*b + r):
//   P_1 sets c_1 = (ab + r) - r_1;  P_i (i>1) sets c_i = -r_i.
```

The SHE approach dominated the first SPDZ implementations (notably SPDZ-2 [3], which added covert security — cheating detected with constant probability rather than overwhelming — at lower cost), but its per-triple public-key operations made the offline phase the bottleneck. Throughput was measured in thousands of triples per second, while the online phase could consume millions [5].

### The preprocessing model II: MASCOT and the OT-based factory

MASCOT [2] replaced public-key homomorphic operations with *oblivious transfer*, exploiting the observation that a field multiplication can be decomposed bitwise. To generate an authenticated triple, each pair of parties runs OTs in which the sender's inputs encode its share bits and the receiver's choice bit selects among them; the OT outputs give the parties *additive shares of cross-terms* *x_i · y_j*. Summing cross-terms reconstructs shares of the full product. Consistency checks — random linear combinations verified with the same MAC-check machinery — catch a malicious party that used inconsistent choice bits across OTs, at an overhead below six times the semi-honest cost [2].

The enabling primitive is *actively secure OT extension with optimal overhead* [7]: from a small seed of base OTs, the parties derive millions of OTs using only symmetric primitives, with a consistency check that upgrades semi-honest OT extension to malicious security nearly for free [2].

A further axis of the design space is the *ring*. SPDZ2k [4] transports the SPDZ blueprint from prime fields to integers modulo *2^k*, matching native CPU arithmetic. Over *Z_{2^k}*, zero divisors break the standard MAC argument, so SPDZ2k authenticates over an extension ring with a *2-adic* soundness analysis [4].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Security theorems

The security of SPDZ is captured by two composition theorems [1]:

> **Theorem (Online phase):** The online protocol UC-realizes the ideal arithmetic-circuit functionality *F_ABORT* in the *F_Triple*-hybrid model against any static malicious adversary corrupting up to *n* minus 1 parties, with statistical error bounded by *2/p* per MAC check.

> **Theorem (Preprocessing):** The SHE-based preprocessing protocol UC-realizes *F_Triple* under the semantic security of the SHE scheme and the soundness of the zero-knowledge proofs of plaintext knowledge; the MASCOT protocol realizes *F_Triple* under the security of OT extension [2], [7].

*Proof sketch of the MAC-check soundness.* Fix the adversary's view before the random vector *r* is sampled: all opened values and MAC shares are determined. Let *Δ_j* be the additive error injected into opened value *a_j*. The check passes only if the adversary's combined MAC share equals *α·(a + Σ_j r_j Δ_j)* where *a* is the honest combination. Since *α* is uniform and independent of the adversary's view at commitment time, the equality holds with probability at most *1/p* unless *Σ_j r_j Δ_j = 0*, which for a nonzero error vector happens with probability at most *1/p* over random *r* — union bound *2/p* [1].

##6 Limitations

SPDZ's guarantees come with sharp, well-understood boundaries.

- **Abort, not fairness or guaranteed delivery.** With a dishonest majority, fairness is impossible in general, and SPDZ does not attempt it: a corrupt party can always abort after learning the output. Applications requiring fairness must look to honest-majority protocols, gradual-release constructions, or blockchain-anchored penalties — none of which SPDZ provides.
- **No protection against input substitution.** MPC guarantees correctness of the computation *on the committed inputs*, not honesty of the inputs themselves; input validation is an application-layer concern.
- **The preprocessing trust anchor.** The original SHE preprocessing needs a distributed key-generation ceremony; a compromised setup poisons every triple. MASCOT's OT-based setup needs only base OTs (realizable from any public-key setup or even a trusted dealer for the seed), which is qualitatively weaker — one reason the field migrated toward OT-based preprocessing [2], [5].
- **Communication lower bounds.** The online phase sends *Θ(n)* field elements per multiplication across all parties. Recent work shows this is not inherent: packed secret sharing achieves *O(1)* amortized communication per gate even with a dishonest majority [8], at the cost of a more complex preprocessing. SPDZ remains the practical choice for moderate party counts, but the asymptotic frontier has moved.
- **Field and ring restrictions.** The prime-field MAC analysis needs *p* large; small fields require repetition or extension fields. SPDZ2k handles powers of two [4], but each new algebraic domain demands a fresh soundness analysis — the MAC is not plug-and-play across rings.
- **Covert vs. malicious.** SPDZ-2 [3] settles for covert security (cheating caught with constant, tunable probability) to gain speed. Whether a given deployment can tolerate a cheating probability of, say, one-half per run is a risk-management decision, not a cryptographic one.

##7 Conclusion

The SPDZ protocol family turned maliciously secure dishonest-majority MPC from a theoretical feasibility result into a deployable technology, and it did so through one durable architectural idea: *decouple authentication from computation*. Authenticated secret sharing with a global MAC key makes the online phase information-theoretic and cheap; the preprocessing model concentrates all public-key cryptography into input-independent triple generation; and the batched MAC check amortizes verification over the entire circuit. Successive backends — SHE [1], covert SHE [3], OT-based MASCOT [2], ring-based SPDZ2k [4] — kept the online phase essentially fixed while driving offline costs down by orders of magnitude, a division of labor now embodied in the MP-SPDZ framework [5].

Open problems remain: sublinear-communication preprocessing for arbitrary corruption thresholds [8], preprocessing with *identifiable* abort, and post-quantum triple factories whose concrete costs match MASCOT's. But the core lesson stands: when no party can be trusted, authenticate everything, verify once, and pay for cryptography only where the inputs cannot see it.
