# Separation Logic and the Verification of Concurrent Programs: From Reynolds–O'Hearn to the Iris Framework

## Abstract
This article traces the evolution of separation logic from a logic of sequential pointer programs to a modern framework for mechanically verified concurrent software. We begin with Reynolds' extension of Hoare logic with the separating conjunction and the frame rule, review O'Hearn's concurrent separation logic (CSL) with its resource invariants and disjoint parallel composition rule, and explain why the soundness of the logic hinges on the subtle precision condition identified by Reynolds, famously illustrated by the unsoundness of the naive conjunction rule. We then show how Brookes' trace semantics rehabilitated the logic, and how the field subsequently generalized invariants into higher-order protocols — deny-guarantee, fictional separation, and ultimately the Iris framework, which reduces the entire edifice of modern concurrent separation logic to partial commutative monoids, ghost state, and invariant allocation. Throughout, we emphasize the single unifying idea: *ownership* as the resource-theoretic content of separation. The treatment is aimed at readers with graduate-level familiarity with program semantics and includes full proof outlines, mechanization notes, and a frank assessment of what the approach still cannot do.

## 1 Intro

Concurrency is the native mode of modern computation, and it is also its most treacherous. A program whose threads interleave arbitrarily through shared memory can fail in ways that no amount of testing reliably exposes: data races corrupt invariants at exactly one interleaving in a million, and a lock-free queue that survives a stress test may still admit an *ABA* anomaly under a scheduler nobody has tried yet [1]. The classical answer of Hoare logic — a global invariant held by every thread at every step — collapses under the weight of aliasing: reasoning about pointers requires saying *who* may touch *what*, and in a concurrent program the answer changes continuously as ownership flows between threads.

Separation logic, introduced by Reynolds as an extension of Hoare logic, answered the aliasing problem for sequential programs with two deceptively simple devices: the *separating conjunction* $P * Q$, which asserts that the heap can be split into two disjoint parts satisfying $P$ and $Q$ respectively, and the *frame rule*, which permits a local specification to be extended with any untouched context [7]. The stroke of genius of O'Hearn's concurrent extension was to realize that disjointness in the heap is *already* a statement about interference: two threads verified against disjoint heaps cannot race, because a race is by definition a conflicting access to *shared* state [1]. The parallel rule of CSL,
$$\frac{\Gamma \vdash \{P_1\}\; C_1\; \{Q_1\} \qquad \Gamma \vdash \{P_2\}\; C_2\; \{Q_2\}}{\Gamma \vdash \{P_1 * P_2\}\; C_1 \parallel C_2\; \{Q_1 * Q_2\}} \quad \text{(PAR)},$$
is therefore the frame rule's concurrent twin: it decomposes a verification problem spatially rather than temporally [2].

But the story is not a straight line. Shortly after O'Hearn proposed the logic, Reynolds discovered a counterexample demonstrating that the logic as stated was *unsound* unless resource invariants were *precise* — a subtlety concerning the interaction of the conjunction rule with disjunctive invariants [3]. It fell to Brookes to construct a semantic model, based on action traces, in which ownership transfer and race-freedom could be formalized and the logic proved sound [2]. And over the following two decades, the community learned that the real generality of CSL lay not in its fixed rule set but in its *method*: ghost state, protocols, and invariants that could be refined until they expressed arbitrarily sophisticated synchronization disciplines. The culmination of that trajectory is **Iris**, a higher-order concurrent separation logic in which nearly all of the logical machinery of modern CSLs — weakest preconditions, view shifts, atomicity — is derived from a tiny kernel of partial commutative monoids and invariant allocation [4, 5].

This article presents that trajectory as a single argument with three movements. First, we establish the sequential core: assertions as resources, the frame rule as local reasoning. Second, we develop CSL proper: resource invariants, critical regions, the precision condition, and the soundness story. Third, we ascend to the higher-order regime: ghost state as the great unifier, the Iris construction from first principles, and a case study of a mechanically checked proof of a fine-grained concurrent data structure. We close with limitations and a research outlook.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Hoare Logic and the Aliasing Catastrophe

Hoare logic reasons about programs via triples $\{P\}\;C\;\{Q\}$: if $P$ holds before $C$ executes, then $Q$ holds after.

> Theorem: No compositional Hoare logic for pointer programs exists without some form of spatial locality. Any sound rule for heap mutation must restrict the assertions it frames over, or track the footprint of every update.

Reynolds' response was to change the ontology of assertions: an assertion no longer describes the whole state, but *owns* a portion of it.

### 2.2 Separating Conjunction and the Frame Rule

The separating conjunction $P * Q$ holds of a heap $h$ iff $h = h_1 \uplus h_2$ with $P$ holding of $h_1$ and $Q$ of $h_2$, where $\uplus$ is disjoint union. The *frame rule*
$$\frac{\{P\}\; C\; \{Q\}}{\{P * R\}\; C\; \{Q * R\}} \quad \text{(FRAME)}$$
encodes locality: a program verified against its footprint $P$ continues to satisfy its specification when embedded in a larger state containing untouched resources $R$. The side condition — that $C$'s free variables are not modified in $R$ — is the familiar one from Hoare logic, but now the *heap* footprint is handled automatically by $*$.

```python
# A toy model of assertions as resource predicates over heaps.
# A heap is a partial map from addresses to values.
Heap = dict  # int -> int

def points_to(x, v):
    """x |-> v : owns exactly the singleton heap {x: v}."""
    return lambda h: h == {x: v}

def sep_conj(P, Q):
    """P * Q : the heap splits into disjoint parts for P and Q."""
    def check(h):
        addrs = list(h.keys())
        for mask in range(1 << len(addrs)):
            h1 = {a: h[a] for i, a in enumerate(addrs) if mask & (1 << i)}
            h2 = {a: h[a] for i, a in enumerate(addrs) if not mask & (1 << i)}
            if P(h1) and Q(h2):
                return True
        return False
    return check

# Example: (x |-> 0) * (y |-> 1) holds of {x: 0, y: 1} but not of {x: 0}.
assert sep_conj(points_to('x', 0), points_to('y', 1))({'x': 0, 'y': 1})
assert not sep_conj(points_to('x', 0), points_to('y', 1))({'x': 0})
```

### 2.3 O'Hearn's Intuitionistic Turn

O'Hearn's earlier work recast separation logic intuitionistically: assertions are interpreted over heaps with *permission weakening*, so that $P$ holding of $h$ implies $P$ holds of any super-heap $h' \supseteq h$ for "intuitionistic" connectives [8].

## 3 Methodology

Our method is the standard one of the separation-logic literature: we present the logic as a proof system over an idealized concurrent language with shared-memory primitives (`fork`, locks, compare-and-swap), prove or sketch the soundness-critical lemmas, and evaluate expressiveness through worked examples.

| Era | Contribution | Key idea |
|---|---|---|
| 2001–2002 | Reynolds, O'Hearn: sequential SL | $*$, frame rule, local reasoning [7] |
| 2002–2004 | O'Hearn's CSL proposal; Reynolds' counterexample | Resource invariants; **precision** requirement [1, 3] |
| 2004–2007 | Brookes' trace semantics | Soundness via action traces; ownership transfer [2] |
| 2007–2012 | Deny–guarantee, fictional separation, CAP | Protocols beyond fixed invariants |
| 2015–2018 | Iris 1.0 → 3.x | PCMs + invariants as the universal substrate [4, 5] |
| 2018–present | RustBelt, RefinedC, Aneris, Simuliris | Industrial-scale mechanized verification |

The CSL critical-region rule is the locus of ownership transfer:
$$\frac{\Gamma, r : R \vdash \{P * R\}\; C\; \{Q * R\}}{\Gamma, r : R \vdash \{P\}\; \textbf{with}\; r\; \textbf{do}\; C\; \{Q\}} \quad \text{(CRIT)}$$
Inside the critical region, the thread *borrows* the invariant $R$ — the shared resource — and must re-establish it on exit. Between critical regions, the invariant is held by the lock itself. This single rule, together with (PAR), expresses message passing, semaphores, and lock-protected data structures with no rely/guarantee conditions at all [1, 8].

```tla+
---- MODULE LockProtocol ----
\* Abstract protocol of a CSL resource invariant for a binary semaphore.
\* The ghost variable "inv" tracks whether the invariant R is currently
\* held by the lock (Locked) or lent to a thread inside with r do (Open).
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES inv, heap_footprint

Init == inv = "Locked" /\ heap_footprint \in HeapsSatisfying(R)

Enter(r) == inv = "Locked" /\ inv' = "Open"
            /\ heap_footprint' = heap_footprint \uplus Footprint(R)

Exit(r)  == inv = "Open" /\ Assert(Reestablished(R, heap_footprint))
            /\ inv' = "Locked"
            /\ heap_footprint' = heap_footprint \ Footprint(R)

Next == \E r \in Locks : Enter(r) \/ Exit(r)
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<inv, heap_footprint>>
====
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Separating Conjunction as an Ownership Discipline

The deepest content of $*$ is not logical but *economic*: it is an accounting discipline for permissions.

This is worth contrasting with the dominant alternative of the era, Jones' **rely–guarantee** reasoning.

### 4.2 Resource Invariants: The Semaphore and Parallel Mergesort

The canonical CSL example is the split binary semaphore.

1. `P(s)` (acquire): from $\{ \text{emp} \}$ the thread enters the critical region, finds the invariant $R \lor \text{emp}$, and exits with either $R$ (success, taking the resource) or $\text{emp}$ (someone else holds it; retry).
2. `V(s)` (release): from $\{R\}$ the thread deposits $R$ back into the invariant.

Notice what is absent: no global invariant, no enumeration of threads, no rely/guarantee.

The flagship application is **parallel mergesort**.

```
{ *[i in [l,r). a[i] |-> v_i] }
l := ...; m := (l+r)/2; (* split point *)
(
    { *[i in [l,m). ...] } msort(l, m) { sorted(l,m) }
  ||
    { *[i in [m,r). ...] } msort(m, r) { sorted(m,r) }
);
merge(l, m, r)
{ sorted(l,r) }
```

The two recursive calls own disjoint array segments, so no interference is possible; the merge step re-acquires the whole.

### 4.3 Precision, the Conjunction Rule, and Reynolds' Counterexample

The most technically delicate episode in the history of CSL is also its most instructive. The standard Hoare-logic **conjunction rule**,
$$\frac{\{P_1\}\; C\; \{Q_1\} \qquad \{P_2\}\; C\; \{Q_2\}}{\{P_1 \land P_2\}\; C\; \{Q_1 \land Q_2\}},$$
is sound for sequential Hoare logic. Reynolds showed that in CSL it is **unsound** as stated — and the culprit is disjunctive resource invariants [3].

> Theorem: The conjunction rule is unsound for CSL unless all resource invariants in the context are *precise*.

A predicate $R$ is **precise** when, for every heap $h$, there is *at most one* subheap $h' \subseteq h$ satisfying $R$.

Reynolds' counterexample exploits this ambiguity: two derivations of the same program can disagree about *which* subheap the invariant governs, and conjoining their conclusions fuses incompatible ownership claims into a consistent-looking but semantically incoherent triple.

```haskell
-- Precision, modelled in Haskell. An assertion is a predicate on heaps;
-- it is precise iff at most one subheap satisfies it.
type Heap = [(Int, Int)]  -- association list, no duplicates

subheaps :: Heap -> [Heap]
subheaps [] = [[]]
subheaps ((k,v):rest) = [s | s <- subheaps rest] ++ [(k,v):s | s <- subheaps rest]

isPrecise :: (Heap -> Bool) -> Heap -> Bool
isPrecise p h = length (filter p (subheaps h)) <= 1

pointsTo :: Int -> Int -> Heap -> Bool
pointsTo x v h = h == [(x, v)]

semaphoreInv :: Int -> Heap -> Bool   -- (x |-> 0) \/ emp
semaphoreInv x h = pointsTo x 0 h || h == []

-- >>> isPrecise (pointsTo 1 0) [(1,0)]            == True
-- >>> isPrecise (semaphoreInv 1) [(1,0)]          == False  -- two witnesses!
```

Brookes' response was to build a semantics in which the problem could be *seen*.

### 4.4 From Fixed Rules to Ghost State: The Road to Iris

Classical CSL has a fixed, small rule set.

The decisive abstraction is the **partial commutative monoid (PCM)**.

**Iris** is the framework that takes this reduction to its conclusion. In Iris 3.x, the *entire* program logic is built from two orthogonal concepts [4, 5]:

1. **Ghost state over PCMs** (cameras, in Iris terminology — step-indexed PCMs), manipulated by the *fancy update modality* ${\Rightarrow\!\!\!\Rightarrow}_{\mathcal{E}_1}^{\mathcal{E}_2}\, P$, which performs a ghost update while temporarily opening the invariants in mask $\mathcal{E}_1$ and closing those in $\mathcal{E}_2$.
2. **Invariants** $\boxed{P}^{\mathcal{N}}$: persistent, duplicable assertions $P$ that may be opened for a single atomic step and must be re-established, generalizing CSL's resource invariants to arbitrary, higher-order, impredicative protocols.

Weakest preconditions, view shifts, logically atomic triples $\langle x.\, P \rangle\; e\; \langle v.\, Q \rangle$ — the whole apparatus of modern CSL — are *derived* in Iris's small base logic rather than primitive [5].

```rust
// The ownership intuition of CSL, as Rust's type system already enforces:
// disjoint mutable borrows are exactly P1 * P2 in the parallel rule.
fn parallel_mergesort(slice: &mut [i32]) {
    let mid = slice.len() / 2;
    let (left, right) = slice.split_at_mut(mid); // left * right: disjoint!
    std::thread::scope(|s| {
        s.spawn(|| mergesort(left));    // {left owned}  ||  {right owned}
        s.spawn(|| mergesort(right));   // no data race: borrow checker = (PAR)
    });
    merge(slice, mid);
}
```

### 4.5 Mechanization: A Treiber Stack in Iris

To make the Iris methodology concrete, consider the **Treiber stack**, the "hello world" of lock-free verification: `push` and `pop` implemented with a single compare-and-swap on a shared head pointer.

1. **Ghost protocol.** A ghost list $\gamma \mapsto_{\text{list}} \ell$ tracks the *logical* contents of the stack. The physical head pointer always points to a linked list whose elements match $\ell$; this agreement is enforced by an invariant $\boxed{\exists h, \ell.\; \text{head} \mapsto h * \text{isList}(h, \ell) * \gamma \mapsto_{\text{list}} \ell}^{\mathcal{N}}$.
2. **Logical atomicity.** Each operation is specified with a *logically atomic triple*: $\langle \ell.\; \text{stack}(\gamma, \ell) \rangle\; \text{push}(v)\; \langle \_.\; \text{stack}(\gamma, v :: \ell) \rangle$. The angle brackets promise the client that the operation *appears* to take effect at a single linearization point, even though the CAS may retry arbitrarily many times.
3. **Linearization.** Inside the proof, the thread opens the invariant, reads the head, attempts the CAS; on success it performs the ghost update $\gamma \mapsto_{\text{list}} \ell \Rightarrow\!\!\!\Rightarrow \gamma \mapsto_{\text{list}} (v :: \ell)$ at the linearization point and closes the invariant. On failure it retries with no observable effect — the fancy update modality guarantees the failed attempt changed nothing logical.

The full proof is a few hundred lines of Coq in Iris Proof Mode; the same pattern scales to RDCSS, Harris's linked list, and the verified concurrent queues of the RustBelt project [4].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

Separation logic is unusual among program logics in that its "experiments" are *mechanized proofs*: the empirical content is the corpus of verified artifacts and the effort required to produce them. Three landmarks structure the evidence:

| Artifact | Logic / Tool | What was proved | Scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parallel mergesort, semaphores, buffers | CSL (O'Hearn 2007) | Race-freedom + functional correctness, on paper | Tens of triples [1] |
| CSL soundness | Brookes trace semantics | Soundness of (PAR), (CRIT), frame | ~40pp journal proof [2] |
| Treiber stack, Michael–Scott queue | Iris / Coq | Linearizability, logically atomic specs | ~10²–10³ lines Coq [4] |
| Rust's type system soundness | RustBelt (Iris) | Type safety of `Cell`, `Mutex`, `Arc`, `rayon` | ~10⁴ lines Coq [4] |
| C verification | RefinedC (Iris) | Foundational correctness of C programs | Active toolchain |

The decisive quantitative claim of the Iris line of work is *compositionality at scale*: the RustBelt verification of Rust's standard synchronization primitives composes *without re-verification* — a client of `Arc<Mutex<Vec<T>>>` reasons from the logically atomic specifications of each component, never from their implementations. This is the frame rule's promise fulfilled one level up: just as (FRAME) lets a sequential proof ignore untouched heap, Iris's logically atomic triples let a concurrent client ignore the implementation's internal interference [4, 5].

A second line of evidence is *negative*: the precision episode (§4.3) shows the methodology is falsifiable in the right way. Reynolds' counterexample was not a bug to be patched but a *discovery* — it revealed that ownership, to be sound, must be unambiguous, and the resulting precision condition is now understood as a special case of a general principle: ghost protocols must determine a unique splitting of resources at every step [3].

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires stating what the framework does not do.

1. **Safety, not liveness.** CSL and Iris prove *partial* correctness: no races, no crashes, functional postconditions *if* the program terminates. Termination of concurrent programs — let alone lock-freedom, wait-freedom, or fairness — needs additional machinery (e.g., TaDA Live, or the busy-waiting logics built atop Iris) [6]. A spinlock verified in Iris is proved *safe*; that it ever acquires the lock is a separate theorem.
2. **The granularity assumption.** Every CSL-family logic assumes a fixed set of *atomic* actions; interference *within* an atomic step is invisible. If the hardware or compiler reorders memory accesses (weak memory models: TSO, ARMv8, C++11), the logic must be rebuilt over a weaker atomicity notion — an active research area (GPS, RSL, and their Iris-hosted successors).
3. **Annotation burden and expertise.** A Treiber-stack proof in Iris is hundreds of lines of Coq written by an expert; the Iris Proof Mode automates bookkeeping, not invention. Choosing the *right* ghost protocol remains a creative act, and there is no decision procedure that invents invariants. Industrial adoption (RefinedC, RustBelt) is real but concentrated where the cost of bugs justifies the cost of proof.
4. **Precision's long shadow.** The conjunction rule's unsoundness is fully understood, but its moral generalizes uncomfortably: any logic with both disjunctive invariants and a conjunction-like rule must tread carefully, and higher-order ghost state reintroduces the danger in subtler forms (hence Iris's step-indexed "later" modality $\triangleright$, which stratifies self-referential protocols) [5].
5. **Closed-world protocols.** Ghost protocols are agreed upon by all verified code; unverified or adversarial code (a malicious thread, a buggy device driver) falls outside the model. Foundational verification of *open* systems remains largely out of reach.

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from Reynolds to Iris is the story of a single idea — *ownership as a logical resource* — discovering its full generality. Reynolds taught us that assertions could own heap; O'Hearn taught us that disjoint ownership is non-interference, and that locks are just invariants with a protocol for borrowing; Reynolds then taught us, via counterexample, that ownership must be *precise* to be sound; Brookes taught us to see ownership transfer in the traces; and Jung, Krebbers, Birkedal, Dreyer and their collaborators taught us that every protocol is ghost state over a PCM, and every logic is derivable from invariants plus fancy updates [1, 2, 3, 4, 5].

The practical consequence is a verification technology that has moved, in twenty years, from paper proofs of semaphores to machine-checked proofs of the concurrency primitives underlying a production systems language. The remaining gaps — liveness, weak memory, automation, open worlds — are genuine, but they are gaps *within* a paradigm that has proved remarkably fertile, not signs of its exhaustion. If there is a lesson for the next twenty years, it is the one the precision episode already taught: in concurrent verification, the deepest progress comes not from adding rules but from understanding, with mathematical exactness, what it means to *own* something.
