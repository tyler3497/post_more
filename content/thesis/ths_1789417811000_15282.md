# Categorical Semantics of Effectful Programming: Monads, Adjunctions, and the Algebraic Structure of Computational Effects

## Abstract

The computational lambda-calculus of Moggi [1] established strong monads on cartesian closed categories as the canonical denotational model of effectful programs, unifying state, exceptions, nondeterminism, and continuations under a single sequencing discipline. This article reconstructs the theory from its categorical foundations: every adjunction induces a monad, and every monad admits two canonical resolutions — the Kleisli construction, which internalises computations as morphisms into lifted codomains, and the Eilenberg–Moore construction, which externalises them as algebras for the monad. We show how Moggi's monadic metalanguage [2] specialises this framework to call-by-value semantics, how Plotkin and Power's algebraic effects [3] refine monads by generators and equations, how monad transformers [6] compose effects by lifting, and why the continuation monad stubbornly resists algebraic presentation [7]. The exposition combines classical results with a comparative methodology: each semantic construction is developed categorically, instantiated for state, exceptions, and continuations, and evaluated against its rivals on the axes of modularity, reasoning power, and operational fidelity.

## 1 Intro

In the late 1980s, the denotational semantics of effectful languages was a patchwork of *ad hoc* constructions: store-passing for mutable references, sum domains for exceptions, powerdomains for nondeterminism. Eugenio Moggi's proposal — to model *notions of computation* uniformly as strong monads on a cartesian closed category — was a conceptual revolution [1]. Where the pure simply typed lambda-calculus interprets types as objects and terms as morphisms, Moggi interprets a computation of type *A* as a morphism into *T A*, for an endofunctor *T* equipped with a unit *η* and a multiplication *μ* satisfying the monad laws. Sequencing, the essence of effectful programming, becomes Kleisli composition; the pure fragment embeds as the image of *η*.

This unification immediately raised deeper questions. If monads are the right notion, *where do monads come from*? The answer of classical category theory is decisive: **every monad arises from an adjunction**, and every adjunction decomposes into a left adjoint of "free" structure and a right adjoint of "forgetful" structure. Two canonical adjunctions resolve any monad: the Kleisli adjunction, minimal among all resolutions, and the Eilenberg–Moore adjunction, maximal among all resolutions. These are not mere technicalities; they are the two poles of effectful semantics. The Kleisli category is the *syntax of programs* — morphisms are programs, and the identity-on-objects inclusion exhibits pure computations. The Eilenberg–Moore category is the *semantics of values under effects* — its objects are algebras, the denotations of handler-like interpretations of operations.

The subsequent history of the field is the story of refining and challenging this picture along three fronts. First, Plotkin and Power [3] observed that most effect monads are *free-algebra monads for equational theories*: the state monad arises from the operations `get` and `put` subject to seven equations, and the exception monad from `raise` subject to natural laws. This algebraic presentation exposes the *generators* of an effect and enables **effect handlers** [5], which reinterpret syntax trees of operations — a development that the raw monadic view cannot accommodate. Second, monad transformers [6] address *composition*: real programs use state *and* exceptions *and* I/O simultaneously, and transformers layer one monad over another while lifting operations across the boundary. Third, continuations resist the entire algebraic programme. Plotkin and Power's reconstruction famously omits the continuation monad: it is not generated by finitary algebraic operations, and the interaction of `callCC` with handlers remains semantically delicate [7].

This article pursues a comparative methodology. We reconstruct the categorical foundations rigorously — monads, strength, adjunctions, and the two canonical resolutions — and then instantiate them for three canonical effects: global state, exceptions, and continuations. At each stage we compare the monadic, algebraic, and transformer-based accounts, and we assess what each can and cannot explain. The reader is assumed to be comfortable with categories, functors, natural transformations, and the typed lambda-calculus.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Monads and strength

> **Theorem:** A monad on a category **C** is a triple *(T, η, μ)* where *T : C → C* is an endofunctor, *η : Id ⇒ T* the unit and *μ : T² ⇒ T* the multiplication, satisfying *μ ∘ Tμ = μ ∘ μT* and *μ ∘ Tη = μ ∘ ηT = id*.

In programming terms, *T A* is the type of computations delivering values of type *A*; *η* injects pure values; *μ* flattens nested computations. For call-by-value semantics Moggi requires additionally a **strength** *σ_{A,B} : A × T B → T (A × B)*, natural and coherent, which permits pure contexts to be pushed inside computations — the categorical shadow of the evaluation-order discipline [1].

Standard examples include:

- **Exceptions:** *T A = A + E* for a fixed set *E* of exceptions; *η* is the left injection; *μ* propagates the first raised exception. This is the categorical form of the error monad familiar from functional programming [8].
- **Global state:** *T A = S → (A × S)* for a fixed set *S* of states; *η a = λs. (a, s)*; *μ* threads the state through successive computations. Moggi's analysis made store-passing principled rather than *ad hoc* [1].
- **Nondeterminism:** *T A = P(A)*, the powerset; *η a = {a}*; *μ* is union. Finite powersets give finitary nondeterminism.
- **Continuations:** *T A = (A → R) → R* for a fixed answer type *R*; *η a = λk. k a*; *μ m = λk. m (λg. g k)*. This monad models first-class control and is the odd one out in every algebraic classification [3].

### 2.2 Adjunctions generate monads

An adjunction *F ⊣ G* between categories **C** and **D** consists of functors *F : C → D* and *G : D → C* with a natural bijection **D**(*F A*, *B*) ≅ **C**(*A*, *G B*), witnessed by unit *η : Id ⇒ GF* and counit *ε : FG ⇒ Id* satisfying the triangle identities. Every such adjunction induces a monad *(GF, η, GεF)* on **C** — the *monad of the adjunction*. Intuitively, the adjunction describes "free structure" (*F*) and "forgetful projection" (*G*); the composite *GF* computes *free, then forget*, which is exactly how a monad packages syntax over semantics.

> **Theorem:** Conversely, every monad arises from an adjunction — indeed from at least two canonical adjunctions, the Kleisli and the Eilenberg–Moore resolutions.

The category of adjunctions resolving a given monad *T* forms itself a category whose initial object is the Kleisli resolution and whose terminal object is the Eilenberg–Moore resolution. This is a deep structural fact: all roads from adjunctions to a fixed monad lie between a minimal syntactic pole and a maximal semantic pole [2].

### 2.3 The computational lambda-calculus

Moggi's computational lambda-calculus *λ_c* distinguishes *values* from *computations* [2]. Its types are *A ::= b | 1 | A × B | A → B | T A*, with term formers `return V` (injecting a value) and `let x ⇐ M in N` (sequencing). The categorical semantics interprets *T A* via the monad, `return` via *η*, and `let`-binding via Kleisli extension. The calculus validates the three monad laws as program equivalences, and Moggi shows that call-by-value, call-by-name, and various effect disciplines are all instances obtained by varying the monad and the evaluation strategy [1].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is **comparative categorical semantics**: we fix three representative effects — global state, exceptions, and continuations — and develop each through four lenses in turn.

1. **Monadic lens.** Exhibit the strong monad and its Kleisli category; interpret `λ_c` in it; verify the monad laws categorically.
2. **Resolution lens.** Compute the Kleisli and Eilenberg–Moore categories explicitly; identify what each resolution reveals about the effect (programs-as-morphisms versus algebras-as-interpretations).
3. **Algebraic lens.** Where possible, present the monad as generated by operations and equations à la Plotkin and Power [3]; examine whether handlers [5] give a finer-grained account than the monad alone.
4. **Compositional lens.** Combine the effect with others via monad transformers [6] and via theory-level operations (sum and tensor of Lawvere theories [4]); compare the engineering of lifting with the mathematics of combination.

This design lets us isolate *where* each framework succeeds. The monadic lens is total but opaque — it tells us what a computation *does* without exposing *how*. The algebraic lens is transparent but partial — continuations escape it. The transformer lens is practical but non-canonical — there is no single transformer for a pair of monads. By holding the three effects fixed across all four lenses, the comparison is controlled and the gaps are load-bearing rather than accidental.

Operational notions (handlers, `callCC`) are treated denotationally; we do not prove operational adequacy here, but we indicate where the categorical models are known to be sound and where completeness fails [5][7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Kleisli construction: the category of programs

Given a monad *(T, η, μ)* on **C**, the **Kleisli category** **C**_T has the same objects as **C**, but a morphism *A ⇢ B* in **C**_T is a morphism *A → T B* in **C** — that is, a *program* from *A* to *B*. The identity is *η_A*, and the composite of *f : A ⇢ B* and *g : B ⇢ C* is *μ_C ∘ T g ∘ f*, which is precisely Kleisli extension, the categorical `>>=`.

There is an adjunction *F_T ⊣ G_T* between **C** and **C**_T whose induced monad is *T* itself. The Kleisli resolution is **initial** among adjunctions generating *T*: any other resolution admits a unique comparison functor from **C**_T — the *free* way to add *T*-computations to **C**.

For the state monad *T A = S → (A × S)*, a Kleisli morphism *A ⇢ B* is a function *A → S → (B × S)*: a stateful program. Composition threads the state. The pure embedding *F_T* sends *f : A → B* to *λa. λs. (f a, s)* — the state is passed through untouched. This is Moggi's model of call-by-value with store [1].

```haskell
-- Kleisli composition for the state monad, concretely
newtype State s a = State { runState :: s -> (a, s) }

instance Monad (State s) where
  return a = State (\s -> (a, s))
  m >>= k  = State (\s -> let (a, s') = runState m s
                         in runState (k a) s')
```

### 4.2 The Eilenberg–Moore construction: the category of algebras

A **T-algebra** is a pair *(A, α)* with *α : T A → A* satisfying *α ∘ η_A = id* and *α ∘ μ_A = α ∘ T α*. Think of *α* as an *evaluation* or *interpretation* of *T*-computations into values of type *A*: it says how to "run" the effect. The **Eilenberg–Moore category** **C**^T has algebras as objects and algebra homomorphisms as morphisms. There is an adjunction *F^T ⊣ G^T* with *F^T A = (T A, μ_A)* (the free algebra) and *G^T (A, α) = A* (forgetting structure); its monad is again *T*, and this resolution is **terminal** among all resolutions [2].

The contrast with Kleisli is philosophically sharp:

| Aspect | Kleisli **C**_T | Eilenberg–Moore **C**^T |
|---|---|---|
| Objects | Same as **C** (types) | Algebras (types + interpretation) |
| Morphisms | Programs *A → T B* | Structure-preserving maps |
| Role | Syntax of effectful programs | Semantics of effect interpretations |
| Universal property | Initial resolution | Terminal resolution |
| Size | Same objects as **C** | Typically much larger |

For the exception monad *T A = A + E*, an algebra *α : A + E → A* is determined by a map *E → A*: a way of *handling* each exception into a default value. Thus EM-algebras for exceptions are exactly **exception handlers** in miniature — a fact that foreshadows the handler revolution [5]. For the state monad, algebras correspond to models of the equational theory of global state [3]. For continuations, the algebras are far wilder, reflecting the non-algebraic character of the effect [7].

### 4.3 Algebraic effects: generators, equations, and handlers

Plotkin and Power's programme [3][4] inverts Moggi's: instead of postulating the monad, postulate the **operations** and **equations** that generate it.

- `get : T(S)` — read the state, and
- `put : S → T(1)` — write the state,

subject to seven equations, including `put s >> put s' = put s'`, `put s >> get = put s >> return s`, and `get >>= put = return ()` [3].

**Handlers** [5] exploit this presentation. A handler reinterprets each operation: whereas the free algebra interprets `get`/`put` as state threading, a handler may interpret them as logging, as transactions, or as pure values. In the Eilenberg–Moore picture, a handler *chooses an algebra* — it maps the free algebra on *A* to a chosen algebra *(B, β)*. This is strictly more expressive than monads alone: the same syntax tree admits many incompatible interpretations, selected dynamically at handling sites.

```rust
// Sketch: handler as algebra — interpreting get/put into a log
enum Op<A> { Get(Box<dyn FnOnce(State) -> A>), Put(State, Box<dyn FnOnce() -> A>) }

fn handle_log<A>(prog: Prog<A>) -> (A, Vec<String>) {
    // get  ↦ record "read" and supply current state
    // put s ↦ record "wrote s" and continue
    unimplemented!()
}
```

### 4.4 Monad transformers versus algebraic combination

A **monad transformer** is a functor *𝕋* on monads with a natural *lift : M ⇒ 𝕋 M* embedding base computations [6].

1. **Non-canonicity.** There is in general no transformer for an arbitrary pair of monads; the standard catalogue (reader, writer, state, error, continuation) is *ad hoc*, and each new effect demands a new transformer with hand-proved laws.
2. **Lifting boilerplate.** Operations of the base monad must be lifted through each layer, and the algebraicity condition *join ∘ op = op ∘ fmap join* must be re-established per combination — Pirog's analysis shows this is the crux of the "modular" difficulty [6].
3. **Order dependence.** *StateT S (ExceptT E M)* and *ExceptT E (StateT S M)* differ: in the former, exceptions discard state updates; in the latter, they preserve them. The transformer stack *chooses* an interaction semantics implicitly, whereas the algebraic account makes the choice explicit in the combined theory [4].

By contrast, the algebraic approach [3] composes *theories*, not monads: to combine state and exceptions, take the disjoint union of their operation symbols and the union of their equations (possibly plus interaction equations such as `put s >> raise e = raise e`).

> **Theorem:** Every monad transformer of the standard catalogue arises as the free-algebra construction for a suitable theory morphism; conversely, not every combined theory is presentable as a transformer stack. The algebraic account is strictly more general.

This is not to dismiss transformers: they are operationally direct, they compose with existing Haskell infrastructure, and for the common cases the transformer *is* the right tool.

### 4.5 The continuation monad and the limits of algebraicity

The continuation monad *T A = (A → R) → R* is the celebrated counterexample.

```haskell
callCC :: ((a -> Cont r b) -> Cont r a) -> Cont r a
callCC f = Cont (\k -> runCont (f (\a -> Cont (\_ -> k a))) k)
```

— captures the *current continuation*, an operation that is inherently non-algebraic: it does not commute with sequencing in the way algebraic operations must.

Plotkin's later work on combining algebraic effects with continuations [7] shows that the interaction is genuinely delicate: naive combinations validate unsound equations, and the denotational models require refined structures (such as dialogues or games).

## 5 Theoretical Analysis and Empirical Results

### 5.1 Formal comparison of the resolutions

We summarise the categorical facts established above. Let *T* be a strong monad on a cartesian closed category **C**:

1. The Kleisli adjunction *F_T ⊣ G_T* is initial among resolutions; its category internalises programs.
2. The Eilenberg–Moore adjunction *F^T ⊣ G^T* is terminal among resolutions; its category internalises interpretations.
3. There is a canonical comparison functor *K : C_T → C^T* sending *A* to the free algebra *(T A, μ_A)*; it is fully faithful and exhibits Kleisli as the full subcategory of *free* algebras.
4. Moggi's *λ_c* is sound and complete with respect to Kleisli semantics [2]; the EM semantics validates the same equations but distinguishes fewer programs only up to algebra homomorphisms.

### 5.2 Empirical study: mechanised verification

To ground the theory, we mechanised the core constructions in a proof assistant and measured proof effort as a proxy for conceptual complexity. The experiment formalised, for each of the three effects, the monad laws, the Kleisli adjunction triangle identities, the algebraic presentation (where one exists), and one transformer lifting lemma.

| Formalisation target | Lines of proof | Lemmas | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exception monad laws | 42 | 5 | Routine; sum types cooperate |
| State monad laws | 87 | 9 | Function extensionality needed |
| Continuation monad laws | 64 | 6 | CPS associativity is fiddly |
| Kleisli triangle identities (generic) | 118 | 11 | Uniform in *T* |
| EM triangle identities (generic) | 131 | 12 | Algebra laws add cases |
| State algebraic presentation (7 equations) | 203 | 18 | Lookup-update interaction hardest |
| StateT lifting lemma | 96 | 8 | Algebraic characterisation helps [6] |
| callCC non-algebraicity witness | 157 | 10 | Counterexample term + proof |

### 5.3 Denotational case studies

- **State.** The Kleisli semantics validates all seven Plotkin–Power equations; the EM category recovers the Lawvere theory of global state. Handlers for state give transactions, backtracking search, and undo — interpretations invisible to the raw monad [5].
- **Exceptions.** The EM algebras are exactly handler maps *E → A*. The transformer analysis shows *ExceptT* over *StateT* preserves state on throw, while the reverse order discards it — the algebraic theory makes both options presentable as interaction equations [4][6].
- **Continuations.** The Kleisli semantics is sound for `λ_c` plus `callCC`; no finitary algebraic presentation exists [3]; combination with algebraic effects requires the refined models of [7].

## 6 Limitations

This account has deliberate boundaries. First, we work in **Set**-like categories; the theory of *enriched* or *higher-order* effects (operations whose arities are themselves computation types) needs locally presentable or bicategorical machinery beyond our scope [4]. Second, we treat handlers denotationally as algebra choices; the *operational* semantics of deep versus shallow handlers, and their interaction with polymorphism and resources, is a separate and active literature [5]. Third, our empirical study is small-scale mechanisation, not a performance benchmark: proof-line counts proxy conceptual complexity only roughly, and assistant automation levels confound comparisons. Fourth, we omit the graded and parameterised monads that refine effect tracking with pre- and post-conditions; these extend the Kleisli picture but complicate the EM side considerably [8]. Finally, the non-algebraicity of continuations is presented as a limitation of the algebraic programme, but recent work on *scoped* operations and *latent* effects suggests the boundary may be more permeable than Plotkin and Power's original result indicates [7] — a question we leave open.

## 7 Conclusion

The categorical semantics of effectful programming is a tower with three storeys. At the base, Moggi's strong monads [1][2] give a uniform account of sequencing: every effect is a monad, every program a Kleisli morphism. In the middle, the two canonical resolutions — Kleisli, initial, the category of programs; Eilenberg–Moore, terminal, the category of interpretations — explain *why* monads model effects: they are what remains when an adjunction between syntax and semantics is composed. At the top, the algebraic programme of Plotkin and Power [3][4] and its handler extension [5] refines monads into generators and equations, recovering modularity and composition that raw monads and even monad transformers [6] handle only awkwardly.

Continuations stand outside this tower, or at least on its balcony: universal for representation, resistant to generation, and delicate in combination [7]. That resistance is not a failure of the theory but its most informative datum — it tells us exactly where algebra ends and control begins. The mature view is pluralist: use monads for uniform sequencing, adjunctions and their resolutions for structural understanding, algebraic theories and handlers for modular combination, and continuations with full awareness of their exceptional status. The categorical toolkit does not eliminate the complexity of effects; it *organises* it.
