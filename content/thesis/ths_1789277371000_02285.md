# Shadow Tomography and Classical Shadows: Sample-Efficient Learning of Quantum States with Randomized Measurements

## Abstract

Learning the properties of an unknown quantum state without paying the exponential price of full state tomography is among the most consequential problems in quantum information science. This article presents a unified treatment of two landmark frameworks that solve it: Aaronson's *shadow tomography*, which estimates the acceptance probabilities of *M* known two-outcome measurements from Õ(ε⁻⁴ log⁴*M* log *D*) copies of a *D*-dimensional state [1], and the *classical shadows* of Huang, Kueng, and Preskill, which replace demanding joint measurements with experimentally friendly randomized single-copy measurements followed by purely classical post-processing [2]. We develop the measurement-channel formalism, the shadow norm that governs sample complexity, and the closed-form snapshot rules for randomized Pauli, global Clifford, and matchgate ensembles. We then cover derandomization for variational quantum eigensolvers [3], fermionic and matchgate shadows for quantum chemistry [4, 5], robustness to hardware noise [7], and applications ranging from reduced density matrix estimation to cross-platform verification [8] and quantum Fisher information [9].

## 1 Introduction

Suppose an experimentalist prepares many identical copies of an unknown *n*-qubit quantum state *ρ*, and wishes to predict the expectation values Tr(*O*₁*ρ*), …, Tr(*O_Mρ*) of *M* observables. The textbook answer is *quantum state tomography*: reconstruct a full classical description of *ρ* and compute everything from it. The price is catastrophic. Even with optimal protocols, learning a *D*-dimensional mixed state to constant accuracy requires Θ(*D*²) copies, and *D* = 2ⁿ grows exponentially with the number of qubits [1]. For the fifty-qubit devices now coming online, full tomography is not merely expensive — it is physically impossible.

Yet the textbook answer is overkill. As Aaronson observed, demanding a full classical description of a quantum system is excessive for most concrete tasks; it is usually enough to predict *certain features* of the state [1]. This shift in perspective — from *learning the state* to *learning linear functionals of the state* — launched two of the deepest ideas in modern quantum learning theory.

The first, **shadow tomography** (Aaronson, 2018), asks: given *M* known two-outcome measurements *E*₁, …, *E_M*, estimate every acceptance probability Tr(*E_iρ*) to additive error *ε*. Remarkably, a number of copies *polynomial* in log *M* and log *D* suffices — exponentially fewer than tomography [1]. The catch is implementability: the original procedure requires joint, entangled measurements across many copies of *ρ* held in quantum memory, together with exponentially long quantum circuits.

The second, **classical shadows** (Huang, Kueng, Preskill, 2020), keeps the sample efficiency while discarding the hardware demands [2]. The protocol is disarmingly simple: apply a *random* unitary to each copy, measure in the computational basis, and classically invert the known measurement statistics. The collected data — the *classical shadow* — is a succinct, reusable sketch of *ρ* from which *many* properties can later be predicted, including properties not specified before the measurements were taken. The guiding slogan is *"measure first, ask questions later."*

This article develops both frameworks with full technical detail: the measurement-channel formalism, the **shadow norm** that controls variance and sample complexity, the two canonical unitary ensembles (randomized Pauli and global Clifford measurements), derandomization, fermionic and matchgate shadows for quantum chemistry, noise robustness, and the empirical record.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The cost of full tomography

A *D*-dimensional density matrix has *D*² − 1 real degrees of freedom. Information-theoretic lower bounds show that Ω(*D*²/ε²) copies are necessary to learn *ρ* to trace distance *ε* in general, and this bound is achievable [1]. For *n* qubits this is Ω(4ⁿ) — the exponential wall that motivates everything that follows.

> **Key insight.** Tomography answers *every* question about *ρ*. Shadow methods answer *M specified* questions — or, in the classical-shadows variant, *any* questions asked *after* the data is collected — at cost scaling with log *M* rather than *D*².

### 2.2 Gentle measurements and the quantum union bound

Aaronson's shadow tomography rests on two quantum-information primitives. The **gentle measurement lemma** (Aaronson, 2005) states that if a two-outcome measurement accepts *ρ* with probability at least 1 − *ε*, the post-measurement state *ρ̃* satisfies ‖*ρ̃* − *ρ*‖_tr ≤ 2√*ε*: measurements that are almost certain barely disturb the state. The **quantum union bound** extends this to sequences: if each of *M* measurements accepts with high probability, the probability that *all* of them accept on a *single* copy is also high, provided the per-measurement failure probabilities are small enough. Together these allow a procedure to *search* over measurements for one on which the current hypothesis is wrong, without destroying the precious copies of *ρ* [1].

### 2.3 Unitary designs and twirling

Classical shadows randomize over a unitary ensemble 𝒰. The analysis uses *twirling*: averaging a channel over 𝒰. The *Clifford group* on *n* qubits is a unitary **2-design**, so twirling over it reproduces the first two moments of the Haar measure. Consequently, the measurement channel induced by random Cliffords takes a simple closed form — a depolarizing channel — whose inverse is known analytically. Randomized *Pauli* measurements correspond to twirling over local single-qubit Cliffords, equivalently to measuring each qubit in a uniformly random *X*, *Y*, or *Z* basis [2].

### 2.4 The median-of-means estimator

Shadow estimators are unbiased but heavy-tailed: individual snapshots *ρ̂* can have large operator norm. The **median-of-means** construction tames this. Split the *N* snapshots into *K* = 2 log(2*M*/δ) groups of *n* = *N*/*K* snapshots each; average the single-shot estimates Tr(*Oρ̂_i*) within each group; then take the median across groups. Each group mean concentrates by Chebyshev's inequality, and the median amplifies the success probability exponentially in *K* via a Chernoff bound — delivering failure probability at most *δ* simultaneously for all *M* observables [2].

## 3 Methodology

The classical-shadow protocol has a clean two-phase structure: a *quantum data-acquisition phase* and a *classical prediction phase*.

**Phase 1 — Data acquisition.** For *i* = 1, …, *N*:

1. Draw a unitary *U_i* from the ensemble 𝒰 (e.g., a tensor product of random single-qubit Cliffords, or a global random *n*-qubit Clifford).
2. Apply *U_i* to a fresh copy of *ρ* and measure in the computational basis, obtaining bitstring *b_i*.
3. Store the pair (*U_i*, *b_i*). The classical shadow is the multiset *S*(*ρ*; *N*) = {*ρ̂*₁, …, *ρ̂_N*}, where each *snapshot* is
   *ρ̂_i* = ℳ⁻¹(*U_i*†|*b_i*⟩⟨*b_i*|*U_i*),
   and ℳ is the **measurement channel**
   ℳ(*ρ*) = 𝔼_{*U*∼𝒰} Σ_b ⟨*b*|*UρU*†|*b*⟩ · *U*†|*b*⟩⟨*b*|*U*.

By construction 𝔼[*ρ̂*] = *ρ*: each snapshot is an unbiased (if noisy) estimator of the state itself.

**Phase 2 — Prediction.** For each observable *O_j*, form single-shot estimates *ô_j^(i)* = Tr(*O_jρ̂_i*) and combine them with median-of-means. Crucially, Phase 1 never uses the observables: the same shadow predicts properties dreamed up *after* the experiment [2].

The following Python sketch implements the complete Pauli-measurement pipeline:

```python
import numpy as np

PAULIS = {'X': np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]]),
          'Y': np.array([[0, -1j], [1j, 0]]),
          'Z': np.array([[1, 0], [0, -1]])}

def random_pauli_snapshot(rho, n):
    """One classical-shadow snapshot via randomized Pauli measurements."""
    bases = np.random.choice(['X', 'Y', 'Z'], size=n)
    # simulate measurement of rho in the chosen Pauli bases
    probs = measurement_probs(rho, bases)          # Born rule
    outcome = np.random.choice(2 ** n, p=probs)
    bits = [(outcome >> q) & 1 for q in range(n)]
    snap = np.array([[1.0]])
    for q in range(n):
        # invert the single-qubit measurement channel: rho_hat = 3 U^dag|b><b|U - I
        proj = np.outer(PAULIS[bases[q]][:, bits[q]], PAULIS[bases[q]][:, bits[q]].conj())
        snap = np.kron(snap, 3 * proj - np.eye(2))
    return snap

def median_of_means(estimates, K):
    groups = np.array_split(np.asarray(estimates), K)
    return float(np.median([g.mean() for g in groups]))

def predict(shadows, observable, K=20):
    shots = [np.trace(observable @ s).real for s in shadows]
    return median_of_means(shots, K)
```

The shadow norm — defined in §4.1 — is the single quantity that determines how many snapshots *N* this procedure needs.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The shadow norm: the master quantity

For a fixed unitary ensemble 𝒰 with measurement channel ℳ, the **shadow norm** of an observable *O* is defined as

‖*O*‖²_shadow = max_σ 𝔼_{*U*∼𝒰} Σ_b ⟨*b*|*UσU*†|*b*⟩ · ⟨*b*|*U*ℳ⁻¹(*O*)*U*†|*b*⟩²,

where the maximum ranges over all quantum states *σ*. It is an upper bound on the variance of the single-shot estimator Tr(*Oρ̂*), uniformly over the unknown state *ρ* [2]. Everything about sample complexity flows from it:

> **Theorem 4.1** (Huang–Kueng–Preskill [2]). Let *O*₁, …, *O_M* be observables with *B* ≥ max_i ‖*O_i*‖²_shadow. Then
> *N* = *O*(*B* log(*M*/δ) / ε²)
> randomized measurements suffice to estimate every Tr(*O_iρ*) to additive error *ε*, with success probability at least 1 − δ, via the median-of-means estimator.

Two remarks give the theorem its power. First, the dependence on *M* is *logarithmic*: exponentially many properties can be predicted from polynomially many measurements. Second, the bound is asymptotically *optimal* for single-copy measurements — no protocol restricted to measuring one copy at a time can do better in general [2]. Choosing 𝒰 well amounts to making ‖*O_i*‖_shadow small for the observables of interest.

### 4.2 Randomized Pauli measurements: the local workhorse

Take 𝒰 = Cl(1)^{⊗n}, the tensor product of single-qubit Clifford groups — operationally, measure each qubit in a random *X*, *Y*, or *Z* basis. The measurement channel factorizes and inverts qubit-by-qubit, giving the celebrated closed form

*ρ̂* = ⊗_{ℓ=1}^{n} (3 *U_ℓ*†|*b_ℓ*⟩⟨*b_ℓ*|*U_ℓ* − *I*).

For a Pauli string *P* of weight *k* (acting nontrivially on *k* qubits), the shadow norm is *exactly*

‖*P*‖²_shadow = 3^k,

and for a general *k*-local observable, ‖*O*‖_shadow ≤ 2^k‖*O*‖_∞ [2]. The sample complexity for *M* weight-*k* Pauli observables is therefore *O*(3^k log *M*/ε²) — exponential in the *locality* *k* but merely logarithmic in the *number* *M* of observables.

This is precisely the scaling behind the **quantum chemistry** use case: a molecular Hamiltonian decomposes into *O*(*n*⁴) Pauli strings, and Pauli shadows estimate all of them from *O*(log *n*) measurement settings — directly attacking the Hamiltonian-averaging bottleneck of the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) [2, 3].

| Ensemble | Snapshot *ρ̂* | Shadow norm² | Sample complexity | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Randomized Pauli (local Clifford) | ⊗_ℓ(3*U_ℓ*†\|*b_ℓ*⟩⟨*b_ℓ*\|*U_ℓ* − *I*) | 3^k for weight-*k* Pauli; ≤ 4^k‖*O*‖²_∞ for *k*-local *O* | *O*(3^k log *M*/ε²) | Local Hamiltonians, VQE, RDMs |
| Global Clifford | (*d*+1)*U*†\|*b*⟩⟨*b*\|*U* − *I*, *d* = 2ⁿ | ≤ 3Tr(*O*²) | *O*(Tr(*O*²) log *M*/ε²) | Fidelity, entanglement entropies |
| Matchgate (fermionic Gaussian) | Gaussian inverse channel | poly(*n*) for fermionic *k*-body ops | poly(*n*, log *M*)/ε² | Fermionic sim., QC-AFQMC |
| Fermionic partial tomography | Majorana twirl inverse | *O*(1) per *k*-RDM element class | poly(*n*)/ε² for *k*-RDM | 2-RDM estimation |

### 4.3 Global Clifford measurements: the nonlocal complement

Take 𝒰 to be the full *n*-qubit Clifford group. Since it is a unitary 2-design, the measurement channel is a depolarizing channel, ℳ(*ρ*) = (*ρ* + *I*)/(*d* + 1), with the elementary inverse

*ρ̂* = (*d* + 1) *U*†|*b*⟩⟨*b*|*U* − *I*,   *d* = 2ⁿ.

The shadow norm obeys ‖*O*‖²_shadow ≤ 3Tr(*O*²) [2]. This ensemble is *terrible* for local Paulis — a weight-*k* Pauli has Tr(*P*²) = *d*, giving exponentially large variance — but *superb* for low-rank global observables. The flagship application is **fidelity estimation**: for *O* = |*ψ*⟩⟨*ψ*|, Tr(*O*²) = 1, so *O*(log *M*/ε²) measurements estimate the fidelity with *M* candidate pure states. The same scaling governs **Rényi-2 entanglement entropy**: Tr(*ρ_A*²) is a degree-2 polynomial in *ρ*, estimated as a *U*-statistic 𝔼_{i≠j}[Tr(*S_A* *ρ̂_i* ⊗ *ρ̂_j*)] with the swap operator *S_A* on subsystem *A* — a *nonlinear* property extracted from *linear* snapshots [2].

The inverse-channel structure is clean enough to express in a pure functional language:

```haskell
-- Inverse measurement channel for the global Clifford ensemble, d = 2^n
type DensityMatrix = Matrix

cliffordInverse :: Int -> DensityMatrix -> DensityMatrix
cliffordInverse n postMeas =
  let d = 2 ^ n
  in  scale (fromIntegral (d + 1)) postMeas `matSub` identity d

-- Single-qubit Pauli ensemble inverse channel (tensor factor)
pauliInverse :: DensityMatrix -> DensityMatrix
pauliInverse postMeas = scale 3 postMeas `matSub` identity 2
```

### 4.4 Derandomization and fermionic shadows

**Derandomization.** Is randomness actually necessary? Huang, Kueng, and Preskill showed it is not [3]. Starting from the randomized Pauli protocol, a greedy procedure iteratively replaces each random single-qubit basis choice by the *deterministic* basis minimizing a cost function that upper-bounds the estimation variance. The derandomized measurement schedule is *guaranteed* to perform at least as well as the random one: for *L* low-weight Pauli observables, *O*(log *L*) measurement settings suffice, and numerics on small molecules show clear wins over Pauli grouping heuristics for ground-state energy estimation [3].

**Fermionic shadows.** Qubit Pauli shadows are mismatched to *fermionic* problems: a fermionic two-body operator maps to a high-weight Pauli string under Jordan–Wigner, inflating the 3^k factor. Zhao, Rubin, and Miyake introduced **fermionic partial tomography via classical shadows**, twirling over fermionic Gaussian unitaries so that *k*-body reduced density matrix (*k*-RDM) elements — the quantities determining molecular energies — are estimated with sample complexity polynomial in the number of modes rather than the number of RDM elements [5]. Wan, Huggins, Lee, and Babbush pushed this further with **matchgate shadows**: they proved that the discrete set of matchgate circuits that are also Clifford unitaries forms a *matchgate 3-design* — its first three moments match the Haar distribution over all fermionic Gaussian unitaries — so the resulting shadows are functionally equivalent to continuous twirling while remaining classically simulable [4]. The payoff is concrete: overlaps ⟨Ψ_T|*φ_i*⟩ between a trial wavefunction and walker states in quantum-classical auxiliary-field quantum Monte Carlo (QC-AFQMC) can be estimated with *polynomial* classical post-processing, removing the exponential post-processing bottleneck of the original Clifford-shadow approach [4].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: why median-of-means achieves Theorem 4.1

Fix one observable *O* with ‖*O*‖²_shadow ≤ *B*. Each group mean *ō*^(k) = (1/*n*)Σ_i Tr(*Oρ̂_i*) is unbiased with variance at most *B*/*n*. Choosing *n* = 34*B*/ε² and applying Chebyshev gives Pr[|*ō*^(k) − Tr(*Oρ*)| > ε] ≤ 1/4 per group. The median over *K* independent groups fails only if more than *K*/2 groups fail; a Chernoff bound drives this below *e^(−K/8)*. Setting *K* = 2 log(2*M*/δ) and union-bounding over *M* observables yields total failure probability ≤ δ. The *O*(*B* log(*M*/δ)/ε²) scaling follows [2].

A compact systems-language implementation of the aggregation step:

```rust
/// Median-of-means aggregation over K groups of single-shot estimates.
fn median_of_means(shots: &[f64], k: usize) -> f64 {
    let n = shots.len() / k;
    let mut means: Vec<f64> = shots
        .chunks(n)
        .map(|g| g.iter().sum::<f64>() / g.len() as f64)
        .collect();
    means.sort_by(|a, b| a.partial_cmp(b).unwrap());
    let m = means.len();
    if m % 2 == 1 { means[m / 2] } else { (means[m / 2 - 1] + means[m / 2]) / 2.0 }
}
```

### 5.2 Proof sketch: Aaronson's shadow tomography

Aaronson's procedure maintains a *hypothesis state* σ_t, initially the maximally mixed state, and repeats: (i) use the **Gentle Search Procedure** — powered by the quantum union bound — to find, among the *M* measurements, one on which σ_t errs by more than ε, consuming only Õ(log⁴*M*/ε²) copies while barely disturbing them; (ii) update σ_t toward consistency with the true outcome via *postselection learning* (later improved to **matrix multiplicative weights**, cutting the iteration count to Õ(log *D*/ε²) and the total to Õ(log⁴*M* log *D*/ε⁴)) [1]. Bădescu and O'Donnell subsequently tightened the analysis and computational efficiency of this quantum-data-analysis pipeline [6].

The data-acquisition loop itself is a finite-state protocol, specified here in TLA+:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE ShadowAcquisition ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS N, Ensemble          \* N snapshots; Ensemble \in {"Pauli","Clifford"}
VARIABLES shadows, i

Init == shadows = {} /\ i = 0

Acquire ==
    /\ i < N
    /\ \E U \in Ensemble, b \in Bitstrings :
         shadows' = shadows \cup {InverseChannel(U, b)}
    /\ i' = i + 1

Next == Acquire
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<shadows, i>> /\ WF_<<shadows, i>>(Next)
THEOREM Termination == Spec => <>(i = N /\ Cardinality(shadows) = N)
======================================================================================
```

### 5.3 The sample-complexity landscape

| Task | Copies needed | Measurements | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full state tomography (*D* dim) | Θ(*D*²/ε²) | informationally complete | [1] |
| Shadow tomography, *M* two-outcome POVMs | Õ(log⁴*M* log *D*/ε⁴) | joint, multi-copy + quantum memory | [1] |
| Classical shadows, *M* observables | *O*(*B* log *M*/ε²), *B* = max shadow norm² | single-copy randomized | [2] |
| Pauli shadows, *M* weight-*k* Paulis | *O*(3^k log *M*/ε²) | single-qubit random bases | [2] |
| Clifford shadows, fidelity w/ pure state | *O*(log *M*/ε²) | global random Clifford | [2] |
| Derandomized Pauli, *L* low-weight Paulis | *O*(log *L*/ε²) settings | deterministic | [3] |

### 5.4 Empirical record

- **HKP numerics** [2]: Clifford shadows estimate the fidelity of a 60-qubit noisy GHZ preparation and Rényi entropies of toric-code ground states from a few thousand measurements — regimes where tomography would need ~10³⁶ copies.
- **Derandomization** [3]: on molecular Hamiltonians (H₂, LiH, BeH₂ in minimal bases), greedy deterministic schedules cut the measurement budget for chemical-accuracy ground-state energies by factors of several over both naive Pauli grouping and the randomized baseline.
- **Cross-platform verification** [8]: Elben *et al.* used randomized-measurement fidelities to compare the outputs of two 10-qubit trapped-ion simulators without reconstructing either state — a task needing 4¹⁰ measurements by direct tomography.
- **Quantum Fisher information** [9]: Rath *et al.* extracted the QFI — a nonlinear, metrologically central quantity — from randomized measurements on IBM superconducting hardware, demonstrating Heisenberg scaling on noisy GHZ states.
- **QC-AFQMC** [4]: matchgate shadows supply the trial–walker overlaps that control the fermion sign problem, with the exponential classical post-processing of earlier Clifford-based schemes eliminated.

## 6 Limitations and Future Work

Classical shadows are not a panacea; their limits are as instructive as their successes.

- **The locality wall.** The Pauli shadow norm grows as 3^k in operator weight. For observables supported on Ω(*n*) qubits — e.g., a Jordan–Wigner-encoded fermionic operator on 50 modes — this is exponential in system size. Clifford shadows have the mirror problem: they assign variance ~2ⁿ to *every* Pauli string regardless of locality.
- **Classical post-processing.** Predicting Tr(*Oρ̂*) from a Clifford snapshot can cost time exponential in *n* for generic *O* (this is exactly the bottleneck matchgate shadows remove for fermionic overlaps [4]). Pauli snapshots, being product operators, evaluate in *O*(2^{2k}) time for *k*-local *O* — cheap precisely where the statistics are good.
- **Noise.** Real devices suffer gate-dependent errors. Koh and Grewal showed the framework survives: by twirling the noise into a learnable channel and calibrating it, *robust shadows* retain their guarantees under gate-independent Markovian noise [7]. Gate-*dependent* noise and measurement crosstalk remain active research fronts.
- **Optimality and quantum memory.** HKP's Ω(log *M*) lower bound is tight only among *single-copy* protocols. Chen, Cotler, Huang, and Li proved *exponential* separations between learning with and without quantum memory: entangled measurements across copies unlock tasks that no single-copy strategy can touch, delineating exactly where Aaronson's joint-measurement machinery is irreplaceable.
- **Verifiability.** Given only a classical shadow, *certifying* that its predictions are correct can be computationally hard — a gap between prediction and trust that recent work has begun to map.
- **Open directions.** *Shallow shadows* interpolate between Pauli and Clifford ensembles via finite-depth random circuits, optimizing the shadow norm for observables of intermediate locality; adaptive and derandomized schedules [3] continue to close the gap to the information-theoretic limits; and rigorous guarantees for higher-order nonlinear functionals beyond purities and entropies remain largely open.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Shadow tomography and classical shadows redefined what it means to "learn" a quantum state. Aaronson showed that the *information* needed to answer *M* questions scales only polylogarithmically in *M* [1]; Huang, Kueng, and Preskill showed that this information can be harvested with the simplest imaginable experiment — random unitaries and computational-basis measurements — and refined *classically*, after the fact, into answers for questions not yet asked [2]. The shadow norm turned protocol design into observable-driven optimization; derandomization [3] removed the randomness tax; fermionic and matchgate shadows [4, 5] carried the machinery into quantum chemistry; and noise-robust variants [7] brought it to real hardware.
