# DAG-Based Byzantine Fault Tolerance: Narwhal-Tusk, Bullshark, and Pipelined HotStuff for High-Throughput Consensus

## Abstract

Classical Byzantine fault tolerant (BFT) state machine replication has historically coupled transaction dissemination with transaction ordering inside a single leader-driven protocol, a design that collapses under the throughput demands of modern permissioned ledgers. This thesis presents a comprehensive treatment of the architectural paradigm shift toward **directed acyclic graph (DAG)** based BFT consensus, in which a high-throughput dissemination layer is cleanly separated from an ordering layer. We analyze the Narwhal mempool abstraction and its embedded zero-message-overhead asynchronous consensus protocol Tusk, the partially synchronous Bullshark protocol that refines this lineage with a practical fast path, and the pipelined HotStuff protocol that both inspired and benefits from this separation. Drawing on published experimental results — including 170,000 transactions per second at 2.5-second latency for Narwhal-HotStuff on a wide-area network and 125,000 transactions per second at 2-second latency for Bullshark with 50 validators — we compare message complexity, latency structure, liveness properties, and deployment experience on production networks such as Sui. We conclude by formalizing open limitations: garbage collection of unbounded DAGs, fairness under censorship, and the persistent latency floor imposed by round-based DAG

## 1 Introduction

Byzantine fault tolerance began as a problem of *agreement*: given *n* replicas of which at most *f* may behave arbitrarily, ensure all correct replicas commit the same sequence of commands despite an adversarial network [1]. The seminal Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) protocol demonstrated that agreement could be achieved with *O(n²)* communication in the common case, but its monolithic leader-centric design forced every byte of every transaction to flow through the leader's proposal pipeline. As blockchains pushed target throughputs from hundreds to hundreds of thousands of transactions per second, this coupling became the dominant bottleneck.

The conceptual breakthrough, articulated most forcefully by Danezis, Kokoris Kogias, Sonnino, and Spiegelman [2], is a **separation of concerns**: a *mempool* protocol specializes in the high-throughput, reliable dissemination of transactions and their causal histories, while a *consensus* protocol specializes in ordering the small, constant-sized certificates produced by the mempool. Once dissemination is factored out, the consensus layer can order certificates rather than transactions, and its throughput becomes decoupled from payload size. The mempool itself is structured as a directed acyclic graph whose vertices are blocks and whose edges are cryptographic references to prior blocks, giving the DAG a *causal* semantics: a vertex implicitly commits its entire causal history.

This thesis studies three pillars of this paradigm:

1. **Narwhal and Tusk** — a DAG-based mempool with an embedded asynchronous consensus protocol requiring zero additional messages [2].
2. **Bullshark** — the first DAG-based BFT protocol optimized for partial synchrony, adding a low-latency fast path while preserving asynchronous liveness [3].
3. **Pipelined HotStuff** — the classical partially synchronous leader-based protocol whose quorum-certificate machinery both inspired the DAG designs and serves as the consensus layer composed with Narwhal [4].

We also situate these designs against their descendants: Mysticeti's uncertified DAGs achieving 3-round commit latency [5], and the Shoal framework's leader-reputation pipelining that reduced Bullshark latency by up to 40% [6].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 System and Threat Model

We adopt the standard permissioned model. A fixed committee of *n = 3f + 1* validators communicate over authenticated point-to-point channels. Up to *f* validators may be Byzantine, deviating arbitrarily from the protocol, including sending conflicting messages and colluding. Honest validators follow the protocol and eventually deliver messages sent by honest validators, though the adversary controls message ordering and may delay delivery up to some bound.

Network models stratify the analysis:

| Model | Timing assumption | Liveness | Safety |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Synchronous** | Known delay bound Δ | Guaranteed | Guaranteed |
| **Partially synchronous** | Unknown GST; after GST, bound Δ holds | After GST | Always |
| **Asynchronous** | No timing assumptions | With randomization | Always |

PBFT and HotStuff target partial synchrony; Tusk and the asynchronous variant of Bullshark target full asynchrony using shared randomness.

### 2.2 Quorum Certificates and the HotStuff Lineage

HotStuff [4] reframed classical BFT around the *quorum certificate* (QC): a threshold signature over a block by *n − f = 2f + 1* distinct validators, serving as compact proof of agreement. Its three-phase structure — **prepare**, **pre-commit**, **commit** — ensures that a committed block carries a chain of three consecutive QCs, and the *pipelined* variant folds these phases into a single repeating round so that the leader of round *r* simultaneously advances the prepare phase of its own block and the later phases of its ancestors.

The safety argument rests on quorum intersection: any two quorums of size *2f + 1* intersect in at least *f + 1* validators, hence at least one honest validator, so conflicting QCs for the same view cannot both exist. The liveness argument rests on *responsiveness*: after GST, a correct leader drives consensus at the pace of actual network delay rather than the worst-case bound.

> **Theorem (HotStuff Safety):** If two conflicting blocks are committed by honest replicas, then at least *f + 1* Byzantine replicas exist — contradicting the fault bound. Hence no two honest replicas commit conflicting blocks [4].

Yet HotStuff's Achilles' heel is throughput: every transaction traverses the leader, so the leader's bandwidth caps the system at roughly 1,800 tx/sec in the published WAN experiments [2]. This is the bottleneck Narwhal attacks.

### 2.3 From Leader Dissemination to DAG Dissemination

In classical protocols, the mempool is an afterthought — a local buffer from which the leader draws transactions. Narwhal elevates it to a first-class distributed protocol: every validator continuously broadcasts its own blocks, each containing a batch of transactions and references to *2f + 1* blocks from the previous round. The resulting DAG has three critical properties [2]:

- **Validity:** every block is signed by its author and references only valid prior blocks.
- **Integrity:** a block appears in the DAG only after *2f + 1* distinct validators have received and stored it (via a consistent broadcast / reliable broadcast primitive).
- **Availability / completeness:** any block referenced by a committed anchor has its full causal history retrievable by all honest validators.

The quorum of *2f + 1* references per vertex is the engine of the design. Because any two such quorums intersect in *f + 1* validators, at least one honest validator vouches for the referenced history — the same intersection logic as HotStuff's QCs, transplanted from the ordering layer into the dissemination layer.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytical and comparative rather than experimental: we synthesize the published specifications, correctness arguments, and benchmark results of the primary papers [2][3][4][5][6], the systematization-of-knowledge survey of DAG-based blockchains [7], and the formal delay analysis of Narwhal/Bullshark commit latency [8]. We proceed in four stages:

1. **Formal reconstruction.** We restate each protocol's core mechanism (Narwhal's round-based DAG construction, Tusk's randomized anchor election, Bullshark's steady-state and fallback commit rules, HotStuff's chained phases) in a uniform notation to enable direct comparison.
2. **Complexity accounting.** We tabulate per-round message complexity, authenticator complexity, and the number of communication steps on the critical commit path.
3. **Empirical comparison.** We collate WAN benchmark figures reported by the original authors, normalizing to transactions per second and end-to-end latency under fault-free and faulty conditions.
4. **Limitation analysis.** We identify structural weaknesses — garbage collection, censorship fairness, and the round-latency floor — and evaluate how subsequent work (Mysticeti [5], Shoal [6]) addresses them.

We do not re-run the benchmarks; all performance claims are attributed to their published sources and should be read as the authors' reported results on their testbeds.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Narwhal: The DAG Mempool

Narwhal's unit of dissemination is the *block*, produced by each validator in each *round*. A block *B* authored by validator *v* in round *r* contains:

```python
@dataclass
class NarwhalBlock:
    author: ValidatorId
    round: int
    batch_digest: bytes        # Merkle root of transaction batch (bulk data lives with workers)
    parents: list[BlockHash]   # exactly 2f+1 references to round r-1 blocks
    signature: bytes
```

The protocol enforces two invariants. First, a validator advances to round *r + 1* only after collecting *2f + 1* distinct round-*r* blocks, guaranteeing the DAG grows in lockstep quorums. Second, blocks are disseminated through a *consistent broadcast* variant (a weaker, cheaper primitive than full reliable broadcast): the author sends the block to all validators, each validator that receives it first returns a signed acknowledgment, and the block becomes *certified* once *2f + 1* acknowledgments are aggregated into a certificate of availability. Certification, not delivery, is what the consensus layer orders.

The engineering masterstroke is the **primary–worker split**: the *primary* handles only small block headers and certificates, while *workers* — arbitrarily many per validator — stream the bulk transaction batches. Because workers are embarrassingly parallel, throughput scales linearly with worker count: the published experiments reach 600,000 tx/sec with no latency increase simply by adding workers [2]. Consensus itself never touches transaction bytes.

```rust
// Simplified primary loop (illustrative)
fn advance_round(state: &mut Primary, certs: Vec<BlockCert>) {
    if certs.len() >= 2 * F + 1 {
        let parents: Vec<Hash> = certs.iter().map(|c| c.digest()).collect();
        let block = sign(Block { round: state.round + 1, parents, .. });
        broadcast(block);
    }
}
```

### 4.2 Tusk: Zero-Overhead Asynchronous Consensus on the DAG

Tusk observes that the Narwhal DAG already contains everything consensus needs. Rounds are grouped into *waves* of three rounds; at the end of each wave, a shared random coin elects a *leader* (anchor) block from the wave's first round. The commit rule is purely local:

1. Let *w* be a wave and *L(w)* its randomly elected leader vertex.
2. *L(w)* is **committed** if at least *f + 1* vertices in the wave's third round reference it (directly or through causal history).
3. Committing *L(w)* transitively commits its entire causal history, ordered deterministically (e.g., by depth-first traversal with ties broken by vertex hash).

The elegance is that **no consensus messages are ever sent**: validators merely interpret the DAG they already built. The *f + 1* threshold matters because *f + 1* references guarantee at least one honest validator observed the leader, and quorum intersection across waves ensures that if one honest validator commits *L(w)*, all honest validators eventually commit it — the *common-core* argument inherited from the asynchronous DAG-Rider lineage [2][9].

> **Theorem (Tusk Agreement):** If an honest validator commits leader *L(w)*, then every honest validator eventually commits *L(w)* and all leaders it causally precedes, in the same relative order.

Because the coin is revealed only after the wave completes, the adversary cannot predict and censor the leader in advance — this is what buys *asynchronous liveness* with expected constant rounds per commit. The measured cost: ~160,000 tx/sec at ~3 seconds latency on a WAN [2], a 20× improvement over the prior asynchronous state of the art.

### 4.3 Bullshark: Making DAG-BFT Practical for Partial Synchrony

Tusk's randomized waves are optimal for asynchrony but wasteful when the network is well-behaved: every commit waits for coin revelation and wave completion. Bullshark [3] introduces **two interlocking commit rules**:

- **Steady-state (fast) path:** each wave designates a *steady-state leader* deterministically (e.g., round-robin). A validator commits it as soon as it observes *f + 1* votes (references) in the following round — typically within two DAG rounds of proposal, with no timeouts and no randomness.
- **Fallback (asynchronous) path:** if the steady-state leader fails to gather support (equivocation, crash, or asynchrony), the protocol falls back to a Tusk-like randomized election over the same DAG, preserving liveness under asynchrony.

Crucially, the fallback never interferes with the fast path in good conditions, and the fast path never compromises the fallback's safety: both rules interpret the *same* DAG, and the commit logic is ordered so that fallback commits cannot contradict steady-state commits. The implementation is strikingly small — roughly 200 lines of code atop a Narwhal mempool [3] — yet achieves 125,000 tx/sec at 2-second latency with 50 validators.

A subtlety worth emphasizing: Bullshark's fast path exploits *synchronous periods* without ever assuming synchrony for safety. Timeouts appear only as a liveness crutch in the partially synchronous standalone variant; the DAG-Rider-derived asynchronous core guarantees progress even if timeouts are misconfigured.

### 4.4 Pipelined HotStuff over Narwhal: Composition

Narwhal-HotStuff composes the two layers with a clean interface: HotStuff orders *certificates of availability*, and execution materializes the referenced batches from the DAG. The leader's proposal in round *r* contains only digests; the *2f + 1* votes certify the digests; and the three-chain commit rule of pipelined HotStuff applies unchanged. Because proposals are tiny, the leader bottleneck evaporates: the same HotStuff logic that managed 1,800 tx/sec alone reaches 130,000–170,000 tx/sec over Narwhal [2].

The TLA+ sketch below captures the composition's safety essence — a certificate for a digest implies availability of the payload, so ordering digests orders payloads:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE NarwhalHotStuff ------------------------------
CONSTANTS Validators, F
VARIABLES dag, committed
Available(d) == Cardinality({v \in Validators : HasStored(v, d)}) >= 2*F + 1
Certified(d) == \E q \in SUBSET Validators : Cardinality(q) = 2*F + 1
                  /\ \A v \in q : SignedAck(v, d)
TypeOK == \A d \in DOMAIN dag : Certified(d) => Available(d)
Safety == \A d1, d2 \in committed : d1 /= d2 => ~Conflicts(d1, d2)
====================================================================================
```

The composition is not free: HotStuff's view-change machinery reintroduces a liveness fragility. Under faults or asynchrony, Narwhal-HotStuff maintains throughput but its latency degrades more sharply than Tusk's, because a stalled leader must be replaced through timeouts rather than being bypassed by the DAG's parallel structure [2].

### 4.5 The Latency Frontier: Mysticeti and Shoal

Two descendants attack the residual latency of round-based DAGs. **Mysticeti** [5] observes that explicit per-block certification costs a full round trip; by building on *uncertified* DAGs with threshold clocks and a novel commit rule, Mysticeti-C reaches the theoretical lower bound of 3 message rounds for commit, achieving 0.5-second WAN commit latency at over 200,000 tx/sec, and its deployment on Sui cut end-to-end latency from 1.9s (Bullshark) to 400ms. **Shoal** [6] keeps certification but adds *leader reputation* — skipping leaders with poor historical support — and *pipelining* of waves, reducing Bullshark latency by up to 40% fault-free and 80% under faults, while introducing *prevalent responsiveness*: timeouts are eliminated in all but pathological cases. Together they suggest the DAG paradigm's latency floor is set by certification and wave structure, both of which are now being dismantled.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Throughput and Latency Benchmarks

The following table collates the headline WAN results reported in the primary papers. Testbeds differ (validator counts, transaction sizes, regions), so these are indicative, not head-to-head:

| Protocol | Throughput (tx/sec) | Latency | Validators | Network model |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HotStuff (baseline) | ~1,800 | ~1.0 s | 10–20 | Partial sync |
| Narwhal-HotStuff | 130,000–170,000 | <2.0–2.5 s | 10–50 | Partial sync |
| Narwhal-HotStuff (multi-worker) | up to 600,000 | ~3.5 s | 10 | Partial sync |
| Tusk | 140,000–160,000 | ~3–4 s | 10–50 | Asynchronous |
| Bullshark | 125,000 | ~2.0 s | 50 | Partial sync (async fallback) |
| Bullshark (standalone psync) | 125,000 | ~2.0 s | 50 | Partial sync |
| Mysticeti-C | >200,000 | ~0.5 s | 100+ (Sui) | Partial sync |

The structural lesson is consistent: **dissemination, not agreement, was the bottleneck**. Once the mempool absorbs payload transfer, consensus over certificates is cheap, and throughput scales with provisioned bandwidth rather than protocol message complexity.

### 5.2 Proof Sketches

We sketch the three safety arguments that recur across the DAG-BFT family.

**Lemma 1 (Quorum availability).** If a block certificate exists (2f + 1 acknowledgments), then at least *f + 1* honest validators store the block's full causal history.

*Proof.* Each honest acknowledger stores the block before acknowledging; at most *f* acknowledgers are Byzantine. ∎

**Lemma 2 (Leader visibility).** If *f + 1* round-*r + 2* vertices reference leader *L*, then every honest validator's DAG eventually contains *L*'s causal history.

*Proof.* Among the *f + 1* referencing vertices at least one is honest; its causal history is retrievable by Lemma 1, and honest validators eventually receive all honest vertices. ∎

> **Theorem (DAG-BFT Agreement):** No two honest validators commit conflicting leaders, and committed leaders induce identical total orders.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose honest *p* commits *L* via the *f + 1* rule. By Lemma 2, every honest validator eventually observes *L* with sufficient support, and the deterministic ordering function applied to identical causal histories yields identical sequences. Conflicting commits would require two leaders with overlapping histories ordered differently — impossible since the traversal is a pure function of the DAG. The full mechanization appears in [2, §5] and [3, §4]. ∎

Liveness for the asynchronous protocols follows from the *common-core* property: in each wave, the set of vertices referenced by *f + 1* honest validators' third-round blocks contains a vertex seen by all honest validators, so the random coin elects a committable leader with probability at least *(f + 1)/(3f + 1) > 1/3* per wave — constant expected waves to commit [2][9].

A formal delay analysis modeling Bullshark's anchor-commit rule as Bernoulli trials over reference probabilities derives expected commit latency as a function of *n*, *f*, and the per-node reference probability *p = 2/3* for honest nodes [8], confirming that commit latency concentrates tightly around 2–3 rounds in the fault-free case.

## 6 Limitations

**Garbage collection.** The DAG grows unboundedly: every round adds *n* vertices. Protocols garbage-collect rounds below the latest committed wave, but a slow or partitioned validator may need ancient history to catch up, forcing either unbounded storage or complex checkpoint/snapshot protocols. Mysticeti embeds epoch-closing checkpoints into the DAG [5]; production systems pair this with state sync, but the interaction between GC and the availability property (Lemma 1) remains delicate — collecting too aggressively can strand honest validators.

**Censorship and fairness.** Leader-based commit rules order a leader's causal history, but a Byzantine leader (or the validators choosing references) can systematically exclude a victim's blocks. Narwhal guarantees *eventual* inclusion only if *f + 1* honest validators reference the victim's blocks; targeted censorship by *f* Byzantine plus *f* slow honest validators can delay inclusion arbitrarily. No deployed DAG-BFT protocol provides a formal censorship-resistance bound comparable to its safety proofs.

**The round-latency floor.** Even Mysticeti's 3-round optimum is a floor, not a ceiling to break: each round costs a network round trip, so WAN deployments cannot commit faster than ~3× RTT no matter how efficient the protocol. Sub-RTT finality requires optimistic fast paths (cf. Mysticeti-FPC for owned objects [5]) that weaken the guarantee to non-conflicting transactions.

**Complexity and auditability.** Bullshark's "200 lines" claim [3] counts only the consensus logic atop a full Narwhal implementation — the mempool, networking, cryptography, and storage layers dwarf it. Auditing the *composed* system (e.g., worker-primary consistency, certificate replay across epochs) is substantially harder than auditing the consensus core, and most published proofs abstract these layers away.

**Evaluation comparability.** Reported throughputs use different transaction sizes (often 512 bytes), validator counts, and cloud regions; the 600,000 tx/sec figure [2] assumes abundant provisioned bandwidth and colocated workers. Production deployments (Sui's 106 validators at ~400ms [5]) show the paradigm works at scale, but independent reproduction of the headline numbers remains rare.

## 7 Conclusion

The DAG-based BFT lineage — from Narwhal's mempool abstraction through Tusk's zero-overhead asynchronous consensus, Bullshark's practical partially synchronous fast path, and pipelined HotStuff's compositional reuse — represents the most significant architectural advance in Byzantine fault tolerance since PBFT. Its central insight is a decomposition: *disseminate at the speed of bandwidth, order at the speed of certificates*. The empirical record supports the decomposition decisively, with two-order-of-magnitude throughput gains over monolithic leader-based designs and a clear path, via uncertified DAGs and reputation-based pipelining, toward the latency lower bounds.

Yet the paradigm is not a panacea. Garbage collection, censorship fairness, and the round-trip latency floor are structural, not incidental, and the proofs that make these protocols trustworthy cover a smaller fraction of the deployed system than their elegance suggests. The next frontier is therefore twofold: *formalizing* the layers the current proofs abstract away (mempool fairness, storage reclamation), and *optimizing* what the current protocols leave on the table (sub-RTT optimistic paths, sparse DAGs that tame the *O(n²)* edge overhead [10]). If that program succeeds, DAG-BFT will have completed the journey from research prototype to the default substrate of high-throughput permissioned consensus.
