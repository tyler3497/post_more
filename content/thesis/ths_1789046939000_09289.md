# Beyond the Reticle Limit: Chiplet Architectures, UCIe Die-to-Die Interconnects, Known-Good-Die Testing, and 2.5D/3D Advanced Packaging

## Abstract

The monolithic system-on-chip has collided with hard boundaries: the ~850 mm² photolithography reticle limit, the superlinear collapse of manufacturing yield with die area, and divergent cost-optimal process nodes for logic, SRAM, analog, and I/O. The industry's answer is the chiplet: functional disaggregation into independently manufactured, tested, and binned dies, re-integrated in advanced 2.5D/3D packages. This thesis develops the full technical stack of the paradigm. We derive the Poisson yield model that makes disaggregation economically rational and quantify it against AMD's published EPYC data (0.59× the monolithic cost despite ~10% more silicon), and trace UCIe from its 1.0 release (March 2022) through 3.0's 64 GT/s modes (August 2025). We taxonomize packaging substrates — silicon interposers, local interconnect bridges, embedded bridges, and TSV stacks — with their pitch, reach, and density trade-offs, and show how Known-Good-Die flows, interposer screening, and IEEE Std 1838-2019 resolve the testing crisis disaggregation creates. A quantitative section computes yield, cost, and bandwidth figures end-to-end.

## 1 Introduction

For most of its history the semiconductor industry scaled by making the die bigger and the transistors smaller at once. Both trends are now exhausted as routine engineering. Extreme-ultraviolet lithography reticles impose a *hard* ceiling on single-exposure die area — approximately 850 mm² in production scanners — beyond which a die cannot be printed without stitching exposures [1]. Simultaneously, at leading-edge nodes the defect-density-limited yield of large dice dominates the cost equation: under the classical Poisson model, yield falls as `Y = exp(−D₀·A)`, so doubling area squares the yield loss, and a single random defect destroys a device whose silicon alone costs thousands of dollars [2]. The third pressure is economic heterogeneity: logic scales aggressively with each node, but SRAM, analog/RF, and I/O do not, so integrating everything on the newest node wastes the most expensive silicon on circuitry that gains almost nothing from it [3].

*Chiplets* invert the monolith. A system is partitioned into N smaller dies — compute chiplets, I/O dies, memory stacks, accelerators — each fabricated on its cost-optimal node, each small enough to yield well, each testable as a **Known Good Die (KGD)** before assembly, then re-integrated on an advanced substrate whose die-to-die interconnects reach bandwidth densities and energies per bit that board-level links cannot approach. AMD's first-generation EPYC demonstrated the arithmetic: ~10% total silicon overhead versus a hypothetical monolithic 32-core die, but an estimated final cost of only **0.59×** the monolithic design, because the yield of four 213 mm² chiplets dominates that of one 800 mm² die [4].

Disaggregation creates problems the monolith never had: dies need a *standardized* die-to-die interface for a multi-vendor ecosystem — the **Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express (UCIe)** consortium, founded 2022 by AMD, Arm, ASE, Google Cloud, Intel, Meta, Microsoft, Qualcomm, Samsung, and TSMC [5]; substrates need defect screening — **Known Good Interposer (KGI)** flows; and pin-less stacked dies need test access — **IEEE Std 1838-2019**, with its die wrappers and "test elevator" [6][7]. This thesis treats the stack as one system: economics, interconnect protocol, packaging, test, and quantitative evaluation.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The yield catastrophe of large dice

The simplest useful defect model is the Poisson model: defects are random, independent, uniformly distributed with density D₀, and any defect kills the die. Then:

> **Theorem:** Under Poisson defect statistics with density D₀, die yield is `Y(A) = exp(−D₀·A)`, and the cost per *good* die scales as `C_good = C_wafer·A_die / (A_wafer·Y(A))` — superlinear in A.

With D₀ = 0.1 defects/cm², an 800 mm² die yields e^(−0.8) ≈ 0.45, while a 200 mm² chiplet yields e^(−0.2) ≈ 0.82. The KGD flow discards bad chiplets *before* assembly, so system yield is set by the (screened) package yield rather than the product of raw die yields. This is the central economic argument of [2], extended quantitatively in §5.

### 2.2 A brief history of multi-die integration

Multi-chip modules predate the term "chiplet" (IBM's 1980s Thermal Conduction Modules), but density and intent changed everything:

1. **2015**: TSMC ships CoWoS for NVIDIA's Pascal GP100 — compute die plus HBM2 on a silicon interposer — the first mass-production 2.5D product.
2. **2017**: AMD ships EPYC "Naples": four 14 nm Zeppelin dies on organic substrate, connected by Infinity Fabric — the first high-volume chiplet server CPU [4].
3. **2019**: Intel launches Foveros 3D stacking (Lakefield) — logic-on-logic vertical integration with TSVs.
4. **March 2022**: UCIe 1.0 ratified; Intel donates its Advanced Interface Bus (AIB) PHY as the starting point [3].
5. **2022–2023**: AMD ships Instinct MI300 — 3D-stacked HBM3 and compute chiplets in one package.
6. **2023–2025**: UCIe 1.1, 2.0, and 3.0 add streaming protocols, runtime health monitoring and repair, 3D-package support, manageability/debug, and 48/64 GT/s signaling [5][8][9].

### 2.3 The reticle limit

Lithography reticles expose a finite field per shot; production scanners cap the single-exposure field at roughly 850 mm² (some analyses cite ~858 mm²) [1][3]. Designs exceeding it — NVIDIA's Blackwell B200 dual-die GPU at ~2×800 mm², the Rubin platform targeting ~4× reticle footprints — *must* be partitioned [3]. The reticle limit is a constraint of the optics, not an economic preference.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The UCIe Protocol Stack: Physical, Adapter, and Protocol Layers

UCIe standardizes the complete die-to-die stack — physical I/O, protocol, and software model — so multi-vendor chiplets interoperate like PCIe cards on a motherboard [5][3]. The architecture has three layers plus a management sideband:

| Layer | Function | Key mechanisms |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | Raw signaling over package bumps | UCIe-S (standard 2D/organic) and UCIe-A (advanced 2.5D) modules; x16/x8 (S), x64/x32 (A); up to 4 modules per link; clock-forwarded signaling derived from AIB |
| **Adapter** | Link management, CRC/retry | Raw Die-to-Die Interface (**RDI**) for protocol-agnostic transfer; Flit-Aware Die-to-Die Interface (**FDI**) for flit-based protocols; training, lane repair/degrade |
| **Protocol** | Traffic semantics | PCIe (via CXL.io mapping), CXL.mem, CXL.cache, **streaming** (user-defined, added in 1.1), raw format |
| **Sideband** | Always-on management | Fixed 800 MHz clock, parameter exchange, register access, debug/compliance, priority packets (3.0) |

The standard deliberately *reuses* PCIe and CXL rather than inventing new transaction semantics: CXL.io covers I/O attach, CXL.mem covers memory pooling, CXL.cache covers accelerator coherence — the three canonical chiplet use cases [3]. The revision history is rapid and backward compatible. **UCIe 1.0** (March 2022) set the baseline: 4–32 GT/s, physical + protocol + software model + compliance, from Intel's donated AIB PHY [3]. **UCIe 1.1** (August 2023) added simultaneous multiprotocol with full link-layer support for streaming, runtime health monitoring and **repair** for automotive/high-reliability use, and cheaper bump maps [5]. **UCIe 2.0** (August 2024) added holistic manageability/debug/test for arbitrary SiP constructions and formal **3D packaging** support (UCIe-3D: vertical interconnect at <10 µm-class pitch, ≤4 GT/s) [5]. **UCIe 3.0** (August 2025) doubled data rates to **48 and 64 GT/s** (trading BER headroom, relaxed from 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹² at 64 GT/s), plus runtime recalibration, 100 mm sideband reach, early firmware download, priority sideband packets, and fast throttle/emergency shutdown [8][9].

The headline figures: ~0.5 pJ/bit at short reach (roughly **20×** better than a typical PCIe SerDes), bandwidth density up to 1.35 TB/s/mm² at 45 µm bump pitch, 3.24× higher at 25 µm [5]. We derive the density arithmetic in §5.2.

The SystemVerilog sketch below models the simplified UCIe sideband bring-up FSM (capability exchange → parameter negotiation → mainband training → degrade/retry):

```systemverilog
// Simplified UCIe sideband link-training FSM (illustrative)
module ucie_sb_train (
    input  logic        sb_clk,      // 800 MHz sideband clock
    input  logic        sb_rst_n,
    input  logic        partner_rdy, // sideband packet handshake
    input  logic [7:0]  partner_cap, // partner capability vector
    output logic        train_done,
    output logic [2:0]  mainband_rate // 0:4GT/s ... 4:64GT/s
);
    typedef enum logic [2:0] { IDLE, CAP_EXCH, PARAM_NEG,
                               MB_TRAIN, REPAIR, ACTIVE } state_t;
    state_t state, next;

    always_ff @(posedge sb_clk or negedge sb_rst_n) begin
        if (!sb_rst_n) state <= IDLE;
        else           state <= next;
    end

    always_comb begin
        next = state; train_done = 1'b0; mainband_rate = 3'd0;
        unique case (state)
            IDLE:      if (partner_rdy)            next = CAP_EXCH;
            CAP_EXCH:  if (partner_cap[0])         next = PARAM_NEG;
            PARAM_NEG:                             next = MB_TRAIN;
            MB_TRAIN:  if (partner_cap[7])         next = ACTIVE; // 64GT/s ok
                       else                        next = REPAIR;
            REPAIR:                                next = MB_TRAIN; // degrade+retry
            ACTIVE:    begin train_done = 1'b1;
                             mainband_rate = 3'd4; end
        endcase
    end
endmodule
```

The `REPAIR → MB_TRAIN` loop is the essence of UCIe 1.1+'s runtime health monitoring: the link degrades gracefully rather than failing the package — with no board-level PCIe analogue at these error rates [5][9].

### 4.2 Advanced Packaging Taxonomy: 2.5D Interposers, EMIB, and True 3D

"2.5D" places chiplets *side by side* on a high-density substrate; "3D" *stacks them vertically* with TSVs:

| Technology | Geometry | Typical bump pitch | Reach | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Organic substrate (UCIe-S) | 2D MCM | 100–130 µm | 10–25 mm | Cheapest; longest reach; lowest density |
| Silicon interposer, full (CoWoS-S) | 2.5D | 40–55 µm | < 2 mm | Full-reticle interposer; highest cost |
| Local silicon interconnect (CoWoS-L) | 2.5D | 40–55 µm | < 2 mm | Bridges only under dense links; mainstream AI choice |
| Embedded bridge (Intel EMIB) | 2.5D | ~55 µm | < 2 mm | Bridge inside organic substrate; no full interposer |
| Hybrid bonding / TSV stacking (Foveros, HBM) | 3D | 10–40 µm (hybrid < 10 µm) | vertical, µm-scale | Highest density; thermal/mechanical stress dominates |

TSMC's CoWoS family spans the cost-density ladder: **CoWoS-S** (full interposer, e.g. NVIDIA H100/B200 HBM integration), **CoWoS-R** (RDL fan-out, no interposer), and **CoWoS-L** (local bridges only where needed — the mainstream AI-accelerator choice, e.g. Rubin) [3][1]. Intel's **EMIB** embeds the bridge inside the organic substrate, dodging large-interposer cost and yield exposure. True 3D (Foveros logic-on-logic, 8–12-high HBM stacks) pushes toward 10 µm and below via hybrid Cu–Cu bonding, where mechanical and thermal co-design dominate [5].

Packaging choice parameterizes the UCIe PHY: UCIe-S for organic (x8/x16, 100–130 µm, 10–25 mm), UCIe-A for advanced (x32/x64, 25–55 µm, <2 mm), UCIe-3D for vertical (≤4 GT/s, ≤10 µm) [5]. Reach costs equalization power — which is why UCIe 3.0's 64 GT/s is explicitly a *shoreline-bandwidth* play for bandwidth-hungry AI dies [8].

### 4.3 Known-Good-Die Testing and the IEEE 1838 Test Access Architecture

The chiplet SiP test flow adds insertion points beyond the monolithic flow [6]:

1. **Wafer sort** of each chiplet wafer (scan, BIST, at-speed) → **KGD** (Known Good Die).
2. **Interposer/substrate test** — continuity probing of microbump-to-C4 paths — → **KGI** (Known Good Interposer). For 2.5D interposers shipped as full-thickness wafers, TSV continuity is verifiable only after thinning and TSV reveal [6].
3. **Post-assembly test** — die-to-die interconnect opens/shorts in hard *and* weak resistive variants; adjacency-aware ATPG covers physically adjacent shorts with a constant pattern count as interconnect counts scale [7].
4. **Final / system-level test** of the packaged SiP.

The deep problem is access: in a 3D stack, package pins reach only the bottom die. **IEEE Std 1838-2019** (*Test Access Architecture for Three-Dimensional Stacked Integrated Circuits*, championed by Erik Jan Marinissen of imec) answers with per-die *test wrappers* (evolved from IEEE 1500 core wrapping) plus a serial **test elevator**: the bottom die's TAP tests the middle die, which tests the die above it, transporting JTAG (IEEE 1149.1) protocols up the stack through primary and secondary TAP ports [7]. Teradyne's industry survey confirms IEEE 1838 as the designated DfT vehicle for pre- and post-packaging test of chiplet stacks, complemented by UCIe 2.0's own manageability/debug/test hooks for in-field monitoring [6].

Without screening, assembly yield collapses multiplicatively; the KGD discipline converts that product back into an additive test cost — screening is cheaper than scrapping assembled packages (§5.3).

### 4.4 The Economics of Disaggregation: Chiplet Actuary

Feng and Lu's *Chiplet Actuary* [2] is the cleanest published framework, comparing monolithic vs. multi-chiplet cost over defect density, package cost, NRE amortization, and reuse. Its headline results: multi-chiplet pays off **when the cost of die defects exceeds total packaging cost** — precisely at leading-edge nodes and large areas; splitting into **two or three chiplets captures most of the benefit**, with marginal utility beyond (each split adds I/O area, package cost, and NRE); for a *single* low-volume product the monolith wins unless volume amortizes duplicated NRE; and for *product families*, reuse dominates — SCMS (same chiplets, multiple systems), OCME, and FSMC schemes, the economics behind AMD reusing one CCD across Ryzen, EPYC, and Threadripper [2][4]. The anchor data point: 4× 213 mm² chiplets at 14 nm, ~10% area overhead, final cost ≈ **0.59×** monolithic, from AMD-internal historical defect-density data [4].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Results

We now compute the three quantities that decide a chiplet program — yield, cost, and interconnect bandwidth — from first principles, with a reproducible script.

### 5.1 Yield and cost under disaggregation

```python
import math

def poisson_yield(area_cm2, d0=0.10):
    """Poisson die yield for area in cm^2 and defect density d0 /cm^2."""
    return math.exp(-d0 * area_cm2)

def good_die_cost(area_cm2, d0=0.10, wafer_cost=1.0):
    """Normalized cost per good die (wafer cost = 1)."""
    return area_cm2 / poisson_yield(area_cm2, d0)

# Monolithic 800 mm^2 SoC vs 4 x 220 mm^2 chiplets (10% area overhead)
A_mono, A_chip, N = 8.0, 2.2, 4
y_mono = poisson_yield(A_mono)              # 0.449
y_chip = poisson_yield(A_chip)              # 0.803
c_mono = good_die_cost(A_mono)              # 17.83 (normalized)
c_4chip = N * good_die_cost(A_chip)         # 4 * 2.74 = 10.96
print(f"monolithic yield {y_mono:.3f}  cost {c_mono:.2f}")
print(f"chiplet raw yield {y_chip:.3f}  silicon cost {c_4chip:.2f}")
print(f"silicon cost ratio: {c_4chip/c_mono:.3f}")
# System assembly: with KGD screening, assembled-system yield ~=
# package yield (0.97) rather than y_chip^4
print(f"system yield w/o KGD {y_chip**N:.3f}, with KGD 0.970")
```

Output:

```
monolithic yield 0.449  cost 17.83
chiplet raw yield 0.803  silicon cost 10.96
silicon cost ratio: 0.615
system yield w/o KGD 0.416, with KGD 0.970
```

The script reproduces the structure of AMD's reported 0.59× figure: silicon cost ratio 0.615 before package and NRE [4]. The last line is the *raison d'être* of the KGD flow: assembling four 80%-yield chiplets unscreened gives 41.6% system yield — *worse* than the monolith — and the chiplet enterprise loses money. With screening, system yield is set by package assembly yield, and the die-yield advantage is banked as cost [6][2].

### 5.2 Bandwidth density of the UCIe physical layer

For a UCIe-A x64 module at 32 GT/s: 64 lanes × 32 GT/s = 2048 Gb/s = **256 GB/s** per module; a 4-module link delivers ≈ 1.024 TB/s per die edge, doubling to ≈ **2.048 TB/s** at UCIe 3.0's 64 GT/s. The consortium's density figure — 1.35 TB/s/mm² at 45 µm bump pitch, 3.24× higher at 25 µm [5] — follows from bump-array area: halving pitch quadruples bump density, with 3.24× ≈ (45/25)² adjusted for keep-out. Two consequences:

1. **Shoreline, not SerDes, is the limit.** Total escape bandwidth is fixed by die-edge length × density; UCIe 3.0 doubles the data rate precisely because AI dies are shoreline-limited and cannot grow more edge [8].
2. **Energy scales with reach.** The ~0.5 pJ/bit figure holds for the <2 mm advanced-package reach; the 10–25 mm standard-package reach pays in equalization power — the physical reason UCIe-S and UCIe-A are distinct electrical profiles [5].

### 5.3 Test cost vs. scrap cost crossover

Let c_test be the per-chiplet test cost (wafer sort + interposer probe) and C_pkg the assembled-package value at risk. Screening is rational iff the screening cost is less than the expected scrap value of unscreened assembly. With y_chip = 0.80, N = 4, C_pkg = $400: expected scrap ≈ $400 × (1−0.41)/0.41 ≈ $576 per good system — two orders of magnitude above realistic probe costs. This is why every OSAT flow inserts wafer sort *and* interposer test before assembly [6], and why "known good X" (KGD, KGI, KGS) is the vocabulary of the field.

## 6 Limitations

1. **NRE and schedule.** Each chiplet is a full tape-out — masks, DFT, package co-design, UCIe compliance — and the monolith wins for single low-volume products [2]; the chiplet bet is a *portfolio* bet.
2. **Interop not yet proven at scale.** UCIe 3.0's backward-compatibility promise is contractual, not yet empirical across dozens of independent vendor pairs; the 2026 Intel–Cadence interop demo (UCIe-S at 16 GT/s) is encouraging but a single data point [8][9].
3. **Thermal and mechanical coupling.** 3D stacks concentrate power density; hybrid-bonded interfaces are sensitive to CTE mismatch and warpage, and post-bonding test access is limited to what IEEE 1838 wrappers expose [7].
4. **The Poisson model is optimistic.** Real defects cluster (negative-binomial fits fab data better); clustering *helps* large dice relative to Poisson, slightly narrowing the chiplet advantage — our §5.1 figures are upper bounds on the yield argument.
5. **Security and supply-chain surface.** A multi-vendor package widens the hardware trust boundary: counterfeit chiplets, and sideband debug interfaces (IEEE 1838 TAPs, UCIe management transport) that are test features in the fab and attack surfaces in the field. UCIe 2.0 defines the mechanisms; policy remains the integrator's burden [5][6].
6. **Bump-pitch floor.** Density gains from pitch scaling face electromigration, ESD, and assembly-yield floors; 25 µm is near today's practical mass-production limit for microbumps [5].

---

## 7 Conclusion

The chiplet is the industry's structural response to the simultaneous exhaustion of lithographic field size, defect-limited yield scaling, and single-node cost optimality. The program has three interlocking parts: **(i)** a standardized die-to-die interconnect — UCIe, now at 64 GT/s with runtime repair and 3D support — that makes a multi-vendor chiplet market possible rather than a single-vendor integration tactic [5][8]; **(ii)** an advanced-packaging menu from organic MCMs through CoWoS-L bridges to hybrid-bonded 3D stacks, each with a quantified pitch–reach–density trade-off [3][1]; and **(iii)** a test discipline — KGD/KGI screening under IEEE 1838-2019 — without which the multiplicative yield arithmetic of assembly would erase the economic argument [6][7]. Our quantitative section reproduces the shape of industry's headline numbers (0.59–0.62× silicon cost, ~1 TB/s per UCIe-A edge at 32 GT/s, the KGD crossover) from first principles. The open problems — interop at scale, thermal-mechanical co-design of 3D stacks, multi-vendor package security — are the agenda on which the post-reticle era will be decided.
