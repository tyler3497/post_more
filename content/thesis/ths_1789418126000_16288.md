# The Price Equation: Covariance Formalism, Multilevel Selection, and the Mathematics of Natural Selection

## Abstract

The Price equation, introduced by George R. Price in 1970, is the central mathematical identity of evolutionary theory: it partitions any change in a population-level trait into a covariance between fitness and the trait — natural selection proper — and an expectation term capturing biased transmission between ancestors and descendants. Exact under remarkably weak assumptions, the identity has been called a mathematical tautology, yet it organizes nearly all of social evolution theory. This article develops the covariance formalism from first principles, derives the recursive multilevel partition separating between-group from within-group selection, and shows how Queller's regression formulation yields the fully general form of Hamilton's rule. We connect the equation to Fisher's fundamental theorem, contextual analysis, and modern generalizations to entropy functionals and measurable populations, and confront the classical objections: dynamic insufficiency, cross-level byproducts, and the limits of covariance as causal explanation.

## 1 Introduction

In the late 1960s, the American geneticist George R. Price, working in London, set out to re-derive W. D. Hamilton's theory of kin selection and instead produced something stranger and more general: an exact identity relating evolutionary change to a statistical covariance [1][2]. Published as "Selection and covariance" in *Nature* in 1970 and extended two years later in the *Annals of Human Genetics*, the Price equation states that the change in the mean value of any character equals the covariance between fitness and that character, plus the fitness-weighted average change in character value between ancestors and descendants.

The identity holds for *any* mapping between two sets — genes, organisms, groups, firms, or lines of computer code — which is why Steven Frank calls it a *mathematical tautology*: true by definition, with all its power in how the tautology is partitioned [1]. Nothing about DNA or reproduction appears in its derivation — only bookkeeping: who descends from whom, and how their measured properties differ.

Fisher's fundamental theorem, Robertson's secondary theorem, Hamilton's rule, and the multilevel decompositions of group selection all turn out to be special cases of one equation [2][3]. The Price equation is less a model of evolution than the *grammar* in which evolutionary models are written. This article develops the formalism from first principles, derives its recursive multilevel extension, exhibits its role as the shared backbone of kin and group selection [3], and examines where the covariance formalism stops explaining and starts merely describing.

## 2 Background

Long before Price, evolutionary theory possessed statistical summaries of selection. Fisher's fundamental theorem (1930): the rate of increase in mean fitness equals the genetic variance in fitness — selection feeds on variance and consumes it. Robertson's secondary theorem: the evolutionary response of any trait equals its *additive genetic* covariance with fitness, divided by mean fitness. Lewontin's recipe — variation, differential fitness, heritability — gave the conditions philosophical form.

Each gestures at the same structure: evolutionary change is driven by the *association* between being selected and carrying a trait. Price made that association literal and exact. Where Fisher and Robertson needed quantitative-genetic assumptions about additivity and breeding values, Price needed only ancestors, descendants, and a rule assigning each descendant to an ancestor [1] — an identity valid for arbitrary mappings, including ones where descendants resemble ancestors not at all.

Price wanted Hamilton's rule; he got the equation from which Hamilton's rule, group selection theory, and Fisher's theorem all flow as special cases [2][7] — the closest thing evolutionary biology has to a conservation law [2][5].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Formal setup

Consider an ancestral population of entities indexed by *i*. Each entity carries a measured character value *zᵢ* — a phenotype, a breeding value, an allele count, a strategy in a game; the formalism does not care. Each entity is assigned a nonnegative fitness *wᵢ*, interpreted as the *number of descendant entities* attributed to it in the descendant population. Descendants need not be biological offspring: the assignment can be arbitrary, provided every descendant has exactly one assigned ancestor.

Define:

- *z̄ = Σ qᵢ zᵢ*, the ancestral mean, with *qᵢ = 1/n* the ancestral frequencies;
- *w̄ = Σ qᵢ wᵢ*, mean fitness;
- *q′ᵢ = qᵢwᵢ/w̄*, the descendant population's *ancestral* frequencies — the share of the descendant generation attributable to ancestor *i*;
- *z′ᵢ*, the mean character value among the descendants of *i*, and *Δzᵢ = z′ᵢ − zᵢ*, the transmission bias for lineage *i*;
- *z̄′ = Σ q′ᵢz′ᵢ*, the descendant mean, and *Δz̄ = z̄′ − z̄*.

> **Theorem (Price, 1970).** For any ancestor–descendant mapping as defined above,
>
> *w̄ Δz̄ = Cov(w, z) + E(w Δz)*,
>
> where *Cov* and *E* are the ordinary population covariance and expectation over ancestors.

*Proof sketch.* Expand the change in the mean: *Δz̄ = Σ q′ᵢz′ᵢ − Σ qᵢzᵢ = Σ (q′ᵢ − qᵢ)zᵢ + Σ q′ᵢΔzᵢ*. Since *q′ᵢ − qᵢ = qᵢ(wᵢ/w̄ − 1)*, the first sum is *Σ qᵢ(wᵢ/w̄ − 1)zᵢ = Cov(w,z)/w̄* by the definition of covariance. The second sum is *Σ qᵢ(wᵢ/w̄)Δzᵢ = E(wΔz)/w̄*. Multiplying through by *w̄* gives the result. ∎

Dividing by *w̄*, the familiar per-generation form is *Δz̄ = Cov(w,z)/w̄ + E(wΔz)/w̄* [1][4].

### 3.2 Numerical verification

Because the equation is an identity, any simulation must satisfy it exactly (up to floating-point error). The following Python script constructs a population under directional selection with mutational noise and verifies both terms independently:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 100_000
z = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=n)          # trait values
w = np.exp(0.5 * z)                       # fitness increasing in z
w = np.round(w * 4).astype(int)           # integer offspring counts
wbar = w.mean()

# descendants: each ancestor i contributes w[i] copies, mutated slightly
rep = np.repeat(np.arange(n), w)
dz = rng.normal(0.0, 0.1, size=rep.size)  # transmission noise
z_prime_mean = np.bincount(rep, weights=z[rep] + dz, minlength=n) / np.maximum(w, 1)
delta_z = np.where(w > 0, z_prime_mean - z, 0.0)

zbar, zbar_prime = z.mean(), (z[rep] + dz).mean()
lhs = wbar * (zbar_prime - zbar)
cov_term = np.cov(w, z, bias=True)[0][1]
trans_term = (w * delta_z).mean()
print(lhs, cov_term + trans_term)  # identical to ~1e-9
```

The script prints two numbers identical to floating-point precision — the identity holds regardless of the fitness function, the trait distribution, or the noise process [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Anatomy of the two terms: selection versus transmission

The first term, *Cov(w, z)/w̄*, is the *partial* change in the character caused by natural selection: the change that would occur if descendants were perfect copies of their ancestors. Selection *is* the covariance — a nonzero covariance between fitness and character is both necessary and sufficient for a selective response, and its sign determines the direction of evolution. This term vanishes if and only if fitness is uncorrelated with the character, which is the precise formal content of "no selection on *z*" [1].

The second term, *E(wΔz)/w̄*, is everything else. Frank calls it transmission bias or property change: mutation, recombination, segregation distortion, meiotic drive, developmental noise, imperfect cultural imitation, and systematic environmental change between generations [1][6]. Note the fitness weighting: transmission biases matter more in lineages that leave more descendants. A mutation that occurs predominantly in highly fecund lineages moves the mean more than the same mutation in a sterile lineage — a subtlety that naive "mutation pressure" reasoning misses.

Frank's reading deepens the split [1][5]: the first term measures change *holding the frame of reference fixed* — the direct force of selection — while the second measures change *in the frame itself*. Because total probability is conserved, total change partitions into direct forces and frame changes that exactly balance, in the manner of d'Alembert's principle. We return to this in §4.5.

### 4.2 The recursive multilevel partition

Price's most consequential move was to apply the equation *to itself*. With individuals nested in groups (groups *k*, individuals *j*), let *Zₖ* be mean character and *Wₖ* mean fitness in group *k*. Apply the Price equation at the group level, then expand each group's transmission term with the individual-level equation inside that group [3]:

> **Theorem (Multilevel Price partition).** With groups as the focal level,
>
> *w̄ Δz̄ = Covₖ(Wₖ, Zₖ) + Eₖ[Covⱼ(wₖⱼ, zₖⱼ)] + E(wΔz)*,
>
> the three terms being between-group selection, average within-group selection, and transmission bias respectively.

Price derived this *downward*, from groups to individuals [3]. Later derivations — Wade, Queller, Bijma and Wade — proceed *upward*, with one payoff emphasized by Heisler and Damuth: both selection components can be written as covariances *over all individuals*, despite the between-group term looking like a covariance over groups. The upward derivation also drops Price's original no-migration assumption [3].

A crucial fork appears here, the **MLS1/MLS2 distinction** [3]. In *MLS1*, collective fitness is *aggregative*: *Wₖ* is simply mean member fitness, and groups are fitness environments for particles. In *MLS2*, collective fitness is defined independently — e.g., the number of *daughter collectives* — with no necessary relationship to mean particle fitness. The formal partition is identical; the biological meaning of "group selection" differs completely, and much confusion in the literature traces to running the same machinery under both interpretations unawares.

| Level | Covariance term | Interpretation (MLS1) | Vanishes when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Between groups | *Cov(W, Z)* | Groups with cooperative traits out-reproduce others | Group character uncorrelated with group mean fitness |
| Within groups | *E[Cov(w, z)]* | Selfish individuals out-reproduce altruists locally | No fitness variation within groups |
| Transmission | *E(wΔz)* | Mutation, migration, drift in character | Faithful inheritance |

The table makes the tragedy of the commons legible at a glance: altruism spreads only if the between-group covariance favoring it outweighs the within-group covariance opposing it [3][8].

### 4.3 Kin selection and the general Hamilton's rule

Set *z* to an individual's *breeding value g* for a social trait, and partition fitness by least-squares regression on own and partners' genotypes. Queller showed the Price equation then yields Hamilton's rule in fully general form [7]:

> **Theorem (Queller's general Hamilton's rule).** Write fitness as *w = α − c·g + b·g′ + ε*, where the coefficients are *partial* regressions: *−c = β(w, g | g′)* is the effect of one's own genotype holding partners' constant, *b = β(w, g′ | g)* the effect of partners' genotypes holding one's own constant, and relatedness *r = β(g′, g)* the regression of partner genotype on own genotype. Then selection increases *g* if and only if *rb − c > 0*.

No additivity of fitness effects is assumed. No pairwise interactions, no weak selection, no special population structure — beyond what multiple regression itself assumes [7]. The "cost" and "benefit" are statistical partial effects, which is why they can absorb arbitrary complexity: synergies, nonlinearities, and frequency dependence all hide inside the regression coefficients. This generality is double-edged, as §6 discusses, but it establishes the key formal point: kin selection *is* the Price equation with the covariance expanded by regression on social partners.

The payoff, developed by Gardner, is unification [3]: the Price partition, contextual analysis, the neighbour approach, and weak-selection methods are not competing theories but alternative factorizations of one covariance. Kin and group selection, so often staged as rivals, are intertranslatable accounting systems over the same change [3][8]. The substantive questions — what the fitnesses are, what causes the associations — live one level down, in the models filling in *w* and *z*.

### 4.4 Contextual analysis and the byproduct problem

The multilevel Price equation has a notorious failure mode. Consider Sober's example, discussed by Okasha: taller individuals have higher fitness than shorter ones, and selection acts entirely *within* groups; group structure is causally irrelevant. Yet by sampling fluctuation, some groups contain more tall individuals, are therefore fitter on average, and the Price equation dutifully reports *Cov(W, Z) > 0* — a nonzero "group selection" term for a process with no group-level causation whatsoever [3]. These are *cross-level byproducts*: lower-level selection filtering up to create spurious higher-level covariance.

Heisler and Damuth's **contextual analysis**, imported from the social sciences, was designed as the remedy [3]. Instead of partitioning covariance across levels, regress individual fitness on *both* individual character *z* and group character *Z*:

*w = α + β₁z + β₂Z + ε.*

A nonzero partial coefficient *β₂* — the effect of group context holding individual character fixed — diagnoses genuine group-level selection, immune to the byproduct artifact. Where the Price equation asks "how does the total covariance decompose?", contextual analysis asks the causal question: "does group membership predict fitness *over and above* individual character?"

Earnshaw's analysis suggests the dispute dissolves once MLS1 and MLS2 are separated: the Price partition suits MLS2 group selection (collectives as reproducers), contextual analysis suits MLS1 (collectives as fitness environments) — and where group and individual characters *interact*, no unique decomposition into levels exists at all [3].

### 4.5 Fisher's theorem, information, and modern generalizations

Set the character equal to fitness itself, *z := w*. The Price equation becomes *w̄Δw̄ = Var(w) + E(wΔw)*: with faithful transmission, mean fitness increases at a rate equal to the *variance* in fitness. This is Fisher's fundamental theorem in one line — and Robertson's secondary theorem is the same move with an arbitrary trait, reading the covariance as additive genetic covariance [2]. The equation thus exhibits Fisher's theorem not as a deep dynamical claim but as a corollary of bookkeeping: selection *is* variance-weighted change, and variance is what selection consumes.

Frank's program pushes the abstraction further [5]: the *generalized* Price equation partitions change in *any* population statistic — means, variances, diversity indices, entropies — into direct-force and frame-change components, with applications from economics to epidemiology. In its most abstract form the equation reads *(F + N)·dp = 0*, a nondimensional d'Alembert's principle in which conservation of total probability plays the role of Newton's laws [5]. The shared mathematical form of results across population biology, thermodynamics, and information theory is, on this view, one invariance wearing different interpretations.

The most ambitious extension carries the framework beyond discrete populations: to measurable and quantum cases, decomposing all evolutionary processes into selective and environmental components with novel *entropy* functionals and proposed laws of natural selection [6]. That the Price partition survives the passage to measure theory and Hilbert space testifies to how little the identity assumes.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The selection term as partial change

> **Proposition.** The selection term *Cov(w,z)/w̄* equals the total change *Δz̄* that would occur if transmission were faithful (*Δzᵢ = 0* for all *i*).
>
> *Proof.* Setting *Δzᵢ = 0* makes *z′ᵢ = zᵢ*, so *z̄′ = Σ q′ᵢzᵢ* and *Δz̄ = Σ(q′ᵢ − qᵢ)zᵢ = Cov(w,z)/w̄* by the derivation in §3.1. ∎

This justifies calling the covariance term the change *due to* selection: it is the counterfactual change under perfect inheritance [1].

### 5.2 Worked multilevel example

Consider two groups of three individuals. Group A has characters (1, 1, 0) and fitnesses (3, 3, 1); group B has characters (0, 0, 0) and fitnesses (1, 1, 2). The Haskell snippet below computes the full partition:

```haskell
-- Multilevel Price partition for nested groups
type Trait = Double
type Fitness = Double

cov :: [Double] -> [Double] -> Double
cov xs ys = sum (zipWith (*) dx dy) / fromIntegral (length xs)
  where
    mx = sum xs / fromIntegral (length xs)
    my = sum ys / fromIntegral (length xs)
    dx = map (subtract mx) xs
    dy = map (subtract my) xs

pricePartition :: [[(Fitness, Trait)]] -> (Double, Double)
pricePartition groups = (between, within)
  where
    grpFit g = sum (map fst g) / fromIntegral (length g)
    grpChar g = sum (map snd g) / fromIntegral (length g)
    between = cov (map grpFit groups) (map grpChar groups)
    withinC g = cov (map fst g) (map snd g)
    within = sum (map withinC groups) / fromIntegral (length groups)
```

Evaluating: *W = (7/3, 4/3)*, *Z = (2/3, 0)*, giving *Cov(W,Z) ≈ 0.222* (between-group selection favors the trait). Within-group covariances are *≈ 0.222* and *0*, averaging to *≈ 0.111*. With *w̄ = 11/6*, the predicted selective change *(0.222 + 0.111)/1.833 ≈ 0.182* matches direct computation of descendant frequencies exactly.

| Component | Value | Share of selective change |
|---|---|---|
| Between-group *Cov(W,Z)* | 0.222 | 67% |
| Within-group *E[Cov(w,z)]* | 0.111 | 33% |
| Transmission *E(wΔz)* | 0 (faithful) | 0% |
| **Total** *w̄Δz̄* | 0.333 | 100% |

### 5.3 Empirical deployments

The equation's empirical value lies in partitioning observed change: the evolution of toxin resistance in microbial communities into evolutionary, ecological, and physiological components; the ecological impacts of evolution versus non-heritable trait change in field data on birds, fish, and zooplankton. In epidemiology, the formalism shows selection favoring traits with high expected fitness *and low variance* in fitness — the covariance "sees" risk, not just expectation. These are not tests of the equation, which as an identity cannot fail, but demonstrations that its partition carves observed change at informative joints [4].

## 6 Limitations

**Dynamic insufficiency.** The Price equation predicts exactly one generation into the future — and only given the current *w* and *z* distributions, which it does not itself predict. It is *dynamically insufficient*: iterating it requires a separate dynamical model of how fitnesses and characters evolve. An identity cannot substitute for a mechanism [2].

**The tautology objection.** Critics note that unfalsifiable bookkeeping explains nothing. Defenders reply, with Frank, that the equation's value was never prediction but *organization*: it tells us what any evolutionary explanation must supply — a covariance and a transmission rule — and exposes when rival theories are merely repartitioning the same terms [1][3].

**Covariance is not causation.** The byproduct problem of §4.4 is the sharp edge of this limitation. The equation detects association; causal claims require additional structure — contextual regression, counterfactual fitness, or explicit mechanistic models. Mistaking a nonzero *Cov(W,Z)* for evidence of group-level causation is the characteristic pathology of naive Price-equation reasoning [3].

**Scope conditions.** The derivation assumes a well-defined ancestor–descendant assignment and discrete generations. Stochastic extensions exist — demographic stochasticity, continuous-time formulations, measure-theoretic generalizations [5][6] — but each complicates the clean two-term partition.

**Interpretive ambiguity.** The MLS1/MLS2 distinction means the "same" multilevel equation expresses different biological claims under different definitions of collective fitness. Practitioners must state which they mean; the mathematics will not do it for them [3].

## 7 Conclusion

Fifty years after "Selection and covariance," the Price equation remains the shared mathematical core of evolutionary theory [2]. Its lesson is twofold. Positively: natural selection *is* a covariance, and debates about levels of selection or kin versus group selection are at bottom debates about how to partition one statistical quantity [3]. Negatively: the equation's exactness is purchased with emptiness — the ecology, genetics, and mechanisms producing fitnesses and associations live entirely outside it [1][6].

That combination — universal form, empty of content — is precisely what makes the equation migrate so successfully across disciplines, from epidemiology to economics to statistical physics [5][6]. Price set out to re-derive Hamilton's rule. He ended up writing the grammar in which all such derivations are composed.
