# Conflict-Driven Clause Learning: Implication Graphs, Clause Minimization, and Certified UNSAT via DRAT Proofs

## Abstract

Conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL) is the dominant algorithmic paradigm in modern propositional satisfiability (SAT) solving, underpinning the solvers that verify microprocessors, synthesize hardware, and discharge proof obligations in software verification at industrial scale. This thesis presents a self-contained treatment of CDCL, beginning with the DPLL procedure and unit propagation, and proceeding through the implication-graph construction, the first unique implication point (1UIP) learning scheme, non-chronological backjumping, the VSIDS decision heuristic, geometric and Luby restarts, and the two-watched-literal propagation engine. A deep dive analyzes learned-clause minimization, literals-blocks-distance (LBD) scoring with permanent glue clauses, certified inprocessing, and the deletion-aware resolution asymmetric tautology (DRAT) proof format that certifies unsatisfiability claims. We survey the proof-complexity landscape—CDCL's equivalence with general resolution and the separation from DPLL—and report empirical structure on benchmark families from SAT competitions. We close with limitations of the paradigm and open questions in proof-optimal clause management.

## 1. Introduction

The Boolean satisfiability problem—deciding whether a propositional formula in conjunctive normal form (CNF) admits a satisfying assignment—is the canonical NP-complete problem, yet modern SAT solvers routinely decide instances with millions of variables and clauses arising from bounded model checking, equivalence checking, and planning. The gap between worst-case complexity and practical tractability is bridged by a family of algorithmic ideas collectively known as *conflict-driven clause learning* (CDCL).

The lineage is instructive. The Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland (DPLL) procedure introduced systematic backtracking search with unit propagation [2]. GRASP (1996, journal version 1999) added the decisive innovation: when search reaches a conflict, *analyze the conflict* to learn a clause that records its cause, and backtrack non-chronologically to the decision level responsible for it [1]. Chaff (2001) supplied the engineering breakthroughs—VSIDS, two-watched literals, restarts—that made CDCL scale to industrial instances [3]. MiniSat (2003) distilled the design into a compact, extensible reference implementation whose clause-minimization and restart disciplines remain influential [4]. Glucose (2009) introduced the LBD metric and aggressive clause deletion, winning multiple competition medals [5]. Most recently, the DRAT proof format (2013–2014) made every unsatisfiability claim *certifiable* by an independent, formally verified checker [6][7].

> **Thesis statement:** CDCL derives its power from a tight feedback loop—propagation produces conflicts, conflict analysis produces learned clauses and non-chronological backjumps, and proof logging produces machine-checkable certificates—whose individual components are now understood well enough to be taught as a coherent engineering discipline, while their joint optimality remains an active research frontier.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews DPLL, unit propagation, and the resolution proof system. Section 3 presents the CDCL methodology: the main loop, 1UIP learning, VSIDS, restarts, and two-watched literals. Section 4 deep-dives into clause minimization, LBD scoring and glue clauses, inprocessing, and DRAT certification. Section 5 covers the proof-complexity characterization and empirical behavior. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The DPLL procedure

A CNF formula $\varphi$ is a conjunction of *clauses*, each a disjunction of *literals* (a variable or its negation). DPLL decides satisfiability of $\varphi$ by depth-first search over a partial assignment (the *trail*), alternating two operations:

1. **Decide:** pick an unassigned variable and assign it a truth value (a *decision*), opening a new *decision level*.
2. **Propagate:** repeatedly assign any literal forced by a *unit clause*—a clause with all literals falsified except one. This is *unit propagation* (UP), also called Boolean constraint propagation (BCP).

If a clause becomes entirely falsified, a *conflict* occurs and DPLL backtracks to the most recent decision, flipping its value. DPLL is sound and complete: it terminates with SAT or UNSAT. Its weakness is memorylessness—every conflict is forgotten, so the same sub-conflict can be rediscovered exponentially many times.

### 2.2 Unit propagation and the implication graph

Unit propagation is the engine of every modern solver. For each propagated literal $\ell$, the solver records a *reason*: the clause $C$ that was unit under the current trail and forced $\ell$. This causality structure is the **implication graph**: a directed acyclic graph whose nodes are assigned literals (decisions and propagations) annotated with decision levels, and whose edges run from each literal of $C \setminus \{\ell\}$ to $\ell$ for the reason clause $C$. A conflict node $\kappa$ represents a clause falsified under the trail; its predecessors are the literals that falsified it.

The implication graph is the object on which all conflict analysis operates. A *cut* separating the decision literals from $\kappa$ corresponds to a clause implied by the formula; learning amounts to choosing the cut wisely.

### 2.3 The resolution proof system

A *resolution* step derives $C_1 \cup C_2 \setminus \{x, \bar{x}\}$ from clauses $C_1 \ni x$ and $C_2 \ni \bar{x}$. A *resolution refutation* of $\varphi$ is a sequence of clauses ending in the empty clause $\bot$, each clause either from $\varphi$ or derived by resolution. Resolution is sound and complete for unsatisfiability, and it is the canonical yardstick for solver power: a CDCL run with conflict analysis can be unwound into a resolution proof, and proof-complexity theory studies precisely which families admit short proofs [8].

| Proof system | Sound & complete | P-simulates CDCL? | Separates DPLL? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tree-like resolution | Yes | No (weaker) | Baseline |
| General resolution | Yes | Yes (CDCL ≡ resolution) | Exponential gap known |
| Extended resolution | Yes | Strictly stronger | Open for CDCL variants |

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The CDCL main loop

The CDCL loop augments DPLL with learning, non-chronological backtracking, and restarts:

```python
def cdcl(phi):
    trail, dl = [], 0                      # trail, decision level
    while True:
        conflict = propagate(phi, trail)   # unit propagation (2-watched)
        if conflict is not None:
            if dl == 0:
                return "UNSAT"             # toplevel conflict
            learnt, btlevel = analyze(conflict, trail)  # 1UIP
            add_learned_clause(phi, learnt)
            backjump(trail, btlevel); dl = btlevel
            if restart_due(): restart(trail); dl = 0
        elif all_assigned(phi, trail):
            return "SAT"
        else:
            v = vsids_pick(phi, trail)     # decision heuristic
            dl += 1
            assign(trail, v, phase_save(v), dl, reason=None)
```

Every conflict either yields a learned clause and a backjump or, at decision level 0, a certified UNSAT verdict.

### 3.2 The 1UIP learning scheme

Given the implication graph and conflict node $\kappa$, conflict analysis resolves the conflict clause backwards through the reason clauses of propagated literals at the *current* decision level, in reverse trail order. A **unique implication point (UIP)** is a node at the current level that dominates $\kappa$—every path from the level's decision literal to $\kappa$ passes through it. The **first UIP (1UIP)** is the UIP closest to $\kappa$.

```python
def analyze_1uip(conflict_clause, trail):
    learnt, seen = set(), set()
    counter, p = 0, None
    clause = conflict_clause
    for lit in reversed(trail):            # walk back through the level
        for q in clause:
            if level(q) == current_level and q not in seen:
                seen.add(q); counter += 1
        if lit in seen:
            counter -= 1
            if counter == 0:               # first UIP found
                p = lit; break
            clause = resolve(clause, reason(lit), lit)
    learnt = {negate(l) for l in clause}
    btlevel = max(level(l) for l in learnt if l != negate(p))
    return learnt, btlevel
```

The learned clause $\neg(\text{cut})$ has exactly one literal from the current decision level—the *asserting literal* $\bar{p}$—so after backjumping to `btlevel`, it is unit and immediately propagates $\bar{p}$. This *asserting* property is why 1UIP dominates alternatives empirically: learning is never wasted on a clause that fails to drive the search forward. The backjump level is the maximum decision level among the remaining literals, yielding *non-chronological backtracking*: the solver can leap over many irrelevant decisions at once [1][3].

### 3.3 VSIDS and phase saving

The **VSIDS** (Variable State Independent Decaying Sum) heuristic maintains an *activity* score per variable [3]:

- Initially 0. On each conflict, bump the activity of every variable appearing in the learned clause (or on the conflict side of the implication graph) by 1.
- Periodically *decay*: multiply all activities by a constant $0 < \rho < 1$ (e.g., $0.95$), so recent conflicts dominate.
- Decide on the unassigned variable of highest activity; tie-break by *phase saving* (reuse the variable's last assigned polarity), which empirically avoids re-exploring symmetric subtrees after restarts.

VSIDS is cheap—amortized near-constant per conflict—and adaptively focuses search on variables implicated in recent conflicts, a property later formalized as maximizing the global learning rate.

### 3.4 Restarts

A *restart* discards the current trail (keeping all learned clauses) and resumes from decision level 0. Counterintuitively, this *helps*: it prevents the solver from becoming trapped in a barren region of the search space and, combined with VSIDS and phase saving, implements a form of iterative deepening. Restart schedules include:

- **Geometric:** restart after $r, r\cdot\alpha, r\cdot\alpha^2, \dots$ conflicts (MiniSat uses $\alpha = 1.5$) [4].
- **Luby:** the universal sequence $1,1,2,1,1,2,4,\dots$, optimal up to constants for Las Vegas algorithms.

Restarts are not merely an engineering hack: proof-complexity results show that CDCL *with* restarts p-simulates general resolution, while CDCL *without* restarts provably cannot [8].

### 3.5 Two-watched literals

Unit propagation dominates runtime, so its data structure is critical. The **two-watched-literal** scheme [3] watches, per clause, only *two* literals:

- A clause is examined during propagation only when one of its watched literals is falsified.
- On falsification, the solver seeks a non-falsified replacement; if none exists and the other watch is unassigned, the clause is unit; if both watches are falsified, conflict.
- On backtrack, watches are *never moved*: watched literals that remain assigned keep their watches, so backtracking is O(1) per clause rather than O(clause length).

This laziness is what lets solvers propagate through million-clause databases at millions of propagations per second.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Implication graphs, cuts, and why 1UIP wins

Every learned clause corresponds to a cut in the implication graph separating decision literals from $\kappa$. Candidate schemes include the *decision* scheme (cut adjacent to decisions), *rel_sat*, and *all-UIP*. Empirically, 1UIP learns the *shortest, most asserting* clauses: the 1UIP cut is the unique minimal cut closest to the conflict, and its asserting literal guarantees immediate unit propagation after backjump. Decision-scheme clauses, while logically stronger, are long and rarely become unit, so they bloat the database without guiding search. The community converged on 1UIP after extensive benchmarking in the Chaff and MiniSat lineages [3][4].

> **Theorem (asserting clauses drive search):** Let $C$ be a 1UIP learned clause with asserting literal $\ell$ at backjump level $b$. After backjumping to $b$, $C$ is unit and forces $\ell$. Hence every conflict analysis produces a clause that is immediately productive.

### 4.2 Clause minimization

Learned clauses routinely contain redundant literals—literals whose removal preserves the clause's logical consequences. **Clause minimization** [4] applies self-subsuming resolution during analysis: for each literal $\ell$ in the learned clause (other than the asserting literal), check whether $\neg\ell$'s reason clause chain implies $\ell$ is redundant, i.e., whether resolving $\ell$ away is subsumed by the remaining clause. MiniSat implements this in two tiers:

1. **Local (binary) minimization:** if $\ell$'s reason is a binary clause whose other literal is already in the learned clause (or at a lower level), drop $\ell$.
2. **Recursive minimization:** DFS through the implication graph; $\ell$ is redundant if all its reason's literals are already marked in the learned clause.

Empirically, minimization shrinks learned clauses by 30–40% on average and is nearly free, since it reuses the `seen` marks from analysis. Smaller clauses propagate more often and subsume more—minimization is one of the highest ROI optimizations in the CDCL pipeline.

### 4.3 LBD scoring and glue clauses

Not all learned clauses deserve to live forever: without deletion, propagation slows to a crawl even when memory is plentiful [9]. Glucose's insight was a *static quality predictor* [5]:

> **Definition (LBD):** The *Literal Block Distance* of a clause $C$ is the number of distinct decision levels among its literals: $\mathrm{LBD}(C) = |\{\,\mathrm{level}(\ell) : \ell \in C\,\}|$.

Clauses with $\mathrm{LBD}(C) \le 2$ are **glue clauses**: they connect literals from at most two decision levels, acting as "glue" between otherwise independent blocks of the search. Glucose *never deletes* glue clauses and aggressively deletes high-LBD clauses (its `reduceDB` keeps only the better half, with the database cap growing slowly). The result was striking: Glucose won gold medals in the SAT competitions, and LBD is now the standard clause-quality metric, often combined with activity-based schemes in hybrid deletion policies [5].

| LBD range | Interpretation | Policy in Glucose |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Glue clause | Kept permanently |
| 3–6 | Good | Retained across several reduceDB rounds |
| > 6 | Low quality | First deleted |

### 4.4 Inprocessing: simplifying during search

Classical solvers *preprocess* once, then search. Modern solvers **inprocess**: they interleave simplification with search, because learned clauses and partial assignments expose new simplification opportunities. Certified inprocessing techniques include [6]:

- **Clause vivification:** re-propagate each learned clause's literals; literals implied by the rest are redundant and removed.
- **Variable elimination** (bounded): replace $x$ by resolving all clauses containing $x$ with all containing $\bar{x}$, when the resolvent set is smaller.
- **Subsumption and self-subsuming resolution:** drop clauses subsumed by shorter ones.
- **Blocked clause elimination:** remove clauses whose every resolvent on some literal is a tautology.

Every inprocessing step must preserve satisfiability *and* be expressible in the proof log—otherwise the final UNSAT certificate would be invalid. This is precisely what the DRAT format enables: any satisfiability-preserving transformation with the RAT property can be logged.

### 4.5 Certified UNSAT via DRAT proofs

For safety-critical applications (hardware verification, certified compilation), "the solver said UNSAT" is not evidence. The **DRAT** (Deletion-aware Resolution Asymmetric Tautology) format makes UNSAT claims independently checkable [6][7]:

> **Definition (RAT):** A clause $C$ has the *resolution asymmetric tautology* property w.r.t. a literal $x \in C$ and formula $F$ if, for every clause $D \in F$ containing $\bar{x}$, unit propagation on $F \land \lnot((D \setminus \{\bar{x}\}) \lor (C \setminus \{x\}))$ derives a conflict. Adding a RAT clause preserves satisfiability.

A DRAT proof is a sequence of clause additions (each RAT or RUP—reverse unit propagation) and clause *deletions* (lines prefixed `d`), ending with the empty clause. Deletion lines are crucial: they let the checker discard clauses, keeping memory bounded. **DRAT-trim** checks proofs *backwards* from $\bot$, marking only clauses that participate in the refutation and emitting a trimmed core plus an optimized proof [6]. The checker itself has been *formally verified* in ACL2/Isabelle (the `cake_lpr` and verified LRAT checkers), so the trusted computing base shrinks to a few thousand lines of verified code [7].

```python
def drat_check(formula, proof):
    F = set(formula)
    for step in proof:
        if step.is_deletion():
            F.discard(step.clause)
        else:
            C = step.clause
            if not (is_rup(F, C) or is_rat(F, C)):
                return "REJECTED"
            F.add(C)
    return "VERIFIED" if EMPTY_CLAUSE in F else "REJECTED"
```

Every major solver now emits DRAT by default, and SAT competitions *require* proofs for UNSAT verdicts—an unprecedented standard of accountability in automated reasoning.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Proof complexity: CDCL versus resolution

The central theoretical result is the characterization of CDCL's power:

- **Buss, Hoffmann, and Johannsen (2004)** showed that clause learning can yield *exponentially* shorter proofs than DPLL on families like the pigeonhole principle—learning is provably more than an optimization [8].
- **Pipatsrisawat and Darwiche (2011)** proved that CDCL with 1UIP learning, non-chronological backtracking, and restarts *p-simulates general resolution*: any resolution refutation can be mimicked by a CDCL run with only polynomial overhead. In this precise sense, CDCL *is* resolution, mechanized.
- **Atserias, Fichte, and Thurley (2011)** proved the converse limitation: CDCL *without* restarts does not even simulate regular resolution—restarts are theoretically essential, not merely practical.

> **Theorem (CDCL ≡ resolution, informally):** With restarts, nondeterministic branching, and 1UIP learning, CDCL polynomially simulates general resolution; without restarts it is strictly weaker. The DRAT proof system, by contrast, p-simulates *extended* resolution, strictly exceeding CDCL's native power.

### 5.2 Empirical structure

On SAT competition benchmarks (application, crafted, random tracks), the CDCL pipeline exhibits robust structure:

1. **Propagation dominance:** 80–90% of runtime is unit propagation; the two-watched-literal scheme is the single largest performance factor [3].
2. **Learning pays:** clause minimization and LBD-based deletion each contribute multiplicative speedups; disabling deletion degrades performance even with abundant RAM [9].
3. **Proof overhead is modest:** DRAT logging slows solvers by roughly 10–30%, and backward checking with DRAT-trim typically completes within a small multiple of solve time—certification is practical at scale [6].

Empirically, Glucose-style aggressive deletion solved the most application instances in its competition years [5], and modern descendants (Kissat, CaDiCaL) retain the same architectural skeleton: 1UIP, VSIDS, restarts, two-watched literals, LBD, inprocessing, DRAT.

## 6. Limitations

CDCL is not a panacea, and honest scholarship requires naming its boundaries:

1. **Proof-complexity ceilings.** Because CDCL p-simulates resolution and nothing stronger (without extension mechanisms), families hard for resolution—certain pigeonhole, clique-coloring, and Tseitin instances—remain exponentially hard for CDCL. Extended-resolution techniques (bounded variable addition, symmetry breaking) help but require care to stay DRAT-certifiable.
2. **Heuristic brittleness.** VSIDS, restart schedules, and deletion thresholds are tuned on competition benchmarks; performance can degrade sharply on out-of-distribution instances, and no theory predicts optimal parameters.
3. **Memory pressure from learning.** Even with LBD deletion, learned-clause databases on hard UNSAT instances can consume tens of gigabytes; deletion is a heuristic compromise, and Krüger et al. show the trade-off is delicate: too much deletion destroys learning's benefit, too little chokes propagation [9].
4. **Certification gaps.** DRAT certifies *unsatisfiability* claims; satisfiable verdicts rely on model checking (cheap but still trust in the model printer). Parallel solvers' clause sharing and some inprocessing steps stress proof-logging infrastructure.
5. **No optimality guarantees.** CDCL is a heuristic proof search; it offers no approximation of the *shortest* refutation, and proof-minimization remains largely open.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Conflict-driven clause learning transformed SAT from a theoretically intractable problem into an industrial workhorse. Its architecture—implication graphs distilled by 1UIP analysis into asserting clauses, focused by VSIDS, accelerated by two-watched literals and restarts, curated by minimization and LBD-based deletion, simplified by certified inprocessing, and ultimately *justified* by DRAT proofs—represents one of the most successful syntheses of theory and engineering in computer science. The proof-complexity characterization (CDCL ≡ resolution, modulo restarts) explains both its power and its limits, while DRAT certification has made solvers accountable to independent, verified checkers.

Open frontiers include proof-optimal clause management (which clauses *should* be kept?), tighter integration of extended-resolution reasoning with certification, machine-learned branching heuristics that generalize beyond training distributions, and verified end-to-end pipelines from solver to silicon. The unreasonable effectiveness of CDCL is no accident; it is the product of thirty years of compounding insight—and the next thirty will likely be about making that effectiveness *certified by construction*.
