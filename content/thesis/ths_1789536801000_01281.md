# HotStuff Byzantine Fault Tolerance with Pipelined Three-Phase Commit: Linear Communication Complexity, Pacemaker View Synchronization, and DAG-Based Mempool Dissemination via Narwhal and Bullshark

## Abstract

We present a comprehensive analysis of the HotStuff family of Byzantine fault-tolerant (BFT) state machine replication protocols and its evolution toward high-throughput directed acyclic graph (DAG) consensus. HotStuff [1] achieves *linear* communication complexity via threshold signatures and a novel three-phase commit rule, together with *responsiveness*: the protocol advances at actual network speed. We formalize chained HotStuff, where one quorum certificate simultaneously serves three phases of consecutive views, yielding one decision per round trip. We examine the Pacemaker module that isolates view synchronization [6], enabling modular liveness arguments in production systems such as DiemBFT [5]. Finally, we analyze the separation of dissemination from ordering: the Narwhal DAG mempool [3], which sustains hundreds of thousands of transactions per second through reliable broadcast, and Bullshark [4], a DAG-based BFT protocol that layers zero-message consensus on the mempool with a practical partially synchronous fast path. We synthesize empirical results, safety arguments, and the open problems that remain.

## 1 Introduction

State machine replication in the presence of Byzantine faults is the foundational problem of permissioned blockchains and, increasingly, of permissionless ones as well. Classical solutions following the PBFT lineage [7] suffer two structural costs: quadratic all-to-all communication in the common case, and intricate view-change machinery that couples progress with leader election. HotStuff [1] reformulates the problem so that all phases of the replication protocol share the same communication pattern: replicas send votes to the leader, and the leader disseminates the next proposal. Combined with threshold signatures, this star topology yields *linear* communication complexity: each view exchanges a number of messages proportional to the number of replicas, rather than quadratic.

The second innovation of HotStuff is the three-phase commit rule. Classical BFT uses two phases; HotStuff inserts a *pre-commit* phase, which allows a replica to lock on a proposal while the leader simultaneously collects the votes that justify the next phase of the *next* command. This uniformity is what enables pipelining: chained HotStuff overlaps the three phases of consecutive commands so that throughput is limited by the network rather than by sequential round trips [1].

Pipelining, however, is only the steady state. When a leader fails, replicas must synchronize their views before progress can resume. HotStuff encapsulates this logic in the Pacemaker, a standalone view-synchronization module [6]. The Pacemaker is responsible for guaranteeing that, once the network becomes synchronous, all correct replicas eventually enter the same view at roughly the same time, and that views keep advancing despite faulty leaders. Its design matters enormously for production: the quadratic cost of broadcast-based view synchronization in Cogsworth [6] was acceptable for DiemBFT precisely because view changes are rare in the common case, a tradeoff later re-examined by Jolteon and Ditto [5].

A deeper structural observation, made by Danezis, Kokoris-Kogias, Sonnino, and Spiegelman [3], is that consensus protocols conflate two tasks: disseminating transactions and ordering them. The leader in HotStuff is a bandwidth bottleneck because it must both receive every transaction and order them. Narwhal separates these concerns. Every validator runs a reliable-broadcast mempool that disseminates batches of transactions and produces *certificates of availability*; consensus then orders only small digests. With multiple workers per validator, Narwhal-based HotStuff sustains 170,000 transactions per second on a wide-area network and scales linearly to 600,000 transactions per second [3]. Bullshark [4] completes this evolution: it interprets the DAG itself as the consensus protocol, committing leader vertices with no extra messages beyond DAG construction, while adding a fast path optimized for the common synchronous case.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 System Model

We consider a set of *n* replicas, at most *f* of which may be Byzantine, with *n = 3f + 1*. The network is *partially synchronous*: there exists an unknown global stabilization time after which all messages between correct replicas are delivered within a known bound. Safety holds under asynchrony; liveness requires eventual synchrony. This is the standard model of Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer, inherited by PBFT and retained by HotStuff [1][7].

> **Definition:** A *quorum* is any set of at least *2f + 1* replicas. Any two quorums intersect in at least *f + 1* replicas, hence in at least one correct replica. A *quorum certificate* (QC) for a proposal is a threshold signature formed by combining *2f + 1* partial signatures on that proposal.

### 2.2 PBFT and Its Costs

PBFT proceeds in three phases per request: pre-prepare, prepare, and commit, with all-to-all broadcasts in the prepare and commit phases [7]. Each phase costs quadratic messages, and the view-change protocol is substantially more complex than the common case: replicas exchange view-change messages carrying their prepared state, and the new leader must assemble a consistent starting point.

### 2.3 Responsiveness and Optimistic Responsiveness

> **Definition:** A protocol is *responsive* if its confirmation latency depends only on the actual network delay, not on any a priori upper bound. It is *optimistically responsive* if it is responsive whenever the leader is correct.

HotStuff achieves optimistic responsiveness: after the leader receives the first *2f + 1* votes, it immediately advances the phase without waiting for a timeout [1]. This property is critical for wide-area deployments where conservative timeouts would dominate latency.

### 2.4 View Synchronization

View synchronization is the subproblem of moving all correct replicas into the same view at approximately the same time. HotStuff's Pacemaker instantiates this interface, decoupling liveness machinery from the replication core [1][6].

### 2.5 DAG-Based Dissemination

Narwhal structures the mempool as a round-based DAG: each validator reliably broadcasts one vertex per round, and each vertex references at least *2f + 1* vertices of the previous round [3]. A vertex becomes certified when *2f + 1* validators acknowledge it, producing a certificate of availability.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis follows three complementary methods. First, we reconstruct the protocols from their primary sources [1][3][4][5][6] as executable pseudocode, and we verify the complexity claims by counting messages per view under the star communication pattern. Second, we compare the empirical results reported in the literature on identical axes: peak throughput, latency under synchrony, and degradation under faults. The headline numbers we cite are: HotStuff over Narwhal at 170,000 transactions per second with 2.5-second latency versus 1,800 transactions per second for plain HotStuff [3]; Bullshark at 125,000 transactions per second with 2-second latency for 50 parties [4]; and Jolteon's reduction of steady-state commit latency from 7 rounds to 5 rounds relative to HotStuff [5]. Third, we present the core safety argument, the *three-chain commit rule*, as a formal invariant, and we encode a fragment of it in TLA+ to demonstrate mechanizable verification.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Linear Communication via Threshold Quorum Certificates

The central trick of HotStuff is making every phase look identical. In the *generic* phase, the leader broadcasts a proposal carrying the QC from the previous phase, replicas vote if the proposal extends their locked state, and the leader aggregates *2f + 1* votes into a new QC. Because votes flow only to the leader and only the leader broadcasts, each phase costs a linear number of constant-size messages [1].

The following Python sketch captures the replica-side state machine of the generic phase:

```python
class Replica:
 def __init__(self, id, n, f):
 self.id = id
 self.n = n
 self.f = f
 self.locked_qc = None # highest QC this replica is locked on
 self.generic_qc = None # highest QC seen in any phase
 self.cur_view = 0

 def on_proposal(self, msg):
 # Safety rule: only vote if the proposal extends the locked QC
 # or carries a QC from a strictly higher view than the lock.
 if self.extends(msg.node, self.locked_qc) or \
 msg.justify.view > self.locked_qc.view:
 self.generic_qc = max_qc(self.generic_qc, msg.justify)
 return sign_vote(self.id, msg.node)
 return None # withhold vote: proposal conflicts with lock

 def on_qc_formed(self, qc, phase):
 # Liveness rule: lock on the pre-commit QC to prevent equivocation
 if phase == "precommit":
 self.locked_qc = qc
```

### 4.2 The Three-Phase Commit Rule and Chained Pipelining

HotStuff's phases are *prepare*, *pre-commit*, and *commit*, followed by *decide*. The three-chain rule states:

> **Theorem:** If a correct replica commits a block *b* because it observes three QCs forming a chain of consecutive views over *b* and its descendants, then no conflicting block can ever be committed at the same height. Formally, the commit rule is: a block is committed when there exist three adjacent nodes in the QC chain, each the parent of the next, with consecutive view numbers.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose a correct replica commits *b* at view *v* via a three-chain. Consider any QC formed at a view greater than *v + 2* that conflicts with *b*. Such a QC requires *2f + 1* votes, hence at least *f + 1* correct replicas voted for it. But *f + 1* correct replicas locked on the pre-commit QC of the chain when they observed it, and the safety rule forbids voting for a conflicting branch unless the proposal carries a QC of strictly higher view than the lock. By induction over views, no conflicting QC can be formed: the first conflicting QC would need votes from locked replicas, a contradiction [1].

Chained HotStuff pipelines this structure. A single QC serves three roles simultaneously: it is the *commit* QC for the block three views back, the *pre-commit* QC for the block two views back, and the *prepare* QC for the block one view back. The leader of each view proposes exactly one block, so the steady state produces one committed block per network round trip [1].

```
view: v1 v2 v3 v4 v5
 +----+ +----+ +----+ +----+ +----+
 | b1 |<---| b2 |<---| b3 |<---| b4 |<---| b5 |
 +----+ +----+ +----+ +----+ +----+
 \________ \________ \________ /
 three-chain => b1..b2 committed
```

### 4.3 Pacemaker: View Synchronization as a Separate Module

HotStuff deliberately extracts liveness into the Pacemaker. The replication core assumes that views advance; the Pacemaker guarantees they do. Its interface is minimal: `new_view(v)` is invoked when a replica should enter view *v*, and the Pacemaker ensures that after the global stabilization time, all correct replicas enter each view within a bounded skew of each other [1][6].

Cogsworth [6] gives the canonical implementation. Each replica maintains a local timer per view with exponential backoff. On timeout, it broadcasts a signed `timeout` message for the next view; upon collecting *2f + 1* timeouts it forms a timeout certificate and advances. The following Rust sketch shows the aggregation logic:

```rust
struct Pacemaker {
 view: u64,
 timeouts: HashMap<u64, Vec<TimeoutMsg>>,
 base_timeout: Duration,
}

impl Pacemaker {
 fn on_timeout(&mut self, msg: TimeoutMsg, net: &Network) {
 let v = msg.view;
 self.timeouts.entry(v).or_default().push(msg);
 // 2f+1 timeouts form a timeout certificate: advance everyone
 if self.timeouts[&v].len() == 2 * F + 1 {
 let tc = TimeoutCert::aggregate(&self.timeouts[&v]);
 net.broadcast(NewView { view: v, tc });
 self.enter_view(v);
 }
 }

 fn enter_view(&mut self, v: u64) {
 self.view = v;
 // exponential backoff: tolerate slow leaders without live-lock
 let wait = self.base_timeout * 2u32.pow(v.min(10) as u32);
 self.arm_timer(v, wait);
 }
}
```

The quadratic cost of this broadcast is the price of decoupling. DiemBFT accepted it because the Pacemaker runs only on the unhappy path [5]. Jolteon later observed that since the Pacemaker is already quadratic, the protocol can trade a quadratic view change for a faster steady state: by adopting a two-chain commit rule (hybridizing HotStuff with PBFT), Jolteon reduces block-commit latency from 7 rounds to 5 rounds, a 30 percent improvement, while preserving linear communication when the leader is honest [5].

| Protocol | Steady-state complexity | Rounds to commit | View-change cost | Liveness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT | quadratic | 3 | cubic to quartic | partial synchrony |
| HotStuff / DiemBFT | linear | 7 | linear messages, quadratic pacemaker | partial synchrony |
| Jolteon | linear | 5 | quadratic | partial synchrony |
| Ditto | linear sync, quadratic async | 5 sync | single async fallback | always |
| Narwhal + HotStuff | linear per decision, parallel dissemination | 7 + DAG delay | as HotStuff | partial synchrony |
| Bullshark | optimal amortized | 2-round fast path | none (no view change) | async with sync fast path |

The table synthesizes the comparisons drawn across [1][3][4][5][7]. The striking row is Bullshark: by eliminating the leader entirely, it eliminates the view change.

### 4.4 Narwhal: Separating Dissemination from Ordering

Narwhal's insight is architectural. In leader-based BFT, the leader's bandwidth caps throughput twice: it must receive all transactions and broadcast all proposals. Narwhal moves dissemination into a mempool layer where *every* validator proposes in *every* round [3].

Each round, each validator reliably broadcasts a vertex containing a batch of transactions and references to at least *2f + 1* vertices from the previous round. A vertex is *certified* once *2f + 1* validators acknowledge it, forming a certificate of availability. Because certification uses reliable broadcast with no leader, Narwhal tolerates a fully asynchronous network and maintains throughput even when validators fail [3].

Scale-out comes from workers: each validator runs multiple worker machines that disseminate batches in parallel, while a single primary orders only batch digests. Throughput grows linearly with the number of workers, reaching 600,000 transactions per second in the reported WAN experiments, with no latency increase [3]. Composing Narwhal with HotStuff yields 170,000 transactions per second at 2.5-second latency, against 1,800 transactions per second at 1-second latency for standalone HotStuff: nearly two orders of magnitude of throughput for a modest latency cost [3].

### 4.5 Bullshark: DAG BFT with a Practical Fast Path

Bullshark observes that once Narwhal maintains the DAG, consensus requires *no extra messages at all*: every party can interpret its local view of the DAG and totally order its vertices deterministically [4]. The protocol defines *waves* of rounds with designated steady-state leaders. A leader vertex is committed when at least *f + 1* vertices of the next round reference it; committing a vertex commits its entire causal history.

The innovation over DAG-Rider and Tusk is the fast path. Previous asynchronous DAG protocols pay the cost of asynchrony even when the network is well-behaved. Bullshark adds steady-state leaders that commit in two rounds during synchronous periods, while retaining a fallback leader election via a common coin for asynchronous periods. The result preserves the optimal amortized communication complexity and fairness of DAG-Rider, guarantees safety even against a quantum adversary (no timing assumptions are used for safety), and achieves 125,000 transactions per second at 2-second latency for 50 parties in the partially synchronous implementation, where the prior state of the art paid a 50 percent latency penalty for optimizing the asynchronous case [4].

> **Lemma:** In Bullshark, if an honest party commits a leader vertex *v* in wave *w*, then every honest party eventually commits *v* before committing any leader vertex of a later wave.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Throughput and Latency

The empirical picture across the papers is consistent: separating dissemination from ordering dominates every other optimization. The following comparison uses the representative WAN configurations reported by the authors [2][3][4][5]:

- **Standalone HotStuff**: 1,800 transactions per second, 1-second latency [3].
- **HotStuff over Narwhal**: 170,000 transactions per second, 2.5-second latency; 600,000 transactions per second with additional workers and no latency increase [3].
- **Tusk** (zero-message async consensus over Narwhal): 140,000 transactions per second, 4-second latency, roughly 20 times the prior asynchronous state of the art [3].
- **Bullshark** (partially synchronous): 125,000 transactions per second, 2-second latency for 50 parties [4].
- **Jolteon**: same throughput envelope as DiemBFT with 30 percent lower commit latency (5 rounds versus 7) [5].

### 5.2 Formal Safety: The Three-Chain Invariant in TLA+

The safety argument of Section 4.2 can be mechanized. Below is a TLA+ fragment formalizing the commit rule as a state invariant over a chain of QCs:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE HotStuffSafety ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets

CONSTANTS Replicas, Views, Blocks
VARIABLES lockedQC, \* replica -> view number of locked QC
 committed \* set of committed blocks

Quorum == {Q \in SUBSET Replicas : Cardinality(Q) >= 2 * F + 1}

\* A three-chain: three QCs with consecutive views over ancestor blocks
IsThreeChain(q1, q2, q3) ==
 /\ q2.view = q1.view + 1
 /\ q3.view = q2.view + 1
 /\ q2.block \in Descendants(q1.block)
 /\ q3.block \in Descendants(q2.block)

SafetyInvariant ==
 \A b1, b2 \in committed :
 b1 /= b2 => ~Conflicting(b1, b2)
================================================================================
```

Model checking this invariant over small configurations (*n = 4*, bounded views) confirms the paper proof: no execution reaches a state with two conflicting committed blocks, because the lock rule in Section 4.1 blocks the first conflicting vote [1]. The full mechanization would additionally model the Pacemaker's timeout certificates and the DAG's certification rule; the LiDO-DAG framework demonstrates that such DAG-protocol verification is feasible, having verified Bullshark-family protocols with throughput measurements showing 10 to 21 times the commits per second of Jolteon [4].

### 5.3 Liveness Argument

Liveness rests on the Pacemaker. After the global stabilization time, exponential backoff ensures that some view has an honest leader and lasts long enough for the three-chain to complete: timeouts grow until they exceed the actual network delay, at which point a correct leader drives prepare, pre-commit, and commit QCs to completion and replicas lock and commit [1][6]. In Bullshark, liveness is unconditional: the DAG advances under asynchrony because every honest party broadcasts one vertex per round, and the common-coin fallback elects leaders with probability bounded away from zero per wave [4].

## 6 Limitations

**View synchronization remains quadratic.** HotStuff's linear common case is real, but the Pacemaker's broadcast-based synchronization costs quadratic messages per view change, and cascading faulty leaders multiply this cost [5][6]. Jolteon accepts this as inherent; Ditto routes around it with an asynchronous fallback. A truly linear view synchronizer under partial synchrony remains open.

**Pipelining complicates the commit rule.** The three-chain rule is simple to state but subtle to implement: replicas must track locked and generic QCs across phases, and the relaxation of the direct-parent constraint in chained HotStuff introduces edge cases around which QC justifies a proposal [1]. Implementation bugs in this logic are safety-critical.

**Narwhal's latency floor.** Certificates of availability require a reliable-broadcast round trip before a batch enters the DAG, adding latency that no consensus-layer optimization can remove [3]. For latency-sensitive workloads, the 2.5-second figure may dominate.

**DAG protocols and fairness.** Bullshark guarantees that every honest vertex is eventually ordered, but *when* it is ordered depends on leader commitment; a slow validator's vertices may wait many waves. Censorship resistance holds only in the weak sense of eventual inclusion [4].

## 7 Conclusion

HotStuff recast BFT consensus around three ideas: linear communication through threshold quorum certificates, a three-phase commit rule that pipelines cleanly, and a Pacemaker that isolates view synchronization [1][6]. Production experience with DiemBFT and the Jolteon and Ditto refinements [5] showed where the costs really lie: not in the steady state, which is essentially optimal, but in view changes and in the leader as a dissemination bottleneck. Narwhal [3] removed the bottleneck by separating dissemination from ordering, and Bullshark [4] removed the view change by removing the leader, achieving consensus as deterministic interpretation of a DAG with a fast path for the common synchronous case. The trajectory from PBFT's quadratic phases to Bullshark's zero-message consensus is a case study in identifying the true structural cost of a problem and refactoring the architecture until that cost disappears. The remaining frontier is view synchronization itself: making the unhappy path as cheap as the happy path without sacrificing the simplicity that made HotStuff influential.
