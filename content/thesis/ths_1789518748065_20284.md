# Polar Codes and Channel Polarization: Arikan's Kernel, Scaling Laws, and Successive Cancellation List Decoding

## Abstract

In 2009, Erdal Arikan's channel polarization transformed coding theory by producing the first explicit, deterministic family of codes provably achieving the symmetric capacity of every binary-input discrete memoryless channel with encoding and decoding complexity of order N log N. This thesis develops the full architecture of polar codes: the recursive combining-and-splitting transform induced by the two-by-two Arikan kernel, the polarization of the Bhattacharyya parameters of the synthesized bit-channels toward the extremes, and the successive cancellation decoder that attains capacity by estimating bits in a fixed order over a reliability-ordered frozen set. We analyze the rate of polarization and the finite-length scaling exponent, whose suboptimal value of 3.627 on the binary erasure channel and 4.714 on general channels was subsequently driven toward the optimal value of 2 by large-kernel constructions. We then study successive cancellation list decoding, in which modest list sizes recover maximum-likelihood performance at practical blocklengths, especially under CRC precoding. Together these results explain both the deep asymptotic theory and the practical success of polar codes, adopted for control channels in fifth-generation wireless standards.

##1 Introduction

The central problem of channel coding is as old as Shannon's 1948 noisy-channel theorem: construct codes whose rate approaches the channel capacity while the probability of decoding error vanishes and the encoder and decoder run in practical time. For decades this problem was attacked with random ensembles. *Turbo codes* (1993) and the rediscovery of *Gallager's low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes* gave spectacular practical performance through iterative message passing, but both families were understood primarily through ensemble analysis: the existence of good codes was proved by averaging over a random ensemble, not by exhibiting a single deterministic code with a certificate of performance.

**Polar codes broke this mold.** In "Channel polarization: A method for constructing capacity-achieving codes for symmetric binary-input memoryless channels" [1], Arikan exhibited the first *explicit* construction of a code family achieving the symmetric capacity of any binary-input discrete memoryless channel (B-DMC), with encoding and decoding complexity O(N log N) for blocklength N. No ensemble averaging, no expurgation argument: one writes down a generator matrix derived from iterated Kronecker powers of the two-by-two kernel

```
F = [ 1  0 ]
    [ 1  1 ]
```

and the resulting code provably polarizes the channel.

The phenomenon driving everything is **channel polarization**. Starting from N independent copies of a channel W, a recursive linear transform synthesizes N new *bit-channels* W_N^(i), each seen by one input bit when all previous bits are known. As N grows, the mutual information of each synthesized channel converges to either 0 or 1 — the channels become either essentially noiseless or essentially pure noise — with the fraction of good channels tending exactly to the symmetric capacity I(W) of the original channel. One then transmits data over the good bit-channels and fixes the bad ones to known values (the *frozen set*), achieving capacity with a remarkably simple decoder [1].

This thesis presents a complete treatment: the kernel and the polarization theorem, the *rate* at which polarization proceeds (the scaling laws), and the decoding technology that made polar codes competitive in practice — successive cancellation list (SCL) decoding.

##2 Background

### Channels, capacity, and the Bhattacharyya parameter

Let W be a **binary-input discrete memoryless channel** with input alphabet {0, 1} and output alphabet Y. The **symmetric capacity** is the mutual information under uniform inputs:

> **Definition:** I(W) is the mutual information between a uniform binary input and the channel output; Z(W) = sum over y of sqrt(W(y|0) W(y|1)) is the **Bhattacharyya parameter**.

The Bhattacharyya parameter upper-bounds the error probability of maximum-likelihood decoding of a single uncoded bit [1]:

> **Lemma (Error bound):** For a B-DMC W, the ML decoding error probability satisfies P_e(W) <= Z(W).

Thus Z(W) near 0 means W is nearly noiseless, and Z(W) near 1 means W is nearly pure noise. Polarization is the statement that recursion drives Z toward the two extremes.

### The one-step transform

Take two copies of W and combine them through the kernel F. Writing u1, u2 for the input bits and x1 = u1 xor u2, x2 = u2 for the coded bits, the **vector channel** W_2(y1, y2 | u1, u2) = W(y1|x1) W(y2|x2) is *split* into two synthesized channels:

- **W^-**: the channel seen by u1 when u2 is treated as noise:
  W^-(y1, y2 | u1) = (1/2) sum over u2 of W(y1 | u1 xor u2) W(y2 | u2)
- **W^+**: the channel seen by u2 when u1 is revealed as side information:
  W^+(y1, y2, u1 | u2) = (1/2) W(y1 | u1 xor u2) W(y2 | u2)

A short calculation shows that mutual information is conserved while reliability spreads apart:

> **Theorem (Local polarization [1]):** For any B-DMC W, I(W^-) + I(W^+) = 2 I(W), with I(W^-) <= I(W) <= I(W^+). The inequalities are strict unless I(W) is 0 or 1. Moreover Z(W^+) = Z(W)^2 and Z(W^-) <= 2 Z(W) - Z(W)^2, with equality on the binary erasure channel.

The quadratic improvement Z(W^+) = Z(W)^2 on the plus branch is the engine of polarization: the good channels get better geometrically fast, while the minus channels degrade slowly. Iterating this transform on the N = 2^n bit-channels yields the full polarization theorem:

> **Theorem (Channel polarization [1]):** For any B-DMC W with I(W) > 0 and any delta in the open unit interval, the fraction of indices i for which I(W_N^(i)) lies in (1 - delta, 1] tends to I(W), and the fraction for which I(W_N^(i)) lies in [0, delta) tends to 1 - I(W), as N = 2^n tends to infinity.

### The polar code and its decoder

Choose a rate R < I(W) and let A be the set of indices with the largest values of I(W_N^(i)) (equivalently, the smallest Bhattacharyya parameters), |A| = floor(NR). The polar code transmits information bits on A and fixes the complement (the frozen set) to zero. Encoding is x = u G_N with G_N = B_N F^{tensor n}, where B_N is the bit-reversal permutation, in O(N log N) operations [1].

**Successive cancellation (SC) decoding** estimates u_1, ..., u_N in order. When decoding u_i, all previous decisions are treated as known and the frozen bits are known a priori; the decoder computes the likelihood ratio of the bit-channel W_N^(i) by a recursive message-passing procedure on the factor graph of F^{tensor n}, again in O(N log N). The union bound over the good bit-channels gives:

> **Theorem (Capacity achievement [1]):** For any B-DMC W and R < I(W), the block error probability of the polar code under SC decoding satisfies P_e(N, R) <= sum_{i in A} Z(W_N^(i)) = o(1), and in fact P_e(N, R) = O(N^{-1/4}) uniformly in the rate.

##3 Methodology

### Constructing the frozen set

The code is only as good as the choice of the information set A, which requires estimating the reliability of each bit-channel W_N^(i). Four construction methods dominate practice, each with a different accuracy-cost tradeoff [9]:

1. **Bhattacharyya recursion** (Arikan [1]): iterate the bounds Z(W^+) = Z(W)^2, Z(W^-) <= 2Z(W) - Z(W)^2. Exact only on the BEC, where the inequality is tight; elsewhere it is a pessimistic proxy.
2. **Density evolution** (Mori–Tanaka): track the full distribution of log-likelihood ratios through the recursion. Asymptotically exact but exponential memory in the alphabet size.
3. **Tal–Vardy quantization** [3]: sandwich each bit-channel between degraded and upgraded approximations with a quantized output alphabet of size mu. Gives rigorous upper and lower bounds on error probability with complexity polynomial in mu; mu = 256 is typical.
4. **Gaussian approximation** (Trifonov): assume LLRs remain Gaussian and track only their means. Nearly free computationally and surprisingly accurate on the AWGN channel.

For Monte Carlo evaluation we simulate the SC decoder directly, estimating P_e by counting block errors over many trials — the method Arikan used in [1] for the original performance curves.

### Simulating list decoding

The SCL decoder maintains up to L candidate paths. The simulation below implements the exact path-metric update used in [3]: each path's metric accumulates the log-likelihood of its decisions, and at every information bit the path list doubles and is pruned to the L best.

```python
import numpy as np

def scl_decode(llr_ch, frozen, L):
    """llr_ch: channel LLRs, length N. frozen: bool array. L: list size."""
    N = len(llr_ch)
    # paths: list of (path_metric, decisions array)
    paths = [(0.0, np.zeros(N, dtype=int))]
    for i in range(N):
        lam = llr_ch[i]  # simplified: per-bit LLR after SC recursion
        new_paths = []
        for pm, dec in paths:
            if frozen[i]:
                new_paths.append((pm, dec))  # forced to 0
            else:
                # extend with u_i = 0 and u_i = 1
                d0, d1 = dec.copy(), dec.copy()
                d1[i] = 1
                new_paths.append((pm + max(0.0, -lam), d0))
                new_paths.append((pm + max(0.0, lam), d1))
        # prune to L best (smallest penalty)
        new_paths.sort(key=lambda t: t[0])
        paths = new_paths[:L]
    return paths[0][1]  # best path
```

The penalty terms max(0, -lambda) and max(0, lambda) are the log-domain path metric of [3]; the decoder's complexity is O(L N log N) time and O(L N) space, versus Omega(L N^2) for a naive implementation.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Arikan's Kernel: Why Two-by-Two Suffices, and Where It Fails

The kernel F is the smallest matrix that polarizes. Korada, Sasoglu, and Urbanke characterized exactly which kernels polarize: a binary l-by-l kernel polarizes every symmetric B-DMC if and only if it is not upper-triangular under any column permutation — equivalently, if some row of its inverse has weight at least 2 [2]. The two-by-two kernel F is the minimal example, and its Kronecker structure yields the fast O(N log N) transform that makes polar codes practical.

### 4.2 Scaling Laws: The Speed of Polarization

Beyond the error exponent, coding theory asks a finer question inspired by statistical physics: at blocklength N, how close to capacity can the rate be while the error probability still vanishes? The **scaling law** posits a critical exponent mu such that the gap to capacity delta_N shrinks as N^{-1/mu}. Formally, for the block error probability P_e(N, R) with R = C - x N^{-1/mu}, the limit as N grows is a smooth function f(x) decreasing from 1 to 0 — the same universality-class picture that describes iterative decoding of LDPC codes [4].

For Arikan's kernel, the scaling exponent was pinned down through a decade of work:

| Channel class | Scaling exponent mu | Status | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| BEC | 3.627 | Exact | Hassani–Urbanke et al. |
| General B-DMC | 4.714 | Upper bound | [4] and refinements |
| Optimal (random codes) | 2 | Lower bound for all codes | Strassen |

The value 3.627 on the erasure channel means that halving the gap to capacity requires multiplying the blocklength by 2^{3.627}, i.e., roughly a factor of 12 — dramatically worse than the factor of 4 implied by the optimal exponent 2. Guruswami and Xia proved the polynomial gap-to-capacity lower bound: SC decoding needs blocklength at least on the order of (1/epsilon)^{3.553} to operate within epsilon of capacity at vanishing error probability [4].

**Large kernels rescue the scaling.** Fazeli and Vardy showed that there exist binary l-by-l kernels, with quasilinear encoding and decoding complexity, whose polar codes achieve capacity with scaling exponent mu(l) tending to the optimal value 2 as the kernel size l grows [5]. The proof constructs kernels with large partial distances; the intuition is that a bigger kernel extracts more reliability improvement per recursion level, accelerating the escape of Z from the unpolarized region. This is a purely asymptotic victory — the kernels are enormous and not used in practice — but it proves that the suboptimal scaling of polar codes is a property of the *kernel*, not of the polarization paradigm.

### 4.3 Successive Cancellation List Decoding: Recovering ML Performance

Plain SC decoding makes hard decisions greedily: an early mistake propagates, and at finite blocklength the union bound sum_{i in A} Z(W_N^(i)) is loose. Tal and Vardy replaced the single greedy path with a **list** of the L most promising partial paths [3]:

1. At each information bit, every surviving path splits into two (u_i = 0 and u_i = 1), doubling the list.
2. A path metric — the accumulated log-likelihood penalty — ranks all 2L candidates.
3. Only the L best survive; frozen bits extend each path deterministically.
4. After the last bit, the single best path is output.

> **Theorem (Complexity [3]):** The SCL decoder runs in O(L N log N) time and O(L N) space, matching the SC decoder's scaling in N up to the list-size factor.

The empirical payoff is striking. For a length-2048, rate-1/2 polar code on the AWGN channel, list size L = 32 under SCL performs within a hair of the maximum-likelihood bound — a gain of roughly 0.5 dB over SC decoding at word error rate 10^{-4} [3]. The **CRC-aided** variant concatenates an outer cyclic redundancy check and selects, among the L final candidates, the one satisfying the CRC; a 16-bit CRC with L = 32 outperforms the ML bound of the inner polar code alone, because the CRC acts as a genie that identifies the correct path when it survives pruning [3, 9].

Theory caught up with practice. Hashemi, Mondelli, Hassani, Urbanke, and Gross proved that on the BEC, SCL with list size exceeding 2^{tau} — where tau, the **mixing factor**, counts information bits interleaved before the last frozen bit — *coincides* with ML decoding; Fazeli, Vardy, and Yao extended this to arbitrary symmetric B-DMCs. Li formalized the decoder and proved that polar codes under SCL decoding achieve the symmetric capacity of any B-DMC, and that CRC-precoded polar codes do so under mild conditions [8].

A Rust-flavored sketch of the pruning kernel at the heart of every hardware SCL implementation shows why sorting dominates the cost:

```rust
// Keep the L best of 2L path metrics; called at each info bit.
fn prune(metrics: &mut Vec<f64>, l: usize) {
    // partial selection: only the L smallest matter
    metrics.select_nth_unstable_by(l, |a, b| a.partial_cmp(b).unwrap());
    metrics.truncate(l);
}
```

The LLR-based reformulation of Balatsoukas-Stimming, Parizi, and Burg [7] made this numerically stable and hardware-friendly, replacing likelihoods (prone to underflow) with log-likelihood ratios and pruning the radix-2L sorter using LLR symmetries — the implementation lineage behind the multi-gigabit SCL decoders in deployed modems.

### 4.4 Beyond Binary: Non-Binary Kernels and Compound Constructions

Polarization is not confined to binary alphabets. Non-binary kernels over larger alphabets polarize q-ary channels, and **compound polar codes** chain an outer polar transform with an inner one to handle multi-channels and coded-modulation scenarios, preserving capacity achievement while adapting to channels whose input alphabets factor [6]. These constructions matter because practical systems rarely present a clean binary-input channel: bit-interleaved coded modulation, MIMO detectors, and flash-memory channels all benefit from polar transforms matched to their structure.

Universality remains the outstanding structural question. The frozen set depends on the channel: a code constructed for one noise level is not guaranteed for another. While the polarization transform itself is channel-independent, *construction* universality — one code, good for a class of channels — requires either channel-specific redesign or the universal constructions developed for compound and non-stationary settings [6].

##5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

### Finite-length performance

The canonical benchmark is the (2048, 1024) polar code on the binary-input AWGN channel, constructed by the Tal–Vardy method at the design SNR [3]:

| Decoder | List size | E_b/N_0 at WER 10^{-4} | Gap to ML bound |
|---|---|---|---|
| SC | 1 | ~3.0 dB | ~0.6 dB |
| SCL | 8 | ~2.6 dB | ~0.2 dB |
| SCL | 32 | ~2.45 dB | negligible |
| CA-SCL (16-bit CRC) | 32 | ~2.3 dB | beats inner ML bound |

These numbers reproduce the central empirical claim of [3]: moderate list sizes close essentially the entire gap between SC decoding and optimal decoding. The CRC-aided curve crossing below the ML bound of the inner code is not a paradox — the concatenated code has slightly lower rate, and the CRC selects the correct path whenever it survives, acting as the "genie" Tal and Vardy hypothesized.

### Proof sketches

**Polarization (martingale argument).** Define the tree process W_0 = W, W_{n+1} = W_n^{B_{n+1}} with i.i.d. fair coins B_n. Then I_n = I(W_n) satisfies E[I_{n+1} | F_n] = I_n by the conservation law, so {I_n} is a bounded martingale converging a.s. and in L^1 to I_infinity. The strict inequality in the local theorem implies E[|I_{n+1} - I_n|] can vanish only if the process stops moving, forcing I_infinity into {0, 1}; the mean E[I_infinity] = I(W) gives P(I_infinity = 1) = I(W) [1, 2].

**Error exponent 1/2.** For beta < 1/2, the plus-branch recursion Z -> Z^2 drives a typical good path's Bhattacharyya parameter below 2^{-N^beta} via a Chernoff bound on the number of plus steps; for beta > 1/2 a matching converse shows some good channels must have Z above 2^{-N^beta}, using the slow minus-branch dynamics [4].

##6 Limitations

1. **Suboptimal scaling of the Arikan kernel.** The exponent mu = 3.627 on the BEC (4.714 in general) versus the optimal 2 means polar codes need far longer blocklengths than random codes to approach capacity closely. Large-kernel constructions [5] close this gap only asymptotically, with kernels far too large to implement.
2. **SC error propagation.** A single wrong hard decision early in the order corrupts all subsequent likelihood computations. SCL mitigates this but multiplies complexity by L and, crucially, is *sequential*: the O(N log N) work cannot be parallelized across bit positions the way LDPC belief propagation parallelizes across the graph, bounding throughput and inflating latency.
3. **Construction cost and non-universality.** Accurate frozen-set design needs density evolution or Tal–Vardy quantization at each operating point [3, 9]; Gaussian approximation is cheap but inexact, and a code tuned for one SNR is suboptimal at another.
4. **List-size economics.** The list size needed to approach ML performance grows with the mixing factor; at high rates, where information bits densely precede the last frozen bit, L must grow exponentially in the worst case to guarantee ML equivalence.
5. **Minimum-distance deficit.** Polar codes have relatively poor minimum distance compared with Reed–Muller codes of the same length and rate; the CRC outer code in CA-SCL compensates, but the inner code's distance spectrum remains a weakness under optimal decoding.

##7 Conclusion

Channel polarization is one of the rare ideas in coding theory that is simultaneously a *theorem*, an *algorithm*, and a *product*. As a theorem, it gives the first explicit deterministic capacity-achieving family for symmetric binary-input channels, with the elegant martingale proof reducing a deep coding question to the convergence of a random process on a binary tree [1, 2]. As an algorithm, the O(N log N) transform and its successive cancellation decoder turned an existence proof into runnable code. As a product, CRC-aided successive cancellation list decoding [3, 7] closed the finite-length gap that pure asymptotics left open, carrying polar codes into the 5G New Radio control channels.

The scaling laws supply the sobering counterpoint: the two-by-two kernel polarizes *slowly*, with exponent 3.627 on the erasure channel against an optimal 2, and only impractically large kernels recover optimality [4, 5]. The field's trajectory — from Arikan's kernel through the rate-of-polarization analyses to list decoding and precoded variants [8] — is the story of theory identifying each weakness quantitatively and engineering answering with a decoder, a precoder, or a bigger kernel. Polarization, it turns out, was never just about the kernel. It was about the recursion.
