# The Geometry of Deep Learning Loss Landscapes: Mode Connectivity, Hessian Spectra, and the Structure of Minima

## Abstract

The empirical loss surface of deep neural networks (DNNs), $L:\mathbb{R}^D \to \mathbb{R}_+$ with $D \gg 10^6$, is simultaneously one of the most consequential and least understood objects in modern machine learning. Classical nonconvex optimization predicts an explosion of saddle points and poor local minima, yet stochastic gradient descent reliably converges to minimizers that generalize. This thesis synthesizes the modern geometric program: (i) *mode connectivity* — distinct minima are joined by low-loss curves, falsifying the isolated-basin picture [1][3]; (ii) *visualization geometry* — filter-normalized 2D slices reveal how skip connections tame chaotic landscapes [2][6]; (iii) *spectral structure* — the Hessian eigenvalue density decomposes into a degenerate bulk, outlier sharp directions, and sign-indefinite saddles whose index correlates with loss height [1][3]; and (iv) *flat-vs-sharp minima* — the long-debated curvature–generalization link, refined by gauge-invariance critiques and MDL/Bayesian formalisms [4][8]. We formalize the curve-finding optimization problem, derive the connection to the lottery ticket hypothesis via connectivity of pruned subnetworks [5], and prove local invariance results for reparameterized sharpness. Experiments on ResNet/VGG benchmarks confirm polygonal one-bend connectivity and trace-based sharpness ranking consistent with generalization gaps.

## 1. Introduction

Deep neural networks optimize an empirical risk functional

$$L(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell(f(x_i;\theta), y_i), \quad \theta \in \mathbb{R}^D$$

over parameter spaces whose dimension $D$ routinely exceeds $10^8$. Classical results in nonconvex optimization suggest that gradient-based methods should become trapped at saddle points or in suboptimal basins. That they instead find minimizers $\theta^\star$ with $\nabla L(\theta^\star) \approx 0$ and $\nabla^2 L(\theta^\star) \succeq 0$ whose test performance is excellent constitutes the central puzzle of the loss-landscape program: *the geometry of $L$ is far more benign — and far more structured — than generic nonconvex functions would suggest.*

Three interlocking discoveries reshaped the field. First, **mode connectivity** [1][3]: independently trained minima are not isolated islands but are joined by simple continuous paths along which train and test accuracy remain nearly constant. Second, **visualization** [2][6]: filter-normalized 1D and 2D projections reveal that architectural choices (skip connections) and optimizer hyperparameters (batch size, learning rate) systematically reshape the geometry around minima. Third, **spectral analysis**: the Hessian $\nabla^2 L(\theta)$ at minima exhibits a bulk of near-degenerate zero eigenvalues — reflecting overparameterization-induced flat directions — plus a handful of large outliers encoding the "sharp" directions [3][1].

The flat-versus-sharp-minima debate [4][8] — the claim that flat minima generalize better — threads through all three, providing both the strongest intuitive picture and the sharpest theoretical objections (notably the gauge-invariance critique). This thesis gives a unified, self-contained treatment: definitions, the curve-finding methodology, Hessian spectral theory with a random-matrix lens, the sharpness debate, the lottery ticket connection, and precise statements of what is proven, what is empirical, and what remains open.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The overparameterized regime and interpolation

Modern networks operate in the *interpolating regime*: $D \gg n$ implies the manifold of global minima

$$\mathcal{M} = \{\theta : L(\theta) = 0\}$$

is typically nonempty and high-dimensional. The solution set is therefore not a point but a manifold, and questions of *which* point on $\mathcal{M}$ SGD selects become geometric questions about the basins, curvature, and connectivity of $\mathcal{M}$ [3].

### 2.2 Linear mode connectivity and its failure modes

Given two independently trained solutions $\theta_a, \theta_b$, the *linear interpolation* $\gamma(t) = (1-t)\theta_a + t\theta_b$ typically encounters a loss barrier: $\max_t L(\gamma(t)) \gg \max(L(\theta_a), L(\theta_b))$ [2]. The early folklore — that minima sit in isolated wells separated by walls — derives from this 1D probe. The crucial refinement is that *nonlinear* paths evade the barrier: there exist curves $\phi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^D$, $\phi(0)=\theta_a$, $\phi(1)=\theta_b$, with $\sup_t L(\phi(t))$ barely above the endpoint losses [1].

### 2.3 Curvature, Hessians, and sharpness

Local geometry at a critical point $\nabla L(\theta^\star)=0$ is governed by the Hessian spectrum $\{\lambda_i\}_{i=1}^D$ of $\nabla^2 L(\theta^\star)$. Two classical sharpness proxies are:

| Measure | Definition | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| Spectral norm | $\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2 L)$ | Local worst-case curvature; scale-sensitive |
| Trace | $\mathrm{tr}(\nabla^2 L) = \sum_i \lambda_i$ | Average curvature; additive over layers |
| Perturbation sharpness | $\max_{\|\delta\|\leq\rho} L(\theta+\delta) - L(\theta)$ | Keskar et al. [4]; gauge-relative |
| PAC-Bayes flatness | KL-based volume bound | Formal generalization link [4] |

The long-standing hypothesis, originating with Hochreiter and Schmidhuber and revived by Keskar et al. [4], is that *flatter* minima (small eigenvalues, small perturbation response) generalize better because the training-to-test shift of the loss surface moves sharp minima off their low-loss point [4][8].

### 2.4 Mode connectivity formalism

Garipov et al. [1] parameterize a *polygonal chain with one bend*: $\phi_\psi(t)$ passing through an intermediate point $\psi$ optimized to minimize

$$\mathbb{E}_{t\sim U(0,1)}\big[ L(\phi_\psi(t)) \big].$$

Draxler et al. (2018) and Fort & Jastrzebski [3] subsequently showed the phenomenon extends to *simplices* of many solutions, not merely pairs — low-loss subspaces connecting sets of optima — and that the connected modes remain functionally diverse, enabling fast geometric ensembling [1].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology for landscape analysis follows the standard empirical protocol, formalized as a reproducible pipeline [2][6].

**Step 1 — Independent training.** Train $K$ models $\{\theta_k\}_{k=1}^K$ from independent random initializations with identical hyperparameters, reaching loss below a tolerance $\epsilon$.

**Step 2 — Curve finding.** Optimize a one-bend polygonal connector:

```python
import torch

def connectivity_loss(theta_a, theta_b, bend, model, data, n=64):
    total = 0.0
    for t in torch.linspace(0, 1, n):
        if t <= 0.5:
            pt = (1 - 2*t) * theta_a + 2*t * bend
        else:
            pt = 2*(1 - t) * bend + (2*t - 1) * theta_b
        model.set_params(pt)
        total += model.loss(data)
    return total / n
```

The bend point $\psi$ is optimized with SGD; the barrier height $\max_t L(\phi(t)) - \max(L(\theta_a),L(\theta_b))$ quantifies connectivity quality [1][7].

**Step 3 — Hessian spectral probing.** Exact eigendecomposition of $\nabla^2 L \in \mathbb{R}^{D\times D}$ is infeasible; we use Lanczos-based stochastic trace estimation (Hutchinson's method) to obtain the spectral density $\rho(\lambda)$, the bulk edge, and the top outliers, following the procedure validated in [3].

**Step 4 — Filter-normalized visualization.** To make 2D slices comparable across architectures, random directions $d_1, d_2$ are *filter-normalized*: $d_{i,j} \leftarrow d_{i,j} \cdot \|\theta_{i,j}\|_F / \|d_{i,j}\|_F$ per filter $j$ of layer $i$, correcting the scale-invariance pathology of ReLU networks that otherwise renders plots meaningless [2].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The topology of mode connectivity

The strongest form of the claim is the **simplicial connectivity** of Fort & Jastrzebski [3]: for $m$ independently trained solutions, there exists a low-dimensional affine subspace (a simplex) whose interior maintains near-minimal loss. This falsifies the "separate wells" mental model decisively: the global-minimum manifold $\mathcal{M}$ is not a union of isolated components but possesses large connected, approximately-flat regions.

> **Theorem (Informal connectivity).** *For overparameterized networks trained on standard benchmarks, independently obtained minimizers $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_m$ admit a simplicial complex $\Delta \subset \mathbb{R}^D$ with $\theta_i \in \Delta$ such that $\sup_{\theta\in\Delta} L(\theta) \leq \max_i L(\theta_i) + \delta$ for small $\delta$; verified empirically on VGG and ResNet families [3][1].*

Consequences are both conceptual and practical:

- **Ensembling without retraining.** Fast Geometric Ensembling (FGE) [1] harvests diverse models along connectivity curves within a *single* training run, matching Snapshot Ensembles at lower cost.
- **Functional diversity despite loss similarity.** Fort et al. [3] show that connected points disagree substantially in prediction space — the connector traverses *functionally distinct* minima, so ensembling gains are genuine, not illusory.
- **Robustness repairs.** The low-loss tunnel can be re-optimized under adversarial objectives to *repair* backdoored models: mode connectivity provides a manifold of functionally equivalent networks, some of which are robust [7].

### 4.2 Hessian spectra: bulk, outliers, and saddles

The empirical spectral density of $\nabla^2 L(\theta^\star)$ at a converged minimum decomposes into three populations [3][1]:

1. **Degenerate bulk at zero.** A mass of eigenvalues with $|\lambda_i| \approx 0$, scaling with overparameterization $D - n$. These are *flat directions*: reparameterizations leaving the realized function (nearly) unchanged — the signature of $\mathcal{M}$ being a manifold rather than a point.
2. **Outlier eigenvalues.** A small set $\lambda_{\max} \gg 0$ encoding the "sharp" directions that genuinely constrain the function. The ratio $\lambda_{\max}/\mathrm{median}(|\lambda|)$ is a robust, gauge-aware sharpness index.
3. **Negative eigenvalues away from minima.** At saddle points and along random directions, the spectrum acquires a negative tail. Choromanska-type analyses [1][6] show the saddle index correlates with loss height: high-loss critical points have many descent directions, which is why SGD escapes them.

A random-matrix perspective clarifies the bulk: the Hessian of an overparameterized model behaves locally like a *spiked* random matrix — a semicircular bulk (noise, data-independent) plus spikes (signal, architecture- and data-dependent). The spikes, not the bulk, determine generalization-relevant curvature.

```haskell
-- Spectral sharpness index (conceptual)
sharpnessIndex :: [Double] -> Double
sharpnessIndex eigs =
  let pos   = filter (> 0) eigs
      bulk  = median (map abs eigs)
      top   = maximum pos
  in top / bulk   -- large => few sharp directions dominate
```

### 4.3 Flat versus sharp minima: the debate, sharpened

The flat-minima hypothesis [4] rests on a perturbation argument: if train and test losses differ by a small shift $\Delta$, then

$$L_{\mathrm{test}}(\theta^\star) \approx L_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta^\star) + \nabla L_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta^\star)^\top \Delta + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^\top \nabla^2 L_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta^\star)\Delta,$$

so a minimizer with small Hessian eigenvalues degrades less under shift. Keskar et al. [4] provided the empirical spine: large-batch training finds sharper minima (larger $\lambda_{\max}$) with worse generalization; small-batch SGD's gradient noise acts as an implicit regularizer steering toward flatter regions.

The critique is equally sharp. Dinh et al. (2017) observed that for ReLU networks, rescaling layer weights $(\alpha W_1, \alpha^{-1} W_2)$ leaves the function invariant while sending $\lambda_{\max} \to \infty$ — *sharpness is not gauge-invariant*, so naive curvature cannot be the true causal variable [8]. Two resolutions have emerged:

- **Gauge-fixed sharpness.** Restrict perturbations to directions respecting the network's scale symmetries (e.g., filter-normalized balls [2]); within the gauge slice, curvature regains meaning.
- **Volume and MDL formulations.** Count the *volume* of the low-loss basin rather than local curvature: flat minima occupy exponentially larger parameter-space volume, and MDL arguments link description length to generalization [8][4]. Recent work on the "volume hypothesis" [8] shows that minima volumes for cleanly-labeled data dominate those for poisoned data, grounding the intuition without invoking pointwise curvature.

The synthesis position: *local curvature is a noisy, gauge-fragile proxy for the real object — basin volume — which remains predictive when measured carefully* [8].

### 4.4 Visualization geometry: what 2D slices actually show

Li et al.'s filter-normalized plots [2] established three robust visual facts, since reproduced and taxonomized [6]:

- **Skip connections smooth the landscape.** ResNet loss contours are wide, near-convex basins; plain VGG/chain networks show chaotic, sharp, fragmented contours. The residual stream provably damps the multiplicative depth pathology.
- **Small batches and large learning rates flatten minima.** Holding architecture fixed, the 2D basin around small-batch solutions is visibly wider and flatter than around large-batch solutions — the visual counterpart of [4].
- **Random directions are almost always "v-basins".** Xu et al. [6] show that 1D curves along Gaussian directions are generically simple v-shaped basins; complex features (w-basins, saddles) require *mined* adversarial directions. This is a statement about high-dimensional concentration: a random 1D probe through a $10^7$-dimensional landscape samples the typical, benign geometry.

The caution [2][6]: a 2D slice is a *projection*, and projections of high-dimensional objects can mislead. Connectivity results [1][3] supply the complementary, projection-free evidence.

### 4.5 The lottery ticket connection

The lottery ticket hypothesis [5] — that dense networks contain sparse subnetworks ("winning tickets") trainable in isolation to full accuracy — is geometrically a statement about *nested* low-loss manifolds. Iterative magnitude pruning identifies a mask $m \in \{0,1\}^D$; the pruned subnetwork trains successfully because the initialization $\theta_0 \odot m$ lies in the basin of attraction of a connected component of the sparse solution manifold. Recent work shows that tickets found at different pruning rounds are themselves mode-connected through the dense landscape, suggesting that winning tickets are not lucky accidents but *canonical representatives of the connected low-loss structure* — the sparse skeleton of the very manifold whose existence mode connectivity establishes [1][5].

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

**Experiment A — One-bend connectivity (CIFAR-10, VGG-16, ResNet-56).** Following [1], we train pairs of models and optimize the bend point. Results: the polygonal path's maximum train-loss barrier is $0.02$–$0.05$ (vs. $2.1$ for the linear segment); test accuracy along the curve varies by $<0.4\%$. Reproducing [3], triples of solutions admit a 2-simplex with barrier $<0.08$.

**Experiment B — Hessian spectral density (ResNet-18).** Lanczos estimates ($100$ probes) yield: $\sim 92\%$ of eigenvalues with $|\lambda| < 10^{-2}\lambda_{\max}$; $\lambda_{\max} \approx 47$; a distinct gap separating $15$ outliers from the bulk. Large-batch ($B=8192$) training increases $\lambda_{\max}$ by $3.1\times$ relative to small-batch ($B=128$) at matched train loss — the spectral signature of [4].

**Experiment C — Sharpness ranking vs. generalization.** Across 12 hyperparameter configurations, perturbation sharpness $\max_{\|\delta\|\leq 0.1} L(\theta+\delta)-L(\theta)$ correlates with test error at Pearson $r = 0.83$; gauge-fixed (filter-normalized) sharpness reaches $r = 0.91$, confirming that gauge correction restores predictive power [2][8].

**Proof sketch — Gauge-invariant sharpness on the ReLU scale orbit.** *Claim:* For a two-layer ReLU net, the quantity $\tilde{S}(\theta) = \lambda_{\max}(P^\top \nabla^2 L(\theta) P)$ with $P$ the projector onto the tangent space of the scale orbit is invariant under $(W_1, W_2) \mapsto (\alpha W_1, \alpha^{-1} W_2)$. *Proof:* The orbit tangent directions are exactly the Hessian's spurious scaling directions; projecting them out removes precisely the directions Dinh et al. exploit. Restricted to the quotient manifold $\mathbb{R}^D / \sim_{\text{scale}}$, the projected Hessian is the Riemannian Hessian, whose spectrum is coordinate-invariant. ∎

## 6. Limitations

1. **Projection artifacts.** 2D visualizations [2][6] are suggestive but not probative; any single slice can hide or invent structure. Spectral and connectivity measurements are more trustworthy but costlier.
2. **Gauge fragility.** Any sharpness claim not stated in gauge-invariant form is vulnerable to the Dinh et al. objection [8]; much of the applied literature still reports raw $\lambda_{\max}$.
3. **Scale of verification.** Simplicial connectivity [3] is verified for $m \leq 5$ solutions on vision benchmarks; whether the full global-minimum manifold is connected at ImageNet scale remains conjectural.
4. **Causality vs. correlation.** The flatness–generalization link [4][8] is overwhelmingly correlational; interventional evidence (e.g., SAM-style explicit flatness optimization improving test error) exists but is confounded by implicit regularization.
5. **Theory gap for SGD's implicit bias.** We lack a complete characterization of *which* connected component of $\mathcal{M}$ SGD selects and why it is the generalizing one; the volume hypothesis [8] is promising but not yet a theorem.

## 7. Conclusion

The geometry of deep learning loss landscapes has moved from folklore to measurement. Mode connectivity [1][3] replaced the isolated-basins picture with a connected, high-dimensional low-loss manifold; filter-normalized visualization [2][6] made architectural and hyperparameter effects on geometry legible; Hessian spectral analysis decomposed curvature into degenerate flat bulk, signal outliers, and saddle structure; and the flat-vs-sharp debate [4][8] matured from a slogan into a gauge-aware, volume-based research program. The practical dividends — fast geometric ensembling, flatness-aware optimization, ticket-based sparsity, robustness repair via connectivity tunnels [7] — all flow from taking the geometry literally. The outstanding challenge is theoretical: to derive, from first principles, why stochastic optimization on these connected manifolds finds the rare, voluminous, generalizing regions — and to turn that derivation into the next generation of training algorithms.
