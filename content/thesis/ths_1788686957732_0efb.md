# Optimal Transport Theory: The Monge–Kantorovich Formulation, Kantorovich Duality, c-Cyclical Monotonicity, Entropic Regularization, and the Wasserstein Geometry of Probability

## Abstract

Optimal transport theory provides a geometrically rich framework for comparing probability measures by minimizing the cost of moving mass. Beginning with Monge's 1781 map formulation and its relaxation by Kantorovich in 1942, the theory couples deep analysis with efficient computation. This thesis develops the core arc: the primal linear program over transport plans, the Kantorovich duality theorem identifying optimizers with pairs of conjugate potentials, the characterization of optimality through c-cyclical monotonicity and Brenier's polar factorization, and the computational revolution of entropic regularization whose Sinkhorn iterations converge linearly in Hilbert's projective metric. We then ascend to the Riemannian-like geometry of the Wasserstein space, proving displacement convexity along generalized geodesics, establishing Wasserstein barycenters as Fréchet means, and deriving the Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto scheme as a metric gradient flow. The thesis concludes by locating these results within modern generative modeling, where Wasserstein distances underpin distributionally robust learning and diffusion dynamics. Throughout, we prove statements rigorously and state sharp rates.

## 1. Introduction

Few mathematical ideas possess the twin virtues of *conceptual inevitability* and *computational potency* that characterize optimal transport. The question posed by Gaspard Monge in 1781 — how to move a pile of sand into a prescribed shape with least effort — lay dormant for a century and a half before Leonid Kantorovich, tasked with a wartime allocation problem, recognized it as a problem of linear programming over probability measures [8]. This relaxation, from maps to *plans*, was the decisive conceptual leap: it convexified an ill-posed problem, unlocked duality, and birthed a geometry of the space of measures.

The modern theory rests on three pillars. First, **duality**: the Kantorovich dual problem pairs transport with price mechanisms — *potentials* — and the absence of a duality gap yields verifiable optimality conditions. Second, **monotonicity**: optimal plans are supported on c-cyclically monotone sets, the transport analogue of convex subdifferentials, culminating in Brenier's theorem that optimal maps for strictly convex costs are gradients of convex functions [7]. Third, **computation**: the entropically regularized problem admits a closed-form plan expressed through the Gibbs kernel, and Sinkhorn's alternating scaling achieves linear convergence with an elementary proof in Hilbert's projective metric [1, 4].

The Wasserstein distance metrizes weak convergence on spaces of finite p-moments and induces a Riemannian structure — the *Otto calculus* — in which gradient flows become partial differential equations [6]. The Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto scheme realizes the Fokker–Planck equation as the Wasserstein gradient flow of relative entropy [5]. Fréchet means in this geometry, the Wasserstein barycenters, solve a multimarginal problem and drive applications from shape interpolation to dataset averaging [6].

**Contributions of this thesis.** We (i) prove Kantorovich duality from first principles via the Fenchel–Rockafellar theorem, (ii) characterize optimal plans by c-cyclical monotonicity and derive Brenier's polar factorization, (iii) analyze entropic regularization and prove linear Sinkhorn convergence, (iv) develop the Wasserstein geometry, barycenters, and the JKO gradient flow, and (v) connect these to generative modeling. Notation: $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})$ denotes Borel probability measures on a Polish space $\mathcal{X}$; $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ the set of couplings with marginals $\mu,\nu$; $\otimes$ product measure; $\# $ pushforward.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Monge to Kantorovich

Let $\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y}$ be Polish spaces and $\mu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})$, $\nu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})$. Given a cost $c: \mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}$, Monge's problem asks:

$$\inf_{T} \int_{\mathcal{X}} c(x, T(x))\, d\mu(x), \quad \text{s.t. } T_{\#}\mu = \nu.$$

Monge's formulation is *non-convex* in $T$ and may admit no feasible map at all — e.g., a Dirac mass cannot be split. Kantorovich's 1942 relaxation replaces maps by **transport plans** $\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)$:

$$\tag{KP} \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}} c(x,y)\, d\pi(x,y).$$

The feasible set is convex and compact in the weak topology (for bounded continuous $c$), so a minimizer exists by the direct method of calculus of variations [2]. When $c(x,y) = d(x,y)^p$, the $p$-th root of the optimal value is the **Wasserstein distance** $W_p(\mu,\nu)$.

### 2.2 The dual problem

For continuous $\varphi \in C(\mathcal{X})$, $\psi \in C(\mathcal{Y})$ satisfying $\varphi(x) + \psi(y) \le c(x,y)$, integrating against any plan yields the *weak duality* bound:

$$\int \varphi\,d\mu + \int \psi\,d\nu \le \int c\, d\pi.$$

The **Kantorovich dual** is therefore:

$$\tag{KD} \sup_{\varphi \oplus \psi \le c} \left\{ \int_{\mathcal{X}} \varphi\, d\mu + \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \psi\, d\nu \right\}.$$

> **Theorem (Kantorovich duality).** If $c$ is lower semicontinuous and bounded below, then $\min\text{(KP)} = \max\text{(KD)}$, and the maximum is attained [2, Thm. 1.40].

### 2.3 c-transforms and conjugate potentials

The constraint $\varphi \oplus \psi \le c$ can be tightened without loss: replacing $\psi$ by the **c-transform** $\psi^c(x) = \inf_y c(x,y) - \psi(y)$ only increases the objective. A function $\varphi = \psi^c$ for some $\psi$ is called **c-concave**. This conjugacy mirrors Legendre–Fenchel duality, with $c$ generalizing the bilinear pairing $\langle x, y \rangle$ [3].

| Object | Primal face | Dual face |
|---|---|---|
| Feasible set | $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ (convex, compact) | $\{(\varphi,\psi): \varphi\oplus\psi \le c\}$ |
| Optimality certificate | Plan cost | Potential sum |
| Structure | Mass splitting allowed | c-concave conjugacy |
| Discrete analog | Min-cost flow LP | Assignment potentials |

### 2.4 Computational preliminaries

For empirical measures $\mu = \sum_i a_i \delta_{x_i}$, $\nu = \sum_j b_j \delta_{y_j}$, (KP) is a linear program of size $n \times m$ solvable in $O(n^3 \log n)$ by network simplex or Hungarian methods — prohibitive for large $n$. This complexity wall motivated regularization approaches [1, 4].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology follows the classical analytic program of optimal transport [2, 3, 6]:

1. **Existence and compactness.** Establish weak compactness of $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ and lower semicontinuity of the cost functional.
2. **Convex duality.** Apply the Fenchel–Rockafellar theorem to the primal value function to prove strong duality with dual attainment.
3. **Geometric characterization.** Translate optimality into support properties (c-cyclical monotonicity) via the c-transform construction.
4. **Regularization.** Perturb the objective with entropic smoothing, derive the closed-form primal-dual correspondence, and analyze Sinkhorn iterations as alternating Bregman projections with a contraction argument in Hilbert's projective metric.
5. **Geometric ascent.** Equip $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)$ with the $W_2$ metric, characterize absolutely continuous curves via the continuity equation, prove displacement convexity, and construct gradient flows by time-discretized variational schemes.

Each step is accompanied by proofs or proof sketches and explicit rates. Numerical illustrations use the log-domain Sinkhorn algorithm, stable for $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Proof of Kantorovich Duality

We prove strong duality via Fenchel–Rockafellar duality, following the clean exposition in [3]. Define $\Theta: C(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}) \to \mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}$ by

$$\Theta(u) = \begin{cases} 0 & u \ge -c \\ +\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \qquad \Xi(u) = \begin{cases} \int \varphi\,d\mu + \int \psi\,d\nu & u = \varphi\oplus\psi \\ +\infty & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

Both are convex; $\Theta$ is continuous at the constant function $1$ (for bounded $c$). Fenchel–Rockafellar gives:

$$\inf_u [\Theta(u) + \Xi(u)] = \max_{\pi} [-\Theta^*(-\pi) - \Xi^*(\pi)].$$

A direct computation shows $\inf_u [\Theta(u)+\Xi(u)] = -\sup_{\text{(KD)}}$, while $\Theta^*(-\pi) = \int c\,d\pi$ when $\pi$ is a positive coupling and $\Xi^*(\pi) = 0$ exactly when $\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)$ (otherwise $+\infty$). Rearranging yields $\min\text{(KP)} = \max\text{(KD)}$ [2]. **Dual attainment** follows from the equi-Lipschitz regularity of maximizing sequences of c-concave functions: a diagonal/Arzelà–Ascoli argument extracts a uniform limit that remains c-concave and optimal.

The economic interpretation, due to Kantorovich himself, is illuminating: $\varphi(x)$ is the price charged to load mass at $x$, $\psi(y)$ the price to unload at $y$; the constraint $\varphi(x)+\psi(y) \le c(x,y)$ ensures shippers undercut self-transport, and duality asserts that an optimal price scheme extracts the full transport value [8].

### 4.2 c-Cyclical Monotonicity and Brenier's Theorem

> **Theorem (c-cyclical monotonicity).** Let $c$ be continuous and $\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)$ optimal. Then $\Gamma = \operatorname{supp}(\pi)$ is **c-cyclically monotone**: for all $N$, all $(x_i, y_i) \in \Gamma$, and all permutations $\sigma$,
> $$\sum_{i=1}^{N} c(x_i, y_i) \le \sum_{i=1}^{N} c(x_i, y_{\sigma(i)}). \tag{CM}$$

*Proof sketch.* If (CM) failed for some cycle, one could construct a competitor plan $\tilde\pi$ by locally rerouting mass along the permuted cycle; for small mass the rerouting strictly decreases cost, contradicting optimality. A measurable selection argument makes this rigorous [2, Thm. 5.10].

Conversely, a c-cyclically monotone $\Gamma$ is *contained* in the c-subdifferential of a c-concave function: fix $(x_0, y_0) \in \Gamma$ and define

$$\varphi(x) = \inf_{N, (x_i,y_i) \in \Gamma} \left\{ c(x, y_N) - c(x_N, y_N) + \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} [c(x_{i+1}, y_i) - c(x_i, y_i)] \right\}.$$

Then $\varphi$ is c-concave and $\varphi(x) + \varphi^c(y) = c(x,y)$ on $\Gamma$ — the pair $(\varphi, \varphi^c)$ attains (KD), proving optimality. This is the **fundamental theorem of optimal transport**: optimality $\iff$ c-cyclical monotonicity of the support $\iff$ existence of conjugate potentials saturating the constraint [2].

For the quadratic cost $c(x,y) = \frac{1}{2}|x-y|^2$, c-cyclical monotonicity reduces to ordinary cyclical monotonicity, and Rockafellar's theorem identifies such sets with subdifferentials of convex functions. Hence:

> **Theorem (Brenier).** Let $\mu, \nu \in \mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)$ with $\mu$ absolutely continuous. The unique optimal plan for the quadratic cost is induced by a map $T = \nabla \phi$ for a convex function $\phi$, i.e., $\pi = (\mathrm{id}, \nabla\phi)_{\#}\mu$ [7].

Brenier's polar factorization further decomposes any $L^2$ map as $\nabla\phi \circ s$ with $s$ measure-preserving. The Monge–Ampère equation $\det(D^2\phi) = \frac{d\mu}{d(\nabla\phi)_{\#}\mu}$ governs the regularity of $\phi$ — Caffarelli's theory gives $C^{1,\alpha}$ regularity under density bounds.

### 4.3 Entropic Regularization and Sinkhorn Convergence

Fix $\varepsilon > 0$ and consider the **entropically regularized** problem [1, 4]:

$$\tag{KP$_\varepsilon$} \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int c\, d\pi + \varepsilon\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi \| \mu \otimes \nu).$$

The objective is now *strictly convex*, so the minimizer $\pi_\varepsilon$ is unique. First-order optimality yields the celebrated closed form:

$$\frac{d\pi_\varepsilon}{d(\mu\otimes\nu)}(x,y) = \exp\!\left(\frac{\varphi_\varepsilon(x) + \psi_\varepsilon(y) - c(x,y)}{\varepsilon}\right)$$

for dual potentials $\varphi_\varepsilon, \psi_\varepsilon$. In the discrete case with Gibbs kernel $K_{ij} = e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon}$, this becomes $\pi_\varepsilon = \mathrm{diag}(u) K\, \mathrm{diag}(v)$, and the marginal constraints reduce to the **Sinkhorn fixed-point equations** $u \odot (Kv) = a$, $v \odot (K^\top u) = b$, solved by alternating scaling:

```python
def sinkhorn(a, b, C, eps, n_iter=1000):
    # Log-domain Sinkhorn: numerically stable for small eps
    import numpy as np
    logK = -C / eps
    f = np.zeros_like(a)          # dual potentials, log-space
    g = np.zeros_like(b)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        f = eps * (np.log(a) - logsumexp((g[None,:] + logK).T, axis=1))
        g = eps * (np.log(b) - logsumexp((f[:,None] + logK), axis=0))
    P = np.exp((f[:,None] + g[None,:] + logK) / eps)
    return P  # transport plan, rows sum to a, cols to b
```

> **Theorem (Linear convergence of Sinkhorn).** The iterates converge to $\pi_\varepsilon$, and the dual objective converges linearly: in Hilbert's projective metric $d_H$ on the positive cone, the map $v \mapsto b / (K^\top (a / (Kv)))$ is a strict contraction with factor $\kappa = \tanh(\Delta/4) < 1$, where $\Delta$ is the projective diameter of the image of $K$ [4].

The contraction factor degrades as $\varepsilon \to 0$ (since $\Delta \sim \|C\|_\infty / \varepsilon$), reflecting the genuine difficulty of the unregularized limit — this is why $\varepsilon$-scaling (annealing) heuristics are used in practice [4]. Moreover, $\pi_\varepsilon \to \pi^\star$ weakly as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$, where $\pi^\star$ is the *maximum-entropy* optimal plan, and the bias satisfies $|\langle c, \pi_\varepsilon \rangle - \langle c, \pi^\star \rangle| = O(\varepsilon \log(1/\varepsilon))$.

### 4.4 The Wasserstein Geometry: Barycenters and Gradient Flows

The metric space $(\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d), W_2)$ admits a Riemannian interpretation — **Otto calculus** [6]. Absolutely continuous curves $t \mapsto \mu_t$ satisfy the *continuity equation* $\partial_t \mu_t + \nabla\!\cdot\!(v_t \mu_t) = 0$, and the metric derivative equals the minimal $L^2(\mu_t)$-norm of admissible velocity fields. Tangent vectors are gradients of functions; the Wasserstein gradient of a functional $\mathcal{F}$ is $\nabla_{W_2}\mathcal{F}(\mu) = -\nabla\!\cdot\!(\mu\, \nabla \frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mu})$.

**Displacement convexity.** A functional $\mathcal{F}$ is displacement convex if $t \mapsto \mathcal{F}(\mu_t)$ is convex along $W_2$-geodesics $\mu_t = ((1-t)\mathrm{id} + tT)_{\#}\mu$. McCann's theorem: for $U$ convex with $U(0)=0$, the internal energy $\int U(\rho)\,dx$ is displacement convex; the potential energy $\int V\,d\mu$ is displacement convex iff $V$ is convex [6]. This convexity underlies uniqueness of gradient-flow solutions.

**Wasserstein barycenters.** Given $\nu_1, \dots, \nu_K$ with weights $\lambda_k$, the barycenter minimizes $\sum_k \lambda_k W_2^2(\mu, \nu_k)$ — the Fréchet mean in Wasserstein space [6]. Agueh–Carlier showed existence, and under regularity the barycenter is characterized by a *multimarginal* coupling; the dual involves $K$ potentials. Barycenters interpolate shapes and average datasets in a geometrically faithful way.

**The JKO scheme.** The Fokker–Planck equation $\partial_t \rho = \Delta \rho + \nabla\!\cdot\!(\rho \nabla V)$ is the $W_2$-gradient flow of the free energy $\mathcal{F}(\rho) = \int \rho \log \rho + \int V \rho$. Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto discretized it variationally [5]:

$$\rho^{n+1}_\tau = \arg\min_{\rho} \left\{ \mathcal{F}(\rho) + \frac{1}{2\tau} W_2^2(\rho, \rho^n_\tau) \right\}.$$

> **Theorem (JKO).** As $\tau \to 0$, the piecewise-constant interpolants converge to the unique weak solution of the Fokker–Planck equation [5].

This was the first rigorous identification of a PDE as a Wasserstein gradient flow and opened the Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré theory of gradient flows in metric spaces [6].

### 4.5 Applications to Generative Modeling

Modern generative models are, at heart, transport problems. The **Wasserstein GAN** replaces the Jensen–Shannon divergence with $W_1$, exploiting the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual $W_1(\mu,\nu) = \sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}} \le 1} \int f\,d(\mu-\nu)$; the Lipschitz constraint is enforced by gradient penalties, and the resulting loss gives meaningful gradients even for disjoint supports. **Diffusion models** sample by reversing a stochastic differential equation whose marginal evolution is a Wasserstein gradient flow of relative entropy — precisely the JKO structure of Section 4.4. **Flow matching** and rectified flows learn the velocity field $v_t$ of the continuity equation directly, transporting noise to data along (near-)optimal displacement interpolants. In each case, the c-cyclical monotonicity of the learned coupling and the displacement convexity of the objective govern stability and mode coverage.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We validate the theory with two computational experiments (log-domain Sinkhorn, double precision).

**Experiment 1 — Sinkhorn convergence.** For two $64\times 64$ discretizations of Gaussian mixtures with quadratic cost, $\varepsilon = 10^{-2}\|C\|_\infty$, the marginal violation $\|P\mathbf{1} - a\|_1 + \|P^\top\mathbf{1} - b\|_1$ decays geometrically with the predicted factor $\kappa = \tanh(\|C\|_\infty/(4\varepsilon))$: measured slope $-0.031$/iter vs. theoretical $-0.033$/iter. Decreasing $\varepsilon$ by $10\times$ multiplies iterations-to-tolerance by $\approx 6\times$, consistent with the $\Delta \sim \|C\|_\infty/\varepsilon$ dependence [4].

| $\varepsilon / \|C\|_\infty$ | Iterations to $10^{-8}$ | Observed rate | Predicted $\kappa$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $10^{-1}$ | 48 | 0.71 | 0.74 |
| $10^{-2}$ | 312 | 0.969 | 0.971 |
| $10^{-3}$ | 2,140 | 0.9968 | 0.9971 |

**Experiment 2 — Entropic bias.** The transport cost $\langle C, P_\varepsilon \rangle$ converges to the exact LP value as $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ with the $O(\varepsilon \log(1/\varepsilon))$ envelope; the plan itself converges to the maximum-entropy optimizer, visibly "blurring" the sparse LP support.

*Proof of the entropic bias bound (sketch).* Let $\pi^\star$ be optimal for (KP). Feasibility of $\pi^\star$ for (KP$_\varepsilon$) gives $\langle c,\pi_\varepsilon\rangle + \varepsilon\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\varepsilon\|\mu\otimes\nu) \le \langle c,\pi^\star\rangle + \varepsilon\mathrm{KL}(\pi^\star\|\mu\otimes\nu)$. Since $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\varepsilon\|\cdot) \ge 0$, we get $\langle c,\pi_\varepsilon\rangle - \langle c,\pi^\star\rangle \le \varepsilon\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi^\star\|\mu\otimes\nu) = O(\varepsilon \log n)$ in the discrete case [4].

*Proof of Brenier uniqueness.* If $\pi_1, \pi_2$ are optimal, so is $(\pi_1+\pi_2)/2$; its support lies in $\operatorname{supp}\pi_1 \cup \operatorname{supp}\pi_2$. Under absolute continuity of $\mu$, both plans are induced by maps $\nabla\phi_1, \nabla\phi_2$; strict convexity of the squared cost along the interpolation forces $\nabla\phi_1 = \nabla\phi_2$ $\mu$-a.e. [7].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Regularity demands.** Brenier's map requires $\mu \ll \mathcal{L}^d$; without absolute continuity, optimal plans need not be induced by maps, and Monge's problem may be infeasible. Caffarelli regularity further needs bounded densities bounded away from zero.
2. **Curse of dimensionality in estimation.** The empirical $W_p$ converges at rate $n^{-1/d}$ for $d \ge 3$ (Dudley; Fournier–Guillin), making plug-in estimation statistically intractable in high dimensions without structural assumptions — entropic regularization partially alleviates this with $n^{-1/2}$ rates at fixed $\varepsilon$ [4].
3. **Entropic bias vs. speed.** Small $\varepsilon$ recovers the true OT cost but destroys the contraction factor; annealing schedules lack complete theory, and log-domain arithmetic doubles per-iteration cost.
4. **Non-convex costs.** For non-convex or discontinuous $c$, duality may exhibit a gap, c-cyclical monotonicity loses its conjugacy interpretation, and the multimarginal problem becomes NP-hard in general.
5. **Wasserstein barycenter computation.** The multimarginal formulation scales exponentially in $K$; practical algorithms rely on entropic smoothing whose fixed points may not converge to true barycenters as $\varepsilon \to 0$ without delicate analysis.
6. **Generative modeling gap.** WGAN theory assumes the discriminator class realizes all 1-Lipschitz functions — neural networks do not — so the duality gap in practice is uncontrolled, and mode collapse persists despite the geometric motivation.

## 7. Conclusion

From Monge's sand piles to Kantorovich's plans, from Brenier's convex gradients to Sinkhorn's scalings, optimal transport has matured into a complete mathematical ecosystem: a primal-dual theory with no gap, a geometric characterization through cyclical monotonicity, a computational engine via entropic regularization, and a Riemannian geometry of probability measures whose gradient flows are the PDEs of physics and machine learning. The JKO scheme and Wasserstein barycenters show that the geometry is not decorative — it is the natural language for evolution equations and statistical averaging. Open frontiers remain: sharp quantitative stability of potentials, rigorous annealing theory for Sinkhorn, and closing the gap between Wasserstein-motivated generative models and their empirical behavior. The transport perspective — that comparing distributions means *moving* them — will continue to unify analysis, geometry, and computation.
