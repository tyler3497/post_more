# Gromov–Witten Theory: Stable Maps, Virtual Fundamental Classes, Localization, and the Mirror-Symmetric Computation of Curve Counts

## Abstract

Gromov–Witten theory converts the classical problem of counting curves on algebraic varieties into intersection theory on moduli spaces of stable maps. This thesis presents the complete architecture of that conversion: Kontsevich's moduli stack of stable maps, its expected dimension, and the Behrend–Fantechi virtual fundamental class built from the intrinsic normal cone and a perfect obstruction theory; the Kontsevich–Manin axioms of cohomological field theories, including the WDVV associativity equations and quantum cohomology; and Atiyah–Bott localization in its virtual form, reducing curve counts to sums over decorated graphs. These foundations power the crown-jewel application: the mirror-symmetric computation of Candelas, de la Ossa, Green, and Parkes, which predicts that the general quintic threefold contains 2875 lines, 609250 conics, and 317206375 twisted cubics — predictions placed on rigorous footing by Givental's equivariant formalism. We give proofs, explicit formulas, and computational checks, and close with the theory's limitations in higher genus and non-convex settings.

---

## 1 Introduction

The question "how many curves of a given type lie on a given variety?" is among the oldest in geometry. Nineteenth-century geometers answered the first great cases by hand: Cayley and Salmon proved in 1849 that a smooth cubic surface contains exactly **27 lines**, and Schubert's calculus of the 1870s turned intersection theory on Grassmannians into a systematic enumerative engine [1]. But Schubert's methods were heuristic, the enumerative geometry of the twentieth century stalled on higher-degree problems, and the famous number **2875** — the count of lines on a quintic threefold, due to Clemens and Katz — required a tour de force of classical degeneration techniques.

Three independent developments in the late 1980s and early 1990s changed everything:

1. **Gromov's pseudoholomorphic curves** (1985), which gave symplectic geometers a flexible substitute for algebraic curves;
2. **Witten's topological sigma models**, which predicted that curve counts assemble into a *quantum* deformation of cohomology;
3. **Mirror symmetry** (1991), when Candelas, de la Ossa, Green, and Parkes used periods of a mirror family to predict infinitely many curve counts on the quintic — predictions no classical method could verify at the time.

The mathematical response was swift. Kontsevich introduced the moduli stack of **stable maps** and derived a recursion computing all rational plane curve numbers [1]; Behrend and Fantechi constructed the **virtual fundamental class** that makes curve counting rigorous in arbitrary genus [2][3]; and Givental proved the mirror theorems that vindicated the physicists [6]. This thesis explains that edifice from the ground up.

Our central claim is that Gromov–Witten theory is *intersection theory done right on the wrong space*: the moduli spaces are singular, oversized, and stacky, yet a canonically defined virtual class of the expected dimension restores enumerative meaning — and localization plus mirror symmetry make the resulting numbers computable.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Classical enumerative geometry

Schubert's problem of four lines — how many lines in $\mathbb{P}^3$ meet four given lines? — is answered by intersecting Schubert cycles in the Grassmannian $G(2,4)$; the answer is 2. The modern formulation replaces ad hoc intersection with Chow rings, but the philosophy persists: *counts are intersection numbers*. The limitation of classical methods is **compactification**: the space of smooth curves of degree $d$ in $\mathbb{P}^r$ is not compact, and naive compactifications (e.g., the Hilbert scheme, the Chow variety) have boundary components of uncontrolled dimension that corrupt intersection numbers.

### 2.2 The symplectic origin

Gromov's insight was to count *parametrized* curves — maps $f: \Sigma \to X$ from a Riemann surface — rather than their images. For a generic almost-complex structure $J$, the moduli space of $J$-holomorphic maps is a smooth manifold of the expected dimension, and Ruan–Tian (1994) built symplectic invariants from it in the semi-positive case. The algebraic analogue must confront non-generic targets, where transversality fails and the moduli space acquires excess dimension. Resolving this is the entire content of §3.

### 2.3 Why stacks?

A map $f: C \to X$ may have nontrivial automorphisms (e.g., a multiple cover composed with a deck transformation), so the moduli functor is not representable by a scheme. Deligne–Mumford stacks are the correct category: they remember stabilizers, admit well-behaved intersection theory (Vistoli, Kresch), and the resulting counts are rational numbers — denominators recording precisely the automorphisms that classical geometry forgot.

## 3 Methodology: Stable Maps, Expected Dimension, and the Virtual Class

### 3.1 The moduli stack of stable maps

Let $X$ be a smooth projective variety and $\beta \in H_2(X,\mathbb{Z})$ an effective curve class.

> **Definition (Kontsevich).** A *stable map* of genus $g$, class $\beta$, with $n$ marked points is a tuple $(C, p_1,\dots,p_n, f)$ where $C$ is a connected nodal curve of arithmetic genus $g$, the $p_i$ are distinct smooth marked points, $f: C \to X$ is a morphism with $f_*[C] = \beta$, and every contracted component of genus 0 (resp. genus 1) carries at least 3 (resp. 1) special points — the **stability condition**, which guarantees finite automorphism groups.

The resulting moduli stack $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)$ is a proper Deligne–Mumford stack [1]. It comes with evaluation maps $\mathrm{ev}_i: \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta) \to X$ and cotangent line classes $\psi_i = c_1(\mathbb{L}_i)$ at the markings.

### 3.2 Expected dimension

Deformation theory predicts the dimension of the moduli space at a map $f$ to be $h^0(C, f^*T_X) - h^1(C, f^*T_X)$ plus the $3g-3+n$ moduli of the domain. By Riemann–Roch this equals

$$ \mathrm{vdim}\, \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta) = (1-g)(\dim X - 3) + \int_\beta c_1(T_X) + n. $$

For a Calabi–Yau threefold ($\dim X = 3$, $c_1 = 0$) the expected dimension collapses to $n$: with no markings, genus-$g$ curve counts are *numbers*, the famous $n_d$ of §4.4. In general the actual dimension exceeds vdim — the obstruction space $H^1(C, f^*T_X)$ refuses to vanish — and intersecting on the honest fundamental class gives wrong answers.

### 3.3 The Behrend–Fantechi virtual fundamental class

The remedy, developed in [2] and applied to stable maps in [3], is a homology class of the *expected* dimension defined even when the space is singular:

> **Theorem (Behrend–Fantechi).** Let $Z$ be a Deligne–Mumford stack equipped with a *perfect obstruction theory* $\phi: E^\bullet \to \mathbb{L}_Z$, i.e. a morphism in the derived category from a two-term complex of vector bundles that is an isomorphism on $h^0$ and surjective on $h^1$. Then the *intrinsic normal cone* $\mathfrak{C}_Z$ embeds into the vector bundle stack $h^1/h^0((E^\bullet)^\vee)$, and intersecting with the zero section defines the **virtual fundamental class**
> $$ [Z]^{\mathrm{vir}} := 0^!\,[\mathfrak{C}_Z] \in A_{\mathrm{vd}}(Z), \qquad \mathrm{vd} = \mathrm{rank}\,E^0 - \mathrm{rank}\,E^1. $$

For stable maps the obstruction theory is $E^\bullet = (R\pi_* f^* T_X)^\vee$ relative to the stack of prestable curves, where $\pi: \mathcal{C} \to \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)$ is the universal curve [3]. When $H^1$ vanishes (e.g., genus 0 maps to a convex variety), $[\overline{\mathcal{M}}]^{\mathrm{vir}}$ reduces to the ordinary fundamental class — the construction is a genuine extension, not a replacement.

### 3.4 Gromov–Witten invariants and the CohFT axioms

The (descendant) Gromov–Witten invariants are the intersection numbers

$$ \big\langle \tau_{a_1}(\gamma_1), \dots, \tau_{a_n}(\gamma_n) \big\rangle_{g,\beta}^X := \int_{[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)]^{\mathrm{vir}}} \prod_{i=1}^n \mathrm{ev}_i^*(\gamma_i)\, \psi_i^{a_i}. $$

Kontsevich and Manin proved in [1] that these numbers satisfy a rigid axiomatic system — the axioms of a **cohomological field theory (CohFT)**:

- **$S_n$-covariance and the fundamental class axiom** (forgetting a marking pushes forward correctly);
- **The divisor axiom**, relating $\mathrm{ev}_{n+1}^*(D)$ insertions to $\int_\beta D$;
- **The splitting (gluing) axiom**, expressing invariants on nodal degenerations through the diagonal class — the engine of all recursions;
- **The genus-reduction axiom**, trading a handle for a node.

The genus-zero sector assembles into an associative product on $H^*(X)$, the **quantum product**, governed by the WDVV equations (§4.3). Teleman's notes [7] give the modern axiomatic treatment, including Givental's quantization formalism that lifts genus-zero data to all genera for semisimple theories.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Nodal degeneration and the boundary stratification

The boundary of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)$ parametrizes maps from nodal domains and is stratified by **dual graphs**: vertices record genera and curve classes of components, edges record nodes, half-edges record markings. The splitting axiom [1] states that restricting the virtual class to a boundary divisor factorizes through the fiber product over the diagonal $\Delta \subset X \times X$. This is not a technicality — it is the geometric source of every recursion in the subject. Kontsevich's graph-sum formula for $\mathbb{P}^r$ (§4.2) and the WDVV equations (§4.3) are both shadows of boundary geometry.

### 4.2 Atiyah–Bott localization and virtual localization

When a torus $T = (\mathbb{C}^*)^m$ acts on $X$, it acts on $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)$, and the Atiyah–Bott localization theorem reduces integrals to the fixed locus:

> **Theorem (Atiyah–Bott).** For a compact oriented $T$-manifold $M$ and $\alpha \in H_T^*(M)$,
> $$ \int_M \alpha = \sum_{F \subset M^T} \int_F \frac{i_F^*\alpha}{e_T(N_F)}, $$
> summing over connected components of the fixed locus with the equivariant Euler class of the normal bundle in the denominator.

Graber and Pandharipande extended this to the virtual setting: the virtual class localizes as

$$ [\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)]^{\mathrm{vir}} = \sum_{\Gamma} \frac{1}{|\mathrm{Aut}(\Gamma)|}\, i_* \frac{[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_\Gamma]^{\mathrm{vir}}}{e_T(N_\Gamma^{\mathrm{vir}})}, $$

where $\Gamma$ runs over **decorated graphs**: vertices map to $T$-fixed points of $X$, edges to $T$-invariant rational curves with degree labels, and the virtual normal bundle's Euler class contributes explicit edge and vertex factors.

For $X = \mathbb{P}^2$ this yields Kontsevich's celebrated recursion for $N_d$, the number of rational degree-$d$ plane curves through $3d-1$ general points:

$$ N_d = \sum_{d_1+d_2=d} N_{d_1} N_{d_2} \left( d_1^2 d_2^2 \binom{3d-4}{3d_1-2} - d_1^3 d_2 \binom{3d-4}{3d_1-1} \right), \qquad N_1 = 1. $$

The same computation in a functional language:

```haskell
-- Kontsevich's recursion for rational plane curves (arXiv:hep-th/9402147)
kontsevich :: Int -> [Integer]
kontsevich n = ns where
  ns = 1 : 1 : [ nd d | d <- [3..n] ]
  nd d = sum [ nsAt (d1-1) * nsAt (d2-1)
             * (fromIntegral (d1*d1*d2*d2) * choose (3*d-4) (3*d1-2)
             -  fromIntegral (d1^3*d2)     * choose (3*d-4) (3*d1-1))
             | d1 <- [1..d-1], let d2 = d - d1 ]
  nsAt i = ns !! i
  choose n k = product [n-k+1..n] `div` product [1..k]

-- kontsevich 5 == [1,1,12,620,87304]
```

Thus $N_3 = 12$ (the classical 12 nodal cubics through 8 points), $N_4 = 620$, $N_5 = 87304$ — numbers inaccessible to nineteenth-century methods, produced by a graph sum [1].

### 4.3 Quantum cohomology and the WDVV system

The genus-zero three-point functions define the **(small) quantum product**

$$ T_i * T_j = \sum_{\beta, k} \langle T_i, T_j, T_k \rangle_{0,\beta}\, q^\beta\, \eta^{k\ell} T_\ell, $$

deforming the cup product. Associativity $(T_i * T_j) * T_k = T_i * (T_j * T_k)$ is equivalent to a system of third-order PDEs for the genus-zero potential $\Phi$ — the **WDVV equations** (Witten–Dijkgraaf–Verlinde–Verlinde):

$$ \sum_{e,f} \frac{\partial^3 \Phi}{\partial t_a \partial t_b \partial t_e}\, \eta^{ef}\, \frac{\partial^3 \Phi}{\partial t_f \partial t_c \partial t_d} \quad \text{is symmetric in } a,b,c,d. $$

Kontsevich and Manin showed [1] that for Fano varieties these equations, plus finitely many initial data, *determine the entire genus-zero theory*: the recursion of §4.2 is WDVV for $\mathbb{P}^2$ in disguise. The small quantum cohomology ring of $\mathbb{P}^r$ is the clean illustration:

| Variety | Classical cohomology | Small quantum cohomology |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{P}^1$ | $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2)$ | $\mathbb{Z}[x,q]/(x^2 - q)$ |
| $\mathbb{P}^2$ | $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^3)$ | $\mathbb{Z}[x,q]/(x^3 - q)$ |
| $\mathbb{P}^r$ | $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^{r+1})$ | $\mathbb{Z}[x,q]/(x^{r+1} - q)$ |

The relation $x^{r+1} = q$ says that $r+1$ hyperplane insertions can be traded for one degree-1 curve — enumerative content in algebraic form. Givental's work [6] reframes this as the statement that the $J$-function, a generating series of one-point descendants, satisfies the quantum differential equations, the starting point of the mirror theorems.

### 4.4 Mirror symmetry for the quintic: Picard–Fuchs and the mirror map

Let $X \subset \mathbb{P}^4$ be a smooth quintic threefold. Since $c_1(TX) = 0$, the virtual dimension with no markings is zero, and the genus-zero invariants are honest numbers $N_d$. The physically predicted *instanton numbers* $n_d$ (counts of degree-$d$ rational curves) are related by the multicover formula

$$ N_d = \sum_{k \mid d} \frac{1}{k^3}\, n_{d/k}, $$

recording that a degree-$d$ map may be a $k$-fold cover of a degree-$(d/k)$ curve.

Candelas, de la Ossa, Green, and Parkes considered the mirror family — the Fermat quintic $\{\sum x_i^5 - 5\psi \prod x_i = 0\}$ quotiented by $(\mathbb{Z}/5)^3$ — and computed its periods. The fundamental period

$$ \omega_0(z) = \sum_{d \geq 0} \frac{(5d)!}{(d!)^5}\, z^d, \qquad z = (5\psi)^{-5}, $$

is annihilated by the **Picard–Fuchs operator**

$$ \mathcal{L} = \theta^4 - 5z\,(5\theta+1)(5\theta+2)(5\theta+3)(5\theta+4), \qquad \theta = z\frac{d}{dz}. $$

The mirror map $t = \omega_1/\omega_0$ (with $\omega_1$ the logarithmic period) and the normalized Yukawa coupling

$$ K(q) = 5 + \sum_{d \geq 1} n_d\, \frac{d^3 q^d}{1 - q^d}, \qquad q = e^{2\pi i t}, $$

yield, upon $q$-expansion, the predictions [4][5]:

| $d$ | $n_d$ (instanton number) | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2,875 | Classical (Clemens–Katz) |
| 2 | 609,250 | Classical (Katz) |
| 3 | 317,206,375 | Ellingsrud–Strømme (1996), via localization |
| 4 | 242,467,530,000 | Verified via Givental's mirror theorem |
| 5 | 229,305,888,887,625 | Verified via Givental's mirror theorem |

Morrison's guide [4] translates the physics into algebraic geometry: the Yukawa coupling is a section of a Hodge bundle, the mirror map is a canonical coordinate at a maximally unipotent boundary point, and the $q$-expansion principle is a theorem about variations of Hodge structure. What remains mysterious is *why* the A-model on $X$ equals the B-model on the mirror — a question the Givental formalism answers computationally, if not conceptually.

## 5 Empirical Results: Verified Counts and the Givental Formalism

The predictions of §4.4 did not remain conjectural. Three independent verifications anchor them:

1. **Classical algebraic geometry.** The numbers $n_1 = 2875$ and $n_2 = 609250$ were known classically (Clemens; Katz).
2. **Torus localization.** Ellingsrud and Strømme (1996) evaluated the Bott residue formula on $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,0}(\mathbb{P}^4, 3)$ and confirmed $n_3 = 317206375$ — the first nontrivial check of mirror symmetry by pure mathematics.
3. **Givental's mirror theorem.** Givental [6] proved that for Fano complete intersections (and, via the quantum Lefschetz hyperplane principle, the quintic), the $J$-function equals an explicit hypergeometric $I$-function after the mirror change of variables. Since the $I$-function is the period vector $\omega_0$, this identifies the A-model generating series with the B-model computation — proving *all* $n_d$ simultaneously.

The extraction of $n_d$ from $K(q)$ is Möbius inversion on the divisor lattice and is readily mechanized:

```python
# Sage/Python: instanton numbers from the Yukawa q-expansion
from sympy import symbols, series, mobius

def instanton_numbers(yukawa_series, max_d):
    """yukawa_series: K(q) - 5 as a power series in q.
       Returns dict {d: n_d} via n_d * d**3 = sum_{k|d} mu(k) * a_{d/k}."""
    q = symbols('q')
    coeffs = [int(series(yukawa_series, q, 0, max_d+1).removeO().coeff(q, d))
              for d in range(max_d+1)]
    result = {}
    for d in range(1, max_d+1):
        total = sum(mobius(k) * coeffs[d//k]
                    for k in range(1, d+1) if d % k == 0)
        assert total % d**3 == 0
        result[d] = total // d**3
    return result
```

Feeding the $q$-expansion of the normalized Yukawa coupling reproduces exactly the table of §4.4 — including the integrality of $n_d$, a highly nontrivial property (the raw GW numbers $N_d$ are merely rational). Givental's equivariant formalism further extends these methods: the same graph-sum technology computes the full genus-zero theory of toric complete intersections, and his quantization conjecture [7] lifts it to higher genus for semisimple Frobenius manifolds.

## 6 Limitations

The theory as presented has sharp boundaries:

- **Higher genus.** Beyond genus zero, the Virasoro constraints and Givental's quantization apply only to *semisimple* theories; the quintic is not semisimple, and higher-genus quintic invariants remain largely conjectural (the BCOV holomorphic anomaly program).
- **Non-convex targets.** When $H^1(C, f^*T_X) \neq 0$ generically, the virtual class is indispensable but computationally opaque; explicit graph sums exist mainly for toric targets.
- **Computational complexity.** Localization sums run over decorated graphs whose number grows superexponentially with degree; $n_5$ already strains naive implementations, and genus $g \geq 2$ graph enumeration is infeasible by hand.
- **The mirror map's mystery.** Givental's theorem proves the mirror identity but does not explain *why* a hypergeometric series should know about rational curves — the conceptual core of mirror symmetry remains open.
- **Open and relative theories.** Boundary conditions (Lagrangians, divisors) require relative and open GW theories whose virtual classes and degeneration formulas are substantially more delicate.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Gromov–Witten theory realizes the nineteenth century's enumerative dream with twentieth-century machinery: Kontsevich's stable maps provide the compact moduli problem [1]; Behrend–Fantechi's virtual class supplies the missing transversality [2][3]; Atiyah–Bott localization turns integrals into graph sums; and mirror symmetry — via Picard–Fuchs equations, the mirror map, and Givental's theorems [4][5][6] — converts those sums into closed-form predictions verified to spectacular precision. The number 2875, once a triumph of classical technique, is now the *first term* of an infinite, rigorously understood sequence.

The deepest lesson is methodological: when a moduli problem is too singular for classical intersection theory, one should not repair the space but *enrich the class*. The virtual fundamental class is the prototype of a broader philosophy — derived algebraic geometry, Donaldson–Thomas theory, and beyond — in which enumerative invariants are extracted from obstruction theories rather than from geometry that was never there.
