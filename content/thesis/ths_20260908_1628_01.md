# Algebraic Effects and Handlers: Operational Semantics, Effect Rows, and Compilation Strategies in Koka and OCaml 5

## Abstract

Algebraic effects and their handlers separate the *declaration* of effectful operations from their *interpretation*, generalising exception handlers to arbitrary computational effects equipped with first-class delimited continuations. This thesis develops a unified account of the theory and engineering of this abstraction. We begin with the Plotkin–Pretnar programme: algebraic signatures with equational theories, free models, and handlers as algebra homomorphisms, together with the evaluation-context-based small-step operational semantics that makes the design implementable [1]. We then examine the dominant typing disciplines: Koka's row-polymorphic effect system, where duplicate labels and scoped rows yield principal inference without qualified constraints [2]; Leijen's type-directed selective CPS compilation [3]; and the generalised evidence-passing translation that compiles Koka to C [4]. Next we analyse the deep–shallow handler distinction and its complexity theory [7], and the liberation of rows with handlers in the Links language [8]. Finally we study OCaml 5, where *linear* effect handlers and runtime fibers deliver direct-style user-level concurrency at performance on par with optimised monadic libraries [5][6]. We close with open problems: effect-inference ergonomics, resource-bounded continuations, and the interplay of effects with parallelism.

## 1. Introduction

Computational effects — state, exceptions, nondeterminism, input/output, concurrency — have been treated historically as *second-class citizens*: features of the runtime or of a particular monad stack, neither named by types nor abstracted by programs. The monad-transformer discipline made effects composable in principle, yet at the cost of rigid, upfront commitment to a fixed interpretation and of the notorious *n²* instances problem: combining *n* effects requires *O(n²)* lifting machinery [1]. Algebraic effects, introduced by Plotkin and Power in 2002 and extended with *handlers* by Plotkin and Pretnar in 2009, propose a different contract [1]: effectful *operations* are declared abstractly as a signature; programs invoke them; and *handlers*, installed dynamically around computations, supply their meaning — with access to the delimited continuation of the invocation site.

This single mechanism subsumes exceptions (handlers that never resume), mutable state (handlers that thread a store through resumptions), nondeterminism (handlers that resume multiple times), coroutines, async/await, and probabilistic choice. Crucially, the meaning of an effect is no longer fixed at the point of use: the same program can be executed under a collecting handler, a stateful handler, or a probabilistic handler, enabling the kind of modular experimentation that monads resist.

The past decade has carried this idea from theory into production compilers. Two systems anchor the engineering story. **Koka**, Daan Leijen's research language at Microsoft Research, pairs algebraic effects with a *row-polymorphic* effect type system and compiles them — via selective CPS and, more recently, *evidence passing* — to efficient C [2][3][4]. **OCaml 5** merged the multicore branch's effect handlers into the mainline compiler, giving the language first-class, runtime-supported delimited continuations (*fibers*) restricted to a *linear*, single-shot discipline, and building its concurrency ecosystem (notably the Eio library) on top [5][6].

This thesis is organised as follows. Section 2 recalls the algebraic foundations. Section 3 fixes the methodology: a core fine-grained calculus with evaluation-context operational semantics used as the common reference. Section 4 is the deep dive: operational semantics (§4.1), effect rows and inference (§4.2), the deep/shallow distinction (§4.3), compilation strategies (§4.4), and the OCaml 5 design (§4.5). Section 5 collects empirical results and proof-theoretic guarantees; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background: From Algebraic Theories to Handlers

### 2.1 Algebraic effects

An *algebraic effect* is given by a signature Σ of operation symbols, each with an arity, together with an *equational theory*: a set of equations over terms built from the operations. The canonical examples are instructive [1]:

- **Exceptions** over a set *exc*: operations `raise_e : 1 → 0` for each *e ∈ exc*, with no equations (each raise aborts).
- **Mutable state** over a store *S*: `get : 1 → S` and `put : S → 1`, subject to the familiar laws, e.g. `put(s); get() = put(s); return s` and `put(s); put(s′) = put(s′)`.
- **Nondeterminism**: `flip : 1 → Bool` (or binary choice `or : 1 → 1 + 1`), with associativity, commutativity and idempotence laws.

The *free model* of the theory — terms modulo the equations — induces a monad: the expected computational monad for the effect. Thus the theory-first view recovers the monadic semantics as a derived consequence rather than a primitive commitment [1].

### 2.2 Handlers as homomorphisms

Plotkin and Pretnar's decisive step was to give *exception handlers* an algebraic treatment and then generalise it [1]. A handler for an effect supplies, for each operation `op`, a *clause* that receives the operation's argument and a *continuation* `k` representing the rest of the computation up to the handler. Schematically:

```haskell
handle M with
  { return x       -> N_ret
  ; op_i(p, k)     -> N_i      -- k : B_i -> C, the delimited continuation
  }
```

Denotationally, a handler defines a *model* of the equational theory on the result type, and the handling construct denotes the *unique homomorphism* from the free model extending the return clause — the universal property doing all the work [1]. Operationally, when `M` performs `op(V)` under the handler, evaluation captures the continuation as a function value and invokes the clause. The clause may resume zero times (exceptions, backtracking cut), once (state, I/O), or many times (nondeterministic search, probabilistic sampling).

The classic illustration is the *collect* handler for binary choice [1]:

```ocaml
handle (fun () -> let b = perform Flip in if b then 1 else 2)
with
| effect Flip k -> (continue k true) @ (continue k false)
| x -> [x]
(* evaluates to [1; 2] *)
```

A single mechanism thereby expresses what previously required bespoke control operators.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis is grounded in a common reference formalism: a fine-grained call-by-value calculus λ_eff with explicit syntactic distinction between *values* and *computations*, following Hillerström and Lindley [7]. The syntax is:

```
V, W ::= x | λx.M | ...
M, N ::= return V | let x ← M in N | do ℓ V | handle M with H
H      ::= { return x ↦ M } ⊎ { ℓ p k ↦ N_ℓ }
```

Evaluation contexts `E` identify the *active* redex, and handler frames `handle E with H` delimit continuations. The key reduction rules are:

> **Theorem (Handler dispatch).** `handle E[do ℓ V] with H ⟶ N_ℓ[V/p, (λx. handle E[x] with H)/k]`, provided no inner handler in `E` handles `ℓ`; `handle (return V) with H ⟶ M[V/x]`.

This context-based presentation — championed for compilation by Leijen, who gives a *direct* operational semantics on syntactic contexts rather than a CPS calculus [3] — makes the cost model explicit: capturing `E` as a continuation is the fundamental implementation operation, and every compilation strategy in §4.4 is a different answer to how `E` is represented.

We evaluate each system along four axes: (i) the *type discipline* for effects (rows, presence flags, or none); (ii) the *handler semantics* (deep vs. shallow, multi-shot vs. linear); (iii) the *compilation strategy* (CPS, evidence passing, runtime fibers); and (iv) the *empirical* evidence (benchmarks, proofs of correctness).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Operational Semantics and Delimited Continuations

The evaluation-context semantics above exposes the essential runtime event: *an operation searches outward through the context for its handler, reifying the skipped segment as a continuation*. Contrast this with exceptions, which *discard* the skipped segment. Leijen's direct semantics [3] formulates this without any continuation-passing in the metalanguage:

```
E ::= [] | E e | v E | handle h E        (effectful contexts)
```

with the perform rule

```
handle h · E · (perform op) v ⟶ f v k
  where (op ↦ f) ∈ h, op ∉ bop(E), k = λx. handle h · E · x
```

and `bop(E)` the set of operations *bound* (shadowed) by handlers inside `E`. This "bound operations" side-condition is the operational analogue of effect *masking*: an inner handler for `op` intercepts operations that an outer handler for the same label must not see — the mechanism behind Koka's `mask` and named-handler shadowing [3].

> **Theorem (Type safety).** Well-typed programs do not go wrong: if `∅ ⊢ M : A ! ε` then either `M` is a value, `M` diverges, or `M ⟶ M′` with `∅ ⊢ M′ : A ! ε`. Leijen proves this for the direct semantics [3]; Hillerström and Lindley prove it for their row-typed calculus with both deep and shallow handlers [7].

Two refinements of the basic story matter for what follows. First, *answer-type* generality: the continuation `k` may itself perform effects handled *outside* the current handler, which row systems track via the open row tail (§4.2). Second, *multi-shot* continuations: nothing in the abstract machine forbids invoking `k` twice, which is what makes `collect` work — and what makes compilation hard, since a captured stack segment must then be *copied* rather than moved (§4.4, §4.5).

### 4.2 Effect Rows, Scoped Labels, and Principal Inference

If effects are to be *typed*, the type of a function must name the operations it may perform. Koka writes this as an *effect row*:

```koka
fun sqr(x : int) { print(x); x * x }   // inferred: int -> <io> int
fun pure-sqr(x : int) { x * x }        // inferred: int -> <total> int
```

An effect type is a row `⟨l₁, …, lₙ | μ⟩`: a multiset of effect labels plus a row variable `μ` for the *open tail*, enabling effect polymorphism — the function above works in any context whose effects *include* `io` [2].

Koka's rows differ from the record-row tradition in one decisive way: **labels may be duplicated**, with `⟨exn, exn | μ⟩ ≡ ⟨exn | μ⟩`. This innocuous-looking equivalence dissolves a classical inference obstacle. Unifying `⟨exn | μ₁⟩` with `⟨exn | μ₂⟩` under set-like rows admits two solutions (`μ₂ = ⟨⟩` or `μ₂ = ⟨exn | μ₃⟩`) — Wand's problem — forcing either *lacks constraints* (qualified types à la Haskell) or per-label presence/absence flags with a kind system tracking them, as in Lindley and Cheney's database effect system [2]. Duplicate labels make `μ₂ = ⟨⟩` the *only* solution, recovering principal unification with ordinary first-order techniques [2]. They also give precise types to *elimination forms*: catching exceptions *removes* the label,

```koka
catch : (() -> <exn|μ> α, exception -> μ α) -> μ α
```

which is expressible precisely because rows are not sets [2].

| System | Row discipline | Inference machinery | Handler model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Koka [2] | Duplicate labels, scoped rows | Plain unification, principal | Named, deep, multi-shot |
| Links/Frank [8] | Presence types `Pre(A)/Abs/θ` | Kind system, row variables | Deep (+shallow in λ† [7]) |
| OCaml 5 [5][6] | *No effect types* | — (effects untyped) | Linear, deep, one-shot |

Leijen further shows a novel *simplification* of polymorphic effect types that keeps inferred signatures readable and, crucially, makes the selective CPS translation efficient: functions whose inferred effect is `total` are never CPS-transformed at all [3].

### 4.3 Deep Handlers versus Shallow Handlers

A *deep* handler re-wraps itself around the captured continuation: `k = λx. handle E[x] with H`, so every subsequent operation is handled by the *same* handler — the handler denotes a fold over the computation tree. A *shallow* handler does not: the continuation is `k = λx. E[x]`, and the handler fires only for the *outermost* operation, after which handling is finished unless the clause reinstalls it [7].

The two are inter-encodable, but the encodings are not cost-free. Hillerström and Lindley formalise both in a single calculus λ† and show simulations up to administrative reduction [7]:

- Deep-to-shallow: a shallow handler can mimic a deep one by explicitly re-handling in each clause — at the price of re-traversing the handler on every operation.
- Shallow-to-deep: a deep handler can mimic a shallow one via a forwarding construction, but the encoding is *non-local* and complicates reasoning.

The practical consequence is asymptotic. Implementing *state* with a deep handler threads the store through re-wrapped continuations and is *quadratic* in the number of operations for naive representations; a shallow handler, or a parameterised handler (which threads an explicit parameter through clauses), achieves the expected linear behaviour [7]. This is why Koka and OCaml 5 both effectively provide deep handlers but optimise the common *tail-resumptive* case (§4.4): when the clause ends in `k v` (or `continue k v`), no re-wrapping work is needed beyond a jump.

> **Theorem (Handler equivalence).** Deep and shallow handlers are mutually macro-expressible, but the translations do not preserve intensional cost: there exist programs whose deep-handler interpretation is asymptotically slower than the shallow one under the same abstract machine [7].

### 4.4 Compilation: Selective CPS, Evidence Passing, and the Koka C Backend

Compiling handlers means compiling *continuation capture*. Three generations of strategy exist.

**Selective CPS (Leijen 2017).** The POPL 2017 compilation translates effectful functions to continuation-passing style *selectively*, guided by the effect types: only functions whose effects are non-total pay the CPS cost, and the simplified effect types of §4.2 keep the translation tight [3]. Handlers become functions manipulating explicit continuations; the translation targets existing runtimes (JavaScript, JVM, .NET), proving that algebraic effects need no exotic substrate.

**Evidence passing (Xie & Leijen 2021).** The generalised evidence-passing translation compiles Koka to *C* — a runtime with no notion of handlers at all [4]. The idea: every handler installation constructs an *evidence vector*, a runtime record (vtable) mapping each effect label to its handler implementation. Evidence is threaded as a hidden parameter through effectful calls; performing an operation is a lookup in the current evidence vector followed by an indirect call. The translation is *type-directed*: the effect row of a function determines exactly which evidence slots it needs, and the proof shows the translation *coherent* (independent of typing derivations) and semantics-preserving [4]. The payoff is dramatic for the common case:

- *Tail-resumptive* handlers (state, exceptions-as-effects, reader) compile to plain function calls — **zero overhead** versus hand-written code.
- *Non-resumptive* handlers compile to early returns.
- Only genuinely *multi-shot* resumption allocates heap continuations, via "yield bubbling".

This is the translation behind Koka's current C backend, and it explains how a language with first-class multi-shot continuations can still emit C competitive with direct-style code [4].

**Runtime fibers (OCaml 5).** OCaml took the opposite fork: rather than compiling continuations away, the *runtime* implements them. Fibers are heap-allocated stack segments managed by the GC; `perform` captures the current fiber up to the handler, and `continue`/`discontinue` resume or abort it [5][6].

### 4.5 Linear Handlers, Fibers, and the Multicore OCaml Design

Multicore OCaml — merged into OCaml 5.0 — made a deliberate semantic restriction: **continuations are linear (one-shot)** [5][6]. Resuming a continuation twice is a runtime error. This single decision unlocks the fiber implementation: a captured fiber can be *moved* rather than *copied*, giving O(1) capture and resume with no allocation beyond the fiber itself, and it keeps the generational GC's invariants intact (no duplicated mutable state reachable from two copies of a stack).

The surface syntax reflects the design:

```ocaml
effect Yield : unit
let comp () = perform Yield; print_endline "resumed"

let () =
  try comp () with
  | effect Yield k -> continue k ()   (* one-shot: second continue k () would raise *)
```

Notably, OCaml 5 ships **no effect types** — effects are dynamically scoped and untyped, a pragmatic concession that avoided upheaval in the type checker and the ecosystem [5]. Safety rests instead on the fiber discipline plus the type system's ordinary guarantees; unhandled effects raise at runtime.

The motivation was concurrency, not abstraction for its own sake. Dolan et al. demonstrate a complete user-level web server and scheduler written in direct style over effect handlers, measuring performance **on par with highly optimised monadic concurrency libraries** while retaining sequential-looking code [5]. Sivaramakrishnan's earlier exposition shows the round-robin scheduler in a few dozen lines: each `Yield` suspends the current fiber onto a run queue, and the handler — not the runtime — *is* the scheduler [6]. The runtime thus sheds its monolithic concurrency machinery; schedulers become libraries. This is arguably the largest-scale validation of algebraic effects ever attempted: millions of lines of existing OCaml code had to keep working, single-threaded performance had to be preserved, and the GC had to become parallel — all while adding first-class continuations [5][6].

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Proofs

The literature offers both mechanised-style guarantees and measured performance:

1. **Type safety.** Leijen proves preservation and progress for the direct context-based semantics [3]; Hillerström–Lindley prove it for λ† with deep, shallow, and parameterised handlers, plus a CPS translation proved correct with respect to the operational semantics and an abstract machine derived from it [7].
2. **Translation coherence.** The evidence-passing translation is proved coherent and semantics-preserving in the ICFP 2021 paper; benchmarks show tail-resumptive handlers at parity with hand-written state-passing code [4].
3. **Handler benchmarks.** Dolan et al.'s web-server benchmark is the headline empirical result for handlers-as-concurrency: effect-handler-based direct-style code matches the throughput of monadic concurrency libraries on highly concurrent workloads, falsifying the early assumption that reified continuations are inherently too slow for systems programming [5].
4. **Asymptotics.** The deep/shallow analysis gives the field its cost model: naive deep-handler state is quadratic; shallow and parameterised handlers recover linearity; tail-resumption is the optimisable common case [7][4].
5. **Ecosystem validation.** The OCaml 5 merge (multicore branch PR #10831, February 2022) and the Eio library constitute a natural experiment: user-level schedulers, previously runtime features, are now ordinary libraries composed from effects [6].

## 6. Limitations

Serious limitations remain, and intellectual honesty requires stating them plainly.

- **Multi-shot continuations are expensive everywhere.** Evidence passing confines the cost to genuinely multi-shot programs, but nondeterministic search over deep stacks still copies segments; there is no free lunch, only accurate billing [4].
- **Effect types vs. ecosystem reality.** Koka's inference is a research triumph, but inferred rows can still leak into error messages and documentation in confusing ways; OCaml 5's choice to ship *no* effect types trades static guarantees for adoption, leaving effect mismatches as runtime failures [2][5].
- **Answer-type and resource polymorphism.** The interaction of handlers with polymorphism (the value restriction), resource cleanup (exceptions crossing handler boundaries), and linear resource disciplines is only partially understood; Kammar and Pretnar's work shows no value restriction is needed for handlers, but practical languages still fence certain combinations.
- **Parallelism hazards.** One-shot fibers compose beautifully with domains-based parallelism in OCaml 5, but *multi-shot* continuations and parallelism are a dangerous mix: duplicating a stack that owns locks or performs I/O is unsound in general, which is one more argument for linearity as the default [6].
- **Debugging and tooling.** Stack traces through handlers, profilers attributing time across resumptions, and debuggers that understand evidence vectors all lag the theory — a practical tax on every production deployment.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Algebraic effects and handlers have completed the journey from Plotkin and Pretnar's 2009 generalisation of exception handling [1] to two production-grade realisations that could hardly be more different: Koka's *typed, compiled-away* effects — row-polymorphic inference with duplicate labels, selective CPS, and evidence-passing compilation to C [2][3][4] — and OCaml 5's *untyped, runtime-supported* effects — linear continuations, fibers, and schedulers-as-libraries [5][6]. Between them sit the semantic clarifications that make engineering possible: direct context-based operational semantics, the deep/shallow taxonomy with its cost model [7], and the row-type theory that liberated handlers from monomorphic ghettos [8].

The enduring lesson is architectural: by separating *what an effect is* from *how it is interpreted*, handlers turn the runtime system from a monolith into a library — schedulers, state disciplines, and search strategies become ordinary code. The open frontier is making that power *safe by default*: ergonomic effect types at ecosystem scale, resource-aware continuations, and a cost model programmers can hold in their heads. The foundations, at least, are now theorems rather than folklore.
