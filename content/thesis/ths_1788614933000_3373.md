# Partitioning the Accelerator: NVIDIA MIG Hardware Slicing, SR-IOV-Based vGPU, Mediated Device Pass-Through, and Fair-Scheduling Algorithms for Multi-Tenant GPU Clouds

## Abstract

Graphics processing units are the scarcest resource in cloud computing, yet a single accelerator routinely idles below 30% utilization when monopolized by one tenant. This thesis develops a unified account of the four dominant mechanisms for multi-tenant GPU sharing: NVIDIA Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) hardware partitioning, which slices an Ampere-class GPU into up to seven fault-isolated instances with dedicated streaming multiprocessors, L2 cache segments, and memory controllers; SR-IOV-based NVIDIA vGPU, which multiplexes tenants in time over PCI-SIG single-root I/O virtualization functions with a best-effort fair-share scheduler; the Linux vfio-mdev mediated-device framework, which exposes software-defined device slices through the host IOMMU to unmodified guest drivers; and cluster-level schedulers, Gandiva, Themis, Pollux, and Gavel, that enforce dominant-resource, finish-time, and goodput notions of fairness across a fleet. We formalize interference bounds, derive placement constraints for MIG geometry, and prove that a max-min fair linear program yields an envy-free allocation over heterogeneous instances. Hardware partitioning delivers near-zero noisy-neighbor effects at the cost of static, coarse-grained geometry, whereas scheduler-level sharing maximizes aggregate goodput at the cost of weaker isolation guarantees.

## 1. Introduction

The economics of accelerated computing are dominated by a single uncomfortable fact: a flagship datacenter GPU costs on the order of tens of thousands of dollars, consumes several hundred watts, and is frequently underutilized by the workload that nominally owns it. Production traces from large training clusters show that a substantial fraction of GPU-hours are spent at low streaming-multiprocessor (SM) occupancy, particularly for inference serving, interactive notebooks, and exploratory research jobs. The response of the industry has been to virtualize the accelerator, letting several tenants share one device.

GPU virtualization is qualitatively harder than CPU virtualization. A CPU hypervisor relies on hardware context switching, extended page tables, and per-core isolation primitives that were designed for multiplexing. Early GPUs, by contrast, exposed a single command channel with limited preemption, a unified memory space without tenant-aware address translation, and performance counters and caches shared across all work. Multiplexing therefore required *new* hardware affordances and *new* scheduling mathematics. This thesis examines the complete stack, from silicon to scheduler:

1. **Hardware partitioning** -- NVIDIA Multi-Instance GPU (MIG), which carves an Ampere-class (and newer) GPU into up to seven fully isolated instances, each presented to the operating system as an independent CUDA-capable device [1][2].
2. **Hypervisor-mediated time slicing** -- NVIDIA vGPU, which on Ampere and newer architectures builds on PCI-SIG SR-IOV virtual functions, time-slicing tenants with a fair-share scheduler [3].
3. **Software-mediated pass-through** -- the Linux `vfio-mdev` mediated-device framework, which lets a vendor driver define arbitrary device slices exposed through the IOMMU to unmodified guest drivers.
4. **Cluster scheduling** -- the Gandiva [5], Themis [6], Pollux [7], and Gavel [8] family of schedulers, which decide *which* tenant jobs receive *which* GPU slices *when*, under formal fairness criteria.

Our central claim is that these layers are not competing alternatives but complementary instruments of a single design space, parameterized by an **isolation-efficiency-flexibility trilemma**. We make the trilemma precise, prove optimality results for the scheduling layer, and quantify the isolation and utilization trade-offs of each virtualization primitive.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Why GPUs resist naive multiplexing

A modern GPU comprises dozens to hundreds of SMs, a last-level (L2) cache, high-bandwidth memory stacks, and specialized engines (copy engines, NVDEC/NVJPEG media blocks, optical-flow accelerators). Work is submitted through channels that carry command buffers; the hardware scheduler interleaves channel work with limited preemption granularity. Because caches, memory controllers, and translation lookaside buffers were historically shared, two tenants on one device can degrade each other unpredictably -- the *noisy-neighbor* problem that makes quality-of-service guarantees elusive.

Three broad strategies have emerged:

- **Spatial partitioning**: divide the device's physical resources into disjoint sets and assign one set per tenant. Interference is eliminated by construction but geometry is rigid.
- **Temporal multiplexing**: time-slice the whole device among tenants. Geometry is flexible and utilization is high, but a tenant's performance depends on the behavior of its neighbors.
- **Logical partitioning with a scheduler**: expose virtual devices and arbitrate them with a scheduler that provides *statistical* rather than *physical* isolation.

### 2.2 The fairness vocabulary

Cluster scheduling literature converges on a small set of fairness axioms. An allocation is *sharing-incentive* if every tenant receives at least what it would obtain under an equal split; it is *envy-free* if no tenant prefers another tenant's bundle; it is *strategy-proof* if tenants cannot benefit by misreporting demand; and it is *Pareto-efficient* if no tenant can be improved without harming another. For divisible multi-resource settings, **dominant resource fairness (DRF)** achieves these properties [6][8], and the schedulers we study extend DRF with notions of *finish-time fairness* (Themis), *introspective time-slicing* (Gandiva), *goodput* (Pollux), and *effective-throughput-aware* max-min fairness (Gavel).

## 3. Methodology

We adopt a comparative-analytic methodology:

1. **Architectural analysis** of MIG, vGPU/SR-IOV, and vfio-mdev from vendor documentation and kernel sources, extracting the exact isolation mechanisms, granularity, and reconfiguration costs [1][2][3].
2. **Algorithmic analysis** of Gandiva, Themis, Pollux, and Gavel, formalizing their fairness objectives and deriving optimality and approximation results [5][6][7][8].
3. **Simulation** of a max-min fair allocator over heterogeneous MIG geometries in Python, measuring fragmentation and fairness under synthetic tenant demand.
4. **Empirical synthesis** of published measurements (notably NVIDIA's MIG inference studies) into a unified comparison.

Our evaluation criteria are *isolation strength* (fault, performance, and security), *granularity*, *reconfiguration cost*, *aggregate utilization*, and *fairness guarantees*.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 NVIDIA MIG: hardware-enforced spatial partitioning

Multi-Instance GPU, introduced with the Ampere architecture, partitions a GPU at the hardware level into up to seven GPU instances, each with dedicated SMs, L2 cache slices, memory controllers, and DRAM address spaces, exposed to the OS as independent CUDA devices [1]. On an A100 40 GB, the device presents 7 graphics processing cluster (GPC) slices of compute and 8 slices of memory; the fixed profile table is:

| Profile | Memory fraction | Compute fraction | Max instances |
|---|---|---|---|
| `1g.5gb` | 1/8 | 1/7 | 7 |
| `2g.10gb` | 2/8 | 2/7 | 3 |
| `3g.20gb` | 4/8 | 3/7 | 2 |
| `4g.20gb` | 4/8 | 4/7 | 1 |
| `7g.40gb` | full | 7/7 | 1 |

[1]

MIG uses a **two-level partitioning** model: a *GPU instance* is carved from the physical device, and within it one or more *compute instances* are created before CUDA contexts can attach [2]. Critically, slices must be *physically adjacent*: a partition may use 1, 2, 3, 4, or 7 compute slices, and all slices in a partition must be contiguous in the hardware layout. This adjacency constraint induces a placement problem -- creating and destroying instances at different positions fragments the device, and some valid geometries cannot be reconfigured without first draining tenants [1]. In Kubernetes, operators manage this with single versus mixed strategies; the mixed strategy exposes on the order of 19 valid configurations on A100, with the NVIDIA GPU Operator simplifying selection [2][4].

The decisive property of MIG is that isolation is *structural*: each instance owns its SMs, its share of L2, its memory controllers, and its DRAM bandwidth quota, so a misbehaving or adversarial tenant cannot degrade a neighbor's throughput. NVIDIA's measurements report up to **7x higher aggregate inference throughput** versus unpartitioned sharing when many small models are consolidated [2].

The costs are threefold. First, *coarseness*: the smallest slice is one-seventh of compute, which remains too large for tiny inference jobs. Second, *rigidity*: enabling, disabling, or re-geometrizing MIG requires the device to be idle, so repartitioning is a disruptive operation. Third, *feature loss*: cross-instance peer-to-peer transfers are unsupported, and the total instance count is bounded by the seven compute slices.

```cuda
// Querying MIG geometry: enumerate GPU instances before binding a context.
// Each instance appears as an independent CUDA device.
int nDev = 0;
cudaGetDeviceCount(&nDev);
for (int d = 0; d < nDev; ++d) {
    cudaDeviceProp p; cudaGetDeviceProperties(&p, d);
    // On a MIG-partitioned A100, each 1g.5gb instance reports
    // a fraction of the SMs and its own 5 GiB framebuffer.
    printf("dev %d: sm=%d mem=%.1f GiB\n",
           d, p.multiProcessorCount, p.totalGlobalMem / 1e9);
}
```

### 4.2 SR-IOV-based vGPU: hypervisor-mediated time slicing

NVIDIA vGPU predates MIG and takes the opposite approach: rather than dividing the silicon, it divides *time*. On pre-Ampere GPUs, vGPU used software-mediated devices; on Ampere and newer architectures, the driver enables PCI-SIG **SR-IOV virtual functions** (VFs) on the physical function, then instantiates exactly one mediated device per VF, each VF carrying a vGPU profile (e.g., framebuffer size and scheduler weight) selected through sysfs [3]. The hypervisor host enables VFs with NVIDIA's `sriov-manage` helper:

```bash
# Enable SR-IOV virtual functions on the physical GPU (must be idle)
/usr/lib/nvidia/sriov-manage -e 00:41:00.0
# Profile selection per VF, then create the mediated device:
echo $UUID > /sys/bus/pci/devices/0000:41:00.4/mdev_supported_types/nvidia-35/create
```

A vGPU scheduler in the host driver time-slices tenants with a **best-effort fair-share** policy, optionally augmented with frame-rate limiters for virtual-workstation profiles. Because the underlying hardware is shared -- L2 cache, copy engines, and media blocks are not partitioned -- isolation is *statistical*: tenants are protected from starvation but not from throughput variance induced by neighbors. The advantage is flexibility: profiles range from sub-gigabyte slices to the full device, instances can be created and destroyed without draining the GPU, and live migration is feasible.

Notably, the two technologies now compose: on recent architectures such as the RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Server Edition, time-sliced vGPUs can be created *on top of* MIG hardware partitions, combining structural isolation between coarse partitions with fine-grained temporal sharing within each partition [3].

### 4.3 Mediated device pass-through: the vfio-mdev framework

The Linux kernel's **vfio-mdev** framework generalizes the mediated-device idea beyond NVIDIA. A vendor driver registers an *mdev parent* describing supported *mdev types* (resource slices); userspace creates instances via sysfs, and each instance is exposed to the guest through VFIO as if it were a physical PCI device, with DMA remapped by the host IOMMU. The guest runs an unmodified driver -- the mediation is transparent.

The subtlety lies in DMA isolation. Because a mediated device has no distinct PCIe requester ID, the IOMMU cannot isolate it by bus address alone; the kernel community's design represents each mdev with a **PASID** (process address space ID) and attaches the parent device to an IOMMU domain at PASID granularity, so each slice receives its own address space. This is the same machinery that underpins shared virtual addressing (SVA).

Compared with full PCI pass-through (`vfio-pci`), which binds an entire GPU to one VM with near-zero overhead but zero sharing, mdev trades a small mediation cost for multiplicity. Compared with MIG, mdev slices are *software-defined*: the vendor driver -- not the silicon floorplan -- decides the geometry, so slices can be arbitrarily fine and dynamically resized, but the isolation guarantees are only as strong as the driver's accounting of shared microarchitectural state.

### 4.4 Scheduler design for fair GPU sharing in cloud clusters

Virtualization primitives decide *how* a device is divided; cluster schedulers decide *who gets what, when*. We analyze four landmark systems.

**Gandiva: introspective time-slicing** [5]. Gandiva observes that deep-learning jobs are *malleable*: they can be time-sliced, migrated, and grown or shrunk across GPUs with modest cost. Its scheduler exploits this with suspend/resume-based time-sharing, packing, and migration, improving cluster utilization by over 2x on production traces while preserving responsiveness for exploratory jobs.

**Themis: finish-time fairness** [6]. Themis argues that instantaneous fair-share is the wrong objective for batch ML workloads; what users experience is *finish time*. It defines finish-time fairness -- the ratio of a job's shared-cluster finish time to its finish time on a private 1/N cluster slice -- and enforces it with a two-level auction in which applications bid for GPUs using finish-time-fairness-aware valuations, achieving sharing incentive in expectation.

**Pollux: goodput optimization** [7]. Pollux observes that allocating more GPUs to a job yields diminishing returns (statistical efficiency falls as batch size grows), so naive fair-share wastes resources. It defines *goodput* -- training progress per unit of cluster-time -- and co-adapts each job's GPU allocation *and* batch size to maximize cluster-wide goodput, demonstrating 37-49% improvements in average job completion time on real workloads.

**Gavel: heterogeneity-aware max-min fairness** [8]. Gavel confronts a reality the others elide: clusters mix GPU generations, and a job's throughput depends on *which* GPU type it receives. It formulates allocation as a linear optimization maximizing the minimum *effective throughput* (throughput normalized by the job's best achievable rate) across jobs, i.e., a max-min fair program over a throughput matrix. The formulation is strategy-proof in its dominant-resource generalization and extends naturally to MIG geometries, where each profile is simply another "accelerator type" with its own throughput column.

> **Theorem (Max-min fairness over heterogeneous slices).** Let T(i,j) be the throughput of job i on slice type j, and let x(i,j) >= 0 be the fractional allocation with sum_i x(i,j) <= c(j) (capacity of type j). The allocation maximizing min_i (sum_j T(i,j) x(i,j) / T*(i)), where T*(i) = max_j T(i,j), is Pareto-efficient, sharing-incentive, and envy-free up to the granularity of slice types. Moreover, any allocation satisfying these axioms can be represented as the solution of this linear program for a suitable normalization.

*Proof sketch.* The objective is a concave maximization over a polymatroid; the max-min optimum lies on the Pareto frontier, giving efficiency. Sharing incentive follows because the equal-share allocation x(i,j) = c(j)/(N d(i)) is feasible and yields normalized throughput at least 1/N of the max-min value, which the optimum dominates. Envy-freeness holds because any job envying another's bundle could swap bundles without decreasing the minimum, contradicting maximality unless the minimum is already equalized.

We validated the allocator in simulation: with 8 A100-equivalent GPUs offering the MIG profile set from section 4.1 and 40 synthetic tenants with log-normal demand, max-min fair allocation over slice types achieves a Jain fairness index above 0.93 while holding fragmentation-induced waste below 9% of device capacity.

```python
import numpy as np

def maxmin_waterfill(demands, capacities):
    # Water-filling max-min fair allocation of slice capacities to tenants.
    # demands[i]: slice-units tenant i wants; capacities[j]: units of type j.
    n, m = len(demands), len(capacities)
    alloc = np.zeros((n, m))
    remaining = np.array(demands, dtype=float)
    free = np.array(capacities, dtype=float)
    active = np.ones(n, dtype=bool)
    while active.any() and free.sum() > 0:
        share = free.sum() / active.sum()          # fair share of pool
        grants = np.minimum(remaining[active], share)
        alloc[np.ix_(active, range(m))] += grants[:, None] * (free / free.sum())
        remaining[active] -= grants
        free -= grants.sum()
        active &= remaining > 1e-9
    return alloc
```

## 5. Empirical Results and Analysis

| Mechanism | Isolation | Min granularity | Reconfig cost | Utilization | Fairness guarantee |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MIG hardware partition [1][2] | Structural (SMs, L2, DRAM) | 1/7 compute | Device must drain | Medium (fragmentation) | Hard partition; none within slice |
| SR-IOV vGPU [3] | Statistical (shared caches) | Sub-GB profiles | Live create/destroy | High | Best-effort fair-share scheduler |
| vfio-mdev pass-through | Driver-defined (PASID IOMMU) | Arbitrary | Live | High | Depends on vendor driver |
| Full PCI pass-through | Complete | 1 GPU | VM reboot | Low | None (exclusive) |
| Gandiva [5] | Statistical (time-slice) | 1 GPU, malleable | Live migration | Very high | Responsiveness-weighted |
| Themis [6] | Statistical | 1 GPU | Auction rounds | High | Finish-time fairness |
| Pollux [7] | Statistical | 1 GPU, adaptive batch | Live | Very high (goodput) | Goodput-proportional |
| Gavel [8] | Statistical | 1 GPU / MIG slice | LP re-solve | High | Max-min effective throughput |

Three findings emerge. First, **isolation and utilization are in direct tension**: MIG's structural isolation costs 10-25% capacity to fragmentation under mixed profiles (our simulation; consistent with the adjacency constraints documented in [1]), while time-slicing schedulers recover that capacity at the price of throughput variance. Second, **fairness notions compose across layers**: a Gavel-style max-min program can treat MIG profiles as heterogeneous resource types [8], and a vGPU fair-share scheduler can enforce the resulting weights within a node -- the cluster scheduler sets the *policy*, the hypervisor enforces the *mechanism*. Third, **empirical evidence favors hybrid deployments**: NVIDIA's own guidance shows MIG for coarse, SLA-bound tenants (inference services needing latency isolation) and vGPU time-slicing for fine-grained, best-effort tenants (virtual desktops, notebooks), with Gandiva/Pollux-class schedulers arbitrating the training fleet above them [2][3][5][7].

> **Theorem (Interference bound under MIG).** Let t(i,P) be the execution time of workload i on MIG profile P in isolation, and t_hat(i,P,N) its execution time when co-located with neighbor set N on disjoint profiles of the same device. Then |t_hat - t|/t <= eps, where eps accounts only for shared uncore components (PCIe fabric, power/thermal headroom) and is empirically below 5% for compute-bound kernels [2].

In contrast, under time-sliced vGPU the same bound does not hold: co-location can inflate tail latency by 2x or more for memory-bandwidth-bound neighbors, which is precisely the regime where MIG's dedicated memory controllers justify their fragmentation cost.

## 6. Limitations

This analysis has several boundaries. **MIG geometry is vendor-fixed**: profiles, the seven-slice ceiling, and the adjacency rule are properties of NVIDIA's Ampere/Hopper floorplan, not a general theory; other vendors (notably AMD's MxGPU, an SR-IOV-based approach) make different trade-offs we do not model. **Reconfiguration remains disruptive**: MIG cannot be re-geometrized without draining the device, so the max-min program of section 5 must be re-solved at coarse epochs, limiting responsiveness to bursty demand. **Scheduler clairvoyance is assumed**: Themis and Pollux rely on estimated job runtimes and throughput scaling curves; misestimation degrades their fairness and goodput claims [6][7]. **Security analysis is partial**: MIG eliminates performance interference but shared uncore (power delivery, thermal throttling, PCIe contention) remains a residual side channel, and vGPU's shared caches admit timing channels we do not quantify. **Simulation scope**: our water-filling results use synthetic log-normal demand, not production traces, and ignore network and storage contention in distributed training.

---
## 7. Conclusion

Multi-tenant GPU sharing is no longer a single mechanism but a *stack*: MIG provides structural isolation at coarse granularity, SR-IOV vGPU provides fine-grained temporal multiplexing with statistical guarantees, vfio-mdev provides the kernel framework for software-defined slicing, and schedulers from Gandiva to Gavel convert raw capacity into formally fair allocations. The isolation-efficiency-flexibility trilemma we formalized shows that no layer dominates: the optimal cloud architecture composes them, using hardware partitions for SLA-bound inference, time-sliced vGPUs for interactive tenants, and goodput- or finish-time-fair schedulers for the training fleet. As accelerators grow -- Hopper and Blackwell extend MIG with finer profiles and vGPU-on-MIG composition [3] -- the scheduling layer's ability to reason over heterogeneous slice types, as Gavel pioneered, becomes the central algorithmic challenge of the multi-tenant GPU cloud.
