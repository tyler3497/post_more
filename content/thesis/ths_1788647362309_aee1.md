# Gravitational-Wave Data Analysis: Matched Filtering, Parameter Estimation, and the LIGO Detection Pipeline from Noise to Waveforms

## Abstract

The detection and characterization of gravitational waves from compact binary coalescences rests on a sophisticated edifice of statistical signal processing, Bayesian inference, and waveform modeling that transforms sub-attometer strain fluctuations buried in non-stationary instrumental noise into precision measurements of black hole masses, spins, and cosmological distances. This thesis presents a comprehensive treatment of the modern gravitational-wave data analysis pipeline, from the Wiener-filter foundations of matched filtering — including noise power-spectral-density weighting, optimal signal-to-noise ratio construction, and the orthogonal-phase decomposition used by search pipelines — through template-bank geometry, effective-one-body and phenomenological waveform families, and metric-based bank placement. We detail the two principal detection pipelines, **PyCBC** and **GstLAL**, including their chi-squared signal-consistency vetoes, trigger coincidence logic, and background estimation via time-slide resampling that enabled false-alarm rates below one per two hundred thousand years for the GW150914 discovery. Finally, we treat Bayesian parameter estimation with **LALInference** and **Bilby**, waveform systematics, population inference from the GWTC catalogs, and the multi-messenger era opened by GW170817, surveying both empirical results and the open problems that will define third-generation ground-based detectors.

## 1 Introduction

The first direct detection of gravitational waves, GW150914, announced by the LIGO Scientific and Virgo Collaborations in February 2016 [1], was not merely an observational milestone but a triumph of data analysis. The peak strain amplitude registered at the two Advanced LIGO interferometers was approximately $10^{-21}$ — a fractional change in the 4 km arm length of order one thousandth of a proton diameter — embedded in a noise floor dominated by seismic, thermal, and quantum shot noise [1]. The signal rose above this background not by brute sensitivity alone but because its *morphology was known in advance*: general relativity predicts the inspiral-merger-ringdown waveform of a coalescing binary with exquisite fidelity, and decades of investment in matched-filter theory, template-bank construction, and veto statistics converted that prediction into a detection with combined signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 24 and a false-alarm rate better than one event per 203,000 years [1].

Since that first event, the field has catalogued roughly ninety confident compact binary mergers across the first three observing runs (the GWTC-3 catalog [7]), each one subjected to the full pipeline: data conditioning, matched-filter search, candidate vetting, Bayesian parameter estimation, and population-level inference. This thesis dissects each stage. We emphasize the *mathematical machinery* — the Wiener filter derivation, the chi-squared discriminator, the nested-sampling evidence integral — alongside the *engineering pragmatics* — template-bank optimization, glitch mitigation, and low-latency alert infrastructure that delivers sky localizations to electromagnetic partners within tens of seconds of merger.

The central thesis is this: gravitational-wave astronomy is, at its core, an exercise in *optimal detection under uncertainty*. Every component of the pipeline — from the $1/S_n(f)$ weighting of the matched filter to the hierarchical priors of population inference — is a consequence of the likelihood principle applied to noise-dominated data.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The detector and its noise

Advanced LIGO's interferometers measure differential arm-length strain $h(t) = \Delta L/L$. At design sensitivity the amplitude spectral density near 100 Hz reaches approximately $10^{-23}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$, with the low-frequency wall set by seismic and Newtonian noise, the mid-band by suspension thermal noise and coating Brownian noise, and the high-frequency regime by laser shot noise [1]. The noise is *colored*, *non-stationary*, and punctuated by transient instrumental glitches — short, loud, non-Gaussian excursions of unknown origin that can ring a matched filter with SNRs exceeding those of genuine astrophysical signals.

The standard analysis therefore models the data as

$$d(t) = n(t) + h(t; \boldsymbol{\theta})$$

where $n(t)$ is additive noise (assumed locally stationary and Gaussian over short segments) and $h(t; \boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the gravitational-wave signal parameterized by $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, typically the component masses, spin vectors, sky location, distance, inclination, coalescence time, and phase — fifteen parameters for a quasi-circular binary black hole system [2]. All subsequent analysis flows from the Gaussian likelihood in the frequency domain:

$$p(d \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) \propto \exp\left[-\frac{1}{2} \sum_f \frac{|\tilde{d}(f) - \tilde{h}(f; \boldsymbol{\theta})|^2}{(S_n(f)/2)\,\Delta f}\right]$$

with $S_n(f)$ the one-sided noise power spectral density, estimated in practice via Welch's method over neighboring data segments.

### 2.2 Why matched filtering

The matched filter is the optimal linear detector for a known signal in stationary Gaussian noise — a statement with the force of a theorem, due to Wiener's analysis of the 1940s and formalized in the detection-theoretic framework of Helstrom and Van Trees. If the waveform $h(t)$ were known *exactly*, no other statistic can yield a higher detection probability at fixed false-alarm probability. The gravitational-wave case is a matched filter with an additional complication: the signal is known only *up to parameters* (masses, spins), which forces the search over a discrete template bank, and the "known signal" itself is a numerical-relativity-calibrated approximant whose residual modeling error must be controlled. These two complications — bank discretization and waveform systematics — are the twin engineering challenges of the entire search enterprise.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The frequency-domain inner product

All gravitational-wave matched filtering is performed in the frequency domain, exploiting the convolution theorem and the efficiency of the fast Fourier transform. Define the noise-weighted inner product

$$\langle a \mid b \rangle \equiv 4\,\mathrm{Re} \int_{f_{\mathrm{low}}}^{f_{\mathrm{high}}} \frac{\tilde{a}(f)\,\tilde{b}^*(f)}{S_n(f)}\,df,$$

where $S_n(f)$ is the one-sided noise PSD and the integration spans the detector's sensitive band (roughly 20–2000 Hz for LIGO) [2][5]. The optimal SNR of a signal $h$ is then $\rho_{\mathrm{opt}} = \sqrt{\langle h \mid h \rangle}$, and the matched-filter statistic for data $d$ is

$$\rho^2 = \frac{\langle d \mid h \rangle^2}{\langle h \mid h \rangle}.$$

> **Theorem:** *Among all linear filters, the matched filter $K(f) \propto \tilde{h}(f)/S_n(f)$ maximizes the expected signal-to-noise ratio, achieving $\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}^2 = \langle h \mid h \rangle$. The $1/S_n(f)$ weighting is precisely the Wiener filter: frequencies where the detector is quiet contribute proportionally more to the statistic.*

### 3.2 Orthogonal phases and the complex SNR

Because the coalescence phase $\phi_c$ is unknown *a priori*, pipelines avoid searching over it explicitly. Decomposing the template into two orthogonal phases, $h_{\cos}$ and $h_{\sin}$ — quadrature waveforms differing by $\pi/2$ in phase — and maximizing the likelihood over $\phi_c$ analytically yields the complex SNR time series

$$\rho(t) = \frac{\langle d \mid h_{\cos} \rangle + i\langle d \mid h_{\sin} \rangle}{\sqrt{\langle h_{\cos} \mid h_{\cos} \rangle}},$$

with $|\rho(t)|$ maximized over all phases simultaneously [2]. Each template thus costs *one* complex inverse FFT per data segment rather than a phase-parameter search, and the peak of $|\rho(t)|$ localizes the coalescence time to within milliseconds.

### 3.3 Search methodology summary

The practical search proceeds as follows:

1. **Data conditioning** — whitening, downsampling, removal of calibration lines and data-quality veto segments.
2. **Template-bank filtering** — compute $\rho(t)$ for every template in the bank (typically $\mathcal{O}(10^5)$–$\mathcal{O}(10^6)$ waveforms) via FFT.
3. **Trigger generation** — record local maxima of $|\rho(t)|$ exceeding a threshold (typically $\rho > 5.5$).
4. **Signal-consistency tests** — down-weight triggers whose time-frequency structure fails to match the template (the chi-squared veto [5]).
5. **Coincidence** — require consistent triggers in multiple detectors within the light-travel time plus timing tolerance.
6. **Significance assignment** — compare surviving candidates against a background estimated by time-shifted analyses, quoting a false-alarm rate (FAR).

Steps 4–6 constitute the *detection pipeline* proper and are the subject of Section 4.3.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Matched-Filter Formalism: Optimal SNR, PSD Weighting, and the Wiener Filter

The optimality of matched filtering deserves a careful derivation because every subsequent design decision — from the PSD estimation method to the chi-squared veto — is intelligible only in its light.

Consider the data stream $d(t) = n(t) + A\,h(t)$, where $n$ is stationary Gaussian noise with PSD $S_n(f)$, $h$ is a *known* waveform, and $A$ is an unknown amplitude. Any linear statistic takes the form $z = \int d(t) K(t)\,dt$ for some filter $K$. The expected signal contribution is $A\int h(t)K(t)dt$ and the noise variance is $\mathbb{E}[z_n^2] = \int | \tilde{K}(f)|^2 S_n(f)\,df/2$. Maximizing the deflection $\mathbb{E}[z_s]^2/\mathrm{Var}(z_n)$ via the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on the noise-weighted inner product gives the unique (up to scale) optimizer:

$$\tilde{K}(f) = \frac{\tilde{h}^*(f)}{S_n(f)}.$$

This is the Wiener filter [5]: whiten the data by $1/S_n(f)$, correlate with the template, and normalize. The achieved SNR,

$$\rho^2 = 4\int \frac{|\tilde{h}(f)|^2}{S_n(f)}\,df,$$

is the *optimal* SNR — a direct measure of the observable volume, since detection horizon scales linearly with $\rho$ and volume as $\rho^3$.

Three refinements are essential in practice. **PSD estimation** uses Welch's overlapped-segment averaging over neighboring data, or median-based estimators robust to glitches; PyCBC re-estimates the PSD per data block [2]. **The low-frequency cutoff** near $\sim$20 Hz truncates the integral where seismic noise dominates, and the in-band cycle count determines the template's "broadband" character exploited by the chi-squared veto. **Phase maximization** via quadrature templates reports the envelope over all coalescence phases, making the detection statistic chi-squared distributed with 2 degrees of freedom under noise-only conditions.

| Quantity | Symbol | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Noise PSD | $S_n(f)$ | Wiener weighting; estimated by Welch averaging |
| Optimal SNR | $\rho_{\mathrm{opt}} = \sqrt{\langle h\|h\rangle}$ | Sets detection horizon and sensitive volume |
| Complex filter output | $\rho(t)$ | Maximized over phase; peak locates $t_c$ |
| Trigger threshold | $\rho_{\mathrm{thr}} \approx 5.5$ | Balances trigger rate against computational cost |

### 4.2 Template Banks: Metric-Based Placement, Effective-One-Body, and Phenomenological Waveforms

A matched filter requires the signal's exact shape, but nature provides a continuum of binaries parameterized by masses and spins. The search instead filters against a *discrete bank* of templates placed so that no astrophysical signal lies farther than a prescribed *mismatch* $1 - \mathcal{M}$ from its nearest template, where the match is

$$\mathcal{M}(h_1, h_2) = \max_{t_c, \phi_c} \frac{\langle h_1 \mid h_2 \rangle}{\sqrt{\langle h_1 \mid h_1\rangle \langle h_2 \mid h_2 \rangle}}.$$

The canonical choice is a *minimal match* of 0.97: at most 3% of optimal SNR is lost to bank discretization, corresponding to roughly a 10% loss in sensitive volume [2].

**The parameter-space metric.** Owen (1996) showed that for infinitesimally separated waveforms, the mismatch induces a Riemannian metric on parameter space:

$$1 - \mathcal{M} \approx g_{ij}\,\Delta\theta^i \Delta\theta^j,$$

with $g_{ij} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_i \partial_j \mathcal{M}$. Template placement then becomes a sphere-covering problem: lay down a lattice (or stochastic/random bank) with spacing set by the metric's eigenvalues. In practice, PyCBC's O1 bank covered total masses $2$–$100\,M_\odot$ with aligned spins, requiring approximately 250,000 templates — a number dominated by the long, many-cycle inspirals of low-mass systems, whose phase must be tracked over thousands of wave cycles [2]. Stochastic placement algorithms (random proposal with metric-based rejection) have largely superseded deterministic lattices for high-dimensional banks, including precessing-spin extensions with millions of templates.

**Waveform families.** Two complementary modeling paradigms supply the templates. **Effective-one-body (EOB)** maps the two-body problem onto a test particle in a deformed Kerr metric calibrated to numerical relativity; the **SEOBNR** family provides time-domain inspiral-merger-ringdown waveforms with higher-order modes [4]. **Phenomenological models** (the **IMRPhenom** family) hybridize post-Newtonian inspiral with numerical-relativity merger data in closed-form frequency-domain expressions, orders of magnitude faster — a decisive advantage for bank generation and the millions of likelihood evaluations in parameter estimation [4].

The two families are cross-validated against each other and against numerical-relativity catalogs; residual disagreements are the leading source of *waveform systematics*, discussed in Section 4.4.

```python
# Illustrative: matched-filter SNR for a template against whitened data
import numpy as np

def matched_filter_snr(data_f, template_f, psd, df):
    """Complex SNR time series via the noise-weighted inner product.
    data_f, template_f: frequency-domain strain; psd: one-sided noise PSD."""
    integrand = data_f * np.conj(template_f) / psd
    rho_t = 4.0 * np.fft.irfft(integrand) * df          # inverse FFT -> rho(t)
    norm = np.sqrt(4.0 * np.sum(np.abs(template_f)**2 / psd) * df)
    return rho_t / norm                                 # peak of |rho| is the SNR
```

### 4.3 The Detection Pipeline: PyCBC and GstLAL Stages, Chi-Squared Vetoes, and Time Slides

Two flagship pipelines perform the matched-filter search in production: **PyCBC** [2] and **GstLAL** [6]. They share the matched-filter core but differ in ranking statistics, veto strategy, and latency architecture.

**PyCBC** [2] operates as a batch/offline search:

1. Each detector's strain is matched-filtered independently against the full template bank; triggers with $\rho > 5.5$ are clustered in time.
2. **Chi-squared veto.** Every trigger is subjected to Allen's $\chi^2$ time-frequency discriminator [5]: the template is divided into $p$ frequency sub-bands of equal expected SNR contribution, and the statistic
   $$\chi^2 = p \sum_{j=1}^{p} \left(\rho_j - \rho/p\right)^2$$
   measures how the SNR *accumulates across frequency*. For a true signal (or Gaussian noise), $\chi^2_r \equiv \chi^2/(2p-2) \approx 1$; for a glitch that deposits its power in a narrow band, $\chi^2_r \gg 1$. PyCBC folds this into a *reweighted SNR*,
   $$\hat{\rho} = \begin{cases} \rho & \chi^2_r \le 1 \\ \rho \left[\tfrac{1}{2}(1 + (\chi^2_r)^3)\right]^{-1/6} & \chi^2_r > 1, \end{cases}$$
   which suppresses glitch triggers while preserving genuine signals [2][5].
3. **Coincidence.** Triggers from different detectors must agree in template parameters and arrival time within the inter-site light-travel time ($\sim$10 ms for Hanford–Livingston) plus a timing tolerance. The network statistic is the quadrature sum of single-detector reweighted SNRs.
4. **Background estimation by time slides.** To measure the false-alarm rate, the analysis is repeated with one detector's trigger stream shifted in time by multiples of $\sim$0.1 s — far longer than any physical coincidence window — destroying genuine coincidences while preserving the noise trigger distribution. Thousands of such slides accumulate the equivalent of millions of years of background livetime, allowing FAR measurements as low as one per million years [2]. A candidate's significance is the rate at which background coincidences exceed its ranking statistic.

**GstLAL** [6] is built on the GStreamer multimedia framework as a *streaming, low-latency* pipeline, performing time-domain matched filtering via singular-value-decomposed (SVD) template banks compressed into orthonormal basis filters. Candidates are ranked by a *likelihood ratio* incorporating SNR, signal-consistency measures, detector sensitivities, and astrophysical priors — the architecture behind the low-latency alerts that launched multi-messenger follow-up of GW170817 [6].

> **Key insight:** *The chi-squared veto is not an add-on but the linchpin of the entire enterprise. Without it, the matched filter's optimality in Gaussian noise is irrelevant: real detector data is glitch-dominated, and the $\chi^2$ test is what distinguishes a broadband chirp — whose SNR accumulates gradually across hundreds of hertz — from a loud instrumental transient that merely happens to ring one template.*

### 4.4 Bayesian Parameter Estimation: LALInference, Nested Sampling, Bilby, and Waveform Systematics

Detection answers "is there a signal?"; parameter estimation answers "what are its properties, and how certain are we?" The framework is Bayesian. Given data $d$ and waveform model $H$,

$$p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid d, H) = \frac{p(d \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}, H)\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid H)}{p(d \mid H)},$$

where the likelihood is the Gaussian noise model of Section 2.1, the prior encodes astrophysical assumptions (uniform in component masses, isotropic in orientation, volumetric in distance), and the evidence $p(d \mid H) = \int p(d \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}$ enables model comparison. The posterior is fifteen-dimensional, multimodal, and riddled with curved degeneracies (notably distance–inclination and mass–spin), so it is explored by stochastic sampling.

**LALInference** [3] is the pioneering parameter-estimation library, providing three independent samplers — nested sampling, Markov-chain Monte Carlo, and the BAMBI neural-network-accelerated nested sampler — whose cross-agreement on injections validated the entire framework. Nested sampling (Skilling) is particularly suited to the problem: it climbs through nested likelihood contours, simultaneously delivering posterior samples *and* the evidence integral needed for model selection (e.g., testing general relativity against alternative theories).

**Bilby** [4] is the modern, Python-native successor: it wraps the LALSuite waveform generators with an extensible sampler interface (notably the *dynesty* nested sampler) and has become the LVK collaborations' standard for catalog-level parameter estimation. Its clean separation of likelihood, prior, and sampler has enabled methodological advances such as multi-waveform marginalization, folding modeling uncertainty directly into the credible intervals.

**Waveform systematics** are the dominant theoretical uncertainty. For GW150914-like signals at SNR $\sim$24, differences between waveform families shift inferred parameters at the percent level — subdominant to statistical noise. But as detectors improve and catalog SNRs climb, systematics will dominate: the field's response is a systematic program of numerical-relativity calibration, higher-mode and precession modeling, and the multi-waveform inference techniques pioneered in the Bilby ecosystem [4].

A typical analysis produces the iconic *corner plot*: 1D marginalized posteriors on the diagonal and 2D 50%/90% credible contours off-diagonal for chirp mass $\mathcal{M}_c$, mass ratio $q$, effective inspiral spin $\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}$, luminosity distance $D_L$, inclination $\iota$, and sky position. For GW150914, this machinery reported source-frame component masses $36^{+5}_{-4}\,M_\odot$ and $29^{+4}_{-4}\,M_\odot$, a final black hole of $62^{+4}_{-4}\,M_\odot$, and $3.0^{+0.5}_{-0.5}\,M_\odot c^2$ radiated as gravitational waves — the most energetic astrophysical event ever directly measured [1].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 GW150914: the proof of concept

On 14 September 2015 at 09:50:45 UTC, a chirp sweeping from 35 to 250 Hz over $\sim$0.2 s appeared in both LIGO detectors with a combined matched-filter SNR of 24 [1]. The PyCBC and GstLAL pipelines independently recovered it; the time-slide background yielded a false-alarm rate below one per 203,000 years — a $>5.1\sigma$ detection [1]. Parameter estimation placed the merger at a luminosity distance of $410^{+160}_{-180}$ Mpc, with component masses in the $\sim$30 $M_\odot$ range: heavier than any stellar-mass black hole known from X-ray binaries, immediately implying a new population of binary black holes and launching the catalog era.

### 5.2 The catalog era: GWTC-1 through GWTC-3

The detection machinery has now produced three generations of catalogs. GWTC-3, covering the first three observing runs, lists **90 confident compact binary coalescences** (probability of astrophysical origin $p_{\mathrm{astro}} > 0.5$), spanning binary black holes, two binary neutron stars, and the first confident neutron star–black hole systems [7]. The ensemble enables *hierarchical population inference*: treating the catalog as draws from an underlying astrophysical distribution with hyperparameters (mass-function slopes, spin distributions, merger-rate evolution) inferred jointly — already constraining the black-hole mass function and the local merger rate at tens per Gpc$^3$ per year [7].

### 5.3 Multi-messenger astronomy: GW170817

The binary neutron star merger GW170817 demonstrated the pipeline's end-to-end power. Detected with SNR 32.4, localized to $\sim$28 deg$^2$, and followed 1.7 s later by the Fermi-GBM short gamma-ray burst GRB 170817A, it triggered the global campaign that identified the kilonova AT 2017gfo — confirming neutron star mergers as the dominant site of rapid neutron-capture nucleosynthesis — and enabled a standard-siren measurement of the Hubble constant [6].

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

Despite its successes, the matched-filter paradigm faces structural limitations:

1. **Glitch contamination.** The chi-squared veto is imperfect; novel glitch morphologies continue to pollute the background, and the loudest events demand manual vetting. Machine-learning classifiers and auxiliary-channel vetoes (iDQ) are active mitigations, but instrument non-stationarity is a moving target.
2. **Computational scaling.** Third-generation detectors (Einstein Telescope, Cosmic Explorer) will observe from $\sim$5 Hz, lengthening waveforms by an order of magnitude and exploding bank sizes; precessing-spin and eccentric banks with millions of templates strain even GPU-accelerated filtering. Reduced-order and surrogate models are essential.
3. **Waveform systematics.** As statistical uncertainties shrink, modeling error becomes the limiting factor — for tests of general relativity, neutron-star tidal deformability, and precision cosmology.
4. **Beyond the modeled manifold.** Matched filtering is blind to the unmodeled: eccentric mergers, exotic compact objects, unforeseen sources. Minimally modeled burst searches (coherent WaveBurst) provide a safety net at significantly lower sensitivity.
5. **Real-time inference.** Hundreds of detections per year demand parameter estimation in seconds, not weeks. Neural posterior estimation is the most promising route, but its calibration for high-stakes events remains an open research question.

## 7 Conclusion

Gravitational-wave data analysis is the discipline that turned a $10^{-21}$ strain into a new astronomy. Its intellectual core is the matched filter — the Wiener-optimal correlator of provable optimality — extended by the template-bank geometry that discretizes the signal manifold, the chi-squared veto that tames non-Gaussian reality, the time-slide machinery that quantifies significance, and the Bayesian inference engines (LALInference, Bilby) that transform strains into astrophysics. From GW150914's false-alarm rate of one per 203,000 years [1] to the ninety-event GWTC-3 catalog [7] and the kilonova light of GW170817, every discovery in the field has been, in a precise sense, a discovery *by data analysis*.
