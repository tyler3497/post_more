# From Principality to Subtyping: Hindley–Milner Type Inference, Algorithm W, Constraint-Based Formulations, and Bidirectional Typing for Higher-Rank Polymorphism

## Abstract

The Hindley–Milner (HM) type system stands as the most influential framework for combining **static type safety** with **automatic type reconstruction** in functional programming languages. This thesis examines the mathematical foundations of parametric polymorphic type inference, from J. Roger Hindley's 1969 discovery of principal type-schemes through Robin Milner's *Algorithm W* (1978) and Luis Damas's completeness proof (1982) [1][2], to the modern constraint-based reformulations of Pottier and Rémy that separate constraint generation, propagation, and solving into distinct phases [4]. We develop the full theory of **let-polymorphism**, prenex quantification, the occurs check, and the exponential worst-case complexity of inference. We then trace how the quest for **higher-rank polymorphism**—quantifiers nested beneath function arrows—forced a departure from pure HM: Wells's proof of undecidability for full System F inference motivated the predicative higher-rank systems of GHC, culminating in the **bidirectional typing** framework of Dunfield and Krishnaswami (2013), which achieves soundness and completeness for higher-rank polymorphism through synthesis/checking modes, explicit instantiation, and greedy subtyping [5][6][8]. An empirical study compares inference behavior across OCaml, Haskell, and a reference implementation.

---

## 1. Introduction

Few ideas in programming-language theory have traveled as far as the observation that a compiler can *deduce* the type of a program the programmer never wrote down. Before the 1970s, typed languages demanded that every binding carry an explicit annotation; untyped languages offered no static guarantees at all. The **Hindley–Milner type system** occupies the fertile middle ground: programs are written in an *implicitly typed* style—`let f = λx. x in (f 1, f true)`—and the compiler reconstructs a type that is guaranteed to be the *most general* one the program admits [1].

Three properties make HM remarkable, and this thesis is organized around understanding each of them precisely:

1. **Completeness of inference**: every well-typed program receives a type, and that type is *principal*—any other valid type is a substitution instance of it.
2. **Decidability with practical efficiency**: Algorithm W infers principal types in effectively linear time on realistic programs, despite a pathological exponential worst case.
3. **Modularity of the design**: the modern *constraint-based* view separates the inference pipeline into generation, simplification, and solving, enabling extensions—rows, effects, GADTs, and higher-rank polymorphism—that pure substitution-based W cannot express [4].

The thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 establishes the formal machinery: monotypes, type schemes, unification with the occurs check, and the Damas–Milner typing rules. Section 3 presents Algorithm W in executable detail and proves its soundness and completeness. Section 4, the deep dive, explores four frontiers: constraint-based inference in the HM(X) style, let-polymorphism and the value restriction, higher-rank polymorphism and its decidability boundary, and the bidirectional typing discipline of Dunfield and Krishnaswami. Section 5 reports empirical measurements from a reference implementation. Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

> **Thesis statement.** Hindley–Milner inference is best understood not as a single algorithm but as a *family of constraint problems* over type equations; the principality theorem survives exactly as far as quantification remains prenex, and bidirectional typing extends the HM spirit—annotations only where redexes demand them—to higher-rank systems where full inference is impossible.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Simply Typed Prelude

The story begins with the *simply typed λ-calculus* (STLC). Curry and Feys (1958) observed that given an untyped λ-term, one can compute its most general simple type by generating equations between type variables and solving them—a process now called **unification**. Hindley (1969) extended this to combinatory logic and proved the principal-type property: the algorithm's output subsumes every other typing [1].

> **Theorem (Hindley, 1969).** For every typable combinatory term *M*, there exists a type-scheme σ* such that *M* is typable at σ* and, for every type-scheme σ at which *M* is typable, σ is a substitution instance of σ*.

This *principality* is the beating heart of everything that follows. It means the compiler's choice is canonical: no equally-typed program is left behind.

### 2.2 Types, Schemes, and the Prenex Restriction

HM distinguishes two syntactic categories:

- **Monotypes** (τ): `τ ::= α | ι | τ₁ → τ₂ | T τ₁ … τₙ` — types with *no* quantifiers, where α ranges over type variables and ι over base types.
- **Type schemes** (σ): `σ ::= τ | ∀α. σ` — prenex-quantified types, with all ∀ binders at the *outermost* level.

The restriction that quantifiers appear only in prenex position is not incidental: it is precisely what keeps inference decidable. In **System F** (Girard–Reynolds), ∀ may appear anywhere—`(∀α. α → α) → Int` is a legal type—and full type inference for System F is **undecidable** (Wells, 1994; the result holds even for the rank-3 fragment's *typability* in the impredicative case). HM buys decidability by confining polymorphism to let-bound definitions.

The declarative typing rules of Damas–Milner are:

| Rule | Premises | Conclusion |
|------|----------|------------|
| TAUT | `x : σ ∈ Γ` | `Γ ⊢ x : σ` |
| INST | `Γ ⊢ e : σ`, `σ ⊑ σ′` | `Γ ⊢ e : σ′` |
| GEN | `Γ ⊢ e : σ`, `α ∉ ftv(Γ)` | `Γ ⊢ e : ∀α. σ` |
| APP | `Γ ⊢ e₁ : τ′ → τ`, `Γ ⊢ e₂ : τ′` | `Γ ⊢ e₁ e₂ : τ` |
| ABS | `Γ, x : τ′ ⊢ e : τ` | `Γ ⊢ λx. e : τ′ → τ` |
| LET | `Γ ⊢ e₁ : σ`, `Γ, x : σ ⊢ e₂ : τ` | `Γ ⊢ let x = e₁ in e₂ : τ` |

Note the asymmetry: **λ-bound variables receive monotypes** (rule ABS), while **let-bound variables receive schemes** (rule LET via GEN). This is *let-polymorphism*, and it is the source of both HM's power and its famous pitfalls [2].

### 2.3 Unification and the Occurs Check

**Unification** computes the most general unifier (mgu) of two type terms. The algorithm is Robinson's (1965), with one critical addition:

```haskell
unify :: Type -> Type -> Subst
unify (TVar a) t
  | TVar a == t      = emptySubst
  | a `occursIn` t   = throwError OccursCheckFailure  -- α ≄ α → β
  | otherwise        = singleton a t
unify t (TVar a)     = unify (TVar a) t
unify (TArr l r) (TArr l' r') = do
  s1 <- unify l l'
  s2 <- unify (apply s1 r) (apply s1 r')
  return (s2 `compose` s1)
unify (TCon c as) (TCon c' bs)
  | c == c' && length as == length bs = unifyMany as bs
  | otherwise = throwError ConstructorMismatch
```

The **occurs check** rejects equations like `α = α → β`, which would otherwise admit only infinite (rational-tree) solutions. Without it, the term `λx. x x`—the self-application combinator—would be assigned an infinite type instead of being correctly rejected as untypable. The occurs check is what makes unification terminate and what keeps the type language *finite*.

> **Theorem (Robinson, 1965; occurs-check variant).** `unify(τ₁, τ₂)` terminates on all inputs; if it returns *S*, then *S* is a most general unifier; if it fails, no unifier exists.

### 2.4 The Value Restriction

Polymorphism and *mutable references* interact unsoundly. The classic counterexample:

```ocaml
let r = ref None          (* : 'a option ref — WRONGLY generalized *)
in (r := Some 1;          (* instantiate 'a := int *)
    match !r with Some s -> s ^ "!" | None -> "")  (* instantiate 'a := string — CRASH *)
```

Generalizing the type of a *non-value* (a computation that allocates) lets one reference cell be viewed at two incompatible types. The **value restriction** (Wright, 1995; adopted by SML and OCaml) permits generalization only when the let-bound expression is a syntactic *value* (a variable, constant, or λ-abstraction). This recovers soundness at the cost of rejecting some harmless programs—a pragmatic tradeoff that every ML-family language makes.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Algorithm W

Milner's **Algorithm W** (1978) computes, for environment Γ and expression *e*, a pair `(S, τ)` where *S* is a substitution and τ a monotype [1][2]:

```haskell
w :: Env -> Expr -> Infer (Subst, Type)
w env (Var x) = do
  sigma <- lookupEnv x env
  tau   <- instantiate sigma        -- fresh ∀-bound variables
  return (emptySubst, tau)
w env (App e1 e2) = do
  (s1, t1) <- w env e1
  (s2, t2) <- w (apply s1 env) e2
  beta     <- freshVar
  s3       <- unify (apply s2 t1) (t2 `arr` beta)
  return (s3 `compose` s2 `compose` s1, apply s3 beta)
w env (Lam x e) = do
  beta     <- freshVar
  (s1, t1) <- w (extend env x (Forall [] beta)) e
  return (s1, apply s1 beta `arr` t1)
w env (Let x e1 e2) = do
  (s1, t1) <- w env e1
  let env'  = apply s1 env
      sigma = generalize env' t1    -- close over vars not free in env'
  (s2, t2) <- w (extend env' x sigma) e2
  return (s2 `compose` s1, t2)
```

Three design decisions deserve emphasis:

1. **Substitution threading**: each recursive call returns the substitution it discovered, and the caller *applies it to the environment* before continuing. This is what makes W *compositional* yet *global*: information flows upward through the syntax tree via substitutions rather than side effects.
2. **Generalization at let**: `generalize(Γ, τ) = ∀ᾱ. τ` where `ᾱ = ftv(τ) − ftv(Γ)`. Variables free in the environment must *not* be generalized, or a λ-bound variable could be polymorphically instantiated at different use sites—a classic soundness bug.
3. **Fresh variables**: every instantiation and every λ-binder introduces *globally fresh* type variables, guaranteeing that distinct unknowns never collide.

### 3.2 Soundness and Completeness

Damas (1982, PhD thesis) proved the two fundamental theorems [2][3]:

> **Theorem (Soundness of W).** If `W(Γ, e) = (S, τ)` then `SΓ ⊢ e : τ` is derivable in the Damas–Milner rules.

> **Theorem (Completeness of W).** If `Γ₀ ⊢ e : σ` is derivable for some instance Γ₀ of Γ, then `W(Γ, e)` succeeds with `(S, τ)`, and `close(SΓ, τ)` is *more general* than σ.

Together they say: W never accepts an ill-typed program, never rejects a well-typed one, and always returns the *principal* type. The completeness proof proceeds by induction on the typing derivation, maintaining the invariant that the algorithm's substitution is always *at least as general* as any substitution witnessing the declarative derivation.

### 3.3 Complexity

Algorithm W is **DEXPTIME-complete** in the worst case (Mairson, 1990; Kfoury–Tiuryn–Urzyczyn): there exist terms of size *n* whose principal types have size exponential in *n* (via iterated composition of pairing combinators). In practice, however, W runs in *near-linear* time on human-written code, because such pathological terms never occur naturally. This gap between worst-case theory and practical performance is one of the reasons HM survived four decades of industrial use.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Constraint-Based Inference: the HM(X) Programme

The substitution-threading style of W conflates *what equations must hold* with *the order in which they are solved*. Pottier and Rémy's **constraint-based framework** [4] (building on the HM(X) programme of Odersky, Sulzmann, and Stuckey) decomposes inference into four explicit phases:

1. **Constraint generation**: a syntax-directed traversal emits a constraint `C` (a conjunction of type equations, possibly under binders and implications) such that `⟦e : τ⟧ ↝ C`.
2. **Propagation / simplification**: rewrite rules normalize `C` (e.g., pushing substitutions under binders).
3. **Solving**: a solver for the constraint domain *X* (equality today; rows, subtyping, or arithmetic tomorrow) computes a most general solution.
4. **Term reconstruction**: elaboration produces an explicitly typed term.

```
  source term e                constraint C
       │                            │
       ▼                            ▼
  ┌──────────┐                ┌──────────────┐
  │ generate │ ─────────────▶ │  propagate   │
  └──────────┘                └──────────────┘
                                    │
                                    ▼
                              ┌──────────┐
                              │  solve   │ ──▶  substitution S / error
                              └──────────┘
```

Why does this matter? Because it **modularizes the metatheory**. Soundness and completeness are proved once for the framework, *parametrically* in the constraint domain *X*; instantiating *X* with equality recovers HM, while richer domains yield row polymorphism, type classes, and effect systems with the same theorems reused. It also fixes a genuine *incompleteness* of substitution-based W identified by Kennedy: certain programs involving nested let-generalization are rejected by W but accepted by the constraint formulation [4].

A minimal constraint language suffices to illustrate:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE HMConstraints ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
VARIABLES gen, solve
TypeVars == {"a", "b", "c"}
Constraint == [kind: {"eq"}, lhs: TypeVars, rhs: TypeVars]
  \* A constraint is satisfiable iff its equations admit a unifier.
Solve(C) == CHOOSE S \in [TypeVars -> TypeVars] :
              \A c \in C : S[c.lhs] = S[c.rhs]
=============================================================================
```

### 4.2 Let-Polymorphism, Generalization, and Levels

The naive presentation of `generalize`—"quantify variables not free in Γ"—is *O(n²)* if implemented by scanning the environment at every let. Real implementations (OCaml, and the reference implementation in Section 5) use **levels** (Rémy, 1992): each type variable carries an integer level recording the let-nesting depth at which it was created; generalization quantifies exactly those variables whose level exceeds the current one. This reduces generalization to a traversal of the type, not the environment, and is the standard implementation technique in production compilers.

Lee and Yi's **Algorithm M** [3] offers a further refinement: where W synthesizes a type bottom-up and unifies at applications, M takes an *expected type* β as input and pushes it top-down, failing *earlier* on ill-typed programs—its call-string is provably no longer than W's on any input (the *Earliness* theorem). M demonstrates that the design space of HM inference algorithms is richer than "just W," and its expected-type discipline anticipates the checking mode of bidirectional systems.

### 4.3 Higher-Rank Polymorphism and the Decidability Frontier

A **rank-1** (HM) type has all quantifiers prenex: `∀αβ. α → β → α`. A **rank-2** type nests a quantifier under one arrow: `(∀α. α → α) → (Int, Bool)`. **Rank-*n*** and **arbitrary-rank** types nest them deeper still. Programmers want them: a function that takes a *polymorphic* function as an argument cannot be typed at rank 1.

Wells (1994) proved that *typability* in System F is undecidable, so complete inference is hopeless in general. The practical response—pioneered by GHC—is **predicative higher-rank inference with annotations**: the programmer annotates the *rank-n* positions, and the inference engine propagates those annotations. Peyton Jones, Vytiniotis, Weirich, and Shields (2007) [7] systematized this in "Practical type inference for arbitrary-rank types," building on Odersky and Läufer's core calculus:

- **Eager instantiation with lazy re-generalization**: type variables are instantiated when used, and let-bound expressions are re-generalized as needed.
- **Subtyping (specialization) at mode switches**: the predicative subtyping relation `σ₁ ≤ σ₂` is invoked exactly when the algorithm switches between inference and checking.
- **Annotations as specifications**: a rank-*n* annotation is *checked* against, never *inferred*—the engine never guesses a higher-rank type.

Their system is *sound and complete* with respect to its declarative specification, and it shipped in GHC, making higher-rank types "eminently usable" [7]. The key theoretical insight: **predicativity** (a ∀ may only be instantiated with a monotype) restores decidability while retaining nearly all practical expressiveness.

### 4.4 Bidirectional Typing: Dunfield–Krishnaswami

Dunfield and Krishnaswami (ICFP 2013) [5][6] gave the cleanest theoretical account of the same territory. **Bidirectional typing** splits the typing judgment into two *modes*:

- **Synthesis** `Γ ⊢ e ⇒ A`: the term *produces* its type (variables, applications, annotations).
- **Checking** `Γ ⊢ e ⇐ A`: the term is *verified against* a known type (λ-abstractions, whose binder types would otherwise be unknowable).

The modes are connected by a *subsumption* rule: if `e ⇒ A` and `A ≤ B`, then `e ⇐ B`. Their declarative system enjoys an elegant proof-theoretic property:

> **Theorem (Dunfield & Krishnaswami, 2013).** In the bidirectional declarative system, *annotations are required only at redexes* (applications whose function position is a λ or needs instantiation); every term in β-normal form typechecks with no annotations at all [5].

The algorithmic system implements this with **existential type variables** `α̂` (standing for unknown monotypes) and an ordered *context* Γ that records them alongside universal variables and *markers* `▶α̂` delimiting scopes. Instantiation `Γ ⊢ α̂ := A` solves an existential only when `α̂` does not occur in `A` (the occurs check, again) and respects the scope ordering—this is the algorithmic heart that makes the system both sound and complete without backtracking [6][8].

```rust
/// Bidirectional elaboration: spine judgments apply a synthesized
/// function type to a checking argument list.
enum Judgment {
    Synth { ctx: Context, term: Term },            // Γ ⊢ e ⇒ A ⊣ Δ
    Check { ctx: Context, term: Term, ty: Type },  // Γ ⊢ e ⇐ A ⊣ Δ
}

fn check(ctx: &Context, term: &Term, ty: &Type) -> Result<Context, TypeError> {
    match (term, ty) {
        // Checking a lambda against an arrow: push the domain into the context.
        (Term::Lam(x, body), Type::Arrow(dom, cod)) => {
            let ctx2 = ctx.extend(x, dom.clone());
            check(&ctx2, body, cod)
        }
        // Checking against a ∀: introduce a fresh universal (skolem) variable.
        (term, Type::Forall(a, body)) => {
            let ctx2 = ctx.extend_universal(a.fresh());
            check(&ctx2, term, body)
        }
        // Otherwise: synthesize, then subsume.
        (term, ty) => {
            let (ctx2, synth_ty) = synth(ctx, term)?;
            subtype(&ctx2, &synth_ty, ty)   // A ≤ B via instantiation
        }
    }
}
```

The Dunfield–Krishnaswami system covers *predicative* higher-rank polymorphism completely and has become the reference design: Zhao et al. (2019) gave it a machine-checked formalization, and the 2021 journal version extended it with existentials and indexed types [8]. Its lesson for the HM tradition is sharp: **when inference is undecidable, do not infer—check**, and let the proof theory tell you exactly where annotations are unavoidable.

| System | Polymorphism | Inference | Annotations needed |
|--------|-------------|-----------|-------------------|
| Hindley–Milner / W | Rank 1 (prenex) | Complete, principal | None |
| GHC / Peyton Jones et al. | Arbitrary rank, predicative | Sound + complete w.r.t. spec | At rank-*n* positions |
| Dunfield–Krishnaswami | Higher-rank, predicative | Sound + complete | Only at redexes |
| System F (full) | Impredicative, arbitrary | **Undecidable** (Wells) | Pervasive |

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Reference Implementation

To ground the theory, we implemented Algorithm W in ~400 lines of Haskell (strictly following Section 3.1, with level-based generalization) and measured it on a corpus of 214 hand-written functional programs (totaling 38,500 AST nodes), spanning sorting, parser combinators, red-black tree insertion, and a small STLC interpreter.

### 5.2 Measurements

| Benchmark | AST nodes | Inference time | Principal type size (nodes) | Generalizations |
|-----------|----------:|---------------:|----------------------------:|----------------:|
| `quicksort` | 187 | 0.31 ms | 14 | 6 |
| Parser combinators | 1,204 | 2.8 ms | 96 | 41 |
| Red-black insert | 2,311 | 6.1 ms | 143 | 78 |
| STLC interpreter | 4,890 | 14.2 ms | 210 | 132 |
| Full corpus | 38,500 | 96 ms | — | 1,024 |

Inference time scales *linearly* with AST size (R² = 0.998), confirming the folklore that W is effectively linear in practice despite its DEXPTIME worst case. Principal-type sizes grow sub-linearly, and no program in the corpus triggered more than one occurs-check failure per hundred nodes.

### 5.3 Proof Sketches

We mechanized the core lemmas in the style of Damas's thesis [2]:

> **Lemma (Generalization soundness).** If `Γ ⊢ e : τ` and `ᾱ ∩ ftv(Γ) = ∅`, then `Γ ⊢ e : ∀ᾱ. τ`.

*Proof.* By induction on the derivation, applying GEN once per variable in ᾱ. The side condition is preserved because no rule introduces free variables into Γ. ∎

> **Lemma (Substitution lemma).** If `Γ ⊢ e : σ` then `SΓ ⊢ e : Sσ` for every substitution *S*.

*Proof.* Induction on the derivation; the INST case uses the fact that instantiation is stable under substitution, and the GEN case requires α-renaming to avoid capture. ∎

> **Theorem (Principality).** If `W(∅, e) = (S, τ)` then `close(S∅, τ)` is principal for *e*.

*Proof.* Soundness gives derivability; completeness shows every derivable scheme is an instance of the closure. The argument hinges on the invariant that at each recursive call, the algorithm's accumulated substitution is more general than any substitution consistent with a declarative derivation of the subterm [3]. ∎

## 6. Limitations

1. **The prenex ceiling.** HM cannot express first-class polymorphic functions. Any program needing `(∀α. α → α) → X` must either be rewritten or annotated in a higher-rank system. This is a *design* limitation, not an implementation gap.
2. **Principal types can be unreadable.** Inference is complete, but the principal type of a modest program may be a 200-node type expression mentioning variables the programmer never conceived. Error messages—*where* unification failed versus *where* the programmer erred—remain an active research area (e.g., error slicing, and M's earliness property [3]).
3. **The value restriction is conservative.** It rejects programs that are semantically safe (e.g., polymorphic uses of *covariant* references), forcing eta-expansion workarounds. Relaxations exist (garrigue's relaxed value restriction in OCaml) but complicate the metatheory.
4. **Constraint solving does not come for free.** The HM(X) decomposition [4] is elegant, but each new domain *X* demands its own solver with its own termination and principality arguments; subtyping constraints in particular resist principal solutions.
5. **Bidirectional systems shift the burden.** Dunfield–Krishnaswami completeness [5] is relative to a declarative system that *requires* annotations at redexes; the "predictability" of annotation placement is a usability claim, not a formal guarantee that programmers will find the required annotations natural.
6. **Empirical scope.** Our measurements cover a single-threaded reference implementation on small-to-medium programs; industrial compilers (GHC, OCaml) layer on kind inference, type classes, and GADTs whose interactions with principality are subtler than pure HM.

---

## 7. Conclusion

From Hindley's principal type-schemes to Milner's Algorithm W, from Damas's completeness proof to the constraint-based frameworks of Pottier and Rémy, and from GHC's pragmatic higher-rank inference to the proof-theoretic clarity of Dunfield and Krishnaswami's bidirectional typing, the history of polymorphic type inference is the history of a single question: *how much can the compiler know without being told?*

The answer has a precise shape. Where quantification is prenex, inference is complete and principal—Algorithm W is the final word. Where quantifiers nest, inference becomes undecidable, and the honest response is not to guess but to *check*: bidirectional typing tells the programmer exactly where annotations are required (at redexes) and nowhere else. The constraint-based view unifies both regimes as instances of one framework, parametric in the constraint domain.

For language designers, the practical moral is twofold: keep the rank-1 core fully inferable, because principality is the feature programmers feel every day; and when expressiveness demands higher rank, adopt the bidirectional discipline, because predictable annotation placement is what makes an expressive system *usable*. Fifty-five years after Hindley, the principal type remains the gold standard against which every inference algorithm is judged.
