# Ricci Flow with Surgery and Perelman's Entropy Functionals: The Geometric Analysis Behind the Poincaré Conjecture

## Abstract

This thesis presents the geometric-analytic machinery by which Grigori Perelman resolved the Poincaré conjecture and Thurston's geometrization conjecture: the Ricci flow with surgery, powered by two monotone quantities — the **W-entropy** and the **reduced volume** — introduced in Perelman's 2002 paper on the entropy formula. We trace Hamilton's program from the Ricci flow equation through its singularity structure, explain how Perelman's entropy rules out collapsing and characterizes singularity models as gradient shrinking solitons, describe the canonical neighborhood theorem that makes topological surgery possible at singular times, and outline how finite-time extinction of the flow proves that every closed simply connected three-manifold is diffeomorphic to the three-sphere. The exposition integrates the historical breakthrough, the core PDE estimates, the surgery algorithm, and the limits of the method, drawing on the original papers and the major expositions of Kleiner–Lott, Cao–Zhu, Morgan–Tian, and Tao.

## 1 Intro

The **Poincaré conjecture**, posed in 1904, asserted that every closed simply connected three-dimensional manifold is homeomorphic — and, after Smale and Freedman, diffeomorphic — to the three-sphere $S^3$. For a century it stood as the most celebrated open problem in topology. Richard Hamilton's proposal in 1982 to attack it via the **Ricci flow**, the nonlinear heat-type equation

$$\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial t} = -2R_{ij},$$

initiated a program of extraordinary depth: evolve a Riemannian metric by its own curvature, let the flow smooth out the geometry, and read off the topology from the long-time behavior [2]. Hamilton proved that the flow improves the metric on positively curved three-manifolds, but the general case produced **singularities** — regions where curvature blows up in finite time and the flow ceases to exist classically.

The decisive advance came from Grigori Perelman in 2002–2003. In a pair of short, remarkably dense preprints, Perelman introduced two integral monotone quantities that tamed the analysis: the **W-entropy** $\mathcal{W}(g,f,\tau)$, modeled on Huisken's monotonicity for mean curvature flow, and the **reduced volume** $\tilde{V}(\tau)$, built from a space-time distance functional analogous to Li–Yau path integrals [5][3]. From these he extracted **no-local-collapsing theorems**, a refined classification of singularity models, and a rigorously defined **Ricci flow with surgery** — the flow continued past singularities by cutting out degenerating necks and capping them, in the spirit of Hamilton's earlier incomplete vision. Subsequent expositions filled in the sketchy details, completing the proof [1][2][4][6].

> Theorem: *(Perelman, 2003)* Every closed simply connected three-manifold is diffeomorphic to $S^3$. More generally, the Ricci flow with surgery, continued for all time, yields Thurston's eight geometric decompositions, proving the geometrization conjecture.

This thesis reconstructs that argument as a single analytic narrative: from the entropy functionals, through the canonical neighborhood structure, to surgery and extinction.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Hamilton's program and the singularity obstacle

Hamilton's Ricci flow is a strictly parabolic system of PDEs on the metric tensor. Short-time existence on closed manifolds was established by Hamilton using DeTurck's trick [6]. In three dimensions, the Riemann curvature tensor is algebraically determined by the Ricci tensor, which keeps the flow tractable: the scalar curvature satisfies a maximum-principle inequality that forces $\min R \to +\infty$ unless the flow is trivial. This suggested a beautiful strategy — run the flow, normalize volume, and obtain convergence to a constant-curvature metric, revealing the topology.

But generic initial metrics do not cooperate. Just as mean curvature flow forms necks, the Ricci flow in three dimensions develops **finite-time singularities** whose blow-up models Hamilton classified as: (i) spherical quotients $S^3/\Gamma$, (ii) cylinders $S^2 \times \mathbb{R}$, (iii) the cigar soliton cross $\mathbb{R}$, and (iv) more exotic ancient solutions. Hamilton developed a **surgery** procedure for certain singularities but was blocked on two fronts: he needed an injectivity-radius estimate to prevent collapse at small scales, and he could not rule out the cigar-like "degenerate" blow-up models, which resisted surgery [2]. Without control of the singularity zoo, the program stalled for two decades.

### 2.2 Entropy in geometric flows: from Huisken to Perelman

The central analytic innovation was to view the Ricci flow as a gradient flow. Huisken had shown that for mean curvature flow, a Gaussian-weighted area integral is monotone, with equality on self-similar shrinkers [3]. Perelman adapted this philosophy. He observed that the Ricci flow can be written as the gradient flow of an Einstein–Hilbert-type functional, and introduced

$$\mathcal{W}(g, f, \tau) := \int_M \left(\tau(R + |\nabla f|^2) + f - n\right) \frac{e^{-f}}{(4\pi\tau)^{n/2}} \, dV_g,$$

under the normalization $\int_M (4\pi\tau)^{-n/2} e^{-f} \, dV_g = 1$. Its key property is **monotonicity**: under the coupled flow $\partial_t g = -2\mathrm{Ric}$ and the conjugate heat equation $\partial_t f = -\Delta f + |\nabla f|^2 - R + n/(2\tau)$, one computes

$$\frac{d}{d\tau}\mathcal{W} = - \int_M 2\tau \left|\mathrm{Ric} + \nabla\nabla f - \frac{1}{2\tau}g\right|^2 (4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-f} \, dV \le 0,$$

with equality precisely on **gradient shrinking solitons** [3][7]. A related quantity, the **reduced volume** $\tilde{V}(\tau) = \int_M (4\pi\tau)^{-n/2} e^{-\ell} \, dV_g$ defined via Perelman's $\ell$-length, is likewise nonincreasing in $\tau$ and constant exactly on shrinkers — a parabolic analogue of the Bishop–Gromov volume comparison [7].

### 2.3 What the entropy buys: non-collapsing and ancient solutions

Two corollaries changed the subject. First, define the scale-invariant quantity $\mu(g,\tau) = \inf_f \mathcal{W}(g,f,\tau)$; its monotonicity yields **Perelman's no-local-collapsing theorem**: curvature controls volume from below at all scales, so blow-up sequences have positive injectivity radius and admit smooth limits [1][5]. This was exactly Hamilton's missing injectivity-radius estimate. Second, combining monotonicity with compactness gives that singularity blow-ups — the ancient, $\kappa$-noncollapsed solutions Perelman called **$\kappa$-solutions** — must be modeled on gradient shrinking solitons [3][5]. The cigar soliton, which is collapsed, is thereby excluded as a singularity model. With the zoo pruned, the remaining models (spheres, cylinders, and their quotients) admit a **canonical neighborhood structure**: at points of large scalar curvature, the geometry is, after rescaling, a neck, a cap, or a closed spherical space form [5].

## 3 Methodology

The proof of the Poincaré conjecture via Ricci flow with surgery proceeds in four analytic stages, each anchored by one of Perelman's monotone quantities.

**Stage 1 — Long-time flow with entropy bounds.** Starting from any Riemannian metric on a closed three-manifold, run the Ricci flow. The W-entropy $\mu(g(t),\tau)$ provides a scale-invariant monotone barrier that prevents the formation of collapsing singularities: a uniform lower bound on $\mu$ implies uniform $\kappa$-noncollapsing on bounded time intervals, so that wherever curvature stays bounded the flow remains smooth and compactness applies [3][5].

**Stage 2 — Canonical neighborhoods at high curvature.** Where scalar curvature becomes large, rescale the flow around the high-curvature point. By non-collapsing and Hamilton's compactness, a subsequence converges to an ancient $\kappa$-solution. Perelman's classification of three-dimensional $\kappa$-solutions — via the entropy and the Harnack-type estimates of Hamilton and Ivey — shows that every point of sufficiently large curvature in the rescaled flow has a neighborhood that is $\varepsilon$-close to one of the standard models: a **neck** $S^2 \times$ (interval), a **cap** (a neck with one end capped by a three-ball), or a closed spherical space form [5][2]. This is the *canonical neighborhood theorem*, the geometric prerequisite for surgery.

**Stage 3 — Surgery.** At a singular time, the high-curvature region is a union of necks, capped necks, and spherical components. Choose surgery parameters: a curvature threshold $r^{-2}$ and precision $\delta$. On each neck where the curvature is maximal, cut the manifold along the central two-sphere and glue in standard caps, discarding components that are spherical space forms or tubes capped at both ends [4]. Crucially, Perelman showed that the surgery can be arranged so that the **reduced volume remains almost monotone** across the surgery time, so the global entropy bounds survive the topological modification [3]. The post-surgery metric is then smoothed at the gluing sites, and the flow restarts. The procedure is shown to terminate: only finitely many surgeries occur in any finite time interval.

**Stage 4 — Finite-time extinction and topological conclusion.** For a simply connected initial manifold, the W-entropy comparison with shrinking solitons drives the flow to **finite-time extinction**: minimal two-sphere areas satisfy a differential inequality $dA/dt \le -4\pi + (3/(4t+1))A$, forcing the area — and hence each component — to vanish in finite time [4]. Since surgery never creates nontrivial topology that could survive (the flow-with-surgery becomes extinct), the initial manifold must have been a connected sum of spherical space forms and $S^2$-bundles over $S^1$; simple connectivity then forces $M \cong S^3$ [2][4].

The following table summarizes the roles of the monotone quantities.

| Quantity | Definition | Monotone in | Constant on | Role in proof |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| W-entropy $\mathcal{W}$ | $\int (\tau(R+\lvert\nabla f\rvert^2)+f-n)\,u\,dV$ | $\tau$ increasing | gradient shrinkers | non-collapsing; stability of blow-ups |

| Min-sphere area $W(\alpha)$ | loop-space min-max | $t$ decreasing | round $S^2$ | finite-time extinction for $\pi_1 = 0$ |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The gradient-flow structure and the conjugate heat equation

Perelman's first insight was variational. The Ricci flow is not the gradient flow of the Einstein–Hilbert functional $\mathcal{E}(g) = \int_M R \, dV_g$ under the $L^2$ metric, because volume changes along the flow; but by introducing a density $e^{-f}$ and the functional $\mathcal{F}(g,f) = \int_M (R + |\nabla f|^2) e^{-f} \, dV_g$, the Ricci flow becomes the gradient flow of $\mathcal{F}$ when the metric on the space of pairs $(g,f)$ is suitably weighted [1]. The **conjugate heat equation**

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = -\Delta f + |\nabla f|^2 - R + \frac{n}{2\tau}, \qquad \tau = T - t,$$

is the adjoint of the linearized flow, and its fundamental solutions $u = (4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-f}$ serve as the weights in the W-functional. Monotonicity of $\mathcal{W}$ then follows from a Bochner-type identity for the Harnack quantity $v = \tau(2\Delta f - |\nabla f|^2 + R) + f - n$; the computation shows $\Box v = -2\tau | \mathrm{Ric} + \nabla\nabla f - g/(2\tau) |^2 u \le 0$, and the maximum principle promotes the pointwise inequality to the integral monotonicity [3][7]. Equality holds exactly when $\mathrm{Ric} + \nabla\nabla f = g/(2\tau)$ — the **gradient shrinking soliton equation** — so the entropy detects self-similar solutions.

### 4.2 The reduced volume as a parabolic Bishop–Gromov theorem

Perelman's second monotone quantity is built from the **$\mathcal{L}$-length**,

$$\mathcal{L}(\gamma) = \int_{\tau_1}^{\tau_2} \sqrt{\tau}\,\big(R(\gamma(\tau)) + |\dot{\gamma}(\tau)|^2\big)\, d\tau,$$

defined over curves in space-time going backward from a base point. Minimizers are $\mathcal{L}$-geodesics, and the **reduced distance** $\ell(q,\tau) = \inf_\gamma \mathcal{L}(\gamma)/(2\sqrt{\tau})$ satisfies differential inequalities mirroring those of the Riemannian distance function: $\partial_\tau \ell - \Delta\ell + |\nabla\ell|^2 - R + n/(2\tau) \ge 0$ [5]. The **reduced volume** $\tilde{V}(\tau) = \int_M (4\pi\tau)^{-n/2} e^{-\ell(q,\tau)} \, dV_g$ is then nonincreasing in $\tau$, with $\tilde{V} \le 1$ and equality only on shrinking solitons. Perelman observed that this is the parabolic analogue of the Bishop–Gromov relative volume comparison: where Bishop–Gromov controls how volume ratios decay under a Ricci lower bound in elliptic geometry, the reduced volume controls how the weighted volume of a backward space-time ball behaves under the flow [3]. Because the reduced volume is defined purely through the $\mathcal{L}$-geometry — no global minimizer $f$ is needed — it **behaves well across surgery**: Perelman arranged the surgery caps so that $\mathcal{L}$-geodesics barely notice the modification, preserving near-monotonicity of $\tilde{V}$ through the singular time [3].

### 4.3 $\kappa$-solutions and the canonical neighborhood theorem

An **ancient solution** is a Ricci flow defined for all $t$ in the unbounded-below interval $(-\infty, 0]$; a **$\kappa$-solution** is an ancient, $\kappa$-noncollapsed, complete solution with bounded nonnegative curvature operator and positive scalar curvature. Blow-up limits of the Ricci flow at singularities are $\kappa$-solutions. Perelman classified three-dimensional $\kappa$-solutions: aside from the shrinking round sphere and its quotients, every $\kappa$-solution is diffeomorphic to $S^2 \times \mathbb{R}$ or a quotient thereof, is a gradient shrinking soliton at the level of asymptotic models, and has vanishing asymptotic volume ratio [5][1]. The key steps — backward limit existence via the reduced volume, the exclusion of the cigar by non-collapsing, and Hamilton's dimension-reduction — are documented in the Kleiner–Lott companion notes [5].

From this classification flows the **canonical neighborhood theorem**: there exist constants such that any point $(x,t)$ in a three-dimensional Ricci flow with normalized initial data and scalar curvature $R(x,t) \ge r^{-2}$ possesses a neighborhood, after rescaling by $R(x,t)$, that is $\varepsilon$-close (in a strong $C^k$ sense) to the corresponding region of a $\kappa$-solution — hence to a neck, a cap, or a closed spherical space form [2][5]. This is the precise sense in which singularities are "standard," and it is what makes surgery a well-defined geometric operation rather than an ad hoc topological one.

### 4.4 The surgery algorithm and extinction

Surgery is performed as follows. Fix parameters $r$ (the canonical neighborhood scale) and $\delta$ (precision). When the flow approaches a singular time $T$, the region $\Omega = \{R > r^{-2}\}$ decomposes, by the canonical neighborhood theorem, into finitely many tubes capped at one or both ends, horns, and closed components [4]. Each **neck** is cut along its central $S^2$; to each resulting boundary sphere a standard **cap** (a three-ball with a controlled metric matching the neck to first order) is glued. Components that are closed spherical space forms, $S^2 \times S^1$ pieces, or doubly capped tubes are discarded. The gluing metric is then mollified so the flow restarts smoothly, and Perelman's delicate estimates show the new metric satisfies the same curvature pinching and non-collapsing bounds, with the reduced volume losing only a controllable amount [3][8].

The analytic punchline is **extinction**.

The core monotone computation can be illustrated numerically on a model shrinking soliton:

```python
import numpy as np

def W_functional_sphere(n=3, tau=1.0, num=200000):
    """Monte-Carlo W on the round shrinking sphere of radius sqrt(2(n-1)tau).
    For the exact shrinker, W is tau-independent: W = -n/2 * log(4*pi*e*tau/(n-1))... 
    up to the soliton potential; here we evaluate the integrand form."""
    r = np.sqrt(2*(n-1)*tau)
    # sample scalar curvature and |grad f|^2 for the Gaussian shrinker model
    R = n*(n-1)/r**2 * np.ones(num)
    f = np.linspace(0.01, 6.0, num)          # radial potential samples
    gradf2 = (f/(2*tau))**2
    u = (4*np.pi*tau)**(-n/2) * np.exp(-f)
    u /= u.mean()                             # normalize to a probability weight
    W = np.mean((tau*(R + gradf2) + f - n) * u) / np.mean(u)
    return W

for tau in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0):
    print(f"tau={tau:4.1f}  W approx = {W_functional_sphere(tau=tau):+.6f}")
# On a true gradient shrinker the value is independent of tau (monotone, constant),
# while on a generic metric it strictly decreases -- the signature of entropy.
```

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The "experiments" of geometric analysis are the theorems themselves; we collect the load-bearing statements and their proof skeletons.

> Theorem: *(No local collapsing I, Perelman)* There exists $\kappa > 0$ depending only on the initial metric such that the Ricci flow is $\kappa$-noncollapsed at scales below a fixed radius on any finite time interval. *Proof sketch.* The quantity $\mu(g(t),\tau) = \inf_f \mathcal{W}$ is nondecreasing in $t$; a uniform lower bound on $\mu$ plus a Sobolev inequality converts a curvature bound $\lvert\mathrm{Rm}\rvert \le r^{-2}$ on a parabolic ball into a volume lower bound $\mathrm{Vol}(B(x,r)) \ge \kappa r^n$ [5][7].

> Theorem: *(Structure of $\kappa$-solutions, Perelman)* Every three-dimensional $\kappa$-solution is, up to isometry, the shrinking round $S^3$ (or a quotient), the shrinking cylinder $S^2 \times \mathbb{R}$ (or a quotient), or a noncompact solution asymptotic to the cylinder with one capped end. *Proof sketch.* The reduced volume $\tilde{V}(\tau)$ has a backward limit $\bar{V} > 0$; rescaling by $\tau \to \infty$ yields a limit that attains equality in the monotonicity, hence a gradient shrinker; dimension reduction and the Hamilton–Ivey pinching force the listed models, while non-collapsing excludes the cigar [5][1].

> Theorem: *(Canonical neighborhoods)* For normalized initial data, points of scalar curvature above a uniform threshold admit $\varepsilon$-canonical neighborhoods modeled on $\kappa$-solutions. *Proof sketch.* Contradiction via blow-up: a sequence violating the conclusion rescales to a $\kappa$-solution (using non-collapsing and Hamilton compactness), which by the structure theorem has standard geometry — contradiction [2][5].

> Theorem: *(Finite-time extinction, Perelman)* If $M$ is closed, simply connected, the Ricci flow with surgery becomes extinct in finite time. *Proof sketch.* Minimal two-sphere area satisfies the differential inequality above; surgery can only decrease the relevant min-max width; hence each component disappears, and only finitely many surgeries intervene [4].

Together these imply the Poincaré conjecture: the flow-with-surgery starts at $M$ and ends at the empty manifold, with topology changing only by the classified surgery moves; reversing the moves reconstructs $M$ as a connected sum of the listed prime pieces, and $\pi_1(M) = 0$ selects $S^3$ [2][4].

## 6 Limitations

The method, for all its power, has genuine boundaries. First, the entire surgery construction is **dimension-specific**: it leans on the algebraic fact that in three dimensions the curvature operator is controlled by the Ricci tensor, on the Hamilton–Ivey pinching estimate (which has no clean analogue in higher dimensions), and on the classification of $\kappa$-solutions (open in dimension four and above). Extending Ricci flow with surgery to four-manifolds remains a major open program, pursued via the more recent theory of singular Ricci flows rather than classical surgery [6].

Second, the proof is **non-effective**: the constants in the canonical neighborhood theorem and the surgery parameters are obtained by contradiction and compactness, so the argument gives no computable bound on the number of surgeries, no explicit diffeomorphism to $S^3$, and no algorithm one could implement — a sharp contrast with, say, normal-surface algorithms in computational topology.

Third, **smoothness versus topological category**: the Ricci flow argument naturally proves the *smooth* Poincaré conjecture in dimension three, which coincides with the topological statement only because of Moise's theorem that every topological three-manifold admits a unique smooth structure. In dimension four the smooth Poincaré conjecture remains open, and Ricci flow offers no traction there.

Finally, the expositional burden is real: Perelman's original papers are famously terse, several key estimates (the $\mathcal{L}$-geometry comparison theorems, the surgery parameter interdependence) required hundreds of pages of clarification [1][2][4][5], and the reduced-volume-across-surgery argument is delicate enough that alternative proofs of extinction (Colding–Minicozzi) were developed partly to circumvent it.

## 7 Conclusion

Perelman's resolution of the Poincaré conjecture is a triumph of **geometric analysis as topology**: a nonlinear heat equation, guided by two monotone entropy functionals, forces a Riemannian three-manifold to reveal its prime decomposition. The W-entropy turned the Ricci flow into a gradient-like flow with a Lyapunov function, ruling out collapse and identifying shrinkers as the universal singularity models; the reduced volume provided a parabolic Bishop–Gromov theory robust enough to survive topological surgery; and the canonical neighborhood theorem converted PDE blow-up analysis into a precise surgical algorithm. Finite-time extinction then closed the loop for simply connected manifolds, while the long-time analysis completed Thurston's geometrization.

The legacy extends well beyond topology. Perelman's entropy ideas have seeded new monotonicity formulas across geometric flows [7], the $\mathcal{L}$-geometry underlies modern work on ancient solutions and singularity formation [3], and the surgery paradigm reshaped how analysts handle finite-time singularities in nonlinear PDE. A century-old question about the shape of space was answered not by cutting and pasting alone, but by letting geometry diffuse — and by measuring, with entropy, exactly what the diffusion remembers.
