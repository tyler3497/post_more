# Neural Ordinary Differential Equations: Adjoint Sensitivity Analysis, Continuous Normalizing Flows, and the Geometry of Infinite-Depth Networks

## Abstract

Deep networks are conventionally organized as discrete compositions of parameterized maps, a convention that couples representational capacity to a fixed integer depth. Neural ordinary differential equations (Neural ODEs) recast this design choice by parameterizing the time derivative of the hidden state with a neural network, *dh(t)/dt = f_theta(h(t), t)*, and computing model outputs through black-box numerical integration [1]. This continuous-depth perspective yields constant-memory training via the adjoint sensitivity method, adaptive computation through solver tolerances, and a natural formalism for irregularly sampled time series. We survey the mathematical foundations of this paradigm: the reverse-mode adjoint dynamics that make backpropagation through solvers memory-efficient, the instantaneous change-of-variables theorem that turns ODEs into continuous normalizing flows trained with Hutchinson's trace estimator [2], and the topological restrictions of flow maps, which are homeomorphisms of the state space and therefore motivate augmented state spaces [3] and stochastic extensions via neural stochastic differential equations [4]. We prove the key density-transport result, review empirical performance on density estimation and sequential modeling, and identify fundamental limitations including stiffness, function-evaluation blowup, and numerical instability of the adjoint.

## 1 Introduction

The dominant architectural pattern of modern deep learning composes a finite sequence of parameterized transformations. Residual networks, the canonical example, iterate

*h_{t+1} = h_t + f_t(h_t, theta_t)*,  *t = 0, ..., T - 1*,

where each *f_t* is a learned residual block. Interpreted as an Euler discretization of a continuous trajectory with step size *Delta t = 1*, this recurrence converges, in the limit of infinitesimally small steps and tied parameters, to an *ordinary differential equation*. Neural ordinary differential equations, introduced by Chen, Duvenaud, and colleagues [1], make this limit explicit: rather than specifying a discrete stack of layers, one specifies a **vector field** *f_theta : R^d x [0, T] -> R^d* parameterized by a single neural network, and defines the model output as the terminal value of the initial-value problem

*dh(t)/dt = f_theta(h(t), t)*,  *h(0) = x*.

The network output is then *h(T) = ODESolve(h(0), f_theta, 0, T)*, computed by any black-box numerical integrator. Three consequences distinguish this construction from discrete-depth models:

1. **Constant memory cost.** Gradients are computed by the *adjoint sensitivity method*, which solves a second, augmented ODE backward in time rather than backpropagating through the internal operations of the solver. Memory is therefore *O(1)* in the number of solver steps, independent of depth.
2. **Adaptive computation.** The solver allocates function evaluations where the learned dynamics demand them: simple inputs integrate cheaply, complex inputs trigger fine-grained stepping. Tolerance parameters trade numerical precision against speed at inference time without retraining.
3. **Native continuous time.** The model is defined for arbitrary evaluation times, making it a natural substrate for irregularly sampled time series, latent-variable dynamics models, and generative flows.

This thesis develops the mathematical core of the Neural ODE program, traces its extension into *continuous normalizing flows* [2], confronts the *topological obstructions* that limit plain ODE flows and the augmentation remedy [3], and follows the generalization into *neural stochastic differential equations* [4] and infinitely deep Bayesian networks [5].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Existence, uniqueness, and the flow map

A first-order ODE *dh/dt = f(h, t)* with *f* Lipschitz continuous in *h* admits, by the Picard-Lindelof theorem, a unique solution through each initial condition. Neural networks built from Lipschitz activations (tanh, ReLU, ELU) with bounded weights satisfy this condition on compact sets, so the Neural ODE is well-posed. For fixed parameters, the map

*Phi_T : h(0) |-> h(T)*

is called the **flow map**. Crucially, it is a *diffeomorphism*: trajectories of an ODE cannot cross, so *Phi_T* is bijective with a differentiable inverse (obtained by integrating backward). This invertibility is the source of both the generative power of continuous normalizing flows and the expressivity limits analyzed in Section 4.3.

### 2.2 The classical adjoint sensitivity method

Optimal control theory has long faced the problem of differentiating a scalar objective *L(h(T))* with respect to the parameters of a dynamical system. Pontryagin's maximum principle introduces the **adjoint** (or costate) *a(t) = dL/dh(t)*, which evolves backward according to

*da(t)/dt = -a(t)^T (partial f / partial h)(h(t), t, theta)*.

Given *a(T) = dL/dh(T)*, solving this linear ODE backward yields the full gradient *dL/dtheta = -integral_0^T a(t)^T (partial f / partial theta) dt* without storing the forward trajectory. Chen et al. [1] recognized that this classical device answers the central engineering question of Neural ODEs: how to train through a black-box solver without access to its internal operations.

### 2.3 Normalizing flows

Normalizing flows [6] construct expressive densities by pushing a simple base distribution through an invertible transformation *x = g(z)* and applying the change-of-variables formula *log p(x) = log p(z) - log |det(dg/dz)|*. Architectures such as RealNVP and Glow restrict *g* to coupling layers or 1x1 convolutions so that the Jacobian determinant stays tractable. Continuous normalizing flows [1] remove these restrictions: when *g* is the flow map of an ODE, the log-determinant collapses to an *integral of the Jacobian trace*, computable in a single augmented integration.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Model specification

A Neural ODE block is specified by three choices: a dynamics network *f_theta(h, t)* (typically an MLP or convolutional network with the scalar time *t* concatenated or broadcast into the input), an integration interval *[t_0, t_1]*, and a solver with tolerances *(rtol, atol)*. For supervised learning, the terminal state *h(t_1)* feeds a linear head and a task loss. For time series, an encoder maps observations to a latent initial state *z(t_0)*, the ODE evolves the latent trajectory, and a decoder emits observations at arbitrary query times -- handling irregular sampling natively, since the solver can be queried at any *t* [1].

### 3.2 The adjoint method for Neural ODEs

Define the adjoint *a(t) = dL/dh(t)* and the parameter adjoint *dL/dtheta*. Differentiating the integral form of the solution and exchanging the order of integration yields the augmented backward system

*d/dt [h(t), a(t), dL/dtheta(t)] = [f_theta, -a(t)^T partial f / partial h, -a(t)^T partial f / partial theta]*,

integrated from *t_1* back to *t_0* with terminal conditions *h(t_1)* from the forward pass, *a(t_1) = dL/dh(t_1)*, and *dL/dtheta(t_1) = 0*. A single call to `ODESolve` on this augmented state returns all gradients. The memory footprint is *O(1)* in solver steps -- the forward activations are never stored -- at the cost of re-solving the forward dynamics in reverse. All terms *a^T (partial f / partial .)* are vector-Jacobian products, computed by reverse-mode autodiff in time comparable to one evaluation of *f_theta*.

```python
# Adjoint-method gradient via torchdiffeq (Chen et al. [1])
import torch
from torchdiffeq import odeint_adjoint as odeint

class ODEFunc(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = torch.nn.Sequential(
            torch.nn.Linear(dim + 1, 64), torch.nn.Tanh(),
            torch.nn.Linear(64, 64), torch.nn.Tanh(),
            torch.nn.Linear(64, dim))
    def forward(self, t, h):
        # time is concatenated so the vector field may be non-autonomous
        tt = torch.ones_like(h[:, :1]) * t
        return self.net(torch.cat([h, tt], dim=1))

f = ODEFunc(dim=32)
h0 = torch.randn(128, 32)          # batch of initial states
t = torch.tensor([0.0, 1.0])
hT = odeint(f, h0, t)[-1]          # forward solve; adjoint handles backward
loss = hT.pow(2).mean()
loss.backward()  # O(1) memory: no solver internals stored
```

### 3.3 Instantaneous change of variables

Let *z(t)* solve *dz/dt = f(z, t)* and let *p(z, t)* be the density induced by pushing the base density *p(z, 0)* through the flow. The continuity equation of fluid mechanics, *partial_t p + div(p f) = 0*, implies along a trajectory:

> **Theorem (Instantaneous change of variables).** Under mild regularity on *f*,
> *d/dt log p(z(t), t) = -tr(partial f / partial z (z(t), t))*.

Integrating from *t_0* to *t_1* gives the exact log-likelihood

*log p(x) = log p(z_0) - integral_{t_0}^{t_1} tr(partial f / partial z) dt*,

where *x = z(t_1)* is the observed datum. The state and its log-density are integrated *jointly* as one augmented ODE, so density estimation reduces to maximum likelihood with no architectural constraints on *f_theta* [1].

### 3.4 Scalable trace estimation

The exact trace costs *O(d)* vector-Jacobian products -- prohibitive for high-dimensional *f*. FFJORD [2] replaces it with **Hutchinson's estimator**,

*tr(A) = E_{epsilon}[epsilon^T A epsilon]*,  *E[epsilon] = 0, E[epsilon epsilon^T] = I*,

so a single VJP with a Rademacher or Gaussian probe vector yields an unbiased estimate of the divergence. The resulting model is a *free-form continuous dynamics* generative model: unrestricted architectures, exact (unbiased) likelihoods, and one-pass sampling by integrating the learned field.

### 3.5 Augmentation

Augmented Neural ODEs [3] evolve the state in *R^{d+p}* with *h(0) = [x; 0]* and read out a projection of *h(T)*. The extra dimensions act as scratch space in which the flow can route around topological obstructions of the original space, restoring expressivity while empirically *reducing* the number of function evaluations, because the dynamics need not contort into near-singular configurations.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The geometry of the adjoint trajectory

The adjoint *a(t)* is not merely a computational device; it is the *costate* of optimal control, the sensitivity of the loss to infinitesimal perturbations of the trajectory at each instant. Geometrically, the pair *(h(t), a(t))* evolves under a Hamiltonian system: defining *H(h, a, t) = a^T f_theta(h, t)*, the forward-backward equations are Hamilton's equations, and the flow is *symplectic*. This structure explains both the elegance and the fragility of the method. Symplectic integrators would preserve the geometry exactly, but standard adaptive Runge-Kutta solvers (e.g., Dormand-Prince) do not; the reconstructed backward trajectory *h(t)* can therefore diverge from the forward trajectory when the dynamics are chaotic or stiff, injecting gradient error. This forward-backward inconsistency is the primary numerical hazard of the adjoint method and motivates checkpointed variants that store a sparse skeleton of the forward states and re-solve locally [1].

A second subtlety is that the adjoint ODE is *linear* in *a* but inherits the Jacobian *partial f / partial h* evaluated along the trajectory, so its stiffness mirrors that of the forward problem: wherever *f_theta* has large local Lipschitz constant, the backward solve demands small steps. Adaptive solvers handle this automatically, but the number of function evaluations (NFE) can grow dramatically during training as the learned field sharpens -- a phenomenon visible as a steadily rising NFE curve in Neural ODE training logs.

### 4.2 Continuous normalizing flows and the trace bottleneck

Continuous normalizing flows reframe generative modeling as *density transport along a learned vector field*. Unlike coupling-based flows, which partition dimensions and restrict each layer's Jacobian to be triangular, a CNF imposes no structural constraint on *f_theta*: any neural network defines a valid bijection through its flow map, and the change-of-variables formula needs only the divergence. FFJORD [2] made this practical at scale by pairing the CNF likelihood with Hutchinson's estimator, demonstrating that unbiased stochastic trace estimates suffice for stable maximum-likelihood training on image data.

The estimator's variance is the central design consideration. For a probe vector *epsilon*, the variance of *epsilon^T A epsilon* scales with the off-diagonal energy of *A = partial f / partial z*; highly non-normal Jacobians produce noisy likelihood estimates and noisy gradients. In practice, Rademacher probes, fixed probe vectors across the integration interval, and mild regularization of the dynamics keep variance manageable. The payoff is a model class surveyed comprehensively in [6] that achieves exact-likelihood density estimation with fully free-form architectures -- a combination no discrete flow attains.

| Model | Transform | Log-det cost | Sampling | Architecture constraints |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RealNVP | Coupling layers | *O(d)*, exact | One pass | Partitioned dims, triangular Jacobian |
| Glow | 1x1 conv + coupling | *O(d)*, exact | One pass | Invertible conv, coupling structure |
| CNF (Chen et al.) | ODE flow map | *O(d)* VJPs, exact trace | ODE solve | None (any *f_theta*) |
| FFJORD | ODE flow map | *O(1)* VJP, unbiased trace | ODE solve | None (any *f_theta*) |

### 4.3 Topological obstructions and augmentation

Because every flow map *Phi_T* is a homeomorphism, a Neural ODE *preserves the topology of its input space*: connected components stay connected, and the map is orientation-preserving. Dupont, Doucet, and Teh [3] proved that this restriction is not academic -- it rules out functions of genuine interest. Their canonical counterexample is the reflection-like map *g(x) = -x* composed with translations in one dimension: no flow on *R* can implement it, because a continuous bijection of *R* is strictly monotone and cannot swap the order of two points. More generally, a Neural ODE followed by a linear layer cannot approximate arbitrary continuous functions without lifting the dimension; the flow would have to tear the space, which diffeomorphisms cannot do.

> **Theorem (Dupont et al. [3], informal).** There exist continuous functions *g : R^d -> R^d* that no Neural ODE flow map on *R^d* can represent exactly. In particular, flows cannot change the topological type of level sets of the learned representation.

Augmentation dissolves the obstruction by embedding the problem in *R^{d+p}*: the flow may now braid trajectories through the extra dimensions, separating regions that were topologically locked in *R^d*, before projecting back. Empirically, ANODEs not only fit the previously impossible targets but train *faster*, because the vector field no longer needs near-singular squeezes to approximate topology-changing maps -- the NFE drops accordingly [3]. The lesson generalizes: whenever a flow-based model struggles, the first suspect should be topology, and the first remedy extra dimensions.

### 4.4 From ODEs to SDEs: the stochastic adjoint

Deterministic flows encode all uncertainty in the initial condition, a poor fit for intrinsically stochastic or chaotic processes. Neural SDEs replace the drift-only dynamics with

*dh_t = f_theta(h_t, t) dt + g_phi(h_t, t) dW_t*,

parameterizing both drift *f* and diffusion *g* by networks. Li, Wong, Chen, and Duvenaud [4] generalized the adjoint sensitivity method to this setting, deriving a *backward SDE* whose solution is the gradient process. Two innovations make it practical: (i) the gradient SDE requires only vector-Jacobian products, never the full Jacobian over parameters, so cost is independent of parameter count; and (ii) the driving Brownian motion is reconstructed during the backward pass from a stored random seed via a memory-efficient noise-caching scheme (a Brownian tree), preserving the *O(1)* memory profile of the deterministic adjoint.

Combined with stochastic variational inference, this yields *latent SDE* models that generalize latent ODEs to noisy dynamics, with competitive results on a 50-dimensional motion-capture benchmark [4]. Xu et al. [5] pushed the program further into Bayesian deep learning: *infinitely deep Bayesian neural networks* place priors over the drift and diffusion of an SDE, so that a distribution over continuous-depth networks -- rather than a single network -- is learned, with the SDE adjoint supplying scalable gradients. The trajectory from [1] through [4] to [5] is thus a coherent research arc: deterministic continuous depth, then continuous stochastic depth, then distributions over continuous stochastic depth.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof of the instantaneous change of variables

> **Theorem.** Let *z(t)* solve *dz/dt = f(z, t)* with *f* continuously differentiable, and let *p(z, t)* be the density obtained by transporting *p(z, 0)* along the flow. Then *d/dt log p(z(t), t) = -tr(partial f / partial z)*.

*Proof sketch.* Mass conservation gives the continuity equation *partial_t p + nabla . (p f) = 0*. Along a trajectory, the material derivative is *d/dt p(z(t), t) = partial_t p + (nabla p) . f*. Substituting *partial_t p = -nabla . (p f) = -(nabla p) . f - p (nabla . f)*, the advective terms cancel, leaving *d/dt p = -p (nabla . f)*. Dividing by *p* and noting *nabla . f = tr(partial f / partial z)* gives the result. Integrating from *t_0* to *t_1* yields the CNF log-likelihood used in [1, 2]. The argument is the Eulerian-to-Lagrangian passage of fluid dynamics applied to probability mass. QED

### 5.2 Representative empirical results

On **density estimation**, FFJORD [2] reported approximately *0.99 bits/dim* on MNIST and *3.40 bits/dim* on CIFAR-10 -- state of the art among exact-likelihood models at publication, achieved with unrestricted convolutional dynamics and Hutchinson trace estimates. The CNF formulation in [1] established the approach on 2-D toy densities and tabular data, where the learned flows visibly morph Gaussian blobs into multimodal targets.

On **supervised classification**, the original Neural ODE [1] reached accuracy competitive with a ResNet of matched capacity on MNIST while using substantially fewer parameters and constant-memory training, demonstrating that continuous depth is not merely a theoretical curiosity.

On **irregular time series**, latent ODE models [1] outperformed recurrent baselines on interpolation and extrapolation of sparsely observed trajectories, precisely because the solver can be evaluated at arbitrary timestamps rather than on a fixed grid. Latent SDEs [4] extended these gains to stochastic regimes, fitting a 50-dimensional motion-capture dataset competitively.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Function-evaluation blowup.** The NFE required per forward pass typically grows during training as the learned vector field becomes more complex; without regularization the solver may take hundreds of steps, erasing the computational advantage over discrete networks. Penalizing the Frobenius norm of the Jacobian or the kinetic energy of the flow mitigates this but adds hyperparameters.
2. **Stiffness.** Learned dynamics can become stiff -- containing widely separated timescales -- at which point explicit solvers like Dormand-Prince become catastrophically inefficient and implicit solvers (with their per-step linear solves) become necessary. Neural stiff ODEs remain an open practical problem.
3. **Adjoint numerical error.** The backward reconstruction of *h(t)* need not match the forward trajectory, particularly for chaotic or stiff systems, producing biased gradients. Checkpointed adjoints trade memory for fidelity but complicate implementation.
4. **Topological expressivity ceiling.** Plain Neural ODEs cannot represent topology-changing maps [3]; augmentation is required, partially surrendering the minimalism of the original formulation.
5. **Trace-estimator variance.** Hutchinson estimates are unbiased but can be high-variance for non-normal Jacobians, injecting noise into both the likelihood and its gradients [2].
6. **Diffusion modeling cost.** Neural SDEs require SDE solvers and noise caching [4]; adaptive high-order SDE solvers are far less mature than their ODE counterparts, and latent-SDE training remains simulation-bound and difficult to parallelize.

## 7 Conclusion

Neural ordinary differential equations reframe depth as a continuous quantity, replacing the integer-indexed stack of layers with a learnable vector field integrated by numerical solvers. The adjoint sensitivity method makes this reframing trainable at constant memory; the instantaneous change-of-variables theorem turns it into a generative model with exact likelihoods; Hutchinson's estimator makes those likelihoods scalable; augmentation repairs the topological blind spots of flow maps; and the stochastic adjoint extends the entire edifice to neural SDEs and infinitely deep Bayesian networks. The paradigm's enduring contribution may be conceptual as much as practical: it demonstrated that the boundary between *model* and *numerical method* is permeable, and that classical applied mathematics -- optimal control, fluid transport, stochastic calculus -- still has new architectures to give deep learning. Its limitations, stiffness and function-evaluation growth above all, mark the frontier where the next generation of continuous-depth models will be decided.
