# Exoplanet Atmospheric Retrieval: Bayesian Spectroscopy, Transmission Spectra, and Biosignature Statistics

## Abstract

Transmission spectroscopy of exoplanets converts photons gathered during planetary transits into probabilistic statements about alien air. This thesis develops the statistical machinery of atmospheric retrieval: the forward model that maps composition, temperature structure, and clouds onto a wavelength-dependent transit depth, and the Bayesian inverse that maps the observed spectrum back onto posterior distributions over candidate atmospheres. We review the radiative-transfer geometry of transmission, the correlated-k compression of molecular line lists containing billions of transitions, and nested-sampling algorithms that deliver both parameter posteriors and the Bayesian evidence used to grade competing hypotheses. Special attention is paid to the degeneracies — cloud-top pressure versus metallicity, stellar contamination versus molecular bands, temperature gradient versus mean molecular weight — that limit what any single spectrum can say. We formalize the statistics of biosignature claims, showing why model-comparison evidence is relative rather than absolute, and we work through the JWST-era case studies of K2-18 b and GJ 1132 b, where dimethyl sulfide, water vapor, and ammonia have each been proposed and each contested. We conclude with what retrieval can and cannot certify about habitability.

---

## 1. Introduction

When a planet crosses the face of its host star, a fraction of starlight filters through the annulus of atmosphere at the terminator — the twilight boundary between day and night. At wavelengths where atmospheric gases absorb, the planet appears larger; at wavelengths where the air is transparent, it appears smaller. The resulting *transmission spectrum* is a fingerprint of atmospheric composition, but reading it is an inverse problem of notorious difficulty: the spectrum depends nonlinearly on dozens of physical parameters, several of which produce nearly identical signatures, and the data arrive corrupted by stellar variability, instrument systematics, and photon noise that rivals the signal itself [1][2].

Atmospheric *retrieval* is the disciplined answer to this predicament. Rather than fitting a single best model, retrieval explores a vast space of atmospheres with Bayesian sampling algorithms, returning probability distributions over every free parameter — molecular abundances, temperature profiles, cloud properties — together with a principled measure, the *Bayesian evidence*, of how well each competing model explains the data relative to its alternatives [3]. Born from optimal-estimation techniques applied to Solar System planets and matured through the Hubble and Spitzer eras, retrieval has become the central analytical engine of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) era of exoplanet characterization [4][5].

> **Theorem:** *No retrieval can extract more information than the data contain.* For any finite-precision transmission spectrum, there exist distinct atmospheric states whose model spectra differ by less than the measurement uncertainty at every wavelength, and Bayesian posteriors must therefore assign comparable weight to all of them. This information-theoretic ceiling bounds every claimed detection.

The stakes of getting the statistics right are high. JWST has already produced claims — water vapor, carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, dimethyl sulfide — that are individually contested, with different teams reporting different molecules from the same datasets [1][6]. The difference between a genuine biosignature detection and a statistical mirage is not instrument hardware but the correctness of the Bayesian reasoning applied after the fact. This thesis is a deep examination of that reasoning.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Transmission Geometry

Consider a transiting planet of radius $R_p$ orbiting a star of radius $R_\star$. In the absence of an atmosphere, the fractional flux dimming — the *transit depth* — is $\delta = (R_p/R_\star)^2$. An atmosphere adds an effective opaque annulus whose altitude varies with wavelength:

$$
R_p(\lambda) = R_{p,0} + H \ln\left(\sum_i \frac{\sigma_i(\lambda) \, X_i \, p}{k_B T}\right)
$$

where $H = k_B T / (\mu g)$ is the pressure scale height, $\mu$ the mean molecular weight, $g$ the surface gravity, $\sigma_i$ the absorption cross-section of species $i$, and $X_i$ its mixing ratio. Because the transit depth scales as $R_p(\lambda)^2$, the amplitude of spectral features scales with $H/R_p$ — the *scale height relative to planet radius* [4].

This single scaling explains the observational hierarchy of the field. Hot Jupiters, with $T \sim 1500$ K and hydrogen-dominated atmospheres ($\mu \approx 2.3$ amu), have scale heights of hundreds of kilometers and transit-depth variations of order $10^{-3}$. Temperate rocky planets around M dwarfs may have $H$ of only tens of kilometers against secondary atmospheres ($\mu \sim 18-44$ amu), pushing feature amplitudes to $10^{-5}$ — a regime where stellar activity often dominates the error budget [2][6].

### 2.2 A Brief History of Retrieval

Retrieval began as *optimal estimation* in the Solar System community — the NEMESIS code of Irwin et al. [5] — where a single best-fitting atmosphere was found by linearizing the forward model around a prior state. Exoplanet work, facing sparse data and multimodal degeneracies, demanded full posterior sampling instead. Three lineages emerged:

- **TauREx** (Waldmann et al.), built specifically for exoplanet transmission and emission spectra with nested-sampling engines [5].
- **CHIMERA** (Line et al.), emphasizing self-consistent thermochemical models and GPU-accelerated opacities, later extended with Gaussian-process noise models to absorb correlated systematics [7].
- **NEMESIS** itself, retrofitted with nested sampling for exoplanet application [5].

Modern frameworks — POSEIDON [3], ARCiS, petitRADTRANS, Aurora, HELIOS-R [8] — share a common skeleton: a parametric atmosphere, a radiative-transfer forward model, a Bayesian sampler, and an evidence-computing nested loop. Comparisons between them reveal that the dominant source of inter-code disagreement is not sampling but *opacity databases*: line lists, pressure broadening, and line-wing cutoffs alter retrieved abundances by factors of several [5].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Bayesian Skeleton

Every retrieval computes the posterior over model parameters $\theta$ given data $D$:

$$
p(\theta \mid D, \mathcal{M}) = \frac{\mathcal{L}(D \mid \theta, \mathcal{M}) \, \pi(\theta \mid \mathcal{M})}{Z(D \mid \mathcal{M})}
$$

where $\mathcal{L}$ is the likelihood, $\pi$ the prior, $\mathcal{M}$ the model (a particular parameterization and physical assumptions), and $Z$ the Bayesian evidence:

$$
Z(D \mid \mathcal{M}) = \int \mathcal{L}(D \mid \theta, \mathcal{M}) \, \pi(\theta \mid \mathcal{M}) \, d\theta.
$$

The evidence is the workhorse of model comparison. The *Bayes factor* between models $\mathcal{M}_1$ and $\mathcal{M}_2$,

$$
K = \frac{Z(D \mid \mathcal{M}_1)}{Z(D \mid \mathcal{M}_2)},
$$

is converted to a "detection significance" by the rough mapping $\ln K \gtrsim 3$ as strong evidence (Kass & Raftery). Whether a molecule is "detected" therefore amounts to comparing a retrieval that includes its opacity against one that does not [1][3].

The standard likelihood assumes independent Gaussian noise:

$$
\ln \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{(D_i - M_i(\theta))^2}{s_i^2} + \ln(2\pi s_i^2)\right],
$$

where the inflated uncertainties $s_i^2 = \sigma_i^2 + \sigma_{\rm extra}^2$ sometimes include a fitted error term. Gaussian-process extensions replace the diagonal covariance with a learned kernel to absorb unmodeled systematics [7].

### 3.2 Nested Sampling

The posterior is typically sampled with nested sampling (MultiNest, PyMultiNest, dynesty). The algorithm maintains a set of $N_{\rm live}$ points, iteratively discarding the lowest-likelihood point and re-sampling above its contour. The evidence emerges as a by-product: the volume-weighted sum of discarded likelihoods. Typical JWST retrievals use 400–2200 live points and evaluate $10^5$–$10^6$ forward models [1][6][8].

```python
import numpy as np
from petitRADTRANS import Radtrans
from pymultinest import run

atmosphere = Radtrans(line_species=['H2O', 'CO2', 'CH4', 'NH3'],
                      rayleigh_species=['H2', 'He'],
                      continuum_opacities=['H2-H2', 'H2-He'],
                      wlen_bords_micron=[0.7, 5.2], mode='c-k')

def loglike(theta):
    """Log-likelihood of a transmission model given JWST-like data."""
    log_x, log_pcloud, t_iso, r_p = theta[:4], theta[4], theta[5], theta[6]
    p = np.logspace(-6, 2, 100)  # pressure grid [bar]
    T = np.full_like(p, t_iso)
    x = {sp: 10**v for sp, v in zip(atmosphere.line_species, log_x)}
    atmosphere.setup_opa_structure(p)
    atmosphere.calc_transm(T, x, 2.3, 9.8, r_p, p0=0.01)
    model = (atmosphere.transm_rad / 1.0)**2  # depth vs wavelength
    s2 = sigma**2 + np.exp(theta[-1])**2      # error inflation
    return -0.5 * np.sum(((data - model)**2) / s2 + np.log(2*np.pi*s2))

run(loglike, prior, n_params=9, n_live_points=1000,
    evidence_tolerance=0.5, outputfiles_basename='run_')
```

### 3.3 The Correlated-k Trick

Molecular line lists (HITRAN, ExoMol) contain up to $10^9$ transitions per species. Computing cross-sections line-by-line at $R \sim 10^5$ for every forward model is prohibitive. The *correlated-k* method sorts opacities within each spectral bin into a cumulative distribution function $k(g)$, $g \in [0,1]$, and integrates radiative transfer over $g$ with a handful of quadrature points — reducing cost by orders of magnitude at the price of assuming the spectral ordering of opacity is *correlated* across atmospheric layers [5]. Retrievals typically operate at $R \sim 1{,}000$–$15{,}000$ in opacity space, degraded to instrument resolution. The choice of line-wing cutoff (commonly 25 cm$^{-1}$) and pressure-broadening parameters remains a leading systematic [5].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Forward-Model Physics: Beyond Isothermal Grey Clouds

The minimal retrieval parameterizes the atmosphere as isothermal with constant-with-altitude mixing ratios, a hard grey cloud deck at pressure $P_c$, and a reference radius. This suffices for Hubble-quality data but misfires on JWST spectra in specific, documented ways:

1. **Non-isothermal profiles.** Real terminators have temperature gradients; retrieving a single $T$ biases abundances because scale height and chemistry both respond to $T(p)$. Six-parameter $T$–$p$ profiles (e.g., Madhusudhan–Seager forms) are now standard [1].
2. **Non-grey clouds and hazes.** Mie-scattering aerosols produce wavelength-dependent opacity $\kappa \propto \lambda^{-\gamma}$; a grey deck over-truncates blue features and under-truncates red ones. Power-law hazes and log-normal particle-size distributions are increasingly included [6][8].
3. **Photochemistry.** Disequilibrium products (SO$_2$ from photolysis, hydrocarbons from vertical mixing) break the equilibrium-chemistry mappings that "self-consistent" retrievals rely on; free-abundance retrievals avoid the trap at the cost of parameter-count inflation [1].

### 4.2 Nested Sampling at Scale: Evidence, Priors, and Convergence

The evidence integral is prior-dependent: widening a prior range by a factor of ten lowers $\ln Z$ by roughly $\ln 10 \approx 2.3$ for an uninformative parameter — enough to flip a "detection" into a non-detection on the Kass–Raftery scale. Three pathologies follow:

- **Prior-volume poisoning.** A model with 20 species and broad priors is penalized relative to a model with 7, even when the extra species are irrelevant — the Occam factor working as intended, but capriciously sensitive to arbitrary prior bounds [4].
- **Convergence theatre.** Nested sampling with too few live points under-explores multimodal posteriors; quoted $\ln Z$ uncertainties ($\sim 0.1$–$0.5$) are internal to the sampler and do not include forward-model error [8].
- **Reference-model relativity.** A Bayes factor of $K = 50$ for "molecule X present" measures preference over *one specific null*, not over all alternatives. Adding an unconsidered species (e.g., propyne, C$_3$H$_4$) can collapse the significance — as demonstrated explicitly for the K2-18 b DMS claim [1].

```haskell
-- Bayesian evidence as a fold over discarded nested-sampling points
data Discard = Discard { lik :: Double, vol :: Double }

evidence :: [Discard] -> Double
evidence = sum . map (\d -> lik d * vol d)  -- Z = ∫ L dX

bayesFactor :: [Discard] -> [Discard] -> Double
bayesFactor m1 m0 = log (evidence m1) - log (evidence m0)
-- significance ~ sqrt(2 * ln K); ln K > 3 "strong", > 5 "decisive"
```

### 4.3 The Degeneracy Zoo

Retrieval degeneracy is not an inconvenience; it is the central phenomenon. The leading members:

| Degeneracy | Parameters entangled | Observable consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Cloud–metallicity | $P_c$ vs $X_i$, $\mu$ | High clouds flatten features identically to high-$\mu$ atmospheres [6] |
| Stellar contamination | Spot/faculae fractions vs molecular bands | Unocculted spots imprint false H$_2$O features [4] |
| $T$–$\mu$–$g$ | Scale height $H = kT/\mu g$ | Feature amplitude alone cannot separate the three |
| Chemistry–photochemistry | Equilibrium mapping vs free abundances | Same spectrum, wildly different elemental ratios [1] |
| Instrument offset | Inter-detector baseline shifts | Absorbed into continuum, corrupting $R_p$ [6] |

The transmission spectrum measures *relative* band depths against a continuum; the continuum itself is degenerate with $R_p$, reference pressure, and cloud opacity. Absolute abundances are therefore intrinsically less constrained than *relative* abundances (e.g., C/O, metallicity ratios) — a fact the literature repeatedly rediscovers [1][5].

Stellar contamination deserves emphasis: transmission spectroscopy divides by the stellar spectrum, but if the transit chord differs from the disk average — unocculted starspots, faculae — the division imprints spurious molecular features. For M-dwarf hosts, the *transit light source effect* can produce features at the tens-of-ppm level, comparable to real atmospheric signals [4].

### 4.4 Biosignature Statistics: From Evidence to "Detection"

The path from spectrum to "biosignature detected" has four stages, each lossy:

1. **Feature detection.** Is there statistically significant deviation from a flat line? (Bayes factor of best model vs flat [6].)
2. **Attribution.** Which molecule's opacity explains the deviation? Requires exhausting plausible alternatives — the step most claims skip [1].
3. **Abundance inference.** Posterior on $X_i$; usually broad, spanning orders of magnitude.
4. **Biological interpretation.** Is the inferred abundance inconsistent with all known abiotic production? Requires photochemical modeling of false positives.

> **Theorem:** *Bayes factors grade models, not reality.* A decisive Bayes factor for model $\mathcal{M}_X$ over $\mathcal{M}_0$ certifies only that $\mathcal{M}_X$ fits better *among the models considered*. It assigns no probability to the proposition "the true atmosphere contains X" unless the model space is exhaustive — which, in practice, it never is.

The K2-18 b saga is the canonical worked example. Madhusudhan and collaborators reported dimethyl sulfide (DMS) and dimethyl disulfide (DMDS) in JWST/MIRI transmission data as candidate biosignatures; subsequent analysis showed the same spectrum is fit comparably by photochemically plausible hydrocarbons such as propyne, that flexible retrievals admit a wide range of compositions, and that equilibrium models constrain essentially nothing [1]. The significance evaporated not because the data changed but because the *model space* was enlarged.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### Case Study A: K2-18 b — the contested biosignature

K2-18 b is a temperate sub-Neptune (a "Hycean" candidate: hydrogen envelope over a water ocean) transiting an M dwarf. JWST/NIRISS + NIRSpec transmission spectra show absorption near 1–5 $\mu$m attributed by Madhusudhan et al. to DMS/DMDS at $\sim 3\sigma$. The critique is statistical, not instrumental [1]:

- Physically motivated equilibrium models do not meaningfully constrain the atmosphere.
- Flexible free retrievals fit the data with many chemically distinct compositions.
- Propyne (C$_3$H$_4$), photochemically produced in H$_2$-rich atmospheres and observed in Solar System giants, yields equal or greater Bayesian preference than DMS/DMDS.
- Retrieved parameters largely reflect their priors; the data are too weak to overcome them.

The conclusion is methodological: *reported detection significances are meaningless without acknowledgment of their relativity to the chosen model space* [1][4].

### Case Study B: GJ 1132 b — the vanishing atmosphere

A panchromatic 0.71–5.16 $\mu$m spectrum of the rocky planet GJ 1132 b, combining Magellan, HST/WFC3, and JWST/NIRSpec, was subjected to retrievals with petitRADTRANS and PyMultiNest over seven molecules, cloudy and clear models, and flat-line nulls [2]. Result: no model beats the flat line; a thin N$_2$-dominated atmosphere with trace NH$_3$ is indistinguishable from bare rock; the observations are best explained by no atmosphere at all ($R_p = 1.129 \pm 0.002\,R_\oplus$). This is retrieval functioning correctly — the evidence framework *rejecting* every atmosphere hypothesis, including the investigators' hopes [2].

### Case Study C: inter-code reproducibility

A benchmark of NEMESIS, TauREx, and CHIMERA on identical synthetic transmission spectra found agreement only after opacity inputs were harmonized; opacity choices — not samplers — dominate inter-code discrepancies [5]. Gaussian-process noise models, layered onto CHIMERA and tested on WASP-96 b JWST data, shift posteriors enough to matter, formalizing the bias–variance tradeoff: flexible noise models reduce bias from unmodeled systematics at the cost of wider posteriors [7].

```tla
--------------------------- MODULE RetrievalLikelihood ---------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals, Sequences
CONSTANTS N, Theta
VARIABLES data, model
LogLike(theta) ==
    LET s2 == [i \in 1..N |-> sigma[i]^2 + sigmaExtra^2]
    IN -0.5 * Sum(i \in 1..N,
            ((data[i] - Model(theta)[i])^2) / s2[i] + Log(2 * 3.14159 * s2[i]))
THEOREM LogLikeSound == \A theta \in Theta : LogLike(theta) \in Real
=============================================================================
```

### A note on "proofs"

Retrieval admits no deductive proof of composition; its guarantees are *statistical*. The rigorous statements available are: (i) consistency — with infinite S/N and a correct model family, posteriors concentrate on the truth; (ii) calibration of the evidence under the assumed noise model; and (iii) the impossibility results of Section 4.4. Everything else is engineering judgment about which physical processes belong in $\mathcal{M}$ [4][8].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Model-space incompleteness.** Every retrieval conditions on an assumed species list, cloud parameterization, and $T$–$p$ form. The true atmosphere may lie outside all of them; the evidence cannot warn you [1].
2. **One-dimensional terminators.** Real terminators have morning/evening asymmetry, 3D circulation, and patchy clouds. POSEIDON's multidimensional mode begins to address this, but 1D retrievals remain the norm and can bias abundances by factors of several [3].
3. **Stellar contamination.** The transit light source effect is the leading systematic for rocky planets around M dwarfs and is itself retrieved with strong priors that may be wrong [4][6].
4. **Opacity errors.** Incomplete line lists (notably for hydrocarbons and sulfur species at high temperature), uncertain broadening, and arbitrary wing cutoffs propagate directly into abundances [5].
5. **Computational cost.** $10^5$–$10^6$ forward models per retrieval at $\sim 0.1$–$1$ s each limits resolution, species count, and the number of model comparisons feasible; GPU and emulator approaches are active research [7][8].
6. **Prior sensitivity of "detections".** As shown, $\ln Z$ shifts of order unity — from prior bounds or species lists — move claims across the "strong evidence" threshold, making $3\sigma$ biosignature announcements fragile [1][4].

---

## 7. Conclusion

Atmospheric retrieval is the most honest analytical framework exoplanet science possesses: it reports not what the atmosphere *is* but the distribution over what it *could be*, given data, physics, and assumptions made explicit as priors. Its Bayesian evidence machinery gives model comparison a rigorous foundation, and the JWST era has shown it working both ways — retrieving CO$_2$ and SO$_2$ in hot Jupiters with confidence, and correctly refusing to certify atmospheres or biosignatures where the data are insufficient, as in GJ 1132 b [2].

But honesty cuts both ways. The K2-18 b controversy demonstrates that a decisive Bayes factor is a statement about a *model comparison*, not about a planet, and that biosignature claims built on narrow model spaces are statistical artifacts waiting to be dissolved by the next plausible molecule [1]. The field's path forward is clear: exhaustive species lists including photochemical products, explicit stellar-contamination modeling, opacity databases with quantified uncertainties, and — above all — a culture in which "the data do not constrain this" is a publishable result. Retrieval cannot manufacture information; at its best, it tells us exactly how much we do not know, and that is precisely the knowledge on which the search for life must be built.
