# Electrochemical CO2 Reduction on Copper: Scaling Relations, Microkinetics, and the Selectivity Challenge for Multi-Carbon Products

## Abstract

Among all elemental electrocatalysts, copper is the *unique* metal capable of reducing carbon dioxide beyond carbon monoxide to multi-carbon hydrocarbons and oxygenates at appreciable rates. This thesis examines the mechanistic and thermodynamic origins of copper's singularity — the binding of key intermediates such as *CO, *CHO, and *COCHO within narrow windows of adsorption energy — and the linear scaling relations that simultaneously explain and constrain its selectivity toward C2+ products like ethylene and ethanol. Drawing on density functional theory frameworks from the computational hydrogen electrode model to ab initio molecular dynamics with explicit solvation, I trace the reaction network from CO2 activation through C–C coupling, analyze microkinetic models mapping elementary-step energetics onto measurable faradaic efficiencies, and confront theory with the empirical product distributions mapped by Hori's foundational experiments and Kuhl's high-sensitivity analysis. I show that facet-dependent energetics — Cu(100) favoring C–C coupling versus Cu(111) favoring hydrogenation to methane — together with triple-phase-boundary engineering in gas diffusion electrodes, constitute the principal levers for breaking scaling-imposed trade-offs, and close with techno-economic constraints defining the performance targets any C2+-selective Cu system must meet.

##1 Intro

The electrochemical reduction of carbon dioxide (CO2RR) on copper sits at the intersection of two grand challenges: *closing the anthropogenic carbon cycle* and *storing intermittent renewable electricity in chemical bonds*. Whereas most metals halt at two-electron products — carbon monoxide on Au, Ag, and Zn, or formate on Pb, In, Sn, and Bi — copper alone drives the deep reduction cascade toward methane, ethylene, ethanol, and n-propanol, involving up to 12–18 proton–electron transfers [1][2].

Hori and coworkers at Chiba University first mapped this uniqueness in the 1980s–1990s, showing that Cu electrodes in aqueous media produce C2H4, CH4, and alcohols with meaningful faradaic efficiency [3]. Product detection has since revealed *sixteen* distinct CO2 reduction products on metallic copper [2], atomistic simulations now resolve free-energy landscapes at the level of individual adsorbates [4][5], and gas diffusion electrodes have pushed C2+ partial current densities beyond 1 A cm−2 [6]. Yet the central puzzle has not changed: **why copper, and how can its selectivity be steered toward a single multi-carbon product?**

This thesis develops the answer in three movements: the thermochemistry of CO2RR on Cu (computational hydrogen electrode formalism and the volcano plot on which copper sits near the peak for *CO binding); the kinetics (microkinetic models connecting elementary-step barriers to potential-dependent faradaic efficiencies); and the selectivity levers (facet control, oxide-derived morphologies, electrolyte engineering, and GDE architectures), each assessed against techno-economic targets [7][8].

##2 Background

### Thermodynamic scaffolding

The reduction of CO2 to any product can be written as a sequence of proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) steps. Under the computational hydrogen electrode model, the free energy of a (H+ + e−) pair at potential *U* (vs. RHE) is referenced to one-half H2 at standard conditions, so the free energy of each elementary step shifts linearly with applied bias [4]:

> **Theorem:** In the CHE model, the reaction free energy of a PCET step consuming one proton–electron pair is ΔG(U) = ΔG(0) − eU, where ΔG(0) is evaluated at zero bias. The *limiting potential* UL is the least negative potential at which every step in the minimum-energy pathway is exergonic; the theoretical overpotential is then U_eq − UL.

The equilibrium potentials for the major reductions are strikingly close: CO2 → CO at −0.10 V, CO2 → CH4 at +0.17 V, and 2CO2 → C2H4 at +0.07 V (all vs. RHE, standard conditions). Yet appreciable C2H4 formation on Cu requires roughly −0.9 to −1.1 V [1][2][3]. This gap is the *overpotential problem*: the early steps of CO2 activation and *CO formation are endergonic at modest bias, while C–C coupling steps carry intrinsic barriers invisible to equilibrium thermodynamics.

### The adsorbate ladder

The consensus reaction network for CO2-to-C2+ conversion on Cu, consolidated in the comprehensive 2019 review by Nitopi and coworkers [1], proceeds through CO as the pivotal intermediate:

1. CO2 activation: linear CO2 → bent *CO2 (chemisorbed) — the highest-barrier entry step, since the homogeneous one-electron reduction to the CO2 radical anion requires nearly −1.9 V vs. NHE.
2. Carboxyl formation: *CO2 + (H+ + e−) → *COOH.
3. CO formation: *COOH + (H+ + e−) → *CO + H2O.
4. Branch point: *CO is either desorbed (→ CO), hydrogenated toward *CHO/*COH (→ CH4 pathway), or dimerized toward C–C coupled intermediates (→ C2+ pathways).

Operando Raman spectroscopy confirms that *CO is a genuine intermediate en route to both methane and ethylene [5]. The formate branch proceeds through a distinct *OCHO intermediate bound through oxygen, which explains why formate faradaic efficiency does not track CO formation across potential [5].

### Copper's position in the periodic landscape

Metals group by their *CO binding behavior. Weak binders (Au, Ag, Zn) release CO as the terminal product; over-binders (Pt, Pd, Ni, Fe) are poisoned by it, and hydrogen evolution dominates; formate-selective metals (Sn, Pb, In, Bi) bind through oxygen via *OCHO instead. Copper's *CO adsorption energy sits in the *intermediate* window — strong enough to retain *CO for further reduction and coupling, weak enough to avoid complete poisoning [1][4]. On a volcano plot of activity versus *CO binding energy, copper lies near the summit, on the strong-binding side: the origin of its uniqueness.

##3 Methodology

The analyses in this thesis rest on three complementary methodological pillars: first-principles thermodynamics, atomistic kinetics, and microkinetic modeling.

### Density functional theory and the CHE model

The CHE framework treats each proton–electron transfer as equilibrated with the H2/H+ redox couple, allowing reaction-intermediate free energies to be computed from total-energy DFT calculations plus zero-point and entropic corrections [4]. A typical setup uses a periodic slab of Cu(111), Cu(100), or Cu(211), four or more atomic layers, a vacuum gap exceeding 12 Å, plane-wave cutoffs near 400–500 eV, and a dispersion correction (BEEF-vdW or PBE-D3) to capture van der Waals interactions for weakly bound species like *CO2.

Reaction free energies ΔG(0) are assembled from electronic energies via

```python
def reaction_free_energy(dE, dzpe, T, dS, dG_solv=0.0):
    """Assemble free energy of a surface elementary step (CHE formalism)."""
    import math
    return dE + dzpe - T * dS + dG_solv

def limiting_potential(free_energies):
    """UL = most uphill step; theoretical overpotential follows."""
    return -max(free_energies)  # in eV per electron
```

Because every PCET step shifts by −eU, the full potential-dependent free-energy diagram is reconstructed from a single set of zero-bias calculations — thermochemistry only: it identifies the potential-determining step but says nothing about rates.

### Ab initio kinetics

Kinetic barriers come from climbing-image NEB or from AIMD free-energy methods (metadynamics, blue-moon sampling) with explicit water at 298 K. The landmark work of Cheng, Xiao, and Goddard [5] used AIMD free energies on Cu(100) to obtain the full atomistic mechanism for CO reduction, showing that C–C coupling via *CO dimerization is kinetically accessible at room temperature on the (100) facet but not on (111). Explicit water stabilizes polar intermediates (*COOH, *CHO) by several tenths of an eV relative to implicit-solvent treatments, and local cation concentration (K+ > Na+ > Li+ in C2+ promotion [3]) modulates the interfacial field, stabilizing the *CO2−/​*CO–CO− dimer dipole.

### Microkinetic modeling

Microkinetic models translate a network of elementary steps — rate constants from DFT free energies via transition state theory — into steady-state coverages and turnover frequencies versus potential [6]. A minimal CO2RR network includes H2O dissociation and *H adsorption (HER branch), CO2 adsorption and *COOH formation, *CO desorption versus hydrogenation versus dimerization, and downstream hydrogenation to C1 and C2 products. The model of Lu, Shinagawa, and Takanabe [6] applied this framework to CO reduction at high-flux gas-diffusion electrodes, showing that the C2+ product distribution is set by the competition between *CO coverage (favored by high local CO flux) and the protonation rate.

##4 Deep Dive

###4.1 Scaling relations: the double-edged descriptor

The most influential theoretical insight into Cu-catalyzed CO2RR is the existence of **linear scaling relations** between the binding energies of carbon-bound intermediates. Because *CO, *CHO, *COH, and *CH2O all anchor through carbon with similar valence-saturation character, their adsorption energies move together when the surface electronic structure changes [4]:

> **Theorem:** For intermediates binding through the same atom with conserved valency, E(*X) ≈ m·E(*CO) + b with slope m near unity. Weakening *CO binding necessarily weakens *CHO binding by a comparable amount, *preserving* the overpotential for the *CO → *CHO step that limits methane formation.

Escaping the scaling prison requires breaking the relation: binding two intermediates at *different* sites, or via different atoms, so their energies decouple. Alloying motifs (p-block elements in M@Cu that bind *CO and *CHO in opposite directions [5]), bimetallic interfaces, and single-site dopants [6] attempt to break the coupling; tandem architectures sidestep it by spatially segregating the CO-forming and CO-consuming functions.

###4.2 Facet dependence: why Cu(100) makes ethylene and Cu(111) makes methane

Single-crystal studies establish a stark facet dependence: Cu(100) favors C2H4, while Cu(111) favors CH4 [1]. Calle-Vallejo and Koper [9] showed that on Cu(100), the square atomic arrangement positions two neighboring *CO adsorbates at an optimal distance for coupling, with the transition state stabilized by the four-fold hollow geometry; the *CO–*CO dimerization barrier drops significantly at CO2RR potentials while the *CO → *CHO hydrogenation barrier stays relatively higher. On Cu(111), the close-packed surface offers no such stabilization: the dimerization barrier stays high and the lowest-energy route is sequential hydrogenation to CH4 [5][9].

| Facet | Dominant C2+ product | Preferred C1 product | Key coupling geometry |
|-------|---------------------|----------------------|-----------------------|
| Cu(100) | Ethylene (C2H4) | Ethanol (minor) | Square lattice; *CO dimer stabilized in 4-fold hollow |
| Cu(111) | Acetate (trace) | Methane (CH4) | Hexagonal packing; high dimerization barrier |
| Cu(110) | Ethylene/ethanol (mixed) | Methane | Row structure; intermediate coupling energetics |
| Cu(211) steps | CO → mixed | Hydrogen favored at defects | Low coordination; enhanced *H coverage |

*Nanostructuring* Cu to expose (100) facets — copper nanocubes — therefore enhances C2+ selectivity, while oxidation-reduction cycles that expose high-index and step sites alter the ethylene-to-methane ratio [1][10]:

###4.3 Oxide-derived copper and defect engineering

Li and Kanan showed that oxide-derived Cu reduces CO2 to CO with faradaic efficiency near 40–50% at modest overpotentials while HER is strongly suppressed, and subsequent work extended the concept: defect-rich, grain-boundary-dense Cu surfaces exhibit enhanced *CO binding and lowered *CO–*CO coupling barriers [1][10]. DFT studies of Cu2O−Cu0 interfacial models show that positively charged Cu atoms at oxide–metal boundaries facilitate CO2 adsorption electrostatically, while water adsorption is strengthened through hydrogen bonding to surface oxygen, *without* commensurately accelerating HER [5][10]. Whether subsurface oxygen persists under strongly reducing conditions is contested, but the morphological imprint — dense grain boundaries and undercoordinated sites — endures and governs selectivity.

###4.4 Mass transport and the triple-phase boundary

On planar Cu in aqueous H-cells, CO2RR is throttled by low CO2 solubility — roughly 33 mM at 1 atm — limiting current densities to tens of mA cm−2. Gas diffusion electrodes bypass this limit by delivering CO2 through a hydrophobic gas-diffusion layer to a **triple-phase boundary** where gas-phase CO2, electrolyte (or ionomer), and the solid catalyst meet [6][7]:

```
              CO2 gas
                 |
                 v
   +---------------------------+
   |  gas diffusion layer (PTFE)|
   +---------------------------+
   |  microporous layer        |
   +---------------------------+
   |  catalyst layer (Cu)      | <-- triple-phase boundary: CO2(g) / electrolyte / Cu(s)
   +---------------------------+
   |  membrane / electrolyte   |
   +---------------------------+
```

At the triple-phase boundary, CO2 flux is no longer limited by aqueous solubility but by gas-phase diffusion, enabling partial current densities for C2+ products exceeding 1 A cm−2 in alkaline flow cells [6]. The microkinetic consequences are profound: Lu and coworkers showed that the *local CO concentration* at the Cu surface — governed by upstream CO generation and gas-phase transport — is the master variable controlling C2+ selectivity at high current densities, because C–C coupling rates scale with *CO coverage squared [6]. This is the engineering realization of the tandem concept: spatial management of the CO intermediate, with segregated CO-producing sites (e.g., a ZnO or Ag layer) feeding a downstream Cu layer, breaks the scaling constraints at the *device* level rather than the atomic level [1].

###4.5 Techno-economics: the performance bar for C2+

Jouny, Luc, and Jiao [7] performed the foundational general techno-economic analysis of CO2 electrolyzer systems targeting 100 tons per day of product:

1. **CO and formic acid** are the most economically favorable products under near-term assumptions.
2. **Ethylene and higher alcohols** become profitable only if aggressive targets are met: roughly 90% faradaic efficiency, cell voltages near 2 V, electricity prices near 0.03 USD kWh−1, and current densities above several hundred mA cm−2.
3. **Electricity dominates operating cost** for high-electron products; a 0.01 USD kWh−1 change in electricity price shifts the net present value of an n-propanol plant by about 40 million USD over 20 years [7].
4. Selectivity is capital cost: poor faradaic efficiency inflates the electrolyzer area and the downstream separation train simultaneously.

These targets convert the scientific question — *how do we make Cu selective for ethylene?* — into an engineering specification: >70% FE for C2+ at ≥500 mA cm−2, cell voltage below 3 V, and durability beyond 10,000 hours.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### The Hori foundation

Hori's program in the 1980s–1990s remains the empirical bedrock: screening metals in CO2-saturated bicarbonate electrolytes, he established the fourfold classification — CO-forming metals (Au, Ag, Zn), formate-forming metals (Pb, In, Sn), CO-poisoned metals where HER dominates (Pt, Ni, Fe), and copper alone producing hydrocarbons and alcohols [3]. He further showed that larger alkali cations (Cs+ > Rb+ > K+ > Na+ > Li+) suppress HER and promote C2+ formation, an effect now attributed to the local electric field and interfacial water structuring at the Helmholtz plane [1][3].

### The Kuhl product census

Kuhl, Cave, Abram, and Jaramillo [2] coupled a custom high-sensitivity cell with gas chromatography and NMR to quantify *sixteen* CO2 reduction products on metallic Cu — five observed for the first time — resolving the potential dependence of each product's turnover frequency. Their potential-resolved faradaic efficiency map:

| Potential (V vs. RHE) | Dominant product regime | Key observations |
|------------------------|------------------------|------------------|
| −0.6 to −0.8 | H2, CO, formate | 2-electron products; *COOH formation rate-determining |
| −0.8 to −1.0 | Ethylene onset | C2H4 FE rises steeply; *CO dimerization becomes accessible |
| −1.0 to −1.1 | Peak C2H4 | Ethylene FE maximum (~30–40% on polycrystalline Cu) |
| −1.1 to −1.3 | CH4 rises | Methane FE grows; *CO → *CHO hydrogenation outpaces coupling |
| < −1.3 | H2 re-emergence | Mass-transport limitation of CO2; HER dominates at extreme bias |

This potential ordering — CO and formate first, ethylene peaking at intermediate bias, methane rising at more negative potentials — is reproduced by DFT-derived free-energy diagrams [4][5] and microkinetic models [6]: a *qualitative proof* of the two-pathway picture, with a potential-independent C–C coupling route (via *CO dimerization) competing against the protonation route to methane.

### Atomistic mechanism with kinetics

Cheng, Xiao, and Goddard [5] supplied the first full atomistic mechanism with explicit kinetics for CO reduction on Cu(100) from AIMD free-energy calculations at 298 K, reproducing the experimental ethylene-to-ethanol branching and identifying *OCCO → *OCCOH → *OCCHO as the key cascade, with dimerization as the rate-determining barrier at operating potentials. Xiao, Cheng, and Goddard separately treated Cu(111), showing the higher dimerization barrier that redirects flux to methane [5]. Together with Calle-Vallejo and Koper's analysis [9], these studies form a coherent picture: **geometry controls the C–C coupling barrier; thermodynamics controls the hydrogenation branch; potential tunes which wins.**

### Tandem and GDE demonstrations

A Cu–ZnO layered electrode, in which ZnO converts CO2 to CO and a downstream Cu layer reduces the enriched CO, achieved 78% faradaic efficiency for C2+ at 466 mA cm−2 in alkaline flow cells [1]. Au/Cu tandem electrodes showed >100-fold rate enhancement for deeper-than-2-electron products at low overpotentials relative to bare Cu, because the Au overlayer saturates the Cu surface with CO — unreachable by direct CO2 reduction under mass-transport limits [1]. These experiments prove that *local CO activity*, not just intrinsic surface energetics, is a first-order selectivity variable.

##6 Limitations

Despite four decades of progress, the field faces hard limitations that the current literature acknowledges frankly [1][8].

**The scaling ceiling persists.** No Cu-based catalyst has decisively escaped the *CO/*CHO scaling relation to achieve >80% faradaic efficiency to a single C2+ product at low overpotential; alloying breaks it only partially and designed sites segregate or reconstruct within hours under cathodic bias.

**Reconstruction under operando conditions.** Copper surfaces restructure dynamically at CO2RR potentials: (100) facets roughen, nanocubes round, and oxide-derived morphologies coarsen [1][10]. The catalyst tested is therefore not the catalyst as synthesized — complicating every structure–selectivity correlation drawn from ex situ characterization.

**The carbonate problem.** In alkaline GDEs that achieve the highest C2+ current densities, CO2 reacts with hydroxide to form carbonate, which migrates to the anode and re-emits CO2 — a parasitic carbon loss that can exceed 50% of the feedstock, devastating carbon efficiency and the techno-economic case [7][8].

**Theory gaps.** The CHE model assumes equilibrium for every PCET step and ignores activation barriers, potential-dependent double-layer effects, and dynamic solvation — precisely the physics distinguishing *CO dimerization (a non-PCET step) from hydrogenation. Grand-canonical DFT with constant potential is improving but remains expensive and sensitive to the solvation model. Microkinetic models [6] are only as good as their elementary-step list; missing intermediates can invert selectivity predictions.

**Economic reality.** Jouny et al.'s targets [7] and de Luna et al.'s analysis [8] agree: renewable electricity below 0.03 USD kWh−1, multi-kA m−2 current densities, multi-thousand-hour durability, and >70% C2+ faradaic efficiency must coincide. Today's best demonstrations satisfy at most two or three simultaneously.

##7 Conclusion

Copper's singular ability to convert CO2 to multi-carbon products rests on one quantifiable fact: its *CO binding energy lies in the narrow window that retains the intermediate for further reduction without poisoning the surface [4]. Linear scaling relations between carbon-bound intermediates [4] explain why this position is a peak rather than a starting point, and facet-dependent geometry explains the branching: square Cu(100) sites stabilize *CO dimerization and yield ethylene, while close-packed Cu(111) favors sequential hydrogenation to methane [5][9].

The path beyond scaling is clear in principle. Atomic-scale strategies — alloying motifs that decouple *CO and *CHO binding, single-site dopants, grain-boundary engineering — chip at the thermodynamic ceiling. Device-scale strategies — triple-phase-boundary GDEs, tandem architectures saturating Cu with locally generated CO, alkaline flow operation — reshape the mass-transport landscape so that C–C coupling wins the kinetic race [1][6]. The empirical record, from Hori's product survey [3] through Kuhl's sixteen-product census [2] to thousand-milliamp tandem demonstrations, is consistent with theory at every scale.

What remains is integration: a Cu-based system delivering >70% C2+ faradaic efficiency, >500 mA cm−2, >10,000-hour stability, and negligible carbonate loss — the specification written by techno-economic analysis [7][8]. Achieving it requires the convergence of catalyst, electrolyte, and reactor design. The reaction network is mapped, the scaling laws are known, and the target is specified. The challenge is execution.
