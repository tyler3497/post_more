# Entropic Regularization of the Monge–Kantorovich Problem: Sinkhorn Distances, Wasserstein Geometry, and Schrödinger Bridges

## Abstract

Optimal transport has evolved from Gaspard Monge's 1781 theory of earth-moving into the geometric language for comparing probability measures. Kantorovich's 1942 relaxation as a linear program over couplings unlocked a duality theory, a Riemannian geometry of the Wasserstein space, and deep connections to PDEs and gradient flows. Yet classical optimal transport remained computationally prohibitive: discrete formulations scale cubically, and empirical estimates suffer the curse of dimensionality. This thesis develops the entropic regularization program initiated by Cuturi in 2013, which perturbs the Kantorovich objective with relative entropy against the product measure, converting an intractable linear program into a strictly convex problem solvable by Sinkhorn's matrix-scaling iteration in quadratic time. We expose the dual structure of the regularized problem, its exact equivalence with the static Schrödinger bridge, the debiased Sinkhorn divergence interpolating between Wasserstein distance and maximum mean discrepancy, and the resulting statistical dividend: entropic estimates escape the dimensional curse and converge at parametric rates. We situate these ideas within Wasserstein geometry — displacement interpolation, the Otto metric, gradient flows, Wasserstein barycenters — and close with the method's limitations and open frontiers.

##1 Intro

In 1781, Gaspard Monge asked a deceptively concrete question: given a pile of earth and a prescribed excavation profile, what is the least-cost way of redistributing the mass [11]? Monge modeled each infinitesimal particle as traveling along a deterministic path, seeking a *transport map* T minimizing the total displacement cost. For more than 150 years this formulation resisted general analysis: Monge's problem is non-convex, the optimal map need not exist, and mass splitting — a pile splitting one grain into two destinations — has no representation in the map-based language at all.

The decisive conceptual move came in 1942, when Leonid Kantorovich relaxed the problem [12]: instead of a deterministic map, one seeks a *coupling* π, a joint distribution on the product space whose marginals are the source and target measures. This convexification made optimal transport a linear program in infinite dimensions, immediately yielding a dual formulation of breathtaking elegance, and eventually a rich interplay with partial differential equations, geometry, and probability [1]. The cost C(μ, ν) = inf over couplings of expected cost defines the *Wasserstein distances*, which metrizize weak convergence and endow the space of probability measures with what Otto formalized as a Riemannian structure — the geometry in which the heat equation is literally gradient descent of the entropy functional [8].

For all its mathematical triumphs, classical optimal transport languished computationally. The discrete Kantorovich problem between two histograms of size n is a network-flow linear program solvable in roughly cubic time; beyond a few hundred points it is hopeless. Statistically, the situation is equally grim: the empirical Wasserstein distance converges at the rate n raised to minus one over d, where d is the ambient dimension — the curse of dimensionality in pure form.

The wall fell in 2013. Marco Cuturi proposed regularizing the Kantorovich linear program with an *entropic* penalty — a maximum-entropy perspective with roots in Wilson's 1969 traffic models [2] — and showed that the regularized optimum is computable through Sinkhorn–Knopp matrix scaling [6] at speeds orders of magnitude beyond classical solvers, while even improving performance on the MNIST benchmark. What began as a computational hack matured within a decade into a coherent mathematical theory: the regularized problem is the *static Schrödinger bridge*; its optimum admits an explicit dual; a simple *debiasing* correction restores positivity and metrization of weak convergence; and — most strikingly — the regularization that was introduced for speed also cures the statistical curse, yielding parametric sample complexity with constants depending gracefully on the regularization strength [4].

This thesis presents that theory end to end. Section 2 recalls the Monge–Kantorovich formulation, duality, Brenier's theorem, and the Wasserstein geometry. Section 3 develops the entropic methodology: the regularized primal, its dual, the Sinkhorn iteration, and log-domain stabilization. Section 4 deepens the analysis across four axes — algorithmic convergence theory, the Schrödinger bridge interpretation, the statistical theory of Sinkhorn divergences, and the embedding of entropic ideas in gradient flows and barycenters. Section 5 sketches the key proofs and reports the empirical picture. Section 6 confronts the limitations: entropic bias, the choice of regularization, and the frontiers that remain.

##2 Background

### The Monge problem and Kantorovich's relaxation

Let μ and ν be probability measures on Polish spaces X and Y, and let c(x, y) be a lower-semicontinuous cost. Monge's problem seeks a Borel map T with T♯μ = ν (pushforward) minimizing ∫ c(x, T(x)) dμ(x). The constraint is non-convex and the infimum need not be attained. Kantorovich replaced maps by *couplings*: the set Π(μ, ν) of joint probabilities with the prescribed marginals. The Kantorovich problem,

min over π in Π(μ, ν) of ∫ c dπ,

is a linear program over a weakly compact convex set; a minimizer always exists under mild conditions [1]. Every Monge map induces a coupling (Id, T)♯μ, so Kantorovich's value is a lower bound — and the gap can be strict precisely when splitting is optimal.

### Duality

The dual program optimizes over pairs of potentials:

> **Theorem:** *(Kantorovich duality [1]).* For μ, ν on complete separable metric spaces and c nonnegative measurable,
> inf over π in Π(μ, ν) of ∫ c dπ = sup over (φ, ψ) with φ(x) + ψ(y) ≤ c(x,y) of ∫ φ dμ + ∫ ψ dν.

Weak duality is a one-line inequality; strong duality is the deep fact. For the quadratic cost c(x,y) = ‖x − y‖²/2 on Euclidean space, the dual collapses to a single *c-concave* potential, and its gradient recovers the optimal map:

> **Theorem:** *(Brenier [10]).* If μ is absolutely continuous with finite second moment, the optimal coupling is unique and concentrated on the graph of T = ∇Φ for a convex potential Φ; equivalently T(x) = x − ∇φ(x) with φ the Kantorovich potential.

Brenier's theorem is the bridge between the map and plan languages: under absolute continuity, no splitting occurs, and the optimal transport is the gradient of a convex function — a monotone rearrangement.

### The Wasserstein space and its geometry

For p ≥ 1, the *p-Wasserstein distance* is Wp(μ, ν) = (inf over π in Π(μ, ν) ∫ d(x,y)^p dπ)^(1/p). On P₂(R^d), the space of measures with finite second moment, W₂ metrizes weak convergence (plus moment convergence) and admits constant-speed geodesics: *displacement interpolation* ρ_t = ((1−t)Id + tT)♯μ along the Brenier map. Otto's formal calculus promotes this picture to a Riemannian geometry: the tangent space at ρ consists of gradient vector fields, the metric tensor is the L²(ρ) norm, and gradient flows of functionals F(ρ) follow the continuity equation ∂_t ρ + ∇·(ρ∇δF/δρ) = 0. In this geometry the heat equation is the Wasserstein gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy — a dictionary between PDE and geometry developed rigorously by Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré [8].

The minimizing-movement (JKO) scheme discretizes these flows: ρ_{k+1} = argmin_ρ F(ρ) + W₂²(ρ, ρ_k)/(2τ). Wasserstein *barycenters* generalize Fréchet means: the barycenter of measures μ_i with weights λ_i minimizes Σ λ_i W₂²(ν, μ_i), characterized by Agueh and Carlier [7] via multimarginal duality, with the Gaussian case solvable in closed form.

The classical theory is thus magnificent and — in discrete form — unusable at scale. Entropic regularization is the technology that carried it into practice.

##3 Methodology

### The entropic Kantorovich problem

Fix a regularization strength ε > 0 and reference product measure μ ⊗ ν. The *entropically regularized* transport cost is

OT_ε(μ, ν) = min over π in Π(μ, ν) of ∫ c dπ + ε H(π | μ ⊗ ν),

where H(π | μ ⊗ ν) = ∫ log(dπ/d(μ⊗ν)) dπ is relative entropy. For discrete measures with marginals r, c in the simplex and cost matrix C, this is

min over P with P1 = r, P^T 1 = c of ⟨P, C⟩ − (1/η) h(P),

with h the Shannon entropy. The entropy term makes the objective *strictly convex*, so the minimizer is unique, and — crucially — it has an explicit exponential form [2]:

> **Theorem:** *(Structure of the entropic optimizer).* The optimal coupling satisfies P^ε = diag(u) K diag(v), where K = exp(−ηC) entrywise and u, v are positive scaling vectors determined by the marginal constraints.

The problem therefore reduces to *matrix scaling*: find diagonal matrices making K doubly consistent with the marginals. That is exactly what Sinkhorn's 1967 iteration does [6]: alternately normalize rows to r and columns to c,

u ← r ./ (Kv),   v ← c ./ (K^T u),

each step a matrix–vector product, converging linearly in the Hilbert projective metric (Franklin–Lorenz 1989; Knight 2008). In the dual, the updates are soft-min operations on the potentials:

```python
import numpy as np

def sinkhorn(a, b, C, eps, n_iter=1000):
    """Entropic OT via Sinkhorn iteration in the log domain."""
    n, m = C.shape
    log_K = -C / eps
    f = np.zeros(n)          # dual potentials
    g = np.zeros(m)
    log_a, log_b = np.log(a), np.log(b)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        # soft-min row update: f = eps * (log a - logsumexp((g - C)/eps))
        f = eps * (log_a - _logsumexp((g[None, :] - C) / eps, axis=1))
        g = eps * (log_b - _logsumexp((f[:, None] - C) / eps, axis=0))
    P = np.exp((f[:, None] + g[None, :] - C) / eps) * np.outer(a, b)
    return P, f, g

def _logsumexp(Z, axis):
    zmax = Z.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    return (zmax + np.log(np.exp(Z - zmax).sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True))).ravel()
```

Working in the log domain avoids the underflow that plagues the naive K = exp(−C/ε) formulation at small ε; each iteration costs O(nm) and the method parallelizes across many histogram pairs as matrix–matrix products on GPUs — the "lightspeed" of Cuturi's title [2].

### Debiasing: the Sinkhorn divergence

Raw OT_ε suffers an *entropic bias*: OT_ε(μ, μ) > 0, so it is not a divergence. Genevay et al. observed that the correction

S_ε(μ, ν) = OT_ε(μ, ν) − ½OT_ε(μ, μ) − ½OT_ε(ν, ν)

restores positivity, convexity in each argument, and metrization of weak convergence [4]. Moreover S_ε interpolates between geometries: as ε → 0 it recovers the Wasserstein distance, while as ε → ∞ it converges to a maximum mean discrepancy with the negative-definite kernel −c. The regularization parameter is thus a *geometric dial* between OT and MMD.

### Unbalanced and structured variants

The framework extends: unbalanced transport relaxes marginal constraints with divergence penalties, low-rank Sinkhorn factorizations scale to millions of points, and Gromov–Wasserstein variants compare measures across incomparable spaces [3].

##4 Deep Dive

### ###4.1 The Sinkhorn Algorithm: Convergence Theory and Numerical Practice

Sinkhorn's iteration is a coordinate ascent on the entropic dual, an unconstrained smooth concave problem. Classical analysis via Birkhoff's contraction theorem shows the iteration contracts the Hilbert projective metric with factor κ(K) < 1, giving *linear* convergence — but the rate degrades as ε → 0, since K becomes nearly sparse. Altschuler, Niles-Weed, and Rigollet provided the first near-linear-time guarantee: with careful parameter choice, Sinkhorn approximates the *unregularized* OT value to accuracy δ in O(n² log n ‖C‖∞³/δ³) operations, and their greedy Greenkhorn variant substantially improves practical constants [5]. Numerically, the log-domain formulation above is essential: at ε small, entries of exp(−C/ε) underflow to zero in double precision, silently destroying the scaling. The stabilized iteration tracks (f, g) directly and only exponentiates differences, a practice now standard in libraries such as POT and GeomLoss.

### ###4.2 Geometry of the Entropic Plan: Schrödinger Bridges

The regularized problem is not merely a computational proxy — it is a classical object of probability under another name. In 1931–32, Erwin Schrödinger asked for the most likely evolution of a particle system between two observed marginals under a reference dynamics; the answer minimizes relative entropy over path measures. The *static* Schrödinger bridge, min over π in Π(μ, ν) of H(π | R) with reference density proportional to exp(−c/ε), is exactly the entropic OT problem. Léonard's survey [9] develops the equivalence of the static and dynamic formulations: the optimal bridge admits an (f, g)-decomposition π̂(dx,dy) = f(x)g(y)R(dx,dy), the continuous analogue of the diag(u)K diag(v) structure, and lifts to path space by mixing Brownian bridges.

### ###4.3 Statistical Consequences: Sample Complexity and Debiasing

The most surprising dividend of entropic regularization is statistical. The empirical Wasserstein distance between n-samples converges at the cursed rate n^(−1/d), but Genevay, Chizat, Bach, Cuturi, and Peyré proved that Sinkhorn divergences escape this fate [4]:

> **Theorem:** *(Sample complexity of Sinkhorn divergences [4]).* The entropic potentials are uniformly bounded in a Sobolev (RKHS) ball independent of the measures; consequently the plug-in estimator of S_ε converges at the parametric rate 1/√n, with constants depending on ε but not on dimension.

The proof reformulates the divergence as a maximization over an RKHS, importing standard empirical-process machinery. The ε-dependence is the price of debiasing the curse: approximation error of OT by OT_ε grows with ε, while statistical error shrinks — a clean bias–variance tradeoff, with the optimal ε decaying slowly in n.

### ###4.4 Wasserstein Gradient Flows, Barycenters, and Entropic Proxies

Entropic ideas permeate the geometric constructions of Section 2. Peyré showed that replacing W₂ by its entropic proxy in the JKO scheme yields a convergent discretization of Wasserstein gradient flows — *entropic gradient flows* — computable by iterated Sinkhorn projections [3]. For barycenters, the multimarginal formulation of Agueh–Carlier [7] becomes a multi-way scaling problem under entropic regularization, solvable by a generalized Sinkhorn iteration. The pattern is uniform: wherever the Wasserstein geometry poses an intractable variational problem, entropy contributes strict convexity, smooth duals, and scaling algorithms.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

**Complexity landscape.** The following table summarizes the computational and statistical costs of comparing two n-samples in R^d, as established across [2], [4], and [5]:

| Method | Time complexity | Sample complexity | Geometry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact OT (network simplex) | O(n³ log n) | n^(−1/d) | Wasserstein |
| Sinkhorn (ε fixed) | O(n²) | 1/√n | Entropic |
| Sinkhorn divergence S_ε | O(n²) | 1/√n | OT ↔ MMD interpolation |
| MMD (characteristic kernel) | O(n²) | 1/√n | Hilbertian |

**Proof sketch — Kantorovich duality.** Weak duality is immediate from φ(x)+ψ(y) ≤ c(x,y) integrated against any coupling. For strong duality, the primal is a convex program over the weakly compact set of couplings; the Fenchel–Rockafellar theorem applied to the pairing between continuous functions and measures yields equality, with the constraint set Φ_c producing the dual supremum [1]. Existence of dual maximizers requires an additional integrability condition on the cost, handled by c-concave envelopes.

**Proof sketch — Sinkhorn structure.** The Lagrangian of the entropic primal with marginal multipliers (f, g) gives first-order conditions with P_{ij} proportional to exp((f_i + g_j − C_{ij})/ε) — the diag(u)K diag(v) form; complementary slackness becomes the scaling equations. Strict convexity of relative entropy on the coupling polytope gives uniqueness [2].

**Empirical validation.** Cuturi's experiments [2] on MNIST digit classification are the canonical benchmark: Sinkhorn distances computed several orders of magnitude faster than exact EMD solvers and — unexpectedly — *outperformed* unregularized OT in nearest-neighbor accuracy, the entropy acting as a beneficial smoother of the transport plan. GPU implementations in the POT toolbox handle n in the tens of thousands, and log-domain stabilization keeps relative errors below 10^(−6) across six decades of ε.

A minimal high-performance kernel in Rust illustrates the log-sum-exp primitive at the heart of stabilized implementations:

```rust
/// Stabilized log-sum-exp reduction over a row of the Gibbs kernel.
pub fn softmin_row(cost_row: &[f64], g: &[f64], eps: f64) -> f64 {
    assert_eq!(cost_row.len(), g.len());
    let mut m = f64::NEG_INFINITY;
    for (&c, &gj) in cost_row.iter().zip(g.iter()) {
        m = m.max((gj - c) / eps);
    }
    let mut s = 0.0;
    for (&c, &gj) in cost_row.iter().zip(g.iter()) {
        s += ((gj - c) / eps - m).exp();
    }
    eps * (m + s.ln())
}
```

And the categorical essence of the construction, in Haskell, as a regularized infimum over the coupling polytope:

```haskell
-- | Entropic optimal transport: strictly convex perturbation of the
-- Kantorovich linear program over couplings.
entropicOT :: Double            -- regularization strength epsilon
           -> (a -> b -> Double) -- cost function c
           -> Measure a -> Measure b
           -> Double
entropicOT eps c mu nu =
  minimum [ expectedCost c pi + eps * relativeEntropy pi (productMeasure mu nu)
          | pi <- couplings mu nu ]
```

##6 Limitations

Entropic regularization is not a free lunch; its limitations are structural and must be stated plainly.

*Entropic bias and blurring.* The regularized plan is *diffuse*: mass that classical OT would move deterministically is smeared across the product space, and the plan never concentrates on a graph even when a Monge map exists. The debiased divergence S_ε corrects the value but not the plan; applications needing sharp correspondences (e.g., registration, matching) must anneal ε → 0, re-entering the numerically unstable regime.

*Choosing ε.* The parameter governs a three-way tradeoff among approximation error (bias vs. unregularized OT, growing in ε), statistical error (shrinking in ε), and conditioning of Sinkhorn (degrading as ε → 0). Adaptive schedules exist but no universal selector; in practice ε is tuned heuristically against the cost scale, typically as a fraction of the median of C.

*Quadratic memory.* The kernel K is n×m dense: exact Sinkhorn needs O(n²) memory, prohibitive beyond n ≈ 10^5 without low-rank, Nyström, or multiscale approximations. Sparse and sliced variants recover tractability at the price of weaker guarantees.

*Geometric distortion.* For ε > 0, OT_ε is not a metric and its geodesics are not Wasserstein geodesics; entropic interpolation diffuses rather than displaces. Quantitative stability of geometric constructions (barycenters, gradient flows) under entropic approximation is an active area with incomplete answers, particularly for the sharp rates as ε → 0 jointly with discretization.

*Constants in high dimension.* The parametric 1/√n rate of [4] carries constants exponential in 1/ε and polynomial in d in the worst case; the theory guarantees escape from the curse asymptotically, but finite-sample performance in genuinely high-dimensional settings still demands careful ε calibration and remains empirically sensitive.

##7 Conclusion

Entropic regularization transformed optimal transport from a beautiful but intractable geometry into a computational workhorse, and in doing so revealed that the regularization was never merely a numerical trick. The entropic Kantorovich problem *is* the static Schrödinger bridge; its dual potentials are the natural coordinates of a smooth, strictly convex landscape; its debiased form interpolates between Wasserstein and MMD geometries; and its statistical behavior defeats the curse of dimensionality that afflicts unregularized transport. These are not separate facts but facets of one phenomenon: relative entropy against the product measure is the canonical smoothing of the coupling polytope, simultaneously convexifying, stabilizing, and regularizing.

The research frontier now moves in two directions. *Downward*, toward sharper quantitative understanding — ε-annealing schemes with certified complexity, dimension-adaptive regularization, and the fine structure of entropic interpolation as ε → 0. *Outward*, toward the geometric constructions that entropic proxies now make computable at scale: mean-field games, Wasserstein barycenters of massive ensembles, and gradient flows in machine learning, where the Sinkhorn iteration has become as fundamental a primitive as the fast Fourier transform. Monge asked how to move earth; Kantorovich taught us to split it; entropy taught us to compute the answer.
