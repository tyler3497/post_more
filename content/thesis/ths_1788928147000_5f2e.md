# Cache-Oblivious B-Trees and Fractal Tree Indexes: Ideal-Cache Model Bounds, van Emde Boas Layout, Buffered Message Injection, and Write-Amplification Analysis

## Abstract

This thesis develops the theory and systems practice of external-memory search trees that are simultaneously oblivious to hardware cache parameters and asymptotically optimal with respect to them. We begin with the ideal-cache model of Frigo, Leiserson, Prokop, and Ramachandran [1] and prove the regularity lemma that reduces multilevel, LRU-based hierarchies to a single two-level ideal cache. We analyze the van Emde Boas (vEB) memory layout [2][9], showing how recursive square-root decomposition yields an asymptotically optimal static cache-oblivious search structure with $\Theta(\log_B N)$ transfers per query. We survey the dynamic cache-oblivious B-tree of Bender, Demaine, and Farach-Colton [2], whose packed-memory-array machinery matches B-tree search, scan, and amortized update bounds. We then turn to write optimization: the $B^\varepsilon$-tree [5][6] and the fractal tree index deployed in TokuDB [7], where message injection into per-node buffers decouples the update path from the search path. Finally we compare write amplification across B-trees, fractal trees, and log-structured merge trees [8][10], formalize the trade-off through the RUM conjecture [12], and close with empirical measurements and open problems.

## 1. Introduction

Modern processors execute instructions orders of magnitude faster than they can fetch data from main memory, which is itself far faster than persistent storage. The *memory wall* means data-intensive programs are dominated not by arithmetic but by transfers between hierarchy levels. Database kernels and filesystems consequently invest enormous effort in *tuning* block sizes, page layouts, and buffer pools to a particular machine — effort redone on every hardware migration.

Two responses dominate the literature. *External-memory (EM)* algorithms explicitly parametrize cache size $M$ and block size $B$ and tune every decision to them [8]. *Cache-oblivious* algorithms pursue the same asymptotic optimality without ever naming $M$ or $B$ in code: a single program is simultaneously efficient at every level of an arbitrarily deep hierarchy [1]. This thesis studies both in the demanding setting of ordered key-value indexes, where the read/write tension is sharpest.

Section 2 fixes the ideal-cache model and explains why its idealizations cost at most constant factors. Section 3 describes our analytical methodology. Section 4 is the deep dive: the van Emde Boas layout, the cache-oblivious B-tree, the $B^\varepsilon$-tree and fractal-tree message machinery, and write-amplification analysis grounded in the RUM conjecture. Section 5 states theorems with proofs and reports measurements [7][11]. Section 6 collects limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Ideal-Cache Model

The *ideal-cache model* [1] idealizes the CPU/memory interaction as a two-level hierarchy:

- The **cache** holds $M$ words in $M/B$ lines of $B$ consecutive words; **main memory** ("disk") is unbounded.
- The cache is **fully associative** with **optimal (Belady) replacement**.
- Only **memory transfers** — block movements between levels — are counted. Cost is the **cache complexity** $Q(N; M, B)$.

A cache-oblivious algorithm *never references $M$ or $B$ in code* yet is asymptotically optimal *for every* $M$ and $B$ [1]. Under the *regularity condition*, results transfer to multilevel LRU hierarchies: optimizing the two-level model optimizes the whole machine.

> **Theorem (Regularity; Frigo et al. [1]):** If $Q(M, B) = O(Q(M/2, B))$ (regularity), then cache complexity on an LRU cache of size $M$ is at most twice the ideal-cache complexity on a cache of size $M/2$.

The common extra hypothesis is the **tall-cache assumption** $M = \Omega(B^2)$, true in practice and required by several optimal dynamic structures [2]. It marks the dividing line between static results (the vEB layout needs none) and dynamic ones.

### 2.2 Classical Predecessors

The cache-aware tradition began with the disk access model (DAM) of Aggarwal and Vitter [8]: B-trees achieve $\Theta(\log_B N)$ I/Os by *choosing* node size equal to $B$; LSM trees [10] achieve write-optimality by batching and merging sorted runs; the buffered repository tree [5] trades read cost against write cost via per-node buffers. The cache-oblivious program [1][3][4] generalizes all of these, with the vEB layout as the primitive that makes search trees oblivious.

## 3. Methodology

1. **Ideal-cache accounting.** Count block transfers under fully associative, optimal-replacement caching; express bounds in $N, M, B$ without the algorithm using $M$ or $B$.
2. **Recursive decomposition.** Divide-and-conquer shrinks data footprints until subproblems fit in cache; transfer recurrences like $Q(N) = 2Q(\sqrt{N}) + O(1)$ are solved for the crossover scale where a subtree fits in $O(1)$ blocks.
3. **Amortized potentials for buffered updates.** Potential functions charge each injected message $\Theta(1/B^\varepsilon)$ flush cost per level descended, yielding amortized update bounds for $B^\varepsilon$-trees and fractal trees.
4. **Write-amplification (WA) accounting.** WA = device bytes written per user byte, decomposed into (i) read-modify-write WA ($B/r$ for an $r$-byte change in a $B$-byte page), (ii) compaction/merge WA, (iii) space WA ($1/\text{occupancy}$), and (iv) logging WA. We evaluate each structure on uniform random inserts of 128-byte records, sequential inserts, and 50/50 read-write mixes, using published measurements [7][11].
5. **Theorem–proof presentation.** Each bound is stated formally with a proof sketch; full proofs are cited to [1][2][5][6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Ideal-Cache Model and Its Justifications

Each idealization of [1] costs at most a constant factor:

- **Associativity.** For the recursive, contiguous access patterns studied here, conflict misses in set-associative caches are bounded by a constant factor.
- **Replacement.** Regularity reduces LRU (what hardware approximates) to optimal replacement at the price of doubling the effective cache. All our recurrences are monotone in $M$, hence regular.
- **Two levels.** Adjacent levels of a multilevel hierarchy each form an external-memory pair $(M_i, B_i)$ with inclusion. A cache-oblivious algorithm optimal for every $(M, B)$ is simultaneously optimal for every adjacent pair, hence the whole hierarchy.

### 4.2 The van Emde Boas Memory Layout

The van Emde Boas layout [9] is the foundational cache-oblivious primitive for search trees. In naive *heap order* (breadth-first) layout, a root-to-leaf search visits one node per level in unrelated array regions — $\Theta(\log N)$ transfers, no better than pointer chasing. The vEB layout instead:

1. Splits the height-$h$ tree into a **top subtree** of height $\lceil h/2 \rceil$ and $2^{\lceil h/2 \rceil}$ **bottom subtrees** of height $\lfloor h/2 \rfloor$.
2. Lays out the top subtree contiguously, then the bottom subtrees left to right.
3. Recurses within each piece.

Each recursion takes a square root of subtree size, so some recursion level produces subtrees of size between $\sqrt{B}$ and $B$ — each retrievable in $O(1)$ transfers. A root-to-leaf path crosses $\Theta(\log_B N)$ such subtrees, because the recursion squares granularity at each level [9][4]. Hence:

> **Theorem (Prokop [3]; cf. Demaine [4]):** A complete binary search tree of $N$ keys in the van Emde Boas layout supports search in $\Theta(\log_B N)$ memory transfers — asymptotically optimal for comparison-based search — for *all* $B$ simultaneously, with no dependence on $M$.

| Layout | Search transfers | $B$-dependence | Dynamic? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sorted array + binary search | $\Theta(\log(N/B))$ | none (worst) | No |
| Heap-order implicit tree | $\Theta(\log N)$ | none | No |
| **van Emde Boas layout** | $\Theta(\log_B N)$ | oblivious-optimal | No (static) |
| B-tree (cache-aware) | $\Theta(\log_B N)$ | tuned to $B$ | Yes |
| Cache-oblivious B-tree [2] | $O(\log_B N)$ | oblivious | Yes |

The vEB layout is *static*: an insertion can disturb the global recursive partition. Dynamic structures need further machinery.

### 4.3 Cache-Oblivious B-Trees: Dynamicizing the Layout

Bender, Demaine, and Farach-Colton [2] made the vEB layout dynamic with polylogarithmic overhead, yielding the first dynamic cache-oblivious dictionary matching B-tree bounds. Three tools:

1. **Strongly weight-balanced trees**, localizing updates to $O(\log N)$ nodes and preserving the recursive partition structure.
2. **The packed-memory array (PMA)** — "a cache-oblivious linked list": an array of size $\Theta(N)$ with per-segment density thresholds, scanning $S$ consecutive elements in the optimal $O(1 + S/B)$ transfers and updating in $O(1 + (\log^2 N)/B)$ amortized transfers.
3. **Exponential decomposition of the layout**: the tree is split into geometrically sized blocks, each internally vEB-laid-out; updates rebuild only affected blocks.

> **Theorem (Bender–Demaine–Farach-Colton [2]):** A dynamic cache-oblivious ordered dictionary on $N$ keys supports **search** in $O(1 + \log_{B+1} N)$ transfers, **scan** of $S$ keys in $O(1 + S/B)$ transfers (optimal), and **update** in $O(1 + \log_{B+1} N + \tfrac{\log^2 N}{B})$ amortized transfers — matching the B-tree whenever $B = \Omega(\log N \log \log N)$, true in external memory though not always in L1/L2 cache.

Later work simplified the construction, added persistence and optimal constants [4]; CORoBTS [13] generalizes it to arbitrary B-tree-like structures.

### 4.4 Fractal Trees and the $B^\varepsilon$-Tree: Buffered Message Injection

The cache-oblivious B-tree optimizes the *read* path; each insert still descends to a leaf. **Fractal tree indexes** [6][7] — the $B^\varepsilon$-tree family commercialized as TokuDB — optimize the *write* path by buffering updates in internal nodes and flushing them in batches.

**Structure.** A $B^\varepsilon$-tree ($0 < \varepsilon \le 1/2$) is a B-tree variant where each internal node carries a **buffer** of size $\Theta(B)$ alongside $\Theta(B^\varepsilon)$ pivot keys and children [5][6]. Updates are *messages* injected at the root buffer; when a buffer overflows, a batch flushes one level down, routed by pivots. A flush moves $\Theta(B)$ messages for $O(1)$ transfers, so each message pays $O(1/B)$ transfers per level.

**Asymptotics.** With height $\Theta((1/\varepsilon)\log_B N)$:

- **Insert/delete:** $O\!\left(\frac{\log_B N}{\varepsilon B^{1-\varepsilon}}\right)$ amortized transfers — for $\varepsilon = 1/2$, $O((\log_B N)/\sqrt{B})$, asymptotically *faster* than a B-tree by $\sqrt{B}$ [6].
- **Point search:** $\Theta((1/\varepsilon)\log_B N)$ — slower than a B-tree by $1/\varepsilon$, since searches must inspect buffers along the path for pending messages.
- **Range scan:** B-tree-competitive, as messages flush ahead of the scan cursor.

This is the canonical **read–write trade-off curve**: $\varepsilon \to 1$ recovers B-tree-like reads, $\varepsilon \to 0$ approaches write-optimality. Brodal and Fagerberg [5] first exhibited this curve; the $B^\varepsilon$-tree makes it continuous and tunable.

**Message injection as a programming model.** Fractal trees generalize "insert a key" to *inject a message* [7]: inserts, delete tombstones, and **upserts** — update-if-present-else-insert messages carrying a function $f$ (e.g., $x \mapsto x + c$). An upsert needs no prior search: it is appended to the root buffer, eliminating the read-modify-write. Most remarkably, **broadcast messages** replicate to *every* child's buffer and can transform *all* rows — e.g., adding a column. Total work is unchanged, but *latency* collapses: once the broadcast sits in the root buffer, every query observes the new schema, while physical rewriting is deferred to flushes that would have happened anyway [7]. Hence TokuDB's online schema changes — "it's just a message" — with the table fully available.

```python
# Fractal-tree message injection (schematic)
def inject(node, msg):
    node.buffer.append(msg)
    if len(node.buffer) > BUFFER_CAPACITY:   # Theta(B)
        for child, batch in partition(node.buffer, node.pivots):
            inject(child, batch)              # amortized O(1) I/Os
        node.buffer.clear()

def upsert(root, key, delta):
    inject(root, UpsertMessage(key, lambda x: x + delta))  # NO search

def add_column(root, default):
    inject_broadcast(root, SchemaMessage(("add", default)))  # instant schema change
```

### 4.5 Write-Amplification Analysis: B-Trees, LSM Trees, and the RUM Trade-Off

**Write amplification (WA)** — device bytes written per user byte — is the currency of storage-engine trade-offs. On random inserts of small records (the LSM-friendly regime [11]):

- **B-tree.** Each insert rewrites a full leaf page: a 128-byte record in a 16 KiB page is WA $\approx 128$ before splits. Page-granular read-modify-write dominates; in-storage transparent compression narrows but does not close the gap [11].
- **Fractal tree / $B^\varepsilon$-tree.** Batched flushes rewrite a node per $\Theta(B)$ messages: amortized WA per insert is $O((\log_B N)/B^{1-\varepsilon})$ — sub-constant in $B$ for $\varepsilon < 1$. Reads pay the $1/\varepsilon$ search penalty plus buffer-merge work: internal *read amplification*.
- **LSM tree [10].** Memtable batching plus leveled compaction gives insert WA $\approx$ levels $\times$ merge fan-in, measured around 10–30$\times$ on RocksDB [11]; point reads probe many levels (Bloom filters help); range scans merge iterators across levels.

| Structure | Point search | Amortized insert | Write amplif. | Read amplif. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B-tree | $\Theta(\log_B N)$ | $\Theta(\log_B N)$ | High ($B/r$ page RMW) | $\approx 1$ |
| Cache-oblivious B-tree [2] | $O(\log_B N)$ | $O(\log_B N + \tfrac{\log^2 N}{B})$ | High (as B-tree) | $\approx 1$ |
| $B^\varepsilon$-tree / fractal tree [6] | $\Theta(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\log_B N)$ | $O(\tfrac{\log_B N}{\varepsilon B^{1-\varepsilon}})$ | Low (batched flush) | Moderate |
| LSM tree [10] | $O(L)$ levels + filters | $O(L \cdot T)$ compaction | Low ($\sim$10–30$\times$) | High |

**The RUM conjecture.** Athanassoulis et al. [12] conjecture that *read, update, and memory* overheads cannot be optimized simultaneously: an access method can improve at most two of {read cost, update cost, memory overhead} at the expense of the third. B-trees buy reads with update + memory overhead (70%-full nodes, page-granular writes); LSM trees buy updates and memory (dense immutable files) with read overhead; fractal trees slide along the curve via $\varepsilon$. The conjecture reframes "which index is best" as "where on the RUM surface does your workload live" — answered by measurement, not asymptotics alone.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

> **Theorem (Static optimality [3][4]):** The vEB layout achieves $\Theta(\log_B N)$ search transfers for all $B$ simultaneously, matching the comparison-based lower bound. *Proof sketch.* A root-to-leaf path has $\log_2 N$ nodes. Recursive halving yields contiguous segments; at the level where segment size first drops below $B$, each segment costs $O(1)$ transfers, and any root-to-leaf path crosses $\Theta(\log_B N)$ such segments because each layout-recursion level squares the granularity. Optimality follows from the $\Omega(\log_B N)$ decision-tree lower bound: each transfer reveals at most $B$ keys. ∎

> **Theorem (Buffered update bound [5][6]):** In a $B^\varepsilon$-tree, amortized insert cost is $O((\log_B N)/(\varepsilon B^{1-\varepsilon}))$ transfers. *Proof sketch.* Let potential $\Phi$ be buffered messages weighted by remaining depth. Injecting at the root raises $\Phi$ by $O((1/\varepsilon)\log_B N)$. Flushing a full $\Theta(B)$-message buffer one level costs $O(1)$ transfers and drops $\Phi$ proportionally, so each message's amortized descent cost is $O(1/B^{1-\varepsilon})$ per level. Summing over $\Theta((1/\varepsilon)\log_B N)$ levels gives the bound. ∎

**Empirical evidence:**

1. **TokuDB / fractal trees [7].** Tokutek's benchmarks showed fractal-tree indexes sustaining insertion throughput an order of magnitude above B-tree engines (notably InnoDB) on random inserts with multiple secondary indexes, while point-query latency stayed within a small constant of B-trees — the $\varepsilon$-trade-off predicted by theory. Compression, a dividend of large sequential node rewrites, further shrank storage.
2. **B-tree vs. LSM write amplification [11].** On 150 GB and 500 GB datasets with 16–128-byte records, RocksDB's measured WA ($\sim$10–40$\times$) was up to $4\times$ *smaller* than a conventional B-tree's under small-record workloads — but the B-tree won for large records (512 B+) or cache-resident working sets, confirming WA comparisons are workload-dependent and page-granular read-modify-write dominates B-tree WA [11].
3. **Cache-oblivious portability [1][9].** Frigo et al.'s experiments showed recursive (oblivious-style) transpose and multiplication matching or beating hand-tuned cache-aware codes across machines; vEB-laid-out search structures inherit the same portability — no retuning when $B$ changes across L1, L2, TLB, and disk pages.

```python
# Write-amplification micro-model (illustrative)
def write_amplification(page_bytes, record_bytes, splits_per_insert=0.02):
    rmw = page_bytes / record_bytes
    return rmw + splits_per_insert * 2.0

print(write_amplification(16384, 128))  # ~128.04  (B-tree, small records)
print(16384 / (128 * 256))              # ~0.5     (fractal tree, amortized)
```

## 6. Limitations

1. **The tall-cache gap.** The dynamic cache-oblivious B-tree's update bound degrades when $B = o(\log N \log \log N)$, possible in L1/L2 caches [2] — oblivious-optimal at the disk/RAM boundary but potentially suboptimal on-chip, where constants matter most.
2. **Idealizations vs. hardware.** The model abstracts prefetching, TLB misses, NUMA, limited associativity, and write-back policies. vEB layouts can interact poorly with sequential-tuned hardware prefetchers, and adversarial patterns can exceed the promised constant factors.
3. **Tail latency of buffered structures.** Amortized bounds hide flush cascades: one injection can trigger $\Theta(\log_B N)$ cascading flushes. Engines deamortize with background flusher threads, adding concurrency complexity and murky worst-case latency.
4. **Read amplification of messages.** Fractal-tree queries must consult buffers along the search path and reconcile upserts/tombstones — CPU and I/O invisible to transfer counting. For read-heavy, cache-resident workloads, B-trees usually win in wall-clock terms.
5. **Concurrency and recovery.** The analyses are single-threaded and failure-free. Production fractal trees add MVCC, write-ahead logging, and checkpointing [7]; LSM compaction adds write stalls and $\sim$2$\times$ space amplification [11] — none captured by cache complexity.
6. **The RUM conjecture is unproven** [12]. No theorem rules out beating all three axes at once, and learned indexes plus hardware compression keep shifting the empirical Pareto frontier.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Cache-oblivious B-trees and fractal tree indexes jointly answer the two hardest questions in external-memory indexing: *how to be optimal without knowing the hardware*, and *how to make writes cheap without sacrificing reads*. The van Emde Boas layout shows one recursive idea — halve the height, lay out contiguously, recurse — attains the $\Theta(\log_B N)$ search lower bound for every block size at once [3][4]. Bender, Demaine, and Farach-Colton [2] prove this need not cost updates, and the packed-memory array has become a general dynamization tool. The $B^\varepsilon$-tree [5][6] and its TokuDB realization [7] expose the read–write trade-off as a tunable continuum, with message injection — search-free upserts, broadcast schema changes — as the systems idea that made the theory deployable.

Write-amplification analysis reframes the B-tree/LSM debate through the RUM conjecture [12]: there is no universally optimal index, only Pareto-optimal points matched to workloads. Open problems: proving or refuting RUM; closing the tall-cache gap on-chip; deamortized, multicore, NUMA-aware fractal trees with worst-case latency guarantees; cache-oblivious learned indexes combining model-based prediction with vEB locality. The ideal-cache model, a quarter-century after [1], remains our sharpest lens — because it lets the algorithm, not the hardware manual, do the talking.
