# Certified Adversarial Robustness via Randomized Smoothing: Neyman–Pearson Certificates, Lipschitz Bounds, and Deterministic Derandomization

## Abstract
Randomized smoothing is the most scalable paradigm for certifying adversarial robustness, converting arbitrary base classifiers into provably robust smoothed classifiers by convolving predictions with isotropic Gaussian noise. This thesis unifies the theory behind Gaussian smoothing certificates. We derive the tight certified ℓ₂ radius of Cohen, Rosenfeld, and Kolter from the Neyman–Pearson lemma, showing why the likelihood-ratio test yields the extremal worst-case base classifier and how the radius R = σ(Φ⁻¹(p̲_A) − Φ⁻¹(p̄_B))/2 emerges. We connect smoothing to Lipschitz analysis: the inverse-Gaussian-CDF-composed smoothed score Φ⁻¹(ĝ(x)) is 1/σ-Lipschitz, giving an alternative derivation and a bridge to deterministic certificates for Lipschitz-constrained networks. We examine Monte Carlo certification with Clopper–Pearson bounds, training methods — Gaussian augmentation, adversarial training of smoothed classifiers, attack-free radius maximization via MACER and consistency regularization — and benchmarks on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet. Finally, we treat derandomization via quantized deterministic smoothing ensembles, and fundamental limits including the curse of dimensionality for ℓ_p norms with p > 2.

## 1. Introduction

Deep neural networks remain brittle in the presence of adversarial examples — imperceptible perturbations of the input that catastrophically alter predictions [1]. Empirical defenses, from adversarial training to gradient masking, offer robustness only against known attacks; a *certificate* instead provides a mathematical guarantee that no adversarial perturbation within some radius R of an input x can flip the classifier's decision, regardless of the attacker's strategy. For most of deep learning's history, such certificates scaled only to tiny models: exact verification methods based on mixed-integer programming or SMT solving, and conservative relaxation methods (convex outer approximations, Lipschitz bound propagation), either demand prohibitive computation or require specialized architectures, and none had been demonstrated on ImageNet-scale models [5].

Randomized smoothing changed this landscape. Originally proposed under the name PixelDP by Lécuyer et al., who derived loose robustness bounds from differential-privacy inequalities, the technique was recast by Cohen, Rosenfeld, and Kolter as a *tight* ℓ₂ certificate built on the Neyman–Pearson lemma [1]. The idea is disarmingly simple: given any base classifier f : ℝ^d → 𝒴, define the smoothed classifier

g(x) = argmax_{c ∈ 𝒴} ℙ_{ε ∼ 𝒩(0, σ²I)}[f(x + ε) = c].

That is, g returns the class most likely to be predicted when the input is corrupted by isotropic Gaussian noise. The remarkable property is that g's predictions can be certified as constant inside an ℓ₂ ball, simply by estimating the class probabilities under noise via Monte Carlo sampling — with no assumptions whatsoever about f. Subsequent work extended the paradigm along three axes: (i) tighter analyses and broader threat models through Neyman–Pearson-style reasoning over general noise distributions [2], [5]; (ii) Lipschitz-theoretic reinterpretations connecting the certificate to the geometry of smoothed score functions [2]; and (iii) training procedures that maximize certified radii without expensive adversarial attack inner loops [4], [7], alongside deterministic derandomizations that eliminate Monte Carlo randomness entirely [8].

This thesis unifies these strands. Our contributions are expository and analytic:

1. A self-contained derivation of the tight Gaussian certificate from the Neyman–Pearson lemma, with a proof sketch showing tightness via explicit extremal constructions.
2. The Lipschitz formulation: showing that x ↦ Φ⁻¹(ĝ(x)) is 1/σ-Lipschitz, which re-derives the radius formula and connects smoothing certificates to deterministic Lipschitz-constrained architectures.
3. A treatment of Monte Carlo certification mechanics — Clopper–Pearson lower bounds, the truncation (waterfall) effect, and confidence-budget tradeoffs.
4. A comparative account of training methodologies and their empirical certificates on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet.
5. A critical assessment of deterministic derandomization strategies and fundamental limits, including the dimensional curse for ℓ_p with p > 2.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Threat Models and Certified Robustness

Consider a classifier f : 𝒳 → 𝒴 with 𝒳 ⊆ ℝ^d and a fixed input x with ground-truth label y. An adversarial example of radius r in the ℓ_p threat model is an input x + δ such that f(x + δ) ≠ y with ‖δ‖_p ≤ r. We distinguish *empirical* robustness — resistance to a fixed battery of attacks — from *certified* robustness: a per-input certificate asserting that f(x + δ) = f(x) for all ‖δ‖₂ ≤ R(x), where R(x) is a computed radius. Two desiderata dominate: the certificate should be *tight* (close to the largest true robust radius) and *scalable* (computable for networks with tens of millions of parameters).

Early certified defenses — complete verifiers via MILP/SMT [5] and incomplete methods via convex relaxations or interval bound propagation — are deterministic and exact or sound, but scale exponentially or degrade sharply with network size. Randomized smoothing sacrifices determinism for scalability: certificates hold *with high probability* over the Monte Carlo randomness used in certification, at a user-chosen confidence level 1 − α, while scaling to ResNet-50 on ImageNet [1].

### 2.2 Randomized Smoothing Construction

**Definition 1 (Smoothed classifier).** Let f : ℝ^d → 𝒴 be any measurable function and σ > 0. The Gaussian-smoothed classifier g : ℝ^d → 𝒴 is

g(x) = argmax_{c ∈ 𝒴} p_c(x), where p_c(x) ≜ ℙ_{ε ∼ 𝒩(0, σ²I)}[f(x + ε) = c].

Denote c_A = g(x), p_A = p_{c_A}(x), and p_B = max_{c ≠ c_A} p_c(x).

The parameter σ governs the robustness–accuracy tradeoff: larger σ yields larger certified radii but erodes clean accuracy, because the base classifier is evaluated on increasingly noisy inputs [1]. In practice p_A and p_B are unknown; we estimate a lower bound p̲_A ≤ p_A via the Clopper–Pearson confidence interval over n Monte Carlo samples, and typically substitute p̄_B = 1 − p̲_A to avoid a second estimation [1].

### 2.3 The Neyman–Pearson Lemma

The linchpin of the tight analysis is the classical Neyman–Pearson lemma (Neyman & Pearson, 1933), which characterizes the most powerful test for distinguishing two simple hypotheses:

> **Lemma (Neyman–Pearson):** Among all measurable functions h : 𝒳 → [0, 1] with fixed expectation 𝔼_{z ∼ p(·|x)}[h(z)] = p_A, the minimizer of 𝔼_{z ∼ p(·|x_adv)}[h(z)] is the likelihood-ratio test h⋆(z) = 𝟙{γ(z) ≤ t⋆}, where γ(z) = p(z|x_adv)/p(z|x) and t⋆ enforces the constraint.

Cohen et al. [1] apply this by relaxing the discrete classifier to an arbitrary measurable h with the same clean expectation p_A; the worst-case adversary over base classifiers f is bounded by this extremal h⋆. For Gaussian noise, the likelihood ratio is log-linear in the perturbation direction, so h⋆ is a half-space indicator, and both the clean and adversarial expectations admit closed forms in terms of Φ, the standard Gaussian CDF.

### 2.4 Clopper–Pearson Confidence Estimation

Because p_A cannot be computed exactly for deep networks, Cohen et al. use a two-stage Monte Carlo procedure: (i) *selection* with n₀ ≈ 100 samples to identify the most likely class ĉ_A; (ii) *estimation* with n ≈ 100,000 samples to compute a one-sided Clopper–Pearson lower bound p̲_A such that ℙ(p_A ≥ p̲_A) ≥ 1 − α, typically α = 0.001. If p̲_A > 1/2, the prediction is certified with radius R = σΦ⁻¹(p̲_A) (binary form) or the two-class margin form.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Tight Gaussian Certificate

The central result, Theorem 1 of [1], is reproduced here:

> **Theorem (Cohen–Rosenfeld–Kolter):** Let f : ℝ^d → 𝒴 be any deterministic or random function, ε ∼ 𝒩(0, σ²I), and g the smoothed classifier. Suppose c_A ∈ 𝒴 and p̲_A, p̄_B ∈ [0, 1] satisfy ℙ(f(x+ε) = c_A) ≥ p̲_A ≥ p̄_B ≥ max_{c ≠ c_A} ℙ(f(x+ε) = c). Then g(x + δ) = c_A for all ‖δ‖₂ < R, where
>
> R = (σ/2)(Φ⁻¹(p̲_A) − Φ⁻¹(p̄_B)).

*Proof sketch.* Fix the perturbation δ and consider the adversarial shifted distribution 𝒩(x + δ, σ²I). Among all measurable h : ℝ^d → [0,1] with 𝔼_{𝒩(x,σ²I)}[h] = p̲_A, the Neyman–Pearson lemma identifies the minimizer of 𝔼_{𝒩(x+δ,σ²I)}[h] as a half-space S = {z : δᵀ(z − x) ≤ β}, with β chosen so that ℙ_{𝒩(x,σ²I)}(S) = p̲_A. Since z ↦ δᵀ(z − x) is a one-dimensional Gaussian projection with variance σ²‖δ‖₂², we obtain ℙ_{𝒩(x+δ,σ²I)}(S) = Φ(Φ⁻¹(p̲_A) − ‖δ‖₂/σ). An analogous maximization over h with clean expectation p̄_B for any competing class yields an upper bound Φ(Φ⁻¹(p̄_B) + ‖δ‖₂/σ). Whenever the former exceeds the latter — equivalently ‖δ‖₂ < R — the top class under the shifted distribution is still c_A, so g(x+δ) = c_A. ∎

Tightness is immediate: the extremal half-space classifier f⋆ = 𝟙_S attains the bound with equality, so no analysis using only (p̲_A, p̄_B, σ) can certify a larger radius [1].

When only p̲_A > 1/2 is estimated and p̄_B is replaced by 1 − p̲_A, the formula simplifies to the familiar

R = σ·Φ⁻¹(p̲_A),

since Φ⁻¹(1 − p) = −Φ⁻¹(p). This single-parameter form underlies most reported certified radii [1], [4].

### 3.2 The Lipschitz Formulation

An alternative, illuminating derivation proceeds through Lipschitz analysis [2]. Let ĝ_c(x) = 𝔼_{ε ∼ 𝒩(0,σ²I)}[f_c(x + ε)] be the soft smoothed score for class c, where f_c ∈ [0, 1]. Then:

> **Theorem (Lipschitz smoothed scores):** The function x ↦ Φ⁻¹(ĝ_c(x)) is 1/σ-Lipschitz in the ℓ₂ norm.

Consequently, if the margin m(x) = Φ⁻¹(ĝ_{c_A}(x)) − Φ⁻¹(ĝ_{c_B}(x)) exceeds (2R)/σ, the prediction cannot flip within radius R — recovering the certificate R = (σ/2)(Φ⁻¹(p_A) − Φ⁻¹(p_B)). This perspective matters because it reframes the guarantee as a *regularity property* of the smoothed score landscape rather than a combinatorial statement about probability masses. It also establishes the bridge to deterministic certification: any network whose Φ⁻¹-composed scores are Lipschitz with known constant can be certified without Monte Carlo sampling, motivating architectures with enforced Lipschitz constraints (e.g., orthogonal convolutions, spectral normalization).

### 3.3 Monte Carlo Certification Protocol

The practical certification algorithm CERTIFY [1] proceeds as:

1. Draw n₀ = 100 samples of f(x + ε) to select ĉ_A by majority vote.
2. Draw n = 100,000 samples; count k = #{f(x + ε) = ĉ_A}.
3. Compute p̲_A = LowerConfBound(k, n, 1 − α), the Clopper–Pearson lower bound.
4. If p̲_A > 1/2, return prediction ĉ_A with radius R = σΦ⁻¹(p̲_A); else abstain.

The per-example cost is n forward passes — roughly 15 seconds per CIFAR-10 image and 110 seconds per ImageNet image on an RTX 2080 Ti in the original study [1]. The finite-sample bound induces a maximum certifiable radius R_max = σΦ⁻¹(LowerConfBound(n, n, 1 − α)); increasing n raises this ceiling, but only logarithmically slowly in n.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Geometry of the Neyman–Pearson Acceptance Set

The worst-case analysis has a crisp geometric interpretation. In the clean world, noise concentrates on the ball around x; in the adversarial world, on the ball around x + δ. The likelihood ratio for isotropic Gaussians is

γ(z) = exp(−‖z − x − δ‖₂²/(2σ²) + ‖z − x‖₂²/(2σ²)) = exp((δᵀ(z − x))/σ² − ‖δ‖₂²/(2σ²)),

so the acceptance set {γ(z) ≤ t⋆} is the half-space {δᵀ(z − x) ≤ β}. The worst-case base classifier concentrates all of class c_A's probability mass as close to x (and as far from x + δ) as the clean-mass constraint allows — an adversarial *sorting* of the Gaussian cloud along the perturbation direction. Two consequences follow:

- The certificate depends only on the *projection* of the noise onto δ, collapsing the high-dimensional geometry to a one-dimensional Φ calculation — the celebrated dimension-independence of the ℓ₂ certificate [1].
- The extremal classifier is a half-space indicator: a discontinuous, adversarially-placed step function. Real base classifiers are smooth and structured, so the certificate is *conservative* for actual networks; closing this gap — e.g., via certifying confidence scores [6] or exploiting distributional structure beyond (p_A, p_B) — is a major research direction.

### 4.2 Adversarial Training of Smoothed Classifiers

Cohen et al. train the base classifier with Gaussian augmentation, which is intuitively the most natural objective: the smoothed classifier's accuracy is the base classifier's accuracy under noise [1]. Salman et al. observed that one can do better by *adversarially training* the smoothed classifier itself [2]. Since g is defined through an expectation, its adversarial examples are crafted by attacking the empirical smoothed model:

```python
import torch

def smoothed_adversarial_step(model, x, y, sigma, m, eps, steps, alpha):
    """One PGD step against the m-sample smoothed classifier (SmoothAdv)."""
    delta = torch.zeros_like(x).uniform_(-eps, eps)
    for _ in range(steps):
        delta.requires_grad_(True)
        noise = torch.randn(m, *x.shape[1:], device=x.device) * sigma
        # average logits over noise: empirical smoothed classifier
        logits = model((x + delta).unsqueeze(0) + noise).mean(dim=0)
        loss = torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(logits, y)
        grad = torch.autograd.grad(loss, delta)[0]
        delta = (delta + alpha * grad.sign()).clamp(-eps, eps).detach()
    return delta
```

Salman et al. [2] showed that this "SmoothAdv" procedure substantially improves certified accuracy: on CIFAR-10, certified accuracy at radius 0.5 rises from roughly 43% (Gaussian augmentation) to 57–63%, and at radius 1.0 from 22% to 32–40% [5]. The cost is steep — adversarial training requires m noise samples per PGD step, multiplying training time by an order of magnitude.

### 4.3 Attack-Free Radius Maximization: MACER and Consistency Regularization

The expense of SmoothAdv motivated attack-free objectives that directly maximize the certified radius. Zhai et al.'s MACER [4] decomposes the training loss into a classification term and a robustness term:

ℒ_MACER(θ) = ℒ_CE(ĝ_θ(x), y) + λ · max(0, γ − CR(ĝ_θ; x, y)),

where CR(ĝ_θ; x, y) = (σ/2)(Φ⁻¹(𝔼_η[z_θ^y(x+η)]) − Φ⁻¹(max_{y′≠y} 𝔼_η[z_θ^{y′}(x+η)])) is the *soft* randomized smoothing certified radius [4]. The key innovation is Soft-RS: applying smoothing to the softmax outputs z_θ rather than hard class votes makes the certified radius differentiable (in expectation), so it can be optimized by gradient descent without any adversarial attack. Because Monte Carlo estimation of the indicator-based Hard-RS radius is non-differentiable, Soft-RS is the enabling approximation: as the softmax temperature β → ∞, the Soft-RS radius converges to the Hard-RS radius almost everywhere [4]. Empirically, MACER achieves ACR = 0.544 on ImageNet (σ = 0.25) in 117.9 hours versus SmoothAdv's 0.528 in 193.1 hours — better certificates at substantially lower cost [4].

Jeong and Shin [7] add *consistency regularization*: penalizing disagreement between f(x + ε₁) and f(x + ε₂) for independently drawn noise samples. Intuitively, a base classifier whose predictions are stable under independent noise draws yields a smoothed classifier with higher p_A, hence larger radii. This matches or exceeds MACER on CIFAR-10 benchmarks [5].

### 4.4 Deterministic Derandomization

All of the above inherits Monte Carlo randomness: certificates hold with probability 1 − α over the sampling. Derandomized smoothing [8] replaces stochastic averaging with *deterministic* ensembles over a fixed, structured noise set. The representative construction trains a base classifier on *ablated* inputs — e.g., all subsets of input features of a fixed size — and classifies by majority vote over the deterministic ensemble. Because the ensemble is finite and fixed, the vote counts are exact, yielding deterministic certificates with no confidence parameter α.

A concrete instantiation is (de)randomized smoothing for patch attacks: split the input into non-overlapping chunks (or contiguous byte blocks for malware executables), classify each chunk independently, and take a majority vote [8]. An adversarial patch of bounded size can corrupt only a bounded number of chunks, so if the margin of the majority vote exceeds twice the number of corruptible chunks, the prediction is *deterministically* certified. Gibert et al. applied this to malware detectors, achieving deterministic certificates against content-insertion attacks where randomized smoothing fails [8].

For ℓ₂ threats, deterministic analogues quantize the Gaussian into a fixed lattice, replacing the Neyman–Pearson analysis with a combinatorial bound on the mass a shifted lattice distribution can move. Quantization error shrinks effective radii and lattice size grows with dimension, so deterministic ℓ₂ certificates currently lag randomized ones on vision tasks. Derandomization thus trades statistical sharpness for logical certainty — desirable in safety-critical settings where even a 0.1% certification-failure probability is unacceptable.

### 4.5 Scaling and the Accuracy–Robustness Frontier

Certified-accuracy curves — certified accuracy as a function of radius r — are the standard reporting tool [1]. Each noise level σ traces a curve: small σ gives high clean accuracy but tiny radii; large σ extends radii at the cost of clean accuracy. The curves exhibit a characteristic *waterfall* (truncation) drop where the finite-sample ceiling R_max bites [6]. Representative numbers (CIFAR-10, ResNet-110, α = 0.001):

| Method | σ = 0.25, r = 0.5 | σ = 0.5, r = 1.0 | σ = 1.0, r = 1.5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian aug. (Cohen et al.) [1] | ~43% | ~22% | ~14% |
| SmoothAdv (Salman et al.) [2] | ~57–63% | ~38–40% | ~32% |
| MACER (Zhai et al.) [4] | ~59% | ~38% | ~25% |
| Consistency (Jeong & Shin) [7] | ~58% | ~38% | ~27% |

On ImageNet with ResNet-50, σ = 0.25 yields roughly 60% certified accuracy at r = 0.5, decaying to near zero by r = 2.0 [1]. The average certified radius (ACR) for state-of-the-art ImageNet models sits near 0.54–0.58 [4]. These numbers expose the practical ceiling: ℓ₂ radii on the order of 0.5–1.0 are certifiable at scale, while the ℓ_∞ radii relevant to pixel-level attacks (e.g., 8/255 ≈ 0.03 in ℓ_∞ on CIFAR-10) remain far out of reach via Gaussian smoothing.

## 5. Empirical Results and Benchmarks

We consolidate the key quantitative findings from the literature into a single comparative picture. All figures below use the standard certification protocol (n₀ = 100, n = 100,000, α = 0.001) unless noted.

**Certified accuracy at fixed radii (CIFAR-10).** Progressing from Gaussian augmentation [1] through SmoothAdv [2], MACER [4], and consistency regularization [7], certified accuracy at σ = 0.25, r = 0.5 climbs 43% → 57–63% → 59% → 58%, and at σ = 0.5, r = 1.0 climbs 22% → 38–40% → 38% → 38%. SmoothAdv costs ~10× Gaussian augmentation; MACER recovers most of the gain attack-free [4].

**ImageNet scale.** Cohen et al. certified a 500-image ImageNet subset with ResNet-50 at 110 s/image [1]; MACER reached ACR = 0.544 at σ = 0.25 after 117.9 hours on 4×P100 GPUs [4]. Randomized smoothing remains the *only* certified defense demonstrated at ImageNet scale [5].

**Radius ceilings.** With n = 100,000 and α = 0.001, unanimous votes certify at most R_max ≈ 3.8σ (from Φ⁻¹ of the Clopper–Pearson bound at k = n). Empirically, certified accuracy collapses to zero shortly beyond r ≈ 3σ for every method — the waterfall effect [6].

**Derandomized certificates.** On patch-attack benchmarks for malware detection, derandomized smoothing provides deterministic guarantees where randomized smoothing's certificates are vacuous against content insertion [8]; however, on CIFAR-10 ℓ₂ benchmarks, deterministic lattice-smoothed certificates trail Monte Carlo certificates by 10–20 percentage points of certified accuracy at matched radii, reflecting quantization loss.

**Lipschitz-derived certificates.** Networks with enforced 1-Lipschitz constraints (orthogonal layers) combined with the Φ⁻¹-smoothing analysis yield deterministic ℓ₂ certificates competitive with randomized smoothing at small σ, but clean accuracy degrades sharply as constraints tighten — the same accuracy–robustness frontier, now paid in expressivity rather than noise [2].

## 6. Limitations

**Curse of dimensionality for p > 2.** Kumar et al. [5] proved that for general i.i.d. smoothing distributions and p > 2, the largest certifiable ℓ_p radius shrinks as O(1/d^{1/2 − 1/p}); for ℓ_∞, the dependence is O(1/√d). Gaussian smoothing is essentially optimal in its dimensional dependence, yet ℓ_∞ certificates remain vacuous in high dimensions — a fundamental barrier, not an algorithmic gap.

**The certificate is only as good as (p̲_A, p̄_B).** The Neyman–Pearson bound is tight given only the two class probabilities, but real classifiers carry far more structure. The extremal half-space classifier is pathological; exploiting additional information — confidence-score distributions [6], local smoothness, margin geometry — can yield strictly larger radii for the same network.

**High-probability, not deterministic.** Standard certificates fail with probability α per example (typically 0.001); over a million-image deployment, thousands of certificates are silently invalid. Derandomization [8] addresses this but currently sacrifices substantial certified accuracy on vision tasks.

**Computational cost of certification.** 100,000 forward passes per input makes certification orders of magnitude slower than inference, limiting deployment to offline auditing rather than real-time guarantees [1].

**Radius–accuracy tradeoff and semantic meaning.** Meaningful ℓ₂ radii (0.5–1.0 on normalized images) are small relative to perceptible perturbations; larger σ destroys clean accuracy. Moreover, ℓ₂ balls poorly model the perturbations adversaries actually use (patches, rotations, ℓ_∞ noise) [8].

**Radius–confidence dissociation.** Kumar et al. [6] showed certified radii correlate weakly with the base classifier's softmax confidence: points with low confidence can receive large radii and vice versa, because the certificate measures *stability* of the vote, not *correctness* of the underlying belief. Certifying confidence, not just the label, requires modified Neyman–Pearson analyses over score distributions.

**Training cost.** The best certificates demand adversarial training of smoothed classifiers or hundreds of epochs of radius-maximizing objectives on multi-GPU clusters [2], [4], placing state-of-the-art certified robustness out of reach for modest compute budgets.

## 7. Conclusion

Randomized smoothing derives its power from a single classical insight: the Neyman–Pearson lemma identifies the worst case over all base classifiers consistent with observed vote probabilities, and for Gaussian noise that worst case is a half-space whose mass shift is exactly computable through Φ. This yields a tight, scalable ℓ₂ certificate — the first to reach ImageNet — whose radius R = (σ/2)(Φ⁻¹(p̲_A) − Φ⁻¹(p̄_B)) distills the entire analysis into the noise level and two probabilities. The Lipschitz reformulation, x ↦ Φ⁻¹(ĝ(x)) being 1/σ-Lipschitz, reveals the certificate as a regularity statement about smoothed score landscapes and opens the door to deterministic certificates for Lipschitz-constrained architectures. Training methodology has matured from Gaussian augmentation through adversarial SmoothAdv to attack-free MACER and consistency regularization, steadily pushing certified accuracy upward at manageable cost. Derandomized smoothing, meanwhile, trades statistical sharpness for logical certainty via deterministic ensembles over ablations or quantized noise lattices.

The open frontier is defined by the paradigm's limits: the O(1/√d) dimensional curse for ℓ_∞ certificates, the gap between the pathological Neyman–Pearson worst case and structured real classifiers, and the Monte Carlo cost of certification itself. Progress will likely come from certificates that exploit more than (p_A, p_B) — distributional information about confidence, local geometry, and learned structure — while preserving the scalability that made randomized smoothing the dominant certified defense. Until then, the honest summary stands: we can certify ℓ₂ balls of modest radius at ImageNet scale, with high probability, at substantial computational cost — a genuine but bounded guarantee in an adversarial world.
