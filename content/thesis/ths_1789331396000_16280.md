# Optimal Transport: Wasserstein Geometry, the Sinkhorn Algorithm, and Gromov–Wasserstein Alignment

## Abstract

Optimal transport (OT) converts the comparison of probability distributions into a variational problem: move mass from one distribution onto another at minimum cost. This thesis develops the full arc of the modern theory, from Monge's original 1781 excavation problem through Kantorovich's convex relaxation, the Wasserstein geometry it induces on the space of probability measures, Brenier's characterization of optimal maps as gradients of convex potentials, and the algorithmic revolution triggered by entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn algorithm. We then extend the framework beyond a shared ambient space to Gromov–Wasserstein alignment of metric measure spaces, which compares internal relational geometry instead of coordinates. Throughout, we combine precise mathematical statements with proof sketches of the central theorems, convergence analyses of the algorithms, and a discussion of statistical versus computational tradeoffs. The result is a self-contained account of how a two-century-old transportation problem became the geometric workhorse of contemporary machine learning.

## 1 Introduction

In 1781, Gaspard Monge asked how to move a pile of excavated soil to a target site with minimal effort — the *problème des déblais et des remblais*. Behind the earthworks metaphor sits a deep question: given two probability measures $\mu$ and $\nu$ on a space $X$, what is the cheapest way to rearrange the mass of $\mu$ into $\nu$? Monge's answer was a *transport map* $T: X \to Y$ pushing $\mu$ forward to $\nu$, chosen to minimize $\int_X c(x, T(x))\, d\mu(x)$ for a ground cost $c$. The problem is famously ill-posed in the map formulation: an optimal map may not exist, the constraint $T_\sharp\mu = \nu$ is violently nonlinear, and the infimum need not be attained [1][5].

The decisive turn came in 1942, when Leonid Kantorovich — motivated not by soil but by the optimal allocation of resources in a planned economy — *relaxed* the problem. Rather than deterministic maps, he allowed *transport plans*: joint probability measures $\gamma \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)$ on $X \times Y$ whose marginals are exactly $\mu$ and $\nu$. Mass may split, the feasible set is convex and compact, and a minimizer always exists. When the cost is a power of a metric, the resulting optimum defines the **Wasserstein distance** $W_p$, a genuine metric on probability measures that metrizes weak convergence — geometry, probability, and optimization fused into one object [1][8].

Three further revolutions completed the picture. *Yann Brenier* (1991) proved that, for the squared Euclidean cost, the optimal plan collapses back to a map — and that this map is the gradient of a *convex potential*, identifying convex gradients as the high-dimensional analogue of monotone rearrangement [4]. *Marco Cuturi* (2013) showed that adding an entropic penalty smooths the Kantorovich linear program into a strictly convex problem solvable by Sinkhorn–Knopp matrix scaling at near-linear cost per iteration, igniting the adoption of OT in machine learning [2][1]. And *Facundo Mémoli* (2011) generalized the framework to *Gromov–Wasserstein* distances, comparing metric measure spaces that share no common coordinates by aligning their internal pairwise geometries [5].

Section 2 develops formulations and duality; Section 3 derives Sinkhorn and Gromov–Wasserstein alignment; Section 4 covers Wasserstein geometry, Brenier's theorem, convergence theory, and tradeoffs; Section 5 gives proofs and benchmarks.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Monge Problem

Let $\mu \in \mathcal{P}(X)$ and $\nu \in \mathcal{P}(Y)$ be probability measures, and let $c$ be a lower semicontinuous cost on $X \times Y$ valued in the nonnegative reals. Monge's problem seeks

$$\inf_{T_\sharp\mu = \nu} \int_X c(x, T(x))\,d\mu(x), \tag{MP}$$

the infimum over measurable maps pushing $\mu$ forward to $\nu$. It may admit no minimizer: splitting mass can be strictly cheaper than any deterministic map, e.g. $\mu = \delta_0$ with a two-atom $\nu$ [1].

### 2.2 Kantorovich's Relaxation

Kantorovich replaced maps by *couplings* — joint laws with the correct marginals:

$$\inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int_{X \times Y} c(x,y)\, d\gamma(x,y), \tag{KP}$$

where $\Pi(\mu,\nu) = \{\gamma : (\pi_X)_\sharp\gamma = \mu,\ (\pi_Y)_\sharp\gamma = \nu\}$. This linear program over a convex compact set of measures admits minimizers by weak compactness. Whenever an optimal plan takes the form $\gamma = (\mathrm{Id} \times T)_\sharp\mu$, it solves the Monge problem too [3][5].

### 2.3 The Wasserstein Metric

When $X = Y$ is a complete separable metric space with distance $d$ and $c = d^p$, the value of (KP) raised to $1/p$ is the **$p$-Wasserstein distance**:

$$W_p(\mu,\nu) = \left(\inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int d(x,y)^p\,d\gamma\right)^{1/p}, \quad p \geq 1.$$

$W_p$ is a genuine metric on measures with finite $p$-th moments, and $W_p(\mu_n, \mu) \to 0$ iff $\mu_n \rightharpoonup \mu$ with convergence of $p$-th moments — the topology of optimal transport is the topology of weak convergence, enriched with geometry [1][8].

### 2.4 Kantorovich Duality and $c$-Transforms

The dual of (KP) is the unconstrained concave program

$$\sup_{\varphi,\psi :\, \varphi(x)+\psi(y) \leq c(x,y)} \int_X \varphi\,d\mu + \int_Y \psi\,d\nu, \tag{DP}$$

where the pair $(\varphi, \psi)$ are *Kantorovich potentials*. For fixed $\psi$, the best $\varphi$ is the **$c$-transform** $\psi^c(x) = \inf_y [c(x,y) - \psi(y)]$, and optimality concentrates on the $c$-subdifferential $\partial^c\varphi = \{(x,y) : \varphi(x) + \varphi^c(y) = c(x,y)\}$ [3]. The pair can be taken to be convex conjugates for the quadratic cost — a fact that unlocks Brenier's theorem.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Entropic Regularization

The computational bottleneck of (KP) is its linear-programming complexity — $O(n^3 \log n)$ via the network simplex for discrete histograms [1][2]. Cuturi's insight was to penalize deviation from the product coupling with negative entropy [2]:

$$\min_{\pi \in \Pi(a,b)} \langle \pi, C \rangle + \varepsilon\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi \mid a \otimes b), \tag{KP-EPS}$$

where $\mathrm{KL}(\pi \mid a \otimes b) = \sum_{i,j} \pi_{ij} \log(\pi_{ij} / a_i b_j)$. The entropy term makes the objective *strictly convex*, guarantees a *unique* optimizer, and — critically — gives it a factored form. The first-order conditions yield $\pi^\star_{ij} = u_i K_{ij} v_j$ with Gibbs kernel $K_{ij} = \exp(-C_{ij}/\varepsilon)$ and scaling vectors $u, v > 0$ fixed by the marginal constraints $\pi^\star \mathbf{1} = a$, $(\pi^\star)^\top \mathbf{1} = b$ [1][2].

### 3.2 The Sinkhorn Algorithm

Sinkhorn–Knopp matrix scaling (Sinkhorn, 1964) alternately normalizes rows and columns of $K$:

```python
import numpy as np

def sinkhorn(a, b, C, eps, max_iter=1000, tol=1e-9):
    """Entropic OT via Sinkhorn iterations. Returns transport plan P and cost."""
    K = np.exp(-C / eps)
    u = np.ones_like(a)
    v = np.ones_like(b)
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        u_prev = u.copy()
        u = a / (K @ v)
        v = b / (K.T @ u)
        if np.linalg.norm(u - u_prev) < tol:
            break
    P = (u[:, None] * K) * v[None, :]
    return P, float(np.sum(P * C))
```

Each iteration costs $O(n^2)$ — matrix-vector products only — and the iterates converge *linearly* in the Hilbert projective metric [1][2]. Block-coordinate ascent on the dual objective explains this: each update is exact maximization in one block. For small $\varepsilon$ the kernel underflows; log-domain stabilization (Schmitzer, 2019) works in $\log u$, $\log v$ with log-sum-exp [1].

### 3.3 Sinkhorn Divergences

The regularized value $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ is biased: $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\mu) \neq 0$. Feydy et al. debiased it via

$$S_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) = \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\mu) - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\nu,\nu),$$

which is positive and definite, metrizes weak convergence as $\varepsilon \to 0$, and keeps the $O(1/n)$ sample complexity of entropic OT instead of the $O(n^{-1/d})$ curse [1][8].

### 3.4 Gromov–Wasserstein Alignment

Classical OT needs a common ground space. To compare objects in *different* spaces — a protein interaction network versus another, or word embeddings from two languages — Mémoli proposed the **Gromov–Wasserstein distance** on metric measure spaces $\mathbb{X} = (X, d_X, \mu)$, $\mathbb{Y} = (Y, d_Y, \nu)$ [5]:

> **Definition (Gromov–Wasserstein).** For $p \geq 1$,
>
> $$\mathrm{GW}_p(\mathbb{X}, \mathbb{Y}) = \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \left(\iint |d_X(x,x') - d_Y(y,y')|^p\, d\pi(x,y)\, d\pi(x',y')\right)^{1/p}.$$

The coupling matches *pairs*: $(x,y),(x',y')$ pays the distortion $|d_X(x,x')-d_Y(y,y')|^p$. $\mathrm{GW}_p$ vanishes exactly for isomorphic metric measure spaces and is a strict metric on isomorphism classes [5]. Discrete GW is a nonconvex quadratic assignment problem — NP-hard in general — solved in practice by entropic relaxation plus projected gradient or Frank–Wolfe steps, each inner iteration a Sinkhorn solve [5][6][7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Wasserstein Geometry: Geodesics, Displacement Interpolation, and Curvature

The space $(\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d), W_2)$ is a genuine *length space*: between any $\mu_0, \mu_1$ there is a constant-speed geodesic. If $T$ is the optimal map, McCann's **displacement interpolation**

$$\mu_t = ((1-t)\mathrm{Id} + tT)_\sharp \mu_0, \quad t \in (0,1),$$

traces this geodesic — particles travel along straight lines at constant speed, never colliding when $T$ is a Brenier map [4]. This is the Benamou–Brenier dynamic formulation: $W_2^2$ equals the minimum kinetic energy $\int_0^1 \int \|v_t\|^2 d\mu_t\,dt$ over velocity fields advecting $\mu_0$ to $\mu_1$, revealing OT as least action.

Displacement *convexity* along these geodesics governs the analysis of gradient flows. A functional $\mathcal{F}$ is displacement convex if $t \mapsto \mathcal{F}(\mu_t)$ is convex; the Boltzmann entropy $\mathcal{H}(\mu) = \int \rho \log \rho$ is displacement convex, which yields exponential convergence of the Fokker–Planck equation to equilibrium — the analytic backbone of Langevin Monte Carlo [1][8].

A striking special case admits closed form: for Gaussians $\alpha = \mathcal{N}(m_\alpha, \Sigma_\alpha)$, $\beta = \mathcal{N}(m_\beta, \Sigma_\beta)$,

$$W_2^2(\alpha,\beta) = \|m_\alpha - m_\beta\|^2 + \mathrm{tr}\left(\Sigma_\alpha + \Sigma_\beta - 2(\Sigma_\alpha^{1/2}\Sigma_\beta\Sigma_\alpha^{1/2})^{1/2}\right),$$

with optimal map $T(x) = m_\beta + A(x - m_\alpha)$, $A = \Sigma_\alpha^{-1/2}(\Sigma_\alpha^{1/2}\Sigma_\beta\Sigma_\alpha^{1/2})^{1/2}\Sigma_\alpha^{-1/2}$ — with symmetric positive-definite $A$ [1].

### 4.2 Brenier's Theorem and the Monge–Ampère Equation

> **Theorem (Brenier, 1991).** Let $\mu, \nu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ have finite second moments, with $\mu$ absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. For the quadratic cost $c(x,y) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^2$, there exists a *unique* optimal transport map $T$ with $T_\sharp\mu = \nu$, and $T = \nabla \varphi$ for a convex function $\varphi$, unique up to an additive constant $\mu$-a.e. [4]

In one dimension this reduces to the monotone rearrangement $T = F_\nu^{-1} \circ F_\mu$; in higher dimensions there is no total order, and cyclic monotonicity — characterized exactly by gradients of convex functions — takes its place [4].

Differentiating the pushforward constraint yields the **Monge–Ampère equation**

$$\det(D^2\varphi(x)) = \frac{f(x)}{g(\nabla\varphi(x))},$$

a fully nonlinear second-order PDE (Caffarelli regularity theory). Numerically, the convexity constraint suggests the *input convex neural network* parametrization: model $\varphi$ with a neural net convex in its inputs, and train $\nabla\varphi$ to match samples — the foundation of neural optimal transport [1].

The type-level structure of the quadratic distortion objective is captured cleanly in a functional language:

```haskell
-- Gromov-Wasserstein distortion over couplings
gwDistortion :: Double -> (Matrix Double, Matrix Double) -> Matrix Double -> Double
gwDistortion p (dx, dy) pi =
  sum [ abs (dx ! (i,j) - dy ! (k,l)) ** p * pi ! (i,k) * pi ! (j,l)
      | i <- indices dx, j <- indices dx, k <- indices dy, l <- indices dy ]
```

### 4.3 Sinkhorn's Algorithm: Convergence Analysis and Log-Domain Stabilization

Franklin and Lorenz (1989) proved linear convergence in the Hilbert projective metric $d_H$: $d_H(u^{(k)}, u^\star) \leq \lambda^k d_H(u^{(0)}, u^\star)$ with $\lambda = \tanh(\Delta/4) < 1$ [1]. The rate degrades as $\varepsilon \to 0$, motivating $\varepsilon$-annealing: solve coarsely, then warm-start finer problems.

| Method | Problem | Complexity | Convergence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Network simplex | Exact discrete OT | $O(n^3 \log n)$ | Finite termination |
| Sinkhorn (standard domain) | Entropic OT | $O(n^2)$ per iter | Linear, factor $\lambda(\varepsilon)$ |
| Log-domain Sinkhorn | Entropic OT | $O(n^2)$ per iter | Linear, numerically stable |
| Greenkhorn (greedy) | Entropic OT | $O(n^2)$ per iter, better constants | Linear, faster in practice |
| Projected gradient + Sinkhorn | Entropic GW | $O(n^2 m + nm^2)$ per outer step | Local minima (nonconvex) |
| Quantized GW (qGW) | Approximate GW | Near-linear in parts | Upper bounds on GW |

*Table 1: Computational complexities of exact and regularized OT solvers. Sources: [1][2][6][7].*

Numerical stability demands the log-domain variant. Writing $f = \varepsilon \log u$, $g = \varepsilon \log v$, updates become stabilized soft-min operations via log-sum-exp — no explicit formation of the underflowing kernel $K$ [1]. The following TLA+ sketch specifies the iteration contract a correct implementation must satisfy:

```tla
---- MODULE Sinkhorn ----
EXTENDS Reals, Naturals
CONSTANTS A, B, C, Eps, Tol
VARIABLES u, v, k
Init == /\ u \in [1..Len(A) |-> 1]
        /\ v \in [1..Len(B) |-> 1]
        /\ k = 0
RowStep == u' = [i \in 1..Len(A) |-> A[i] / Sum(j, K(i,j) * v[j])]
ColStep == v' = [j \in 1..Len(B) |-> B[j] / Sum(i, K(i,j) * u[i])]
Next == /\ RowStep /\ ColStep' /\ k' = k + 1
Converged == MaxAbsDiff(u * K * v, A, B) < Tol
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<u,v,k>> /\ WF_<<u,v,k>>(Next)
THEOREM Spec => []<>(Converged)
====
```

### 4.4 Gromov–Wasserstein: Alignment Across Incomparable Spaces

The GW objective is quadratic in the coupling, hence nonconvex; the discrete problem is a quadratic assignment problem, NP-hard in the worst case [5][6]. The practical workhorse is *entropic GW*: add $\varepsilon\,\mathrm{KL}$ to the GW objective and alternate (i) computing the pseudo-cost matrix $\tilde{C}(\pi) = -4\,C_X \pi C_Y$ (for the squared distortion) and (ii) solving the resulting *linear* entropic OT with Sinkhorn — a projected-gradient/Frank–Wolfe scheme converging to stationary points [6][7].

Scalability breakthroughs decompose spaces hierarchically. *Quantized Gromov–Wasserstein* (qGW) treats partition blocks as fundamental objects, yielding a hierarchy of upper bounds at scales an order of magnitude beyond prior art — over one million points [7]. The metric axioms matter here: $\mathrm{GW}_p$ satisfies the triangle inequality only at the optimum; heuristic solvers return approximations that need not be true metrics, a caveat for downstream pipelines [5][6].

Applications span single-cell genomics, graph matching, shape correspondence, heterogeneous domain adaptation, and cross-lingual word-embedding alignment — every setting where coordinates are meaningless but *relations* are not [5][6][7].

| Variant | Compares | Convex? | Definite? | Sample complexity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $W_1$ (Earth mover's) | Distributions, shared space | Yes (LP) | Yes | $O(n^{-1/d})$ |
| $W_2^2$ | Distributions, shared space | Yes (LP) | Yes | $O(n^{-1/d})$ |
| Entropic $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ | Distributions, shared space | Strictly | No (biased) | $O(1/n)$-like |
| Sinkhorn divergence $S_\varepsilon$ | Distributions, shared space | No | Yes | $O(1/n)$-like |
| $\mathrm{GW}_p$ | Metric measure spaces | No (QAP) | Yes (mod isometry) | $O(n^{-1/d})$-like |
| Entropic GW | Metric measure spaces | No | Approx | Improved in practice |

*Table 2: The optimal-transport family tree. Sample complexity refers to empirical-vs-population convergence; $d$ is intrinsic dimension. Sources: [1][5][6][8].*

The discrete Kantorovich LP — the exact baseline everything else approximates — is compactly expressed in a systems language:

```rust
/// Discrete Kantorovich OT: minimize <P, C> over the transportation polytope.
/// Vertices of the polytope are permutation matrices (Birkhoff–von Neumann).
struct KantorovichProblem {
    a: Vec<f64>,          // source histogram, sums to 1
    b: Vec<f64>,          // target histogram, sums to 1
    c: Vec<Vec<f64>>,      // ground cost matrix
}

impl KantorovichProblem {
    fn feasible(&self, p: &[Vec<f64>]) -> bool {
        // row sums == a, column sums == b, entries >= 0
        p.iter().zip(&self.a).all(|(row, &ai)| row.iter().sum::<f64>() - ai < 1e-12)
            && (0..self.b.len()).all(|j| p.iter().map(|row| row[j]).sum::<f64>() - self.b[j] < 1e-12)
    }
    fn objective(&self, p: &[Vec<f64>]) -> f64 {
        p.iter().zip(&self.c).map(|(row, crow)| {
            row.iter().zip(crow).map(|(x, y)| x * y).sum::<f64>()
        }).sum()
    }
}
```

### 4.5 Statistical and Computational Tradeoffs

The deepest tension in modern OT is statistical: estimating $W_p$ from $n$ samples converges at the cursed rate $O(n^{-1/d})$ in dimension $d$ — the empirical measure simply cannot fill space [8]. Entropic regularization breaks the curse: $S_\varepsilon$ and $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ attain near-parametric $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ rates, because the entropy term smooths the dual potentials into a low-complexity function class [1][8]. The price is *bias*: $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon \neq W_p$, and the bias decays only as $\varepsilon \to 0$ — where Sinkhorn becomes numerically treacherous. Choosing $\varepsilon$ is thus a bias–variance–stability trilemma, not a free lunch [1][2].

In the GW setting, entropic limit theory (asymptotic normality, bootstrap consistency, semiparametric efficiency) now supplies the inferential tools classical OT enjoyed a decade earlier [6].

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof Sketch: Existence for Kantorovich, Nonexistence for Monge

*Existence (KP).* The set $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ is tight, hence weakly compact (Prokhorov); the cost functional $\gamma \mapsto \int c\,d\gamma$ is lower semicontinuous for l.s.c. $c \geq 0$. A minimizing sequence has a weak cluster point, which is a minimizer. ∎

*Nonexistence (MP).* Take $\mu = \delta_0$, $\nu = \tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{-1} + \tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{+1}$ with $c(x,y) = |x-y|$. Every map sends $0$ to one point; only a splitting plan achieves the optimum — and with strictly convex costs, mass-splitting plans beat every map. ∎

### 5.2 Proof Sketch: Brenier's Theorem

Let $\pi$ be optimal for the quadratic cost and $(\varphi, \varphi^\star)$ optimal convex-conjugate Kantorovich potentials, so $\int \langle x, y \rangle d\pi = \int \varphi\,d\mu + \int \varphi^\star d\nu$ [3][4]. Since $\varphi(x) + \varphi^\star(y) \geq \langle x, y \rangle$ always, the integrand $\varphi(x) + \varphi^\star(y) - \langle x, y \rangle \geq 0$ integrates to zero — hence vanishes $\pi$-a.e. Thus $y \in \partial\varphi(x)$ for $\pi$-a.e. $(x,y)$. Absolute continuity of $\mu$ implies $\varphi$ is differentiable $\mu$-a.e. (Rademacher), so $y = \nabla\varphi(x)$ $\pi$-a.e., and $\pi = (\mathrm{Id} \times \nabla\varphi)_\sharp\mu$. Uniqueness follows from strict convexity of the cost in $y$. ∎

### 5.3 Proof Sketch: Linear Convergence of Sinkhorn

In the Hilbert projective metric $d_H$, the map $u \mapsto a / (K(b/(K^\top u)))$ is a contraction with factor $\lambda = \tanh(\Delta/4) < 1$, where $\Delta = \max_{i,j,k,l} \log(K_{ik}K_{jl}/K_{il}K_{jk})$ is the projective diameter of $K(\mathbb{R}^n_{>0})$ (Birkhoff–Hilbert theorem). Banach's fixed-point theorem gives a unique fixed point — the entropic optimizer — and $d_H(u^{(k)}, u^\star) \leq \lambda^k d_H(u^{(0)}, u^\star)$ [1][2].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Curse of dimensionality (statistical).** Exact Wasserstein estimation converges at $O(n^{-1/d})$; entropic debiasing helps but the bias–stability tradeoff caps achievable fidelity [8][1].
2. **GW nonconvexity.** The Gromov–Wasserstein objective is a nonconvex quadratic program; scalable solvers guarantee only stationary points, and the returned distance may violate the triangle inequality [5][6].
3. **Isometry blindness.** GW identifies spaces up to measure-preserving isometry — it cannot distinguish mirror images or symmetric structures, and spurious near-isometries can fool the alignment [5].
4. **Cost specification.** Both $W_p$ and GW are only as meaningful as the ground cost or internal metric; misspecified costs answer the wrong question faithfully [1].
5. **Scalability ceilings.** Naive Sinkhorn is $O(n^2)$ per iteration in memory and time; beyond $n \sim 10^5$, multiscale, low-rank, or sliced approximations become mandatory [1][7].
6. **Regularity requirements.** Brenier's theorem needs absolute continuity of the source; for singular measures the Monge problem may have no solution and the PDE theory degenerates [4].

## 7 Conclusion

Optimal transport has traveled from Monge's earthworks to the center of machine learning without losing its geometric soul. Kantorovich turned an ill-posed problem into a linear program and a metric; Brenier revealed the hidden convex structure of its solutions; Cuturi's entropic regularization turned the metric into a practical algorithm; Mémoli's Gromov–Wasserstein freed it from any common coordinate system. The trajectory is unified by a single idea: *mass, moved optimally, defines geometry*.

Open frontiers: closing the entropic–exact statistical gap, certifiable GW optimization, OT on non-Euclidean spaces, and Monge–Ampère regularity under minimal assumptions. Two and a half centuries after Monge picked up his shovel, the pile of sand is still teaching us how to measure the shape of data.
