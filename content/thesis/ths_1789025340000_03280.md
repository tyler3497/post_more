# DAG-Based Byzantine Fault Tolerant Consensus: Narwhal Mempool Separation, Bullshark Partial Synchrony, and Mysticeti Fast-Path Linearization for 500k TPS

## Abstract

Classical Byzantine fault tolerant (BFT) replication couples transaction dissemination with ordering inside a leader's critical path, capping throughput near a few thousand transactions per second. This thesis develops a unified theory of *DAG-based* BFT consensus, in which every validator continuously disseminates data as vertices of a round-structured directed acyclic graph, and ordering is derived from the graph's causal structure with little or no extra communication. We decompose the design space into three layers — the Narwhal mempool, which separates reliable dissemination from ordering and scales out through per-validator workers [1]; Bullshark, which layers a partially synchronous fast path over the DAG while preserving an asynchronous fallback [2]; and Mysticeti, which eliminates explicit vertex certification to reach the three-message-round latency lower bound, weaving a fast commit path for owned-object transfers into the DAG [3]. Tracing the lineage from PBFT [7] and HotStuff [5] through DAG-Rider [4], we formalize each generation's commit rules with safety and liveness theorems and analyze published evaluations showing WAN throughput from 130,000 to 600,000 transactions per second at sub-second to few-second latency.

## 1 Introduction

Byzantine fault tolerant (BFT) consensus is the algorithmic backbone of permissioned blockchains, financial settlement networks, and replicated control planes. For two decades after the publication of Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) [7], the field converged on a single architectural template: a designated leader proposes a block, replicas exchange prepare and commit votes, and the system advances through a sequence of leader-driven views. This template is *optimal* in latency — PBFT commits in three message delays — yet it is structurally hostile to throughput. The leader is the sole source of proposals, so every transaction must traverse the leader's bandwidth and CPU before it can be ordered; HotStuff [5] reduced the communication complexity to linear and made view changes linear as well, but the leader bottleneck remained, yielding roughly 1,800 tx/sec in realistic wide-area deployments [1].

The DAG-based revolution, initiated by Narwhal and Tusk [1], reframes the problem. Instead of funneling all data through one leader, *every* validator proposes concurrently, and the proposals are arranged into a round-structured directed acyclic graph whose edges record causal history. Consensus then reduces to *interpreting* the DAG — electing leaders from within it and ordering their causal histories — rather than to an additional round of message exchange. This thesis argues that the DAG architecture is not a single protocol but a *design space* with three orthogonal axes:

1. **Dissemination architecture** — how data enters the DAG (Narwhal's worker/primary split with quorum certificates).
2. **Commit-rule synchrony** — what network assumption governs liveness (DAG-Rider's asynchrony, Bullshark's dual steady/fallback modes, Mysticeti's partial synchrony with three-round optimality).
3. **Execution fast paths** — whether commutative or owned-object transactions can finalize without full consensus ordering (Mysticeti-FPC).

We survey each axis, formalize the central safety and liveness theorems, and synthesize the empirical record — from the original Narwhal evaluation (130,000 tx/sec at under 2 seconds latency on a WAN, scaling linearly to 600,000 tx/sec with additional workers [1]) to Mysticeti's 0.5-second WAN commit latency at over 200,000 tx/sec on the Sui mainnet lineage [3].

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the leader-based lineage from PBFT to HotStuff and the impossibility results that bound it. Section 3 presents the methodology: the DAG construction, reliable broadcast semantics, and the two-layer paradigm of communication followed by zero-overhead ordering. Section 4 deep-dives into Narwhal, DAG-Rider, Bullshark, and Mysticeti with their respective commit rules. Section 5 gives proof sketches and a consolidated empirical evaluation. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes with future directions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The leader-based lineage

PBFT [7] established the canonical three-phase protocol — *pre-prepare*, *prepare*, *commit* — over $n = 3f + 1$ replicas tolerating $f$ Byzantine faults. Its safety rests on quorum intersection: any two quorums of $2f + 1$ replicas share at least one correct replica, guaranteeing agreement. Its cost is quadratic all-to-all communication and a quadratic view-change subprotocol.

> **Theorem (PBFT Safety, informal [7]):** In a system of $n = 3f+1$ replicas with at most $f$ Byzantine faults, if two correct replicas commit requests $r$ and $r'$ in the same view and sequence number, then $r = r'$. Safety holds under pure asynchrony; liveness requires eventual synchrony.

HotStuff [5] modernized this design with two contributions: a *three-phase* (later two-phase, in HotStuff-2) structure in which each phase is driven by threshold-signature quorum certificates (QCs), and *pipelining* — chaining phases so that one block's prepare doubles as the previous block's pre-commit. Communication became linear in the common case, and view change became a linear, leader-driven operation. HotStuff was rapidly adopted: LibraBFT/DiemBFT, Flow, and Cypherium all instantiate its chained variant [5].

Yet both protocols share a structural invariant: **the leader proposes, and only the leader proposes**. Dissemination and ordering are fused. Every byte of client data crosses the leader's network interface, so throughput is bounded by one machine's capacity, and censorship resistance depends entirely on leader rotation fairness.

### 2.2 Impossibility boundaries

Three classical results delimit the space:

- **FLP impossibility** [7's context; Fischer–Lynch–Paterson]: deterministic consensus is impossible in pure asynchrony with even one crash fault. DAG-Rider [4] and Tusk [1] evade FLP through a shared random coin; Bullshark [2] and Mysticeti [3] assume partial synchrony for liveness.
- **The $3f+1$ bound:** optimal resilience for asynchronous Byzantine agreement with cryptography.
- **The three-delay lower bound** [6]: any BFT protocol requires at least three message delays to commit in the fault-free case. Mysticeti-C is the first DAG protocol to attain it [3].

### 2.3 From broadcast to graph

The key insight of the DAG line of work is that reliable broadcast (RBC) already constructs a causal structure. If every validator reliably broadcasts one vertex per round, and each vertex references a quorum of vertices from the previous round, the union of all views forms a DAG with two critical properties: **non-equivocation** (RBC delivers the same vertex to all correct validators) and **completeness** (a vertex's causal history is eventually available wherever the vertex is). Ordering then becomes a *local* computation: validators run identical deterministic commit rules over locally observed DAGs and derive identical total orders — the *zero-overhead ordering layer* of DAG-Rider [4].

| Protocol | Year | Network model | Latency (message delays) | Throughput (WAN) | Key innovation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [7] | 1999 | Partial sync | 3 | ~10k tx/s | Quorum intersection, 3-phase |
| HotStuff [5] | 2019 | Partial sync | 3 (pipelined) | ~1.8k tx/s | Linear comms, QC chaining |
| Narwhal+Tusk [1] | 2021 | Async | ~7 rounds | 160k tx/s | Mempool/consensus separation |
| DAG-Rider [4] | 2021 | Async | ~4 waves | — | Zero-overhead ordering, coin leaders |
| Bullshark [2] | 2022 | Partial sync + async fallback | ~2 rounds (steady) | 125k tx/s | Dual leaders, fast path |
| Shoal++ [6] | 2024 | Partial sync | 4.5 avg | ~130k tx/s | Pipelined anchors, 60% latency cut |
| Mysticeti-C [3] | 2024 | Partial sync | **3 (optimal)** | 200k+ tx/s | Uncertified DAG, every block commits |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 System model

We consider $n = 3f + 1$ validators, at most $f$ of which are Byzantine. Validators communicate over point-to-point authenticated channels. We analyze protocols under two network models:

- **Asynchrony:** messages are eventually delivered, with no bound (DAG-Rider [4], Tusk [1]).
- **Partial synchrony:** there exists an unknown global stabilization time (GST) after which message delays are bounded by $\Delta$ (Bullshark [2], Mysticeti [3], Shoal++ [6]).

Execution proceeds in **rounds**. In each round $r$, every validator $p_i$ broadcasts at most one *vertex* $v_{i,r}$ containing client transactions (or batch digests) and edges to vertices of round $r - 1$. A vertex is *admitted* to a validator's local DAG only after its causal history is complete.

### 3.2 The two-layer paradigm

Following DAG-Rider [4], we decompose every protocol into:

1. **Communication layer.** Validators reliably broadcast vertices and assemble a structured DAG. Narwhal refines this with certificates: a *certificate of availability* for a batch is formed from $2f + 1$ acknowledgments, and each vertex carries $2f + 1$ certificates of the previous round [1].
2. **Ordering layer.** Validators apply a deterministic commit rule to their local DAG — electing *leaders* (anchor vertices) and ordering each leader's causal history with a fixed tie-breaking rule. No extra messages are exchanged for ordering; hence **zero overhead** [4].

```python
# Pseudocode: generic DAG commit rule skeleton (after [4], [2])
def order_dag(local_dag):
    ordered = []
    for wave in waves(local_dag):          # partition rounds into waves
        leader = elect_leader(wave)        # coin or round-robin
        if committed(leader):              # commit rule: f+1 support
            for v in causal_history(leader):
                if v not in ordered:
                    ordered.append(v)      # deterministic DFS order
    return ordered
```

### 3.3 Validity and fairness

DAG-based protocols inherit a distinctive *fairness* property: because every validator continuously injects vertices, every correct validator's transactions are eventually included in some committed leader's causal history. DAG-Rider proves *validity* — all proposals by correct processes are eventually delivered — as a first-class property [4], whereas leader-based protocols guarantee only that *some* proposal is decided per view.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Narwhal: separating dissemination from ordering

Narwhal's central contribution is architectural: it splits the validator into a **primary** and multiple **workers** [1]. Workers disseminate transaction batches and collect $2f+1$ signed acknowledgments into *certificates of availability*; the primary proposes vertices containing only certificates (small metadata), so consensus operates over kilobytes while workers move megabytes. Throughput scales out linearly with the number of workers per validator — the evaluation reports 130,000 tx/sec with a single worker per validator on a WAN, rising linearly to 600,000 tx/sec with ten workers, with *no latency increase* [1].

> **Theorem (Narwhal Availability [1]):** If a certificate for batch $b$ exists, then $b$ is stored by at least $f + 1$ correct validators and is retrievable by any correct validator. Hence consensus over certificates is as safe as consensus over full data.

The DAG structure itself is round-based: each primary vertex references at least $2f + 1$ certificates from the previous round. Because certificates are quorum-sized, any two vertices in consecutive rounds share causal overlap, which is the combinatorial engine behind every commit rule that follows.

**Tusk**, Narwhal's companion consensus, runs *inside* the DAG with zero additional messages: every three rounds form a wave, a global perfect coin elects a leader vertex retrospectively, and the leader commits if it has $f + 1$ supporters in the next round — otherwise the wave is skipped and the protocol retries [1]. Tusk achieves 160,000 tx/sec at roughly 3 seconds latency on a WAN, a $20\times$ improvement over prior asynchronous protocols [1].

```rust
struct Batch { txs: Vec<Transaction>, digest: Hash }

    let acks = broadcast_and_collect(batch.digest, quorum = 2*f+1);
    Certificate { digest: batch.digest, signatures: acks }
}
```

### 4.2 DAG-Rider: asynchronous atomic broadcast with optimal complexity

DAG-Rider [4] is the theoretical keystone: the first asynchronous Byzantine *atomic broadcast* with optimal resilience ($n = 3f + 1$), optimal amortized communication complexity, and optimal expected time complexity. Its construction is strikingly clean. The communication layer builds the DAG via reliable broadcast; the ordering layer partitions rounds into **waves of four rounds**, uses a global perfect coin to elect one leader per wave *retrospectively*, and commits the leader when $2f + 1$ vertices in the wave's final round have strong paths to it.

> **Theorem (DAG-Rider Agreement [4]):** If a correct process commits leader $v$ in wave $w$, then every correct process eventually commits $v$ in wave $w$, and all correct processes order the causal histories of committed leaders identically.

Two properties deserve emphasis. First, **post-quantum safety**: instantiated with an information-theoretic threshold coin, DAG-Rider uses no digital signatures for its safety argument [4]. Second, **validity with fairness**: because leaders are elected by a coin *after* the DAG is built, a Byzantine adversary cannot permanently censor any correct validator's vertices — every wave's leader is equally likely to be any validator.

### 4.3 Bullshark: making DAG-BFT practical under partial synchrony

Bullshark [2] observes that DAG-Rider's elegance comes at a latency cost: committing requires traversing full waves and waiting for coin revelation. Bullshark introduces **two leader types per wave**: *steady-state leaders*, chosen deterministically by round-robin every two rounds, commit on a fast path requiring only $f + 1$ votes in the following round (roughly two message delays after GST); *fallback leaders*, elected by the global coin as in DAG-Rider, preserve asynchronous liveness when the network misbehaves. The partially synchronous variant drops the fallback entirely and is, remarkably, implementable in about 200 lines on top of a Narwhal mempool [2].

Bullshark's commit rule is the template for the entire partially synchronous DAG family:

1. Anchor $a$ in round $r$ commits if at least $f + 1$ vertices in round $r + 1$ reference it.
2. Anchors are processed in increasing round order; committing a later anchor recursively commits all earlier uncommitted anchors in its causal history.
3. All other vertices are ordered by deterministic traversal of committed anchors' causal histories.

The quorum arithmetic is what makes the rule safe: with $n = 3f + 1$ and $2f + 1$-sized reference sets, any committed anchor is visible to every future anchor's quorum, so two correct validators can never commit conflicting anchor sequences. Evaluation shows 125,000 tx/sec at 2–3 seconds latency with 50 validators [2] — and the Shoal framework later cut Bullshark's latency by up to 40% failure-free (80% under faults) via leader reputation and pipelining, introducing the notion of *prevalent responsiveness* [6's predecessor work, Shoal].

### 4.4 Mysticeti: uncertified DAGs and the latency lower bound

Every protocol above *certifies* DAG vertices — via reliable broadcast, quorum certificates, or threshold signatures — before the commit rule may use them. Certification costs message rounds and, more importantly, forces the DAG to advance at the pace of its slowest quorum. Mysticeti [3] discards certification entirely. Validators broadcast vertices directly (best-effort broadcast with threshold clocks for round advancement), and the commit rule is redesigned so that **every block can be a leader**: a block commits when a quorum of blocks in later rounds *supports* it, where support is defined recursively over the uncertified DAG.

> **Theorem (Mysticeti-C Optimal Latency [3]):** Mysticeti-C commits blocks in three message delays in the steady state — matching the lower bound for Byzantine consensus — while sustaining over 200,000 tx/sec on a WAN. It is the first DAG-based protocol to achieve both bounds simultaneously.

Three mechanisms make this possible:

- **Threshold clocks** replace round synchronization: a validator advances to round $r + 1$ upon observing $2f + 1$ vertices of round $r$, with no certificates.
- **Pipelined multi-proposers:** every validator's block is a potential commit candidate, eliminating the leader bottleneck and the wave structure.
- **Mysticeti-FPC (fast path):** for *owned-object* transactions (which commute by construction in the Sui object model), finality is achieved by weaving $2f + 1$ validator signatures into the DAG itself — no separate consensus or certification protocol — yielding sub-second finality for the common payment case [3].
## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketches

**Safety (all DAG protocols).** The core lemma is *anchor visibility*: if an anchor $a$ satisfies its commit rule at validator $p$, then every anchor $a'$ committed later (at any correct validator) has $a$ in its causal history. Proof: the commit rule requires a quorum $Q$ ($f + 1$ or $2f + 1$ supporters) in the round(s) after $a$; $a'$'s vertex references a quorum $Q'$ of the previous round; $|Q \cap Q'| \geq 1$ correct validator by quorum intersection, and that validator's vertex links the histories. By induction, all correct validators commit the same anchor sequence, and deterministic traversal of causal histories yields identical total orders [2][4].

**Liveness.** Under asynchrony, Tusk and DAG-Rider rely on the global perfect coin: each wave elects a uniformly random leader, and with probability at least $(2f+1)/(3f+1) > 2/3$ the leader is proposed by a correct validator whose vertex is widely referenced, so the expected number of waves per commit is constant [1][4]. Under partial synchrony, Bullshark's steady-state leaders commit within a bounded number of rounds after GST [2]; Mysticeti's threshold clocks guarantee round progress under partial synchrony, and its support rule commits every correct validator's blocks without waiting for stragglers [3].

**Validity/fairness.** Because every validator's vertex enters the DAG every round and committed anchors' causal histories include all referenced vertices, each correct validator's transactions are eventually ordered — a strictly stronger guarantee than leader-based validity [4].

### 5.2 Consolidated performance record

| System | Validators | Throughput | Latency | Setting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HotStuff (baseline) [1] | 10–20 | 1,800 tx/s | ~1 s | WAN |
| Narwhal–HotStuff [1] | 50 | 130,000 tx/s | <2 s | WAN |
| Narwhal (10 workers) [1] | 50 | 600,000 tx/s | <2 s | WAN |

Two scaling laws emerge. **Throughput scales with proposers, not with the leader**: Narwhal's worker scale-out and Mysticeti's every-block-a-leader design both convert validator count from a liability (quadratic voting) into an asset (parallel dissemination) [1][3]. **Latency scales with certification rounds, not with throughput**: removing certification (Mysticeti) collapses commit latency to the three-delay optimum without sacrificing the throughput curve [3].

> **Theorem (Throughput–Latency Decoupling):** In DAG-BFT architectures, steady-state throughput is bounded by aggregate validator bandwidth $\sum_i b_i$ while commit latency is bounded below by the certification depth of the commit rule; the two bounds are independently tightenable. *Proof sketch:* Narwhal attains the bandwidth bound via workers [1]; Mysticeti attains the latency bound via uncertified commit [3].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Memory and garbage collection.** The DAG grows unboundedly; validators must garbage-collect rounds below the committed frontier without breaking availability of causal histories needed by slow validators. Bullshark addresses this with timestamped vertices and a median-timestamp GC rule, but only guarantees fairness *after* GST [2].
2. **Censorship resistance vs. latency.** Fast paths (Bullshark steady leaders, Mysticeti-FPC) favor responsive validators; an adversary controlling network scheduling can delay specific validators' vertices past commit cutoffs. DAG-Rider's coin-based fairness is stronger but slower [4].
3. **Shared-object contention.** Mysticeti-FPC's sub-second finality applies only to owned objects; shared objects still traverse full consensus, and contended workloads see little benefit [3].
4. **Cryptographic assumptions.** Post-quantum safety holds only with information-theoretic coins [4]; production deployments (Sui, Aptos) use threshold BLS signatures and inherit their assumptions.
5. **Implementation complexity.** Uncertified DAGs trade protocol-message simplicity for intricate local reasoning — equivocation handling, threshold-clock edge cases, and support-rule bookkeeping are notoriously subtle to implement correctly [3].
6. **Evaluation realism.** Published numbers use small (512-byte) transactions and synthetic workloads; real smart-contract execution, state I/O, and adversarial conditions are under-explored across all cited evaluations.

## 7 Conclusion

DAG-based BFT consensus has, in under five years, rewritten the performance envelope of Byzantine agreement: from HotStuff's 1,800 tx/sec leader bottleneck [5] to Narwhal's 600,000 tx/sec worker-scaled dissemination [1] and Mysticeti's three-message-delay optimal latency at 200,000+ tx/sec in production [3]. The unifying principle is the separation of *dissemination* from *ordering*: a causally structured broadcast graph already contains the information consensus needs, so ordering becomes interpretation, not communication. Bullshark showed that partial synchrony buys a fast path without surrendering asynchronous safety [2]; Mysticeti showed that even certification is dispensable [3].

Open frontiers include memory-bounded asynchronous garbage collection with full fairness, fair ordering under adversarial scheduling, and extending fast-path linearization to shared-object workloads. If the trajectory of the last five years continues, the next generation will push DAG-BFT from hundreds of thousands toward millions of transactions per second — at which point the bottleneck will no longer be consensus at all, but execution.
