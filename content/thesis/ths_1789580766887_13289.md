# Modal Type Theories for Staged Metaprogramming: Fitch-Style Box Introduction, Kripke Semantics, and Normalization

## Abstract

Modal type theories interpret the necessity modality of intuitionistic S4 as the type of code, turning staged metaprogramming into a problem of modal proof theory. This article surveys and unifies the three presentations that have shaped the field: the Davies–Pfenning λ→□ calculus with its Kripke-inspired context stacks, the Fitch-style formulation in which box introduction and elimination are governed by lock operators and subordinate proofs, and the multimodal type theory (MTT) framework that generalizes both to arbitrary mode theories. We reconstruct the Kripke semantics of the box modality, explain how locks enforce stage discipline at the level of typing derivations, and give a semantic account of normalization by evaluation for Fitch-style calculi via presheaf models indexed by worlds. A staged power-function example shows how binding-time correctness reduces to modal correctness, connecting the theory to practice in MetaOCaml. We close with the metatheoretic state of the art, including Gratzer's normalization theorem for MTT, and the open problems that remain for effectful and intensional staging.

##1 Intro

The idea that programs can *generate* programs is as old as Lisp quotation, but quotation alone offers no static guarantees: a macro can produce ill-typed or ill-scoped code, and the error surfaces only when the generated program is compiled. Staged metaprogramming seeks to eliminate this gap by making the *stage* at which each subexpression runs a statically checked property. The decisive move of Davies and Pfenning was to observe that this staging discipline is exactly the discipline of modal logic: the type □A of intuitionistic S4 denotes *code of type A*, a closed program fragment whose evaluation is deferred to a later stage [1]. Under this reading, constructing a boxed term corresponds to quoting, and unboxing corresponds to splicing or running, while the modal proof rules enforce hygiene and scope safety by construction.

That single correspondence has since ramified into several distinct proof-theoretic presentations, and choosing among them is far from cosmetic. The original Davies–Pfenning formulation works with *context stacks*, in which each world of the Kripke semantics is represented by a separate context in a stack, and box introduction pushes a fresh context [1]. A *dual-context* variant, developed in the lineage of Pfenning and Wong, splits assumptions into a modal and an ordinary zone. And a third, increasingly influential presentation revives *Fitch-style* natural deduction, after Borghuis, in which a dedicated *lock* operator is inserted into the context to mark the boundary of a subordinate proof; opening the lock enters the next world, and shutting it returns [2]. Each style internalizes the same Kripke semantics, yet each yields different substitution calculi, different normalization arguments, and different ergonomics for the metaprogrammer.

This article has three goals. First, to give a self-contained exposition of the Fitch-style calculus — locks, box introduction, and the delicate variable rule — and to show precisely how it mirrors Kripke's possible-worlds semantics. Second, to explain *normalization by evaluation* (NbE) for modal calculi, in which terms are evaluated into a presheaf semantics over worlds and then reified back into normal forms, yielding both a decision procedure for conversion and a semantic completeness argument [3]. Third, to situate these ideas in the contemporary landscape of *multimodal* type theory, where Gratzer's MTT framework and its normalization theorem [5] now provide a single umbrella under which staging modalities, guarded recursion, and parametricity can coexist. Running through the exposition is the concrete thread of staged programming practice, anchored by the Davies–Pfenning embedding theorem and by MetaOCaml's brackets, escape, and run [6][7].

##2 Background

### Intuitionistic S4 and its Kripke semantics

Intuitionistic modal logic extends intuitionistic propositional logic with the necessity modality □, governed by the necessitation rule (from ⊢ A infer ⊢ □A) and the K axiom □(A → B) → □A → □B. The logic S4 adds the T axiom □A → A and the 4 axiom □A → □□A. Its semantics is given by *Kripke frames*: a preorder (W, ≤) of possible worlds equipped with a *monotone* valuation V assigning to each world a set of true atoms, monotone in the sense that truth persists upward along the order. The forcing clause for the modality is the familiar one:

> **Definition (Forcing).** A world w forces □A, written w ⊩ □A, just when v ⊩ A for every world v accessible from w, that is, for every v with w ≤ v.

The axioms of S4 correspond to frame conditions: T holds exactly on reflexive frames, and 4 on transitive ones, so S4 is the logic of preorders. The intuitionistic reading matters for computation: monotone valuations mean that once a proposition is established at a stage, it remains available at all later stages — the semantic shadow of *cross-stage persistence* in staged languages.

### The Davies–Pfenning calculus

Davies and Pfenning's λ→□ is a natural-deduction calculus for intuitionistic S4 whose judgments are taken over *context stacks* Γ₁; …; Γₙ, where each context in the stack stands for one Kripke world [1]. The crucial rule is box introduction: to form a term of type □A in the current world, one derives a term of type A in a *fresh* world, represented by pushing an empty context onto the stack. Dually, box elimination pops the stack. The Kripke heritage is unmistakable — the presentation is often called the *Kripke style* — and the conversion between context stacks and Fitch-style locks is largely mechanical [4].

The computational payoff is the centerpiece of [1]: Nielson and Nielson's two-level functional language, the classic vehicle of binding-time analysis, embeds *conservatively* into an extension Mini-ML□ of the modal calculus. On this fragment, *binding-time correctness is equivalent to modal correctness*: a program is well-staged exactly when it is well-typed in the modal system. Beyond the fragment, Mini-ML□ expresses immediate evaluation and the sharing of generated code across stages, supporting run-time code generation as well as partial evaluation.

### Fitch style and locks

The Fitch-style presentation goes back to Borghuis's investigation of modal deduction in the 1990s, in which modalities are eliminated by *opening* a subordinate proof and introduced by *shutting* one. Clouston revived the idea as a basis for modal lambda calculi with good computational properties across a range of intuitionistic modal logics [2]. In the Fitch style there is a single context, but the context may contain a distinguished *lock* marker that separates the outer assumptions from the inner ones. Intuitively, the segment of the context to the right of the rightmost lock is the current world; everything to the left is locked away. We develop this machinery in detail in §4.

##3 Methodology

This article is a comparative survey with a reconstructive aim: rather than merely cataloguing results, we re-derive the central constructions — the lock discipline, the forcing semantics, and the NbE model — in a uniform notation, so that the passage from one presentation to another is visible as a sequence of small, motivated steps. Our selection of material is guided by three criteria:

1. **Fidelity to Kripke semantics.** Every rule should be justifiable as the proof-theoretic shadow of a semantic operation on worlds. This is what makes the lock, rather than being an ad hoc device, the syntactic counterpart of world accessibility.
2. **Suitability for normalization by evaluation.** We privilege the Fitch-style calculus because the cleanest semantic analysis of NbE for modal lambda calculi — Valliappan, Ruch, and Cortiñas's construction of a possible-worlds model, formalized in Agda — is carried out in exactly this setting [3].
3. **Expressiveness for staging.** Theoretical elegance is measured against the practical demand: can the calculus type the staged programs people actually write, from the power function to run-time code generators? The Davies–Pfenning embedding theorem [1] and the MetaOCaml design [6][7] serve as our empirical touchstones.

Where the literature offers competing formulations — idempotent versus non-idempotent S4, locks versus context stacks — we present both and explain the computational stakes of the choice. Code illustrations use a small Haskell-like pseudocode for the metatheory and MetaOCaml surface syntax for staging examples, keeping each fragment self-contained.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Fitch-Style Box Introduction: Locks and Subordinate Proofs

The Fitch-style calculus for intuitionistic S4 extends the simply typed lambda calculus with a single new type former □A and two new term formers, `box t` and `unbox t`. The novelty lies entirely in the *contexts*, which may contain a lock marker:

```haskell
-- Fitch-style syntax for intuitionistic S4

data Ty  = Base String | Arr Ty Ty | Box Ty
data Ctx = Empty | Snoc Ctx (String, Ty) | Lock Ctx
data Tm  = Var String | Lam String Ty Tm | App Tm Tm
         | BoxIntro Tm | BoxElim Tm
```

Typing judgments Γ ⊢ t : A are governed by the familiar rules for abstraction and application, together with a *modified variable rule* and the modal rules [3]:

- **Variable.** From Γ, x : A, Γ′ ⊢ x : A, provided no lock occurs in Γ′. A variable is visible only within its own world; a lock between the binding site and the use site seals it off.
- **Box introduction.** From Γ, lock ⊢ t : A infer Γ ⊢ box t : □A. To build code of type A, open a subordinate proof — a fresh world — and derive A there, using only assumptions that survive the lock.
- **Box elimination.** The precise form depends on whether the modality is *idempotent*. In the non-idempotent calculus λIK, from Γ, lock, Γ′ ⊢ t : □A with no lock in Γ′ infer Γ, lock, Γ′ ⊢ unbox t : A: a boxed value available in the current world may be opened exactly one level. In the idempotent S4 formulation of Clouston, the elimination instead uses a lock-erasing operation that deletes all locks, reflecting the collapse of all previously visited worlds into one [4].

The distinction between idempotent and non-idempotent S4 is not a scholastic nicety; it is the exact point where logic meets staging practice. In multi-stage programming, □A denotes code generated in *one* stage, while □□A denotes code generated in *two* stages — generating a generator.

Locks also give a crisp account of *hygiene*. Because the variable rule forbids reaching across a lock, a boxed term can never capture a variable from the enclosing stage: scope extrusion is a typing error, not a run-time surprise.

### 4.2 Kripke Semantics: Worlds, Accessibility, and Forcing

The lock is syntax; the world is semantics; and the two are joined by a straightforward dictionary. Read each maximal lock-free segment of a context as a world, and read moving rightward past a lock as moving to an accessible world. Then box introduction says: to prove □A at w, prove A at an *arbitrary* accessible world — exactly the forcing clause.

Categorically, Clouston interprets the calculus in cartesian closed categories equipped with an adjunction of endofunctors, the necessity modality being the right adjoint; where that functor is an idempotent comonad, a coherence result yields a calculus for intuitionistic S4 simpler than its predecessors [2].

This semantic perspective is what makes normalization by evaluation possible. Valliappan and collaborators construct their NbE model as an instance of the possible-worlds semantics for the modal logic IK: semantic values live in a presheaf over the category of worlds, restriction maps transport values along accessibility, and the modal type □A is interpreted by quantifying over future worlds — the forcing clause, internalized [3].

### 4.3 From Modality to Staging: Code Types in Practice

The Davies–Pfenning embedding theorem turns the modal calculus into a staging discipline. Under the identification of □A with *code of type A*, the two-level language of binding-time analysis maps onto the modal fragment: static (compile-time) expressions become ordinary terms, and dynamic (run-time) expressions become boxed terms. The theorem states that this embedding is conservative, so a two-level program is binding-time correct if and only if its image is modally well-typed [1].

> **Theorem (Davies–Pfenning [1]).** The embedding of Nielson and Nielson's two-level functional language into Mini-ML□ is conservative; binding-time correctness coincides with modal correctness on this fragment.

MetaOCaml is the living embodiment of this idea [6]. Its three staging constructs line up with the modal calculus almost verbatim: *brackets* quote an expression, corresponding to box introduction; *escape* splices a computed code value into a larger quotation, corresponding to box elimination; and *run* executes a closed code value, corresponding to the T axiom's counit at the top level.

```haskell
-- Staged power function (MetaOCaml-style surface syntax)
spower :: Int -> Code (Int -> Int)
spower 0 x = Brack 1
spower n x | even n    = Brack (App Square (Esc (spower (n `div` 2) x)))
           | otherwise = Brack (App2 Mul (Esc x) (Esc (spower (n - 1) x)))
```

Here applying `spower` to 3 evaluates *now*, at stage 0, to the code value representing `fun x -> x * (x * (x * 1))` — a residual program with all dispatch on `n` discharged and no interpretive overhead remaining. In modal typing terms, `spower n : □(Int → Int)` for each concrete `n`, and the escape is well-typed precisely because the spliced code was built in a subordinate proof whose locks guarantee closedness.

Two further MetaOCaml features illuminate the modal account. *Cross-stage persistence* — using a stage-0 variable inside brackets — is the computational face of monotone valuations: values established at an earlier world remain available later.

### 4.4 Normalization and the Multimodal Frontier

Normalization by evaluation decides conversion without rewriting: a term is *evaluated* into a semantic domain and then *reified* — quoted back — into a normal form. For Fitch-style modal calculi, the semantic domain is the presheaf model over worlds described above. The pipeline has three stages:

1. **Evaluation.** Terms are mapped to semantic values in the presheaf model, with the modal cases following the forcing clause: evaluating `box t` quantifies over accessible worlds, and evaluating `unbox t` instantiates at the current one.
2. **Reflection.** Neutral terms — variables and stuck eliminations such as `unbox` applied to a neutral — are embedded into the semantics, giving meaning to open code.
3. **Reification.** Semantic values are read back as η-long β-normal syntactic forms; at modal type, reification under a box introduces a fresh lock, mirroring box introduction.

> **Proposition (Soundness and completeness [3]).** Terms related by β-conversion evaluate to equal semantic values; terms with equal denotations reify to identical normal forms.

The procedure has been formalized in Agda, giving machine-checked normalization for λIK and its S4 extensions.

The frontier is *multimodality*. Real systems need more than one modality at once — staging, guarded recursion, and internalized parametricity each want their own box — and Gratzer, Kavvos, Nuyts, and Birkedal's *multimodal type theory* (MTT) provides the general framework. MTT is parameterized by a *mode theory*: a strict 2-category whose objects are modes, whose morphisms are modalities, and whose 2-cells witness transformations between them. Contexts carry locks indexed by modalities, and the Fitch-style discipline generalizes smoothly: each modality opens its own kind of subordinate proof [5].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

We now make the metatheory concrete along four axes: a worked NbE trace, the shape of the embedding proof, a comparison of the three presentations, and the mechanization record.

**1. A worked normalization trace.** Consider the Fitch-style term `box (λx. x)` at type □(A → A) in the empty context. Evaluation interprets it at the current world as the semantic function that, given any accessible world and any value of type A there, returns that value — the identity, transported along restriction maps. Reification at type □(A → A) opens a fresh lock, reifies the body at function type by η-expansion, and closes the box, yielding the normal form `box (λx. x)` itself. The interesting case is a redex under a box: evaluation computes the β-reduction in the semantics, where substitution is just function application, and reification returns the normal form — without any syntactic rewriting pass.

**2. The embedding proof, in outline.** The Davies–Pfenning conservativity argument proceeds by a type-directed translation from the two-level language into Mini-ML□ and a back-translation on the modal fragment [1]. The key lemma is that a two-level judgment at binding time b translates to a modal judgment whose context stack has depth exactly b — the binding time *is* the world index. Substitution lemmas for the two-level language become instances of the modal substitution principles, and adequacy follows because both translations preserve and reflect typing.

**3. Comparing the presentations.** The following table summarizes the trade-offs a language designer faces:

| Aspect | Context stacks (Kripke style) [1][4] | Dual contexts | Fitch style with locks [2][3] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Worlds represented by | Separate contexts in a stack | Modal vs. ordinary zones | Lock-delimited segments |
| Substitution calculus | Explicit stack operations | Zone-aware substitution | Standard, with lock side-conditions |
| Idempotency handling | Structural, via stack depth | Structural | Via lock-erasure operation |
| NbE formalization | Less developed | Partial | Full Agda development [3] |
| Staging fidelity | Excellent (stage = depth) | Good | Excellent (stage = lock count) |
| Multimodal extension | Awkward | Awkward | Smooth via indexed locks [5] |

The table explains the field's drift toward Fitch style: it is the presentation in which the metatheory — substitution, NbE, and multimodal generalization — is smoothest, while losing nothing for staging.

**4. Mechanization.** The NbE development for Fitch-style calculi is formalized in Agda [3], and Gratzer's normalization proof for MTT is likewise carried out with machine-checked components, the synthetic Tait computability argument being internalized in a glued topos.

##6 Limitations

The picture is not complete, and the gaps are instructive. First, the *idempotency question* remains unsettled in practice: while the non-idempotent reading matches staged programming's need to count stages, several categorical models naturally validate idempotency, and the Fitch-style elimination rule for the idempotent variant — with its lock-erasing operation — is markedly subtler than the introduction rule [4]. A calculus that lets the programmer *choose* per-modality, as MTT's mode theories in principle allow, is still awaiting ergonomic surface syntax.

Second, *effects* strain the modal account. MetaOCaml supports references, exceptions, and foreign code inside brackets, but the Davies–Pfenning theory is pure: extending □ with effects requires either a modal treatment of effect disciplines or a retreat to dynamic checks, and neither is fully worked out [6]. The clean story told here covers the typed, pure core; production staging lives partly outside it.

Third, *intensional* analysis of code — pattern-matching on the structure of a boxed term, as in typed quotation frameworks or in layered modal theories — goes beyond what □ provides. The box modality is *extensional*: it classifies code by its type, not its shape. Recent layered theories add exactly this capability, but their metatheory is younger and their normalization arguments more delicate [5].

Finally, MTT's normalization theorem reduces decidability to the *mode theory*, which means the framework guarantees nothing until the mode theory is shown decidable — a genuine proof obligation for each new modality a language designer dreams up [5].

##7 Conclusion

Modal type theories have turned staged metaprogramming from a bag of implementation tricks into a chapter of proof theory. The Davies–Pfenning correspondence — □A as the type of code, box introduction as quotation, unboxing as splicing — gave staging its first prescriptive type discipline and its landmark theorem: binding-time correctness *is* modal correctness [1]. The Fitch-style reformulation, with its locks and subordinate proofs, made that discipline proof-theoretically perspicuous and semantically transparent, aligning each rule with an operation on Kripke worlds [2]. Normalization by evaluation then turned the semantics into an algorithm, deciding conversion by evaluating into presheaf models and reifying back to normal forms, with machine-checked proofs to back it [3]. And multimodal type theory has begun to unify the menagerie of modalities — staging, guarded recursion, parametricity — under a single framework with a single normalization theorem [5].

What remains is to close the gap between the pure, verified core and the effectful, intensional practice of systems like MetaOCaml [6][7]. If the history recounted here is any guide, the closing will come the same way the opening did: by finding the modal logic that was already there, waiting to be read as a programming language.
