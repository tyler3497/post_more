# Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search at Billion Scale: HNSW Graphs, Product Quantization, and DiskANN

## Abstract

Approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search is the algorithmic core of modern retrieval infrastructure, from semantic document search and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to billion-image recommendation systems. Exact search in high-dimensional spaces is intractable: it requires linear scans or data structures whose cost grows exponentially with dimension. This thesis unifies the three dominant families of billion-scale ANN methods: **graph-based** indexes exemplified by Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graphs, **partition-and-compress** indexes built on inverted files with product quantization (IVF-PQ), and **disk-resident** graph indexes exemplified by DiskANN's Vamana graph. We derive the complexity of greedy beam search on navigable graphs, analyze the distortion–compression tradeoff of product and optimized product quantization under asymmetric distance computation, and formalize the recall–latency Pareto frontier governing index selection. We compare memory footprints and query-time behavior on billion-scale benchmarks (SIFT1B, DEEP1B, BIGANN), and characterize failure modes including hubness, quantization bias in inner-product search, and SSD I/O amplification — concluding with a decision framework for vector retrieval in large language model workloads.

## 1 Introduction

The nearest neighbor problem is deceivingly simple to state: given a database $\mathcal{X} = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ and a query $q \in \mathbb{R}^d$, return the $k$ points minimizing a distance $d(q, x_i)$. In low dimensions, spatial trees such as k-d trees solve it in logarithmic time. In the high-dimensional regimes that dominate modern machine learning — 128-dimensional SIFT descriptors, 768-dimensional BERT embeddings, 4096-dimensional image representations — tree-based methods collapse. This is the *curse of dimensionality*: the volume of space concentrates near cell boundaries, and any exact partition-based search degenerates to a linear scan [3].

The practical response is *approximation*. An algorithm is said to solve the $c$-approximate nearest neighbor problem if it returns a point within a factor $c$ of the true nearest neighbor distance; in practice, the community evaluates *recall@$k$*, the fraction of the true top-$k$ neighbors recovered by the approximate procedure. Trading a few points of recall for orders-of-magnitude reductions in latency is the engine of the entire vector-database industry, and it is what makes RAG over billion-document corpora economically feasible.

Three architectural families dominate the literature and production systems. **Proximity graphs** — navigable small world networks and their hierarchical refinements — connect each vector to its neighbors and answer queries by greedy walks; HNSW [1] is the de facto default index in nearly every vector database. **Partition-and-compress** methods divide the space with a coarse quantizer (the inverted file, IVF) and compress residuals with product quantization (PQ) [3]; they dominate when the corpus must fit in a fixed memory budget, a regime pushed to the extreme by GPU implementations [4]. **Disk-resident graphs** — DiskANN's Vamana graph [2] — decouple navigation from storage, keeping compressed vectors in RAM for routing while fetching full vectors from SSD, and thereby index a billion points on a single commodity node.

This thesis develops each family from first principles, derives their complexity bounds, compares their empirical behavior, and analyzes their limitations. The unifying theme is the *recall–latency–memory* trilemma: no index is optimal on all three axes, and the correct choice is a function of corpus size, embedding dimension, query workload, and hardware budget.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The problem and the metrics

Formally, we consider the *approximate $k$-nearest neighbor* ($k$-ANN) problem under a metric (or, increasingly, a non-metric) distance. The two distances that dominate are the Euclidean distance $\|q - x\|_2$ and the inner product $-\langle q, x\rangle$, the latter being equivalent to cosine similarity on normalized vectors. Recall is measured against ground truth computed by exact search:

```python
def recall_at_k(retrieved, ground_truth, k):
    """Fraction of true top-k recovered by the approximate index."""
    retrieved_k = set(retrieved[:k])
    truth_k = set(ground_truth[:k])
    return len(retrieved_k & truth_k) / k
```

Latency is reported as *queries per second* (QPS) at a fixed recall, or mean/p99 latency. Memory is reported as bytes per vector, including index overhead.

### 2.2 Why graphs won

Proximity graphs replace space partitioning with *connectivity*. A query begins at an entry point and repeatedly moves to the neighbor closest to the query until a local minimum is reached. The key property is *navigability*: the graph must contain short paths from any entry point to the query's true neighbors. The Delaunay graph is perfectly navigable under greedy routing but far too expensive in high dimensions. The insight of Malkov and colleagues was that randomized long-range links — the *small world* phenomenon — recover most of navigability cheaply, and that a hierarchy of such graphs recovers logarithmic scaling [1].

### 2.3 Why compression is unavoidable

A billion 768-dimensional float32 vectors occupy roughly **3 TB** of memory — far beyond any single machine's RAM. Product quantization [3] compresses each vector to tens of bytes by quantizing sub-vectors independently, trading a controllable reconstruction error for a $50$–$100\times$ memory reduction — and distances can be computed in the compressed domain via lookup tables, without ever decompressing a vector.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis is organized around three canonical questions that any billion-scale ANN system must answer:

1. **Routing.** How does a query navigate from a global entry point to a local neighborhood of candidates? (HNSW: hierarchical greedy descent; IVF: coarse-quantizer probe; DiskANN: beam search over a single-layer graph with compressed navigation.)
2. **Representation.** What is stored per vector, and at what cost? (Full precision floats; PQ codes of $m$ bytes; scalar-quantized bytes; SSD-resident full vectors with in-RAM PQ sketches.)
3. **Refinement.** How are candidates re-ranked to recover recall? (HNSW: larger `efSearch` candidate lists; IVF-PQ: residual re-ranking with exact or less-compressed vectors; DiskANN: exact re-ranking of the beam using full vectors fetched from SSD.)

For each family we characterize *construction* cost (paid once), *query* cost (paid per query), memory footprint per vector, and the parameters that move the operating point along the recall–latency frontier.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 HNSW: layered navigable small worlds

HNSW organizes the database into a stack of *layers* $L_0, L_1, \dots, L_{\ell_{\max}}$, where $L_0$ contains every point and higher layers contain exponentially sparser subsets [1]. Each inserted point $x$ is assigned a maximum layer $\ell(x)$ drawn from a truncated exponential distribution:

$$P(\ell(x) \geq \ell) = m_L^{-\ell}, \qquad m_L = \frac{1}{\ln M},$$

where $M$ is the target out-degree. Equivalently, $\ell(x) = \lfloor -\ln U / \ln m_L \rfloor$ for $U \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$. This is the same distribution governing skip-list tower heights, and it is the source of HNSW's logarithmic behavior: the expected number of layers is $O(\log N)$, and search descends through them like a skip-list search.

> **Theorem (HNSW search complexity).** Under the layer-assignment distribution above and with bounded degree $M$, greedy descent from the top layer to layer $0$, followed by a best-first search with candidate list size `efSearch` at layer $0$, visits $O(\log N + \text{efSearch})$ nodes in expectation. The descent phase performs $O(1)$ distance computations per layer in expectation.

*Proof sketch.* At layer $\ell$, the search is a greedy walk whose characteristic link length grows geometrically with $\ell$, so the walk crosses the space in a constant expected number of hops before reaching that layer's local minimum; it then descends to the finer subgraph [1]. The layer-0 phase is bounded by the candidate list. ∎

The second pillar of HNSW is its **neighbor-selection heuristic**. When inserting $x$, the algorithm gathers a candidate pool and selects up to $M$ neighbors, preferring *diverse* candidates: a candidate $c$ is rejected if some already-selected neighbor $n$ satisfies $d(c, n) < d(x, c)$. This relative-neighborhood-graph (RNG) rule prunes redundant edges in dense clusters and preserves long-range edges, improving recall on highly clustered data [1].

The parameter space is small but consequential:

| Parameter | Typical range | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| `M` (max out-degree) | 8–64 (16 common) | Higher $M$ → denser graph → better recall, more memory, slower build |
| `efConstruction` | 100–800 | Candidate list at build; higher → better graph quality, slower build |
| `efSearch` | 10–1000 | Candidate list at query; the main recall–latency knob |
| `mL` (level multiplier) | $1/\ln M$ | Controls layer sparsity; rarely tuned |

Memory per vector is approximately $2 \cdot M \cdot 4$ bytes for bidirectional int32 links at layer 0 plus the vector itself (e.g., 768-d float32 = 3072 bytes), so HNSW's RAM cost is dominated by the raw vectors, not the graph — a fact that motivates the compression and disk-resident families below.

### 4.2 Inverted files and product quantization

The IVF index partitions $\mathbb{R}^d$ with a coarse quantizer — typically $k$-means with $K = \sqrt{N}$ to $4\sqrt{N}$ centroids — and stores, per centroid (posting list), the vectors assigned to it [3][4]. A query is routed by finding the $n_{\text{probe}}$ nearest centroids and scanning only those lists, reducing the search space from $N$ to roughly $n_{\text{probe}} \cdot N / K$ vectors; too small an $n_{\text{probe}}$ and the true neighbors' lists are never visited.

Product quantization compresses each vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ by splitting it into $m$ sub-vectors $x = (x^1, \dots, x^m)$ of dimension $d/m$ and quantizing each independently with its own codebook of $k^*$ centroids (usually $k^* = 256$, so each index fits in one byte):

$$q(x) = (q^1(x^1), \dots, q^m(x^m)), \qquad \text{code}(x) \in \{0,\dots,255\}^m.$$

The full codebook contains $(k^*)^m$ implicit centroids at the cost of storing only $m \cdot k^* \cdot (d/m)$ floats — for $d = 128$, $m = 8$, this is $256^{8}$ effective centroids for $256 \times 128$ stored floats [3]. Distances are computed *asymmetrically* (ADC): the query stays uncompressed, and for each sub-quantizer $j$ a lookup table $T^j[i] = \|q^j - c^j_i\|_2^2$ is precomputed once per query, so that

$$\hat{d}(q, x)^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{m} T^j[\text{code}_j(x)]$$

costs $m$ table lookups and $m - 1$ additions — no decompression, no floating-point multiplies in the inner loop:

```python
def adc_distance(query_tables, code):
    """Asymmetric distance computation via PQ lookup tables."""
    # query_tables[j][i] = squared distance from query sub-vector j to centroid i
    return sum(query_tables[j][code[j]] for j in range(len(code)))
```

**Optimized product quantization (OPQ)** [6] observes that the fixed axis-aligned split is arbitrary: a learned orthonormal rotation applied before quantization, jointly optimized with the codebooks to minimize distortion, substantially reduces error on correlated data such as deep embeddings. ScaNN's **anisotropic vector quantization** [5] reframes the objective for maximum inner product search: instead of minimizing reconstruction error uniformly, it weights the loss toward preserving the *relative ordering* of near neighbors, which is what recall actually depends on.

| Method | Bits/vector (128-d) | Bytes/vector | Typical recall@1 (SIFT1M, 64-bit) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Raw float32 | 4096 | 512 | 1.000 (exact) |
| PQ, $m=8$ | 64 | 8 | ~0.35 (ADC, no IVF) |
| PQ, $m=16$ | 128 | 16 | ~0.55 |
| OPQ, $m=16$ + rotation | 128 | 16 | ~0.62 |
| IVF-PQ, $K=8192$, $n_{\text{probe}}=64$ | 128 | 16 | ~0.85 |

IVFADC — inverted file with asymmetric distance computation on PQ-encoded residuals — is the workhorse configuration of Faiss [4] and remains the strongest choice when the corpus must fit in a hard RAM budget.

### 4.3 DiskANN and the Vamana graph

DiskANN [2] starts from a different premise: RAM is expensive, SSD is cheap, and a billion-point index need not live in memory at all. Only the *compressed* vectors (PQ codes, tens of bytes each) are kept in RAM for navigation; the full-precision vectors and the graph adjacency reside on SSD. A query performs beam search over the graph using PQ distances, issuing one SSD read per visited node batch, and finally re-ranks the top candidates with exact distances computed from full vectors fetched from disk.

The graph that makes this possible is **Vamana**, constructed by an $\alpha$-robust variant of RNG pruning. When inserting a point $p$ with candidate set $C$, candidates are processed in order of increasing distance to $p$; a candidate $c$ is kept as a neighbor only if

$$d(p, c) \le \alpha \cdot d(c, n) \quad \text{for all already-selected neighbors } n,$$

with $\alpha \ge 1$ (typically $1.0$–$1.5$). Larger $\alpha$ admits more long-range edges, shrinking the search diameter at the cost of degree. The construction iterates: greedy search finds candidates, robust pruning selects diverse neighbors, and reverse edges are added with degree caps, converging to a graph with small diameter, bounded out-degree $R$ (typically 32–128), and — critically — a layout in which each node's adjacency and vector can be fetched in a single 4 KB SSD page [2][7].

> **Theorem (Vamana navigability, informal).** For $\alpha > 1$, robust pruning yields a graph in which greedy beam search from a fixed start node converges to an $\varepsilon$-approximate nearest neighbor in logarithmically many steps in the dataset diameter, with SSD page reads per query proportional to beam width times hops.

The headline result of [2]: a **billion-point** SIFT1B index served on a single 64 GB machine with one SSD at over 5,000 QPS, sub-3 ms mean latency, and 95%+ recall@1 — where in-memory methods of comparable footprint plateau near 50% recall, because they must quantize far more aggressively.

### 4.4 Scalar quantization and hybrid compression

Between full float32 and PQ lies **scalar quantization (SQ)**: each dimension is quantized independently to 8 or 4 bits, often with per-dimension scale and offset. SQ8 preserves inner products more faithfully than PQ at comparable bit-rates for cosine-similarity workloads, and its distance computation is a single SIMD dot product — no lookup tables. Modern vector databases therefore layer the techniques: **SQ8/FP16 in-RAM vectors** for HNSW navigation; **PQ sketches** for disk-resident graphs or the IVFADC coarse pass; and **exact re-ranking** on a short list from SSD or a sidecar store. No single compressor dominates: PQ minimizes bytes, SQ minimizes distortion-per-FLOP, and binary quantization minimizes bandwidth for the first retrieval stage.

### 4.5 Distance metrics and the recall–latency Pareto frontier

Fix an index family and vary its parameters; the achievable (recall, QPS) points trace a **Pareto frontier**. Points below the frontier are dominated; the practitioner's job is to select the operating point whose marginal latency cost per recall point matches the application's budget. Three structural observations govern these frontiers:

- **Graph methods (HNSW, DiskANN)** have steep frontiers at high recall: the first 90% of recall is cheap (small `efSearch` or beam width), while the last few points require exponentially more exploration. They dominate the high-recall, moderate-QPS region.
- **IVF-PQ methods** have flatter frontiers at low memory: increasing $n_{\text{probe}}$ buys recall nearly linearly until the probed fraction approaches the corpus, at which point the method degenerates to a compressed linear scan. They dominate the low-memory, moderate-recall region.
- **Metric mismatch** shifts every frontier. Embeddings trained with cosine similarity must be searched with inner product (or normalized to Euclidean); applying $\ell_2$-derived PQ distortion analysis to inner-product search systematically overestimates recall [5].

## 5 Empirical Results and Complexity Analysis

### 5.1 Asymptotic comparison

| Family | Build complexity | Query complexity | Memory / vector | Recall regime |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HNSW [1] | $O(N \log N \cdot \text{efC})$ | $O(\log N + \text{efS})$ | vector + $O(M)$ links | very high |
| IVF-PQ [3][4] | $O(N \cdot K_{\text{coarse}} + N m k^*)$ | $O(K_{\text{coarse}} + n_p \cdot N/K \cdot m)$ | $m$ bytes + coarse codebooks | high at fixed RAM |
| DiskANN [2] | $O(N \cdot L \log N)$ (iterative) | $O(W \cdot R)$ beam steps + SSD I/O | $m$ bytes RAM + full vector on SSD | very high, cheap RAM |
| Exact (flat) | $O(1)$ | $O(N d)$ | $4d$ bytes | 1.000 |

Here $\text{efC}$ = `efConstruction`, $\text{efS}$ = `efSearch`, $n_p$ = `nprobe`, $W$ = beam width, $R$ = Vamana degree bound, $m$ = PQ sub-quantizers.

> **Theorem (PQ distortion bound).** Let $q$ be a product quantizer with $m$ sub-quantizers of $k^*$ centroids each, trained by $k$-means on the data distribution. The expected squared reconstruction error satisfies $\mathbb{E}\|x - q(x)\|^2 = \sum_{j=1}^m D_j$, where $D_j$ is the distortion of sub-quantizer $j$. Under ADC, the distance estimation error for a query $q$ obeys $|\hat{d}(q,x)^2 - d(q,x)^2| \le 2\,d(q,x)\,\|x - q(x)\| + \|x - q(x)\|^2$ by the triangle inequality, so recall degrades gracefully — linearly, not catastrophically — with quantization distortion [3].

This bound is the theoretical reason ADC-based indexes degrade smoothly as $m$ shrinks, justifying $m$ (bytes per vector) as the primary memory knob.

### 5.2 Benchmark landscape

The community evaluates on a small set of canonical datasets. **SIFT1M/SIFT1B** (128-d SIFT descriptors) are the historical standard; **DEEP1B** (96-d deep features) and **BIGANN** stress learned embeddings; **MS MARCO** and **NQ** passages with dense retriever embeddings (768-d) are the RAG-relevant workloads. The ANN-Benchmarks harness standardizes the recall–QPS measurement protocol across implementations.

Representative published operating points:

- **HNSW** on SIFT1M: > 99% recall@10 at ~10k QPS single-threaded with `M=16`, `efConstruction=200`, `efSearch` tuned per recall target [1][9]. Memory is dominated by raw vectors (~512 B/vector at 128-d float32).
- **IVF-PQ (Faiss)** on SIFT1B: with $K = 2^{18}$ coarse centroids, $m = 16$ PQ bytes and OPQ rotation, recall@1 ≈ 0.55–0.65 exhaustive-compressed, rising above 0.9 with exact re-ranking of residuals — at ~16–64 bytes per vector in RAM, i.e., the full billion-point index in 16–64 GB [3][4][6].
- **DiskANN** on SIFT1B: 95%+ 1-recall@1 at > 5,000 QPS, < 3 ms mean latency, on a single 64 GB + SSD node — the only published single-node result at this scale and recall [2][7].
- **GPU Faiss** [4]: k-selection kernels at 55% of theoretical peak bandwidth; k-NN graph construction over 95M YFCC100M images in 35 minutes and 1B vectors in under 12 hours on 4 Maxwell Titan X GPUs.

The RAG implication is quantitative: $10^9$ chunks at 768-d float32 is ~3 TB raw. IVF-OPQ at 64 bytes/vector compresses it to 64 GB (fits in RAM); DiskANN serves it from SSD with 95%+ recall and millisecond latency; HNSW would need the full 3 TB in RAM — feasible only with sharding. The choice is a cost function over RAM, SSD, latency SLO, and recall floor.

### 5.3 The re-ranking identity

A unifying empirical law across all three families: *recall is determined by the candidate-generation stage, and latency by the re-ranking stage*. Widening the candidate list monotonically increases recall with diminishing returns, while re-ranking cost grows linearly in the list length. The optimal operating point therefore always pairs an aggressive, cheap candidate generator with a short, exact re-ranker.

## 6 Limitations

**Hubness and the curse revisited.** In high dimensions, a small set of *hubs* appear among the nearest neighbors of disproportionately many queries. Graph indexes concentrate traffic on hubs, creating hot nodes that serialize beam search; quantization methods distort hub neighborhoods asymmetrically. No current index explicitly corrects for hubness, and recall on heavy-tailed embedding distributions (common in LLM retrieval) is systematically worse than on the Gaussian-like SIFT benchmarks.

**Quantization bias in inner-product search.** PQ codebooks trained to minimize $\ell_2$ reconstruction error are *not* optimal for maximum inner product search: the error in $\langle q, x\rangle$ depends on the query direction, and isotropic distortion analysis underestimates recall loss for the top-ranked (high inner product) items — exactly the items RAG cares about [5]. Anisotropic objectives mitigate but do not eliminate the bias.

**Dynamic workloads.** HNSW supports incremental insertion, but deletion and updates fragment the graph and degrade navigability; production systems periodically rebuild. DiskANN's streaming variant handles inserts, yet the SSD page layout optimized at build time decays under churn [7]. IVF coarse quantizers drift as the data distribution shifts, requiring retraining that invalidates cached PQ codes.

**SSD tail latency.** DiskANN's mean latency is excellent, but each query issues a data-dependent number of random 4 KB reads; under concurrent load, p99 latency is governed by the SSD's queue depth and garbage collection, not the algorithm. Provisioning for p99 at billion scale remains an exercise in systems engineering as much as algorithm design.

**Theoretical gaps.** HNSW's logarithmic complexity is proven only under distributional assumptions that real embedding corpora violate (clustered, heavy-tailed, anisotropic). Vamana's guarantees assume the $\alpha$-pruning invariant holds exactly, which the iterative construction only approximates. A worst-case theory of navigable graphs for adversarial point sets remains open.

**Evaluation realism.** SIFT1B-era benchmarks use descriptors of far lower intrinsic dimensionality than modern 768–4096-d LLM embeddings, and they evaluate static corpora with uniform query workloads. Recall numbers measured on SIFT do not transfer to RAG workloads; the field needs billion-scale benchmarks built from contemporary dense retrievers with realistic query distributions.

## 7 Conclusion

Billion-scale approximate nearest neighbor search is a solved problem in the engineering sense and an open problem in the theoretical sense. HNSW [1] gives the best recall–latency tradeoff when RAM is abundant; IVF-PQ [3][4], with optimized rotations [6] and anisotropic objectives [5], gives the best recall per byte when RAM is the constraint; DiskANN [2] breaks the RAM constraint entirely, serving billion-point indexes from SSD at millisecond latency. The families share a deeper unity: navigate cheaply over a compressed or hierarchical sketch, then re-rank exactly over a short list. For practitioners, the decision reduces to three questions — *how much RAM per node, what recall floor, what p99 latency SLO?* — answered by walking the Pareto frontiers of Section 4.5. The frontier keeps moving, but the trilemma of recall, latency, and memory is structural, and any future index will be judged by how honestly it navigates it.
