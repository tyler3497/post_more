# In-Network Computing with P4 Programmable Data Planes: P4Runtime Control, PSA and PNA Architectures, and Line-Rate Aggregation for Distributed ML Training

## Abstract

Programmable data planes have moved in-network computing from a research curiosity to a production mechanism for latency-critical and throughput-critical workloads. This thesis develops a unified account of P4-programmable forwarding pipelines as general in-network compute substrates. We present the P4_16 language and its two canonical architecture models—the Portable Switch Architecture (PSA) and the Portable NIC Architecture (PNA)—and show how the P4Runtime control protocol decouples control-plane intent from target-specific data-plane binaries through a gRPC-based API anchored on P4Info metadata. We formalize a methodology for mapping arithmetic workloads, in particular distributed gradient aggregation for machine learning, onto the constraints of protocol-independent switch architectures: bounded per-stage memory, atomic register updates, and strict stage-timing budgets. A detailed case study of SwitchML-style in-network Allreduce demonstrates how on-switch addition with worker-count synchronization achieves line-rate aggregation at tens of terabits per second. We analyze empirical results, prove correctness properties of the aggregation protocol, and identify the fundamental limitations of finite on-chip state, single-pass pipelines, and approximate arithmetic on current hardware.

## 1 Introduction

The central abstraction of software-defined networking for two decades was *fixed-function forwarding*: a switch parsed a known set of headers, matched against tables populated by a controller, and executed a fixed repertoire of actions. OpenFlow's header-field set grew from 12 fields in version 1.0 to 41 fields in version 1.4, and each new encapsulation—VXLAN, Geneve, NSH—required a specification revision and a hardware respin [1]. The P4 language, introduced by Bosshart et al., inverted this relationship: instead of the specification enumerating protocols, the data-plane programmer declares headers, parsers, match-action pipelines, and deparsers, and a target-specific compiler maps that description onto hardware [1][2].

Once the parser and pipeline are programmable, a deeper question arises: if the switch can run arbitrary per-packet logic at line rate, why should it be restricted to *forwarding*? The field of **in-network computing** treats the switch as a computational node that executes application logic—aggregation, filtering, key-value caching, consensus acceleration—on traffic in flight.

This thesis makes three contributions. **First**, we present a coherent treatment of the P4_16 programming model together with its two standard architecture models, PSA and PNA, emphasizing the precise points at which programmability meets hardware constraint. **Second**, we analyze P4Runtime, the silicon-independent control-plane API that makes multi-vendor programmable networks operable, and show how its entity model (table entries, action profile members, counters, meters, registers) exposes exactly the state a controller needs to manage in-network compute. **Third**, we develop a formal methodology and case study for the most consequential in-network computing workload to date: *line-rate gradient aggregation for distributed machine learning*, as pioneered by SwitchML [5] and ATP [6], and we prove the correctness of the underlying aggregation protocol.

The argument is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys the language and hardware substrate. Section 3 formalizes the design methodology for in-network compute workloads. Section 4 develops the deep technical core: PSA/PNA semantics, P4Runtime control, and the aggregation protocol. Section 5 presents empirical results and proofs. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From OpenFlow to protocol independence

OpenFlow's design coupled the *control* interface to a *fixed data-plane feature set*. Every new match field required consensus in the specification process, a cycle measured in years while protocol innovation was measured in months [1]. P4 broke this coupling by making the parser a programmer-defined finite-state machine over bit-level header declarations. The language deliberately restricts expressiveness to what hardware can implement: no unbounded loops, no dynamic memory allocation, no floating point in the general case. This restriction is a feature, not a deficiency—it guarantees that any well-formed P4 program compiles to a pipeline with deterministic per-packet timing.

### 2.2 Protocol-Independent Switch Architecture (PISA)

The canonical hardware model is the **Protocol-Independent Switch Architecture (PISA)**: a parser, followed by a sequence of match-action stages, followed by a deparser, with stateful memory (registers, counters, meters) distributed across stages. Each stage operates within a fixed clock budget—typically a few nanoseconds—and can perform one table lookup, a bounded ALU operation, and a stateful read-modify-write. Aggregate throughput scales because *packets*, not instructions, are the unit of parallelism: with a deep pipeline, one packet occupies each stage simultaneously, yielding line-rate processing at tens of terabits per second.

The programming contract is therefore unusual. The programmer writes what appears to be sequential per-packet code, but the compiler spatially distributes it across stages subject to *resource constraints*: TCAM/SRAM capacity, stage count, ALU width, and the atomicity of stateful operations. Any in-network computing workload must be expressed within this contract.

### 2.3 Early in-network computing systems

The first generation of in-network computing systems demonstrated the principle on switches:

- **NetCache** [7] implemented a key-value cache in the data plane, absorbing hot-key reads at line rate.
- **NetChain** provided sub-RTT coordination for strongly consistent storage using in-network sequence numbers.
- **HULA** implemented scalable, congestion-aware load balancing entirely in hardware.
- **SwitchML** [5] and **ATP** [6] moved the field's center of gravity to machine learning, performing gradient aggregation inside the switch fabric.

These systems share a design pattern: identify a commutative, associative, or idempotent operation on the critical path, and relocate it into the pipeline where it executes at line rate with deterministic latency.

## 3 Methodology

We formalize the methodology for designing in-network compute on P4 targets as a sequence of five disciplined steps.

**Step 1: Operation characterization.** The workload's core operation *f* must be expressible as a *streaming, bounded-state* function over packet fields. We classify operations by their algebraic requirements:

| Property | Example workload | Hardware cost |
|---|---|---|
| Associative + commutative | Gradient sum, counters | Single register + ALU |
| Idempotent | Deduplication, caching | Exact-match table + registers |
| Order-dependent | Sequence numbering | Atomic register increment |
| Approximate-tolerant | Sketches, telemetry | Small register arrays |

**Step 2: State budgeting.** On-chip SRAM is the binding constraint, typically tens of megabytes shared across all pipeline state. We require an explicit *state budget*: the number of register cells, their width, and their stage placement. A design that exceeds per-stage SRAM cannot be compiled, regardless of its algorithmic elegance.

**Step 3: Atomicity analysis.** Concurrent packets from different ports may access the same register cell in the same clock cycle. P4's `RegisterAction` (P4_16 v1.2+) provides atomic read-modify-write within a single stage. Any update that spans stages or requires read-then-decide-then-write across packets must be restructured or moved to the control plane.

**Step 4: Control/data partitioning.** Following the P4Runtime model [3][4], we partition the system into (a) a *fast path* in the pipeline executing the per-packet operation, and (b) a *slow path* in the controller handling exceptions, reconfiguration, and policy. The interface between them is precisely the P4Runtime entity set.

**Step 5: Verification.** Because the pipeline executes concurrently across packets and stages, we verify the protocol logic using a combination of symbolic reasoning (Section 5) and behavioral-model simulation (the `simple_switch` / BMv2 reference and target-specific simulators).

This methodology disciplines what is otherwise an ad hoc process of "fitting code into the pipeline," and it is the lens through which we now examine the three pillars of the P4 in-network computing stack.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The P4_16 language and the PSA pipeline model

P4_16 programs are structured around *architecture models*: abstract machine definitions that declare the programmable blocks and the fixed-function glue between them. The **Portable Switch Architecture (PSA)** [4] is the standard model for switches. It defines six programmable blocks—`IngressParser`, `Ingress`, `IngressDeparser`, `EgressParser`, `Egress`, `EgressDeparser`—separated by a fixed traffic manager that handles replication, queuing, and scheduling.

The PSA `Ingress` control is where in-network computing lives. Its signature exposes intrinsic metadata (ingress port, packet length, timestamps) and, crucially, `PacketOut`/`PacketIn` headers for controller communication. A minimal in-network aggregation block in P4_16 looks like this:

```p4
#include <core.p4>
#include <psa.p4>

header agg_h {
    bit<32> worker_id;
    bit<32> chunk_id;
    bit<32> num_values;
    bit<32> values;      // one 32-bit gradient element per packet (simplified)
}

struct metadata_t {
    bit<32> partial_sum;
    bit<32> workers_seen;
}

control Ingress(inout headers_t hdr,
                inout metadata_t meta,
                in    psa_ingress_input_metadata_t  ig_intr_md,
                inout psa_ingress_output_metadata_t ostd) {
    Register<bit<32>, bit<32>>(MAX_CHUNKS) chunk_sum;
    Register<bit<32>, bit<32>>(MAX_CHUNKS) chunk_count;

    RegisterAction<bit<32>, bit<32>, bit<32>>(chunk_sum)
    accumulate = {
        void apply(inout bit<32> reg, out bit<32> rv) {
            reg = reg + hdr.agg.values;   // atomic read-modify-write
            rv = reg;
        }
    };

    apply {
        if (hdr.agg.isValid()) {
            meta.partial_sum = accumulate.execute(hdr.agg.chunk_id);
            // threshold check and multicast handled below
        }
    }
}
```

Three properties of this fragment deserve emphasis. *First*, the addition executes inside a `RegisterAction`, which the architecture guarantees to be atomic with respect to concurrent packets—the hardware serializes accesses to the same cell within the stateful ALU. *Second*, the register index (`chunk_id`) partitions the aggregation state so that many gradient chunks are reduced concurrently, each in its own slot. *Third*, the entire operation consumes exactly one pipeline stage of stateful ALU budget, leaving the remaining stages free for forwarding logic.

> **Theorem:** *Atomic single-stage aggregation.* If all updates to a register cell occur within `RegisterAction` bodies executing in the same pipeline stage, then the final register value equals the sum of all contributing packet values regardless of arrival order or port-level concurrency. *Proof sketch:* the stateful ALU serializes cell accesses into a total order; each access applies the associative operation exactly once; the result is order-independent by commutativity of addition. ■

### 4.2 P4Runtime: silicon-independent control

A programmable data plane is only operable if its control interface is as standardized as its forwarding behavior. **P4Runtime** [3] is that interface: a gRPC- and Protobuf-based API through which a controller manages forwarding state without knowledge of the target's internals. The compiler emits a **P4Info** file—a target-independent description of tables, actions, counters, meters, registers, and their IDs—and the controller programs the switch purely in terms of P4Info entities.

The entity model covers the complete state surface relevant to in-network computing:

1. **Table entries** — match-action rules, including the aggregation dispatch table that steers gradient packets to the compute path.
2. **Action profile members/groups** — for ECMP-style distribution of aggregation traffic across pipeline replicas.
3. **Counter and meter entries** — per-chunk packet counts used by the controller to detect stragglers and lost contributions.
4. **Register entries** — direct read/write access to on-chip aggregation state for initialization, checkpointing, and recovery.
5. **Packet I/O** — `PacketOut` injection and `PacketIn` reception for control-plane-mediated exception paths.
6. **Master arbitration** — a role-based election protocol so multiple controllers can share a switch safely, with write access scoped by device and role.

A controller configures an aggregation deployment through a canonical sequence: *SetForwardingPipelineConfig* pushes the compiled pipeline and P4Info; *Write* RPCs install table entries mapping the aggregation UDP port to the compute action; register *Read* calls poll per-chunk counters at millisecond granularity to drive the reliability protocol.

### 4.3 PNA: in-network computing at the edge

While PSA models the switch, the **Portable NIC Architecture (PNA)** [4] models the SmartNIC—the programmable device sitting between the host and the network. PNA differs from PSA in architecturally significant ways for in-network computing: it exposes host-to-network and network-to-host paths, supports `add_on_miss` table actions (the data plane can request the control plane to insert an entry upon a miss, with the packet held), and includes externs for hardware accelerators such as crypto engines.

PNA matters for distributed ML because the aggregation endpoint need not be a top-of-rack switch. A SmartNIC on each worker host can perform *hierarchical* aggregation: partial sums are computed at the NIC, and only the partial results traverse the fabric to the switch-level aggregator. This two-level reduction halves the bisection-bandwidth consumption of the Allreduce and localizes straggler handling to the host. The PNA `add_on_miss` mechanism is particularly valuable here: the first packet of a new chunk can trigger on-demand installation of the chunk's aggregation entry without a controller round trip in the common case.

| Dimension | PSA (switch) | PNA (SmartNIC) |
|---|---|---|
| Position | Fabric core / ToR | Host edge |
| Pipeline direction | Ingress + egress | Net-to-host, host-to-net |
| Miss handling | Drop / punt | `add_on_miss` with packet hold |
| Natural role in ML | Global Allreduce | Hierarchical pre-aggregation |
| State scale | 10s of MB shared | MBs, per-host |

The two architectures compose: PNA pre-aggregates at the edge, PSA completes the reduction in the fabric, and P4Runtime manages both through the same entity model.

### 4.4 Line-rate gradient aggregation for distributed ML

Distributed data-parallel training synchronizes model replicas through the **Allreduce** collective: each of *W* workers contributes a gradient vector *g_w*, and every worker must obtain the sum *G = Σ_w g_w*. Classical algorithms—ring Allreduce, tree Allreduce—exchange *O(W)* or *O(log W)* messages per worker and consume bandwidth proportional to the model size on every link. **SwitchML** [5] reframed the problem: the switch itself performs the summation, so each worker sends its gradient once and receives the sum once, yielding bandwidth cost independent of *W*.

The protocol operates on *chunks*: the gradient vector is partitioned into chunks of *k* elements (SwitchML uses small *k* to fit the per-stage ALU budget), and each chunk is assigned a *slot* in on-switch register arrays. The packet format carries `(worker_id, chunk_id, num_values, values)`. The switch program implements the following logic per packet:

```p4
// Per-packet aggregation decision (conceptual)
bit<32> seen = count_reg.execute(hdr.agg.chunk_id); // atomic increment
bit<32> sum  = sum_reg.execute(hdr.agg.chunk_id);   // atomic add
if (seen == NUM_WORKERS) {
    hdr.agg.values = sum;                 // attach reduced result
    multicast_to_workers();               // replicate to all W ports
    sum_reg.write(hdr.agg.chunk_id, 0);   // reset slot
    count_reg.write(hdr.agg.chunk_id, 0);
} else {
    mark_to_drop();                       // wait for remaining workers
}
```

Several design decisions are load-bearing. **Quantization:** switch ALUs operate on integers, so gradients are quantized (typically to scaled fixed-point or via lossy low-precision formats) before transmission; the aggregation is exact *in the quantized domain*. **Slot reuse:** the number of slots bounds in-flight chunks, so workers pipeline chunk transmission—a new chunk enters a slot only after the previous occupant completes—which the end-host library manages with a credit scheme. **Loss handling:** because the fast path drops non-terminal packets, a lost contribution stalls its slot; SwitchML's transport adds timeouts and retransmission coordinated through the controller's counter reads, exactly the slow path described in Section 4.2.

**ATP** [6] extends this design to multi-tenant datacenters, adding dynamic slot allocation and fair sharing of the switch's aggregation memory across concurrent jobs, plus gradient-aware scheduling that prioritizes chunks on the training critical path. Together, SwitchML and ATP establish in-network aggregation as the state of the art for communication-bound distributed training.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Performance envelope

The defining empirical claim of in-network aggregation is *worker-count independence*: aggregation throughput does not degrade as workers are added. SwitchML's evaluation on a Tofino-based testbed reported end-to-end training speedups exceeding 2× over NCCL-based ring Allreduce for communication-bound models, with per-worker bandwidth consumption flat across worker counts where ring Allreduce's per-link traffic grows [5]. ATP's multi-tenant evaluation further showed that dynamic slot allocation preserves these gains under contention from concurrent jobs [6].

### 5.2 Correctness of the aggregation protocol

We now prove the core safety and liveness properties of the slot-based aggregation protocol of Section 4.4. Let *W* be the number of workers, *S* the set of slots, and for slot *s* let *c(s)* be the worker counter and *a(s)* the accumulator.

> **Theorem:** *Aggregation safety.* For any slot *s*, the value multicast upon completion equals Σ over exactly one contribution from each of the *W* workers for the chunk assigned to *s*. *Proof.* By the atomicity theorem of Section 4.1, each packet's contribution is added to *a(s)* exactly once, in some total order. The counter *c(s)* is incremented atomically once per packet, so *c(s)* equals the number of contributions. Multicast fires precisely when *c(s) = W*. The transport guarantees at-most-once delivery of distinct contributions per worker per chunk (duplicates carry identical sequence numbers and are filtered before the atomic update), so the *W* counted contributions are exactly one per worker. ■

> **Theorem:** *Slot liveness.* If every worker eventually transmits its contribution for every chunk and the transport retransmits lost packets, every slot eventually completes and is reset. *Proof.* Consider slot *s* assigned chunk *x*. Each worker's contribution for *x* is retransmitted until acknowledged; the controller's counter reads detect any stall and reset wedged slots. Hence *c(s)* reaches *W* in finite time, multicast fires, and the reset writes restore *a(s) = 0, c(s) = 0*. By induction over chunks, all slots complete. ■

> **Theorem:** *Bandwidth optimality.* In-network aggregation exchanges exactly 2 messages per worker per chunk (one contribution, one result), independent of *W*, which is optimal for Allreduce up to constant factors. *Proof.* Each worker must send its gradient (1 message, information-theoretic lower bound) and receive the sum (1 message). No protocol can use fewer. Ring Allreduce uses *2(W−1)* messages per worker; tree Allreduce uses *2log W*. ■

### 5.3 Quantization error bounds

Because switch ALUs lack floating point, gradients undergo quantization *Q* before aggregation. If *Q* is an unbiased stochastic quantizer with variance bounded by *σ²*, then the aggregated estimate *Ĝ = Σ_w Q(g_w)* satisfies *E[Ĝ] = G* and *Var(Ĝ) ≤ W·σ²*. Standard SGD convergence analysis then shows the additional error term decays as *O(1/√T)* with iterations *T*: quantization noise does not change the asymptotic convergence rate [5].

## 6 Limitations

The constraints that make line-rate computing possible are also its boundaries.

1. **Finite on-chip state.** Aggregation memory is tens of megabytes of SRAM shared with forwarding tables. The slot count bounds in-flight chunks; large models with many concurrent jobs exhaust slots, and ATP's dynamic allocation [6] mitigates but does not eliminate this bound. Spilling to off-chip DRAM is architecturally impossible within the per-stage timing budget.

2. **Single-pass pipelines.** A packet traverses each stage once; there are no loops and no revisiting state. Algorithms requiring iteration—multi-round consensus, iterative solvers—must be unrolled across packets or recirculated, and recirculation halves effective throughput per pass.

3. **Restricted arithmetic.** Integer addition, comparison, and bit manipulation are native; multiplication is limited, division and floating point are generally absent. Workloads must be quantized or reformulated, introducing the approximation error analyzed in Section 5.3.

4. **No inter-packet ordering guarantees.** Packets from different ports interleave arbitrarily. Protocols must be designed for arbitrary arrival orders, which rules out naive sequential algorithms and forces the atomic-counter discipline of Section 4.4.

5. **Control-plane latency for exceptions.** The P4Runtime slow path operates at millisecond granularity—three orders of magnitude slower than the data plane. Exception-heavy workloads (frequent misses, stragglers) collapse to control-plane speed; in-network computing is therefore suited to *common-case* acceleration, not arbitrary computation.

6. **Debuggability and verification.** Concurrent, distributed, timing-sensitive pipeline logic is notoriously hard to test. Behavioral models diverge from hardware timing, and production bugs manifest only under specific interleavings. This remains an open tooling problem.

## 7 Conclusion

P4 programmable data planes, governed by the PSA and PNA architecture models and operated through the P4Runtime control API, constitute a mature substrate for in-network computing. The case study of line-rate gradient aggregation shows the methodology's power: by quantizing gradients, partitioning them into slots, and exploiting atomic single-stage accumulation with worker-count synchronization, SwitchML-style systems achieve worker-count-independent Allreduce at multi-terabit rates with provable safety, liveness, and bandwidth optimality.

The limitations are fundamental rather than incidental: finite SRAM, single-pass pipelines, integer-only arithmetic, and millisecond-scale control loops bound what the fabric can compute. The research frontier therefore lies not in pretending the switch is a general-purpose processor, but in identifying the *algebraically simple, communication-bound* operations—reductions, sketches, filters, caches—where these constraints are irrelevant and the performance advantage is decisive. As programmable NICs (PNA) and switches (PSA) converge under unified P4Runtime management, hierarchical in-network computing—edge pre-aggregation composed with fabric-level reduction—is poised to become the default communication architecture for large-scale distributed machine learning.
