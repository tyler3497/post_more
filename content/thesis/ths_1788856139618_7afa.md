# io_uring and the Redesign of the Kernel–User I/O Boundary: Submission/Completion Rings, Fixed Resources, Zero-Copy Networking, and Command Passthrough ## Abstract The Linux `io_uring` interface, introduced in kernel 5.1 (2019) by Jens Axboe, replaces the syscall-per-operation contract of classical Unix I/O with a **shared-memory completion-queue protocol**: user space and the kernel communicate through two lock-free ring buffers — a submission queue (SQ) and a completion queue (CQ) — with submission and completion decoupled across batched, often syscall-free transitions [3][7]. This paper analyzes io_uring as a redesign of the kernel–user boundary rather than merely a faster `read()`. We formalize the S/C ring protocol and its memory-ordering contract, show how registered buffers, registered files, and provided-buffer rings eliminate mapping and lookup overheads, and compare the polling modes `IOPOLL` and `SQPOLL`. We then examine zero-copy networking (`MSG_ZEROCOPY`, `IORING_OP_SEND_ZC`, multishot receive) and the generic `io_uring_cmd` passthrough used by the NVMe driver, and close with the enlarged security surface (LSM/audit hook gaps, seccomp considerations) [5]. Evidence comes from peer-reviewed and preprint studies, including a PVLDB 2026 analysis reporting up to 14% gains in PostgreSQL's io_uring integration [1], and documented production benchmarks [9][10]. --- ## 1. Introduction Every classical Unix I/O interface — blocking syscalls, `select`/`poll`/`epoll` readiness notification, POSIX AIO and the older Linux AIO (`libaio`) — preserves one architectural constant: **the kernel–user boundary is crossed by an explicit trap per unit of work**, and arguments cross that boundary by copy. An application that wants to know *whether* an operation *can* proceed (readiness) issues `epoll_wait`; to *do* it, it issues `read`; to learn the outcome it reads the return value. Two syscalls, one copy to kernel, one copy back — per operation, in the best case. When NVMe devices deliver millions of IOPS at single-digit microsecond latencies [1], this constant dominates. `io_uring` attacks the constant itself. Instead of trapping into the kernel to *describe* work, the application describes work in shared memory the kernel already sees; instead of trapping to *collect* results, it reads results from shared memory the kernel already wrote. The submission queue (SQ) and completion queue (CQ) are mmap'd ring buffers, and the entire protocol — submit, issue, complete — is a pair of single-producer/single-consumer (SPSC) queues with a memory-ordering contract in place of locks [3][7]. The central claim of this paper is that io_uring is best understood not as "async I/O, but faster" but as a **re-axiomatization of the kernel–user boundary** along four axes: 1. **Protocol**: the S/C ring replaces trap-and-copy with shared-memory message passing, batching arbitrarily many operations into one `io_uring_enter` — or zero, under polling modes. 2. **Resource binding**: registered buffers and files move expensive setup (page pinning, `fget`) out of the hot path, making each operation a cheap index lookup. 3. **Data path**: zero-copy send/receive, provided buffers, and multishot operations restructure networking so the kernel, not user space, drives the event loop. 4. **Generality**: `io_uring_cmd` turns the ring into a generic asynchronous command bus for drivers (NVMe passthrough first), not merely an I/O interface [4][8]. Section 2 recalls why earlier interfaces failed to scale; Section 3 states our method; Section 4 develops the four axes in depth; Section 5 assembles the empirical record; Section 6 is honest about limitations and the security surface; Section 7 concludes. --- ## 2. Background ### 2.1 The interfaces that came before **Linux AIO (`libaio`).** The `io_submit`/`io_getevents` API of kernel 2.5+ restricted true kernel-side asynchrony to `O_DIRECT` block I/O, copied `iocb` structures across the boundary per batch, and suffered from a completion model that scaled poorly. Its adoption remained confined to databases willing to pay its complexity tax [3]. **POSIX AIO (`aio_read` et al.).** Implemented in glibc with user-space threads, it is asynchronous only in the way a thread pool is — every operation still pays full syscall and copy costs. **Readiness: `epoll`.** The `epoll` family solved the *scalability of waiting* (O(1) readiness for N descriptors) but preserved the trap-per-operation structure: `epoll_wait` tells you a socket *can* be read; `recv` performs the read. The latency anatomy of one network operation is therefore two mandatory kernel crossings plus two data copies [10]: > **Theorem (readiness floor).** Under any readiness-based interface, completing one I/O operation requires at least two user→kernel transitions (notify, then execute) and one kernel→user data copy. The per-operation latency is lower-bounded by 2·*T_trap* + *T_copy* + *T_io*. `io_uring` is a *completion-based* interface: one submits a description, and the result materializes in the CQ. The trap and the copy are amortized or eliminated, not merely reduced. ### 2.2 The 2019 inflection Axboe's design notes and the LWN coverage of the 5.1 merge describe the goals explicitly: broad operation coverage (any syscall as an opcode, not just block I/O), efficiency, low latency, scalability [3]. The initial opcode set — `NOP`, `READV`/`WRITEV`, `READ_FIXED`/`WRITE_FIXED`, `FSYNC`, `POLL_ADD`/`POLL_REMOVE` — was deliberately small; the ring protocol, not the opcode list, was the contribution. What followed (5.2–6.x) was an expansion of *what* the ring can carry: `SENDMSG`/`RECVMSG` (5.3), timeouts, `OPENAT`/`STATX` (5.6), provided buffers (5.7), multishot accept/recv (5.19), direct descriptors and `SEND_ZC` (6.0/6.1), `io_uring_cmd` passthrough (5.19) [3][8]. A feature matrix of the kind maintained by downstream projects is effectively a kernel-version compatibility table, and applications must probe capabilities at runtime [11]. | Kernel | Milestone | Relevance to this paper | |---|---|---| | 5.1 | `io_uring` introduced (SQ/CQ model) | Baseline ring protocol | | 5.3 | `IORING_OP_SENDMSG`/`RECVMSG` | Networking enters the ring | | 5.7 | Provided buffers (`IOSQE_BUFFER_SELECT`) | Kernel-side buffer selection | | 5.11 | Registered buffers/files matured | Fixed-resource fast path | | 5.19 | Multishot accept/recv; `io_uring_cmd` | Kernel-driven networking; NVMe passthrough | | 6.0/6.1 | Direct descriptors; `IORING_OP_SEND_ZC` | Zero-syscall submission; zero-copy send | | 6.15+ | Zero-copy receive (`recv_zc`) | Zero-copy data path completed | --- ## 3. Methodology This is an analytical and synthetic study, not a new experiment. Our method has three legs: 1. **Protocol reconstruction** from primary sources: the `io_uring(7)` and `io_uring_setup(2)` man pages [7], LWN's contemporaneous design coverage [3], and the uring_cmd/NVMe patch series on the kernel mailing lists [4][8]. Where the paper states a kernel-internal behavior (e.g., head/tail ordering), it is the documented contract, cross-checked against independent kernel-internals write-ups. 2. **Formal sketching**: we express the ring's visibility invariant in TLA+ to make the memory-ordering contract precise. The sketch is illustrative, not machine-checked; its purpose is to pin down *why* the protocol is correct rather than to prove the kernel. 3. **Empirical synthesis**: all quantitative claims are attributed to published studies and documented benchmarks: the PVLDB 2026 DBMS study by Jasny et al. [1], the complexity analysis of Pestka et al. [2], the fkvs project's io_uring findings (zero-copy thresholds, batch-latency tables) [9], and the ferrings and powpow open benchmark reports [10]. Numbers are reported with their provenance and should be read as existence proofs of attainable regimes, not as universal constants — the hardware, kernel version, and workload dependence is itself a finding [1][2]. Scope: we analyze mainline Linux `io_uring` as of the 6.x series. `liburing` is treated as the reference user-space binding. --- ## 4. Deep Dive ### 4.1 The Submission/Completion Ring Protocol **Shared-memory topology.** `io_uring_setup(entries, params)` creates an anonymous file descriptor identifying the ring context and returns, via `params`, the offsets needed to `mmap` three regions: the SQ ring (indices), the CQ ring (completion entries), and the SQE array itself [7]. The SQ is an *indirect* ring: `sq_array` maps submission order to slots in the SQE array, so the kernel consumes entries in submission order even though the application may fill SQE slots out of order. Each SQE is 64 bytes (or 128 with `IORING_SETUP_SQE128`); each CQE is 16 bytes (32 with `IORING_SETUP_CQE32`). **The protocol in code.** The canonical submission loop, eliding error handling, is: ```c /* liburing-style submission: user space is the SQ producer */ struct io_uring_sqe *sqe = io_uring_get_sqe(&ring); io_uring_prep_read(sqe, fd, buf, nbytes, offset); sqe->user_data = 0x42; /* opaque tag, echoed in the CQE */ io_uring_submit(&ring); /* bump sq_tail, maybe io_uring_enter */ struct io_uring_cqe *cqe; io_uring_wait_cqe(&ring, &cqe); /* consume from CQ */ ssize_t res = cqe->res; /* the syscall's return value, or -errno */ io_uring_cqe_seen(&ring, cqe); /* advance cq_head */ ``` The kernel side mirrors it: it drains SQEs between `sq_head` and `sq_tail`, dispatches each to the opcode handler (inline for non-blocking fast paths, or via the `io-wq` thread pool when the operation would block), and posts CQEs by advancing `cq_tail`. User space owns `sq_tail`/`cq_head`; the kernel owns `sq_head`/`cq_tail`. This is the entire concurrency control — there are no locks on the rings. **The ordering contract.** Correctness rests on a single memory-ordering rule, which we state formally: > **Theorem (Ring visibility).** Let *S* be the store of an SQE's fields and *T* the store publishing `sq_tail`. The kernel observes a *coherent* SQE at index *i* iff *S* is *sequenced-before* *T* under release semantics, i.e. `smp_store_release(&sq_tail, tail+1)` after all SQE stores, and the kernel reads `sq_tail` with `smp_load_acquire`. Dually, a CQE is observable by user space iff the kernel publishes `cq_tail` with release semantics after writing the CQE, and user space reads it with acquire semantics. In TLA+ the invariant is a two-variable mutual-exclusion property over the four indices: ```tla ---- MODULE IoUringRing ---- EXTENDS Naturals CONSTANT N \* ring capacity, power of two VARIABLES sq_head, sq_tail, cq_head, cq_tail TypeOK == /\ sq_head \in 0..N /\ sq_tail \in 0..N /\ cq_head \in 0..N /\ cq_tail \in 0..N \* Single-producer/single-consumer: each side only advances its own indices SQInvariant == sq_head <= sq_tail /\ sq_tail - sq_head <= N CQInvariant == cq_head <= cq_tail /\ cq_tail - cq_head <= N \* Visibility: kernel consumes only published SQEs; user consumes only published CQEs ConsumeOK == [](SQInvariant /\ CQInvariant) ==== ``` The practical consequence: on weakly-ordered architectures (ARM, POWER, RISC-V) the barriers are load-bearing, and `liburing`'s helpers encode them. Bugs here are silent data corruption, not crashes — which is why the protocol deserves a specification, not just an implementation [7]. **Submission batching and the wakeup flag.** `io_uring_enter(fd, to_submit, min_complete, flags)` is the single remaining syscall: it tells the kernel how many new SQEs exist and optionally waits for completions. With `IORING_SETUP_SQPOLL` the kernel polls the SQ itself and the syscall can vanish entirely (Section 4.3). The `IORING_SQ_NEED_WAKEUP` flag in the shared `sq_flags` word solves the sleep/wake race: user space sets it by reading the flag before deciding whether to call `io_uring_enter`, so the SQPOLL thread is never left sleeping on a non-empty queue [7]. **CQ overflow.** If the kernel produces completions faster than user space consumes them and the CQ ring fills, it sets `IORING_SQ_CQ_OVERFLOW` and spills the count into a side counter visible via `fdinfo`. Overflowed completions are not lost — they are queued internally and re-posted as CQ space frees — but overflow is the canonical backpressure signal that the application's reap loop is undersized [7]. --- ### 4.2 Registered Buffers, Registered Files, Provided Buffers, and Multishot Operations The ring protocol removes the *trap*; the resource-registration machinery removes the *per-operation setup*. Three mechanisms, in increasing sophistication: **Registered buffers (`IORING_REGISTER_BUFFERS`).** Each `read`/`write` on a normal buffer forces the kernel to pin user pages (`get_user_pages`) and build a scatter-gather mapping — work proportional to buffer size, repeated per operation. Registering a buffer set once pins the pages and pre-builds the mappings; `IORING_OP_READ_FIXED`/`WRITE_FIXED` then reference a buffer by *index*. The per-operation cost collapses from page-pinning to an array lookup. The trade is explicit lifetime management: a registered buffer must not be unregistered or reused while an operation referencing it is in flight, a discipline the application (not the kernel) enforces [1][7]. **Registered files (`IORING_REGISTER_FILES`).** Symmetrically, each operation on a normal fd pays `fget`/`fput` — atomic refcount traffic on a shared `struct file`, a notorious cache-line ping-pong under multithreaded submission. Registered files snapshot the file table once; operations index into it. Kernel 6.0's *direct descriptors* go further, allocating fds in a per-ring table that bypasses the process file-table locks entirely [3]. **Provided buffers and buffer selection.** The inversion of control comes with `IOSQE_BUFFER_SELECT`: instead of the application choosing a buffer for each `recv`, it registers a *pool* (a buffer ring, `IORING_REGISTER_PBUF_RING`, since 5.7/5.13 depending on API flavor), and the *kernel* picks a buffer per completion, reporting the chosen buffer's id in the CQE's `flags` (`IORING_CQE_F_BUFFER` plus the buffer id in the upper bits). The application replenishes the pool as buffers are consumed. This eliminates the application's per-recv buffer-management decisions and, combined with the next mechanism, removes the re-arm loop. **Multishot accept/recv.** `IORING_OP_ACCEPT` with `IORING_ACCEPT_MULTISHOT` and `IORING_OP_RECV`/`RECVMSG` with `IORING_RECV_MULTISHOT` (5.19+) arm a *persistent* SQE: one submission generates one CQE per accepted connection or received datagram/segment, until error or explicit cancel. The classical accept loop — `epoll_wait`, `accept`, re-arm — becomes a single SQE that the kernel re-arms internally. Measured against libuv/epoll stacks, the multishot + provided-buffer path roughly halves syscalls per connection (5.9 vs 11.7 in one documented HTTP comparison) while doubling throughput [10]. The composition is the point: *multishot recv + provided buffers + registered files* is a networking data path in which the kernel accepts connections, selects buffers, and posts completions with **zero syscalls and zero per-operation user-space decisions** in steady state. The application becomes a CQE consumer — a pure completion-driven event loop. ### 4.3 IOPOLL and SQPOLL: Removing the Kernel Thread from (or into) the Hot Path Two setup flags push the design to its logical extremes. **`IORING_SETUP_IOPOLL`.** For `O_DIRECT` block I/O on devices supporting polling, the application busy-waits on the CQ ring via `io_uring_enter` with `IORING_ENTER_GETEVENTS`, and the kernel *polls the device* for completions instead of taking interrupts. Submission, polling, and completion all occur in the application's own thread: no IRQ, no `io-wq` worker, no context switch attributable to I/O. The cost is literal — a spinning CPU — and the restriction is real: only pollable opcodes on `O_DIRECT` fds are allowed [7]. Hybrid polling (`IORING_SETUP_HYBRID_IOPOLL`, newer kernels) spins briefly then sleeps, recovering most of the latency win at a fraction of the CPU cost [1]. **`IORING_SETUP_SQPOLL`.** The mirror image: a kernel thread polls the *submission* queue, so the application never calls `io_uring_enter` to submit — it only writes SQEs and bumps `sq_tail`. Combined with CQ polling, the steady state is **zero syscalls per I/O**. The price is a dedicated (usually pinned, via `IORING_SETUP_SQ_AFF`) kernel thread that must be woken when it sleeps (`sq_thread_idle` bounds the sleep; the `NEED_WAKEUP` protocol of Section 4.1 avoids lost wakeups), and the privilege to create one requires `CAP_SYS_NICE` in many configurations — a deliberate gate, since an unprivileged user burning a kernel thread per ring is a trivial DoS vector [7]. > **Theorem (Zero-syscall steady state).** A ring configured with `SQPOLL`, with completions reaped by CQ polling, performs submission and completion with zero traps in steady state, provided (a) the SQ never empties while the poller sleeps without the wakeup protocol firing, and (b) no operation requires blocking resources unavailable to the poller. The per-operation cost is then bounded below by ring-pointer traffic plus the I/O itself. The empirical guidance is consistent across studies: polling wins when the device is fast enough that interrupt and scheduling latency dominate — NVMe with sub-10µs media — and loses (on efficiency) when the CPU could have slept [1][9]. ### 4.4 Zero-Copy Networking: `MSG_ZEROCOPY`, `IORING_OP_SEND_ZC`, and the Receive Side The ring eliminates control-path copies; the data path needs its own treatment. Linux's `MSG_ZEROCOPY` (since 4.14) lets `sendmsg` hand user pages to the NIC via DMA, with completion signaled out-of-band through the socket's error queue (`SO_EE_ORIGIN_ZEROCOPY`). io_uring absorbs this awkward API into the ring: `IORING_OP_SEND_ZC` (6.0+) performs a zero-copy send whose lifecycle is expressed as **two CQEs** — one carrying the send result, and a later *notification* CQE flagged `IORING_CQE_F_NOTIF` that releases the buffer. The application must not reuse or free the buffer until the notification arrives; the two-CQE contract makes the lifetime rule machine-checkable in the event loop rather than a matter of polling `recvmsg(MSG_ERRQUEUE)` [9][11]. The measured crossover is instructive: below ~1 KB, zero-copy send *loses* to plain sends because pinning and completion accounting outweigh the avoided copy; above ~1 KB, registered-buffer zero-copy achieves up to ~3.5× fewer cycles per byte. The fkvs project's findings distill this into a deployment rule — zero-copy for bulk flush paths (tens of KB), multishot recv for small frames [9]. On the receive side, the combination of multishot `recv` with provided buffers is already effectively zero-copy *of control* (no per-recv syscall, no user-space buffer selection), and true zero-copy receive (`io_uring_prep_recv_zc`, kernel 6.15+, requiring NIC header-splitting support) extends DMA directly into provided buffers — the last copy in the path, between the NIC descriptor ring and the application buffer, disappears for large receives [11]. The trajectory is clear: each kernel generation moves one more copy from software into hardware or into the registration phase. A Rust-flavored sketch of the send_zc discipline shows how the two-CQE contract shapes application code: ```rust // Conceptual: buffer lifetime tied to the NOTIF cqe, enforced by the event loop fn on_cqe(cqe: &Cqe, bufs: &mut Slab<Buffer>) { let tag = cqe.user_data; if cqe.flags & IORING_CQE_F_NOTIF != 0 { bufs.free(tag); // DMA done: buffer reusable } else if cqe.res < 0 { bufs.free(tag); // send failed: also terminal metrics::send_failed(cqe.res); } else { pending_notif.insert(tag); // result known; await release } } ``` ### 4.5 `io_uring_cmd`: The Ring as a Generic Command Bus, and the Security Surface **Passthrough design.** Some operations are irreducibly device-specific — `ioctl`'s eternal dumping ground. `io_uring_cmd` (5.19, `IORING_OP_URING_CMD`) gives any file operation a `->uring_cmd()` hook invoked with the SQE's inline command payload: 16 bytes in a standard 64-byte SQE, or 80 bytes with `IORING_SETUP_SQE128`; results return via the CQE, with `IORING_SETUP_CQE32` providing a second 16-byte result word for commands (like NVMe passthrough) that need two [4][8]. The io_uring layer performs *no interpretation* of the payload — it is a transport for someone else's command set, with async dispatch, batching, and polling inherited for free. The NVMe driver was the first provider (`NVME_URING_CMD_IO` over `/dev/ngXnY`), and measured passthrough IOPS exceed the equivalent `READ`/`WRITE` opcode path because the translation layers are skipped [8]. The deeper significance: `uring_cmd` plus the ring protocol is a **general asynchronous execution substrate** for Linux — any subsystem can expose any command set through the same completion-queue discipline. The BPF-extended event loop (programs that enqueue work in response to completions without returning to user space [6]) pushes the same idea one step further: the kernel–user boundary becomes programmable. **The security surface.** Generality has a price, and io_uring has paid it publicly. Three episodes define the problem: 1. **Missing LSM/audit hooks.** Early io_uring bypassed Linux Security Module and auditing hooks entirely — operations that would have been mediated via the syscall path executed outside any security module's view. The hooks were retrofitted after the fact, a case study in how a new abstraction layer can silently void a system's reference-monitor assumptions [5]. 2. **`uring_cmd` as an ioctl amplifier.** Passing opaque, uninterrupted commands to drivers re-creates `ioctl`'s attack surface with a faster, more concurrent delivery vehicle. The LWN security discussion of `uring_cmd` notes the ease with which requirements "can be overlooked in a complex system with no overall supervision" [5]. 3. **Privilege-adjacent features.** SQPOLL kernel threads, registered-buffer page pinning (memory-lock limits, `RLIMIT_MEMLOCK`), and `IOPOLL`'s CPU burning are all resource-amplification primitives in the wrong hands [7]. The hardening posture that follows is now standard guidance: sandbox rings with seccomp (allowlist the three io_uring syscalls only where needed), treat `io_uring_setup` as a privileged-adjacent operation for untrusted tenants, keep kernels current (the hook coverage improved markedly after 5.19–6.1), and prefer registered/direct resources — which are *easier* to audit, since the resource set is fixed at registration time — over per-operation fds [5][7]. Several container runtimes and distributions have at various points restricted or filtered io_uring for untrusted workloads; the interface's power and its risk are the same property viewed from opposite sides. --- ## 5. Empirical Evidence and Proofs ### 5.1 The cost model, then the measurements The analytical cost model is simple. Let an operation under the classical path cost 2·*T_trap* + *T_setup* (fd lookup, page pin) + *T_copy* + *T_io*. io_uring with batching of *B* operations, fixed resources, and polling drives this toward *T_io* + (*T_ring* + *T_cqe*)/*B* + amortized *T_setup*. The model predicts: (a) gains concentrate where *T_io* is small (fast NVMe, loopback networking) and *B* is large; (b) naive opcode-for-syscall substitution without batching or registration yields little; (c) polling trades CPU for the last microseconds. The published record confirms all three [1][2][9]. ### 5.2 What the studies report **Databases (Jasny et al., PVLDB 2026).** The most rigorous recent study integrates io_uring into a storage engine's buffer manager and a distributed analytics shuffle path on 400 Gb/s networks. Findings: registered buffers and passthrough I/O move end-to-end performance; naive substitution does not; and applying the derived guidelines to PostgreSQL's io_uring backend yields a **14% throughput improvement** over its baseline io_uring implementation.
