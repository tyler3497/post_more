# Progressive Lowering from Tensor Programs to Accelerators: MLIR Dialect Definitions, the Affine and Linalg Abstractions, and Hardware-Targeted Compilation

## Abstract

Domain-specific compilers for machine learning have historically been built as monolithic systems, each re-implementing lexing, SSA construction, optimization, and code generation for a single hardware target. MLIR (Multi-Level Intermediate Representation) replaces this fragmentation with a uniform, extensible SSA-based infrastructure in which computation is expressed at multiple levels of abstraction simultaneously and progressively lowered through composable dialects [1]. This thesis develops a formal account of MLIR's core mechanisms — operation, region, and dialect definitions via the Operation Definition Specification (ODS) — and analyzes two dialects that carry the bulk of numerical compilation: the affine dialect, which embeds the polyhedral model with affine maps and integer sets [3], and the linalg dialect, which captures structured tensor operations with iteration-domain semantics [2]. We trace the canonical lowering path from high-level tensor programs through tiling, vectorization, and bufferization down to GPU and accelerator dialects, as realized in production systems such as IREE [6] and Triton [4], and characterize the algebraic invariants that make progressive lowering both expressive and tractable.

---

## 1. Introduction

The end of Dennard scaling and the collapse of general-purpose performance improvements have shifted the burden of acceleration onto domain-specific hardware: GPUs, TPUs, and an expanding zoo of neural-processing units. Each accelerator arrives with its own programming model, memory hierarchy, and preferred data layout. A conventional compiler stack — one fixed intermediate representation, one lowering pipeline — cannot absorb this diversity without heroic, per-target engineering. The result has been *software fragmentation*: every ML framework maintains its own IR, its own fusion heuristics, and its own code generator, duplicating decades of compiler research [1].

MLIR attacks fragmentation not with another single IR but with an **infrastructure for defining IRs**. Abstraction level is a property of the *dialect* — a modular unit bundling operations, types, attributes, and semantics — not of the compiler. A single module may mix `tensor`, `linalg`, `affine`, `scf`, `memref`, `vector`, `gpu`, and `llvm` ops, with passes rewriting fragments from high- to low-level dialects one region at a time. This is **progressive lowering**: never commit prematurely to one abstraction; each step preserves the semantic information the remaining transformations need.

This thesis is organized around four contributions:

1. A precise exposition of MLIR's core data model — operations, values, regions, and the ODS framework for declarative dialect definition — grounding the discussion in the official language reference [5].
2. A technical treatment of the **affine dialect**, showing how it internalizes the polyhedral model (affine maps, integer sets, dependence analysis via isl-style machinery [8]) and enables provably legal loop transformations.
3. A technical treatment of the **linalg dialect**, whose structured ops encode iteration-domain semantics declaratively, and the composable lowering path — tiling, fusion, bufferization, vectorization — developed in the structured-code-generation line of work [2].
4. An analysis of hardware-targeted lowering to GPUs and accelerators, instantiated by IREE's end-to-end flow [6], Triton's tile-based GPU compilation [4], and the OpenXLA ecosystem.

Throughout, we emphasize the *invariants* that make the approach sound: SSA dominance, region isolation, affine restrictions, and destination-passing-style bufferization.

> **Thesis statement:** Multi-level compilation succeeds precisely when each level of abstraction carries machine-checkable invariants — affine bounds, structured iteration domains, explicit memory effects — that let the next lowering stage reason *locally* about correctness while acting *globally* on performance.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The fragmentation of ML compilers

Before MLIR, the landscape of tensor compilers was a archipelago of mutually incompatible systems: TensorFlow's XLA with its HLO dialect, TVM with Relay and Tensor Expression, Glow, nGraph, PlaidML, and Halide-derived pipelines. Each implemented its own graph representation, its own loop nest IR, and its own backend, re-solving the same scheduling, fusion, and code-generation problems. Lattner et al. quantify this duplication as the primary motivation for MLIR [1]: the cost of building a domain-specific compiler should be dominated by domain knowledge, not by compiler infrastructure.

### 2.2 The polyhedral lineage

The affine dialect is the direct descendant of the polyhedral compilation tradition. Bondhugula et al. demonstrated with Pluto that automatic parallelization and locality optimization of affine loop nests could be driven end-to-end by integer linear programming over dependence polyhedra [3]. The underlying mathematics — parametric integer sets, affine scheduling, code generation from unions of polyhedra — is implemented in libraries such as **isl** (integer set library) [8]. MLIR's contribution is to make these concepts *first-class IR citizens*: `affine.for`, `affine.load`, and `affine.apply` carry their bounds and access functions as `AffineMap` attributes, so every downstream pass can recover the polyhedral description without re-analysis.

### 2.3 Structured tensor compilation

The linalg dialect's intellectual lineage runs through Tensor Comprehensions and, most directly, the structured and retargetable code-generation framework of Vasilache et al. [2]. Where the polyhedral model discovers structure by analysis, linalg *declares* it: every named op (e.g., `linalg.matmul`) specifies its indexing maps and iterator types, so tiling, fusion, and vectorization become mechanical rewrites over a small algebraic core. This declarative posture is what makes linalg the preferred substrate for the middle of the lowering stack.

### 2.4 Tile-based GPU compilation

Triton [4] occupies a complementary point in the design space: a tile-centric language and compiler that lowers tiled neural-network computations to GPU code rivaling cuBLAS/cuDNN. Its block-level programming model anticipates the `vector` and `gpu` dialect abstractions, and its parametric tiles prefigure linalg's tile-and-fuse pipelines.

### 2.5 Production deployments

Two systems demonstrate MLIR at production scale. **IREE** [6] compiles ML models through a full MLIR pipeline — from `linalg` on tensors down to `hal`/`vm` executables — targeting CPUs, GPUs, and accelerators with a single compiler binary. **OpenXLA** continues the XLA lineage on MLIR-based infrastructure. Both validate the thesis that one infrastructure can span the abstraction gap from framework graphs to machine code.

## 3. Methodology

Our method is *constructive and invariant-driven*: we reconstruct the lowering stack from first principles, identifying at each level the invariant the next transformation depends on — structural well-formedness at the core, declarative semantics in ODS, decidable dependences in the affine dialect, type-preserving rewrites in linalg, and explicit address spaces and execution scopes at the hardware boundary. We ground each claim in the language reference [5] and the primary literature [1][2][3][4], exhibiting concrete IR fragments to make the mechanisms tangible.

## 4. Deep Technical Dive

### 4.1 MLIR Core: Operations, Values, Regions, and Dialects

Every MLIR program is a hierarchy of **operations**. An operation has a name (`"linalg.matmul"`), operands and results (SSA **values**), attributes (compile-time constants), and zero or more **regions** — themselves sequences of basic blocks containing further operations. Unlike LLVM IR, which fixes the abstraction level of its instruction set, MLIR fixes only the *container*: the meaning of an op is supplied by its **dialect**.

```python
# Conceptual sketch of MLIR's core data model (not executable MLIR)
class Operation:
    name: str                 # e.g. "affine.for"
    operands: list[Value]     # SSA operands
    results: list[Value]      # SSA results, each with exactly one def
    attributes: dict          # e.g. {"lower_bound": AffineMap(...)}
    regions: list[Region]     # nested CFGs; enable multi-level nesting

def verify(op: Operation) -> bool:
    # Structural checks (SSA dominance, types), then the dialect verifier.
    ...
```

Three design decisions deserve emphasis. **Regions** let high-level ops *contain* their own lowering substrate — a `linalg.generic` holds its scalar body, an `scf.for` its loop body, a `gpu.launch` its kernel. **Attributes** such as `AffineMap` and `IntegerSet` are typed, verifiable data that analyses consume directly rather than strings or comments. **Dialects** are the unit of reuse: nothing prevents a hardware vendor from defining an accelerator dialect and plugging it into the same pass manager — the decisive factor in industry adoption [1].

> **Theorem (Dialect composition):** If every op satisfies its dialect verifier and each lowering pass preserves the verifiers of dialects it does not rewrite, the module remains verifiable after any pass pipeline — verification is per-op and compositional. ∎

### 4.2 Dialect Definition via ODS: Declarative Compiler Construction

Writing a dialect by hand — parser, printer, verifier, C++ builder APIs — is the historical tax that fragmented the ecosystem. MLIR's **Operation Definition Specification (ODS)**, built on LLVM TableGen, inverts the default: the dialect author writes a *declaration*, and the framework generates the machinery.

A representative ODS fragment for a hypothetical structured op illustrates the pattern:

```tablegen
def MatMulOp : LinalgStructuredOp<"matmul"> {
  let indexingMaps = [
    AffineMap<(m, n, k) -> (m, k)>,   // lhs:  (m,k)
    AffineMap<(m, n, k) -> (k, n)>,   // rhs:  (k,n)
    AffineMap<(m, n, k) -> (m, n)>    // out:  (m,n)
  ];
  let iteratorTypes = ["parallel", "parallel", "reduction"];
  let hasCanonicalizer = 1;
}
```

From this declaration, TableGen derives the parser and printer (the textual form `linalg.matmul ins(...) outs(...)` round-trips exactly), the verifier (rank/arity consistency of indexing maps against operand types becomes a compile error, not a miscompilation), and builder APIs plus documentation from the same source of truth, eliminating documentation drift [5].

The methodological payoff: *semantics become data*. Because the iteration domain of `linalg.matmul` is recoverable from its ODS-declared indexing maps, the tiling pass need not understand matrix multiplication — only indexing maps. Domain knowledge lives in the dialect definition; transformation logic in generic passes. That separation is the engine of progressive lowering.

### 4.3 The Affine Dialect: The Polyhedral Model as IR

The **affine dialect** restricts loop bounds, conditionals, and memory accesses to *affine functions* of enclosing loop indices and symbolic constants. An affine map has the form:

$$f(i_1, \dots, i_n)[s_1, \dots, s_m] \mapsto A\mathbf{i} + B\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{c}, \quad A \in \mathbb{Z}^{d \times n}, B \in \mathbb{Z}^{d \times m}$$

This restriction is deliberately severe, and deliberately powerful: with it, every data dependence becomes a Presburger-definable relation, and legality questions — *"may these two loop iterations be reordered?"* — become integer linear feasibility problems solvable by isl [8].

Consider matrix multiplication in the affine dialect:

```mlir
affine.for %i = 0 to %M {
  affine.for %j = 0 to %N {
    affine.for %k = 0 to %K {
      %a = affine.load %A[%i, %k] : memref<?x?xf32>
      %b = affine.load %B[%k, %j] : memref<?x?xf32>
      %c = affine.load %C[%i, %j] : memref<?x?xf32>
      %p = arith.mulf %a, %b : f32
      %s = arith.addf %c, %p : f32
      affine.store %s, %C[%i, %j] : memref<?x?xf32>
    }
  }
}
```

Because the subscripts are affine, the compiler computes the exact dependence polyhedron between the store to `C[%i,%j]` and subsequent loads, and therefore proves that **tiling** the `%i`/`%j` loops preserves semantics — no runtime alias analysis required. This is the Pluto result [3] internalized as IR: `-affine-loop-tile`, `-affine-loop-fusion`, and `-affine-parallelize` are mechanical rewrites whose legality rests on the affine invariant. `affine.if` with `IntegerSet` conditions additionally models non-rectangular domains (triangular solves, wavefronts) that rectangular-only IRs cannot represent without over-approximation.

> **Theorem (Affine schedule legality):** Let $S$ be a statement in an affine loop nest with dependence polyhedron $D_S \subseteq \mathbb{Z}^{2n}$. An affine schedule $\Theta(\mathbf{i}) = T\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{t}$ is semantics-preserving iff $T \cdot \mathbf{d} \succ_{lex} 0$ for every non-zero dependence distance $\mathbf{d} \in D_S - D_S$. *This is the classical Feautrier condition; in MLIR it is discharged by isl-backed utilities over the maps attached to affine ops [8].* ∎

The consequence: transformations that are heuristic guesses in general-purpose IRs become verified rewrites in the affine dialect, because the IR refuses to express what it cannot analyze.

### 4.4 The Linalg Dialect: Structured Ops and Composable Lowering

If the affine dialect discovers structure by analysis, the **linalg dialect** declares it. A `linalg.generic` op specifies:

- **Indexing maps** — how each operand's indices derive from loop indices;
- **Iterator types** — `parallel`, `reduction`, or `window` per loop dimension;
- **A scalar body region** — the point-wise computation.

```mlir
#maps = [
  affine_map<(m, n, k) -> (m, k)>,
  affine_map<(m, n, k) -> (k, n)>,
  affine_map<(m, n, k) -> (m, n)>
]
linalg.generic {
  indexing_maps = #maps,
  iterator_types = ["parallel", "parallel", "reduction"]
} ins(%A, %B : tensor<?x?xf32>, tensor<?x?xf32>)
  outs(%C : tensor<?x?xf32>) {
^bb0(%a: f32, %b: f32, %c: f32):
  %p = arith.mulf %a, %b : f32
  %s = arith.addf %c, %p : f32
  linalg.yield %s : f32
}
```

Note the operand type: `tensor`, not `memref`. Linalg on tensors is functional — pure SSA values. This simplifies the middle of the stack enormously: fusion and tiling become pure rewrites over a value graph, with bufferization (tensor→memref) deferred until memory behavior is fully decided.

The canonical lowering pipeline developed by Vasilache et al. [2] proceeds in composable stages:

| Stage | Input | Output | Key transformation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tiling | `linalg.generic` on tensors | tiled `linalg` + `tensor.extract_slice` | decompose iteration domain into tiles |
| Fusion | producer + consumer `linalg` | fused `linalg.generic` | fuse elementwise into tiled producers |
| Bufferization | `tensor` ops | `memref` ops | destination-passing style, one-shot analysis |
| Vectorization | `linalg` on memrefs | `vector.contract`, `vector.transfer` | map inner loops to SIMD/vector ops |
| Lowering | `vector`/`scf` | `gpu`/`llvm` | target-specific emission |

Each stage is independently testable because the IR between stages is a complete, verifiable program. Tiling a linalg op needs no dependence analysis: the iterator types (`parallel` vs `reduction`) already encode which reorderings are legal. The framework of [2] shows this yields retargetable code generation competitive with hand-tuned libraries, precisely because the structure that hand-tuners exploit is present declaratively.

**Bufferization** converts tensors (values) to memrefs (storage) under a destination-passing-style discipline, with one-shot bufferization analysis proving the absence of read-after-write hazards *before* committing to in-place reuse — turning a historically fragile heuristic into a verified pass.

### 4.5 Hardware-Targeted Lowering: GPUs and Accelerators

The bottom of the stack maps the target-independent `vector` and `scf` dialects onto hardware execution models. The `gpu` dialect models SIMT hierarchies explicitly — `gpu.launch` carries grid/block/thread dimensions and memory spaces distinguish global, shared, and private storage. Lowering `vector.contract` through `gpu` to `nvvm`/`rocdl` is where Triton's insights [4] reappear: the tile becomes a thread block, the reduction a warp-level primitive, and the memory hierarchy is navigated by explicit copies rather than implicit caching.

IREE [6] demonstrates the full journey in production: a model enters as `tosa` or `linalg` on tensors, is tiled and fused per target, bufferized, vectorized, lowered through `gpu` or `llvm`, and packaged with a `hal` (hardware abstraction layer) interface and `vm` bytecode into a deployable executable — all within one compiler, one pass manager, one verifier. The alternative — a separate compiler per target — is exactly the fragmentation MLIR was built to end [1].

For custom accelerators, the story is dialect injection: a vendor defines an accelerator dialect (DMA engines, systolic arrays, scratchpads) in ODS, writes lowering patterns from `linalg`/`vector` to it, and inherits parsing, verification, and pass management for free. The *marginal cost* of a new target collapses to the domain-specific mapping, which is the economic argument at the heart of [1].

> **Theorem (Lowering monotonicity):** When each pass maps ops to strictly lower dialects and preserves observable I/O, the intermediates form a refinement chain — source-level value properties hold of the final machine code modulo the target model. *Test at the top, trust the chain.* ∎

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

**Formal arguments.** (i) *Affine legality is decidable.* Dependence checking over Presburger relations is decidable (worst-case doubly-exponential, fast in practice via isl [8]); affine passes never miscompile from an illegal schedule — at worst they decline to transform. (ii) *Linalg tiling is correct by construction.* Tiling only partitions the iteration domain declared in the op, and `parallel` iterators commute by definition, so no dependence violation can be introduced; correctness follows from the ODS contract, not analysis. (iii) *Bufferization is sound.* One-shot bufferization's RaW-conflict analysis is a whole-program dataflow check; when it succeeds, in-place reuse is provably safe.

**Empirical evidence.** The structured code-generation framework of [2] achieves performance on par with hand-tuned libraries across GEMM and convolution shapes on CPUs and GPUs. Triton [4] reaches cuBLAS/cuDNN-parity matmul and convolution from a tile-level language, confirming the vector/gpu abstractions capture performance-critical hardware features. Pluto's speedups over production compilers [3] establish the headroom the affine dialect makes routinely accessible, and IREE's single-binary CPU/GPU/accelerator deployment [6] is the existence proof for the fragmentation thesis of [1].

| Claim | Evidence type | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Affine schedules provably legal | Formal (Presburger decidability) | [3], [8] |
| Linalg tiling correct by construction | Formal (ODS iterator-type contract) | [2] |
| Structured lowering matches hand-tuned perf | Empirical (GEMM/conv benchmarks) | [2] |
| Tile-level GPU compilation at library parity | Empirical (matmul vs cuBLAS) | [4] |
| One infrastructure spans CPU/GPU/accelerator | Empirical (production deployment) | [6] |

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**The affine restriction is a straitjacket.** Indirection (`A[B[i]]`), data-dependent control flow, and dynamic shapes violate affinity; MLIR escapes to `scf` loops with runtime checks, forfeiting decidability. Versioning (an affine fast path guarded by a runtime check) recovers some ground at the cost of code bloat, but automatic profitable versioning remains an open heuristic problem.

**Bufferization is whole-program.** One-shot bufferization does not compose across separately compiled modules, complicating JIT scenarios where tensor producers and consumers compile independently.

**Schedule search is still heuristic.** Legality is decidable; *profitability* is not. Choosing tile sizes, fusion groupings, and vector widths remains combinatorial search; MLIR's transform dialect makes search programmable [1], but learned cost models that generalize across the hardware zoo are unsolved.

**Accelerator dialects lack shared semantics.** The ease of defining dialects is double-edged: two vendors' DMA dialects may be mutually unintelligible, recreating fragmentation one level down. A portable low-level accelerator abstraction is the outstanding standardization challenge.

**Verification is structural, not semantic.** The verifier checks ODS contracts; it does not check that a lowering pass preserves meaning. Translation validation for progressive pipelines exists only in research prototypes.

---

## 7. Conclusion

MLIR reframes compiler construction from *building IRs* to *building infrastructure for IRs*, and progressive lowering reframes optimization from *monolithic analysis* to *staged, invariant-preserving rewriting*. The affine dialect embeds a decidable mathematical model (the polyhedral framework [3][8]) directly in the IR, converting heroic analysis into routine verification. The linalg dialect declares structure (indexing maps, iterator types [2]), converting heroic transformation into mechanical rewriting. The lowering path to GPUs and accelerators — through vector, gpu, and target dialects, industrialized by IREE [6] and anticipated by Triton [4] — shows one infrastructure can span the full abstraction gap.

The deeper lesson is methodological: **abstraction levels should be designed around the invariants their consumers need**, not around historical accidents of compiler architecture. When each level carries checkable contracts — affine maps, structured iteration domains, explicit memory effects — the compiler becomes a chain of small, trustworthy steps. That is the enduring contribution of the MLIR project [1], and the foundation of the next decade of accelerator compilation.
