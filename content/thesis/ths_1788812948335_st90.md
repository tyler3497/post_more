# Verified Information Flow in the seL4 Microkernel: Intransitive Noninterference, Capability Attenuation, and Timing-Channel Analysis in Isabelle/HOL

## Abstract

We present a deep technical treatment of the machine-checked information-flow security proof for the seL4 microkernel, the only general-purpose operating-system kernel whose *confidentiality*, *integrity*, and functional correctness have all been formally verified down to the C implementation. We reconstruct the architecture of the proof: a variant of *intransitive noninterference* — von Oheimb's *nonleakage* — discharged not by direct induction over executions, but by a set of *unwinding conditions* in the tradition of Goguen–Meseguer and Rushby, each of which reduces the global trace property to a local obligation on single kernel transitions. We show how capability derivation disciplines and the partition scheduler's equivalence relations conspire to make those local obligations provable, how a proof calculus over a nondeterministic state monad scales the argument across the entire kernel interface, and how refinement preservation transports the result from the abstract specification to the C implementation. We then analyse the integrity counterpart of Sewell et al., the capability-based access-control mechanism that underwrites the unwinding, and the covert timing channels that the formal statement deliberately excludes. The result is a case study in the largest security proof ever constructed for an OS kernel.

## 1 Introduction

The promise that an operating-system kernel *enforces* a security policy, rather than merely implementing it, is thirty years older than the first proof that came close to honouring it. From Lampson's confinement problem through the Bell–LaPadula model, the military evaluation regimes (TCSEC, Common Criteria EAL7), and the separation-kernel standards (SKPP, MILS), the demand has always been the same: demonstrate, with mathematical rigour, that a system cannot move information along paths the policy forbids. And the obstacle has always been the same too: kernels are large, concurrent, hardware-facing, and shot through with shared state, so any plausible security argument must confront the implementation rather than an idealised model.

The seL4 microkernel broke this impasse in stages. First, Klein et al. (2009) proved *functional correctness*: every behaviour of the ~9,300-line C implementation is contained in the behaviours of an abstract specification written in Isabelle/HOL, at a cost of roughly twenty person-years and two hundred thousand lines of proof [2]. Functional correctness alone rules out buffer overflows, null dereferences, and undefined behaviour, but it does not say what security *means*. Sewell et al. (2011) then proved *integrity*: that kernel state and authority cannot be modified except through held capabilities, anchoring an authority-confinement argument in the capability-derivation rules [4]. Finally, Murray et al. (2013) proved *information-flow enforcement*: a variant of intransitive noninterference over the kernel's abstract specification, preserved by refinement to C, establishing seL4 as a separation kernel for configured systems [1]. This thesis reconstructs that third result in depth — its property, its proof method, the unwinding machinery, the capability discipline that makes the unwinding provable, and the residual timing channels it leaves on the table.

The stakes of the analysis are twofold. Scientifically, the seL4 information-flow proof remains the largest machine-checked noninterference proof ever completed, and its techniques — the nonleakage formulation, the `confidentiality-u` unwinding condition, the proof calculus for the state monad — are now the template for any serious kernel verification effort. Practically, understanding exactly *what* was proven, and precisely where the proof's assumptions end and hand-waving begins, is the prerequisite for deploying seL4 as the isolation foundation of high-assurance systems.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Noninterference and its unwinding

The classical formulation of information-flow security is due to Goguen and Meseguer (1982): a system satisfies *noninterference* when the actions of High-security subjects have no observable effect on Low-security subjects. Rushby's subsequent insight — the *unwinding theorem* — was that this global, trace-quantified property is equivalent to a conjunction of local conditions on individual state transitions, provided the system is deterministic and the security policy is transitive. The proof engineer checks, for each step, that (i) steps of a domain preserve the equivalence of states as seen by other domains (*step consistency*), and (ii) steps do not reveal their own domain's secrets (*local respect* / *output consistency*). Noninterference on traces then follows by induction.

Real kernels break both preconditions. Their policies are *intransitive*: information may flow from partition A to partition B and from B to partition C, while A-to-C flow remains forbidden, because B is a trusted intermediary (a crypto service, a downgrader, a device driver with a sanitisation role). Their behaviour is *nondeterministic*: interrupts arrive at arbitrary points, and the scheduler's choices are underspecified. The modern response, due to von Oheimb (2004), is *noninfluence*, decomposed as *noninterference + nonleakage*: nonleakage captures the information-theoretic content of the policy — that no entity can learn anything about data it is not permitted to observe — while remaining definable for nondeterministic, intransitive systems [6]. Murray et al. adopted a nonleakage variant for seL4.

### 2.2 The seL4 verification stack

seL4's assurance rests on a *refinement tower*. The abstract specification (in Isabelle/HOL) describes kernel behaviour as a nondeterministic state monad over an explicit state record; the executable Haskell prototype refines it; the C implementation refines that. The crucial meta-theorem is that the information-flow property is *preserved by refinement*: prove it once, at the abstract level where reasoning is tractable, and it descends to the C code for free [1]. All information-flow reasoning therefore lives in the abstract world of `KernelState`, capabilities, and the partition scheduler, and the unwinding conditions are statements about abstract transitions.

### 2.3 Capabilities and authority

seL4's access-control substrate is a *capability system* in the L4 tradition. Every kernel object — thread control blocks (TCBs), endpoints, notification objects, page tables, CNodes, untyped memory — is named only through capabilities held in CNode slots; every system call is authorised by the capabilities the invoking thread presents. Capabilities can be *derived* (copied, minted with attenuated rights, or retyped from untyped memory) only with strictly non-increasing authority: derivation is a *monotone attenuation* along a derivation tree. This discipline is what makes the unwinding conditions dischargeable: the set of objects a partition can *affect* is bounded by the capabilities it holds, and the kernel's job is to make that bound airtight.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in four stages, mirroring the structure of the original proof effort:

1. **Property reconstruction.** We state the nonleakage property (`nonleakage`) and the auxiliary unwinding condition (`confidentiality-u`) as they appear in the Isabelle/HOL development, and explain the role of the partition set function `sources` and the scheduler equivalence `∼_{PSched}`.
2. **Unwinding decomposition.** We show how the global trace property is reduced to per-transition obligations, and how a proof calculus over the nondeterministic state monad discharges those obligations uniformly across the kernel's system-call interface.
3. **Capability-integrity analysis.** We reconstruct the integrity argument of Sewell et al. and show how capability derivation invariants feed the information-flow unwinding: without integrity, the "who can affect what" premise of every unwinding step would be unprovable.
4. **Covert-channel audit.** We delineate the timing channels the formal statement excludes, and survey the mitigation discipline (partition scheduling with microarchitectural state flushing) that the deployment guidance prescribes instead [7].

We take the proof artefacts themselves — the Isabelle/HOL theories, the refinement theorems, and the published papers — as primary sources, and we treat the proof's *assumptions* (compiler, assembler, hardware, boot code correctness) as first-class objects of analysis rather than footnotes.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The nonleakage property and the `sources` function

The heart of the result is `nonleakage`, a variant of intransitive noninterference parameterised by the number of execution steps `n`:

> **Theorem: nonleakage.** For all step counts `n`, states `s`, `t`, and partitions `p`: if `s` and `t` are reachable, agree on the partition scheduler's private state (`s ∼_{PSched} t`), and agree on the state of every entity in the *extent* of `p` over `n` steps (`sources n s p`), then after `n` transitions the two states still agree on `p`'s observable state.

```haskell
-- Pseudocode of the property in the abstract specification's metalanguage
nonleakage :: Nat -> State -> State -> Partition -> Prop
nonleakage n s t p =
  reachable s && reachable t
  && equiv PSched s t
  && (forall (q, obj) in sources n s p. equiv obj s t)
  ==> equivN p n s t
```

The subtlety is `sources n s p`: the set of entities permitted to send information to `p` *transitively*, through the intransitive policy, within `n` steps. It is defined by iterating the one-step information-flow relation — the *extent* — and it is this dynamic, step-indexed characterisation that lets the property handle trusted intermediaries: B may legitimately relay A's data to C while A-to-C flow stays forbidden, because `sources` tracks the policy's transitive closure rather than a static lattice [1].

### 4.2 The `confidentiality-u` unwinding condition

Proving `nonleakage` directly by induction over traces is intractable: the induction hypothesis must relate pairs of runs across arbitrary nondeterministic choices. The unwinding reduces it to `confidentiality-u`, a *single-step* condition:

> **Theorem: confidentiality-u (unwinding).** For all partitions `p` and reachable states `s`, `t`: if `s` and `t` agree on `p` (`s ∼_p t`), agree on the scheduler (`s ∼_{PSched} t`), and — whenever `s` is currently executing in `p` — agree on the current partition's state, then every single transition from `s` and `t` preserves `p`-equivalence. And `confidentiality-u` implies `nonleakage`.

In Isabelle/HOL pseudo-notation over the state monad:

```haskell
-- confidentiality-u: the local condition implying the global property
confidentialityU :: Partition -> State -> State -> Prop
confidentialityU p s t =
  reachable s && reachable t
  && equiv p s t && equiv PSched s t
  && (inPartition s p ==> equiv (currentPartition s) s t)
  ==> stepPreserves p s t

-- unwinding meta-theorem (machine-checked in Isabelle/HOL)
unwindingTheorem :: Prop
unwindingTheorem = forall p. confidentialityU p ==> nonleakage
```

The proof of the unwinding meta-theorem itself is the *only* part of the development that reasons about traces; everything else reasons about single steps. This is the decisive engineering move: it converts a property over *pairs of executions* into a property over *pairs of states*, and the kernel developers then discharge it with a proof calculus that symbolically executes each system call in the nondeterministic state monad, tracking which partitions' state each operation can read or write [1].

### 4.3 The state-monad proof calculus and the scheduler equivalence

Discharging `confidentiality-u` across the whole kernel interface — every system call, every interrupt path, the scheduler itself — by hand would be hopeless. Murray et al. built a *proof calculus*: a set of compositional rules for the `do`-notation of the state monad (`bind`, `return`, `gets`, `modify`, failure, nondeterministic choice) that compute, for any kernel operation, an over-approximation of its *read set* and *write set* in terms of partitions. A typical rule says that `modify f` preserves `p`-equivalence whenever `f` touches only state that `p` is permitted to observe given the current partition's authority — and the side conditions are discharged by the capability invariants.

Two design decisions make the calculus sound:

- **The scheduler is part of the state.** `∼_{PSched}` equivalence requires that two runs agree on the partition scheduler's private state: the current partition, the schedule, and the per-partition time-slice accounting. The proof *assumes* a static partition schedule (round-robin over partitions with fixed slices) and, crucially, *forbids* asynchronous interrupt delivery to user partitions, since an interrupt is an information channel from the device's partition to the interrupted one.
- **Refinement carries the property down.** Because `nonleakage` is preserved by refinement, the calculus reasons only about the abstract specification. The C code inherits the guarantee through the refinement theorems of Klein et al. [2][3]. Nondeterminism in the abstract spec is an *over-approximation* of implementation behaviour, so the proof must be robust to the implementation resolving nondeterminism adversarially — which is precisely why the property quantifies over all reachable state pairs.

|---|---|---|---|
| Functional correctness | Abstract → C refinement | ~200k lines of proof | Behavioural containment |
| Integrity (`seL4 Enforces Integrity`) | Abstract spec | Authority confinement | Who may modify what |
| Information flow (`confidentiality-u` + calculus) | Abstract spec | ~30+ person-years total effort | Nonleakage via unwinding |
| Timing-channel mitigation | Deployment configuration | Not formally proven | Flush microarchitectural state |

### 4.4 Integrity: the capability derivation argument

Sewell et al.'s integrity proof is the *precondition* of the information-flow proof, though it is often presented as a sibling result. Its statement: the kernel maintains an *authority confinement* invariant — no thread can obtain a capability it is not entitled to, and no kernel object can be modified except through a held capability with sufficient rights [4]. The proof rests on invariants over the capability derivation tree:

1. **Derivation monotonicity.** Every `seL4_CNode_Mint`, `seL4_CNode_Copy`, and `seL4_Untyped_Retype` invocation produces capabilities whose rights are a subset of the source's; untyped retype additionally consumes the source memory so no aliasing escapes the tree.
2. **Zombie and finalisation discipline.** Object deletion revokes the entire derivation subtree (via the *zombie* mechanism), so dangling capabilities cannot outlive their objects — a classic source of confused-deputy and use-after-free channels.
3. **Kernel-memory isolation.** Capabilities to kernel-internal objects (scheduling contexts, the idle thread's TCB, interrupt state) are never derivable to user level, which is what lets the unwinding treat scheduler state as `PSched`-private.

> **Theorem: authority confinement.** In every reachable state, the set of capabilities held by each partition is exactly the set derivable from its initial capabilities by the monotone derivation rules; hence no partition can acquire read authority over another partition's objects unless the policy explicitly grants it.

Without this theorem, `confidentiality-u`'s premise — "the current partition can only affect state it is authorised for" — would be an uncheckable assumption rather than a discharged obligation. Integrity is the *static* half of the security story; nonleakage is the *dynamic* half.

### 4.5 Covert timing channels: what the proof excludes

The formal statement covers *storage channels* and *logical time*: the sequence of observable events and the partition-schedule's logical clock. It explicitly does **not** cover fine-grained *timing channels* — variations in execution latency caused by shared microarchitectural state (caches, TLBs, branch predictors, interconnect contention) [1]. The information-flow proof assumes that the abstract machine's timing behaviour is a function only of the logical schedule, which is false on real hardware; Ge et al.'s survey documents the breadth of the gap [7].

The deployment answer is *mitigation, not proof*: configure seL4 as a separation kernel with a static partition schedule, disable asynchronous interrupt delivery to partitions, and *flush all shared microarchitectural state on every partition switch* — TLBs, caches, and predictor state — so that the residual timing channel's bandwidth is bounded by the (configurable) switch cost rather than by the victim's secret-dependent access pattern. This is a probabilistic, bandwidth-reduction argument, not a noninterference theorem, and the original authors are explicit that on commodity hardware the *absence* of timing channels is not fully enforceable [1].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

The "empirical" content of a verification project is the proof artefact itself: its size, its checkability, and what it caught. The headline numbers:

- **Scale.** The functional-correctness proof spans roughly 200,000 lines of Isabelle/HOL; the security proofs (integrity + information flow) add tens of thousands more. The information-flow proof alone consumed multiple person-years, making it the largest noninterference proof ever machine-checked [1][3].
- **Bugs found.** The verification effort discovered on the order of 150+ bugs in the design and implementation across the project's lifetime — including, during the information-flow work, places where the original seL4 design *violated* the intended policy and had to be changed (e.g., restrictions on interrupt delivery and scheduling behaviour) [1]. A proof that cannot fail is not a proof; this one failed, repeatedly, until the system was fixed.
- **Independence of checking.** Every result is checked by the Isabelle/HOL kernel — a small, trusted logical core. The trusted computing base of the *proof* is thus the prover, the specification's fidelity to intent, and the classic assumptions: compiler, assembler, hardware, and boot code correctness [2].
- **Preservation.** The refinement theorems mean the abstract-level nonleakage result holds of the C implementation without re-proof — the only general-purpose kernel in existence with a machine-checked confidentiality argument at the implementation level [3].

## 6 Limitations

No honest treatment can omit the proof's boundaries, and the original authors enumerate them with unusual candour [1]:

1. **Timing channels are out of scope.** As analysed in §4.5, fine-grained timing behaviour is not modelled; mitigation is configurational and probabilistic.
2. **The policy must be configured.** The theorem proves that seL4 *enforces* an intransitive policy; it does not prove that any particular deployment's policy is *sensible*. A misconfigured system — capabilities handed to the wrong partitions — satisfies the theorem and leaks anyway. "We prove everything else" assumes the initial capabilities encode the intended policy [1].
3. **Hardware, compiler, boot code.** The proof trusts the C compiler, the assembler, the hardware's conformance to its ISA model, and the unverified boot code that establishes the initial state. A hardware trojan or a miscompiled fence instruction voids the guarantee.
4. **Availability is not proven.** The result says nothing about denial of service: a partition may starve others of CPU within its authority, and the static schedule only bounds this by configuration.
5. **Proof maintenance cost.** At roughly 50 lines of proof per line of C, the proof is a *liability* as well as an asset: every kernel change risks breaking the unwinding's side conditions, which is why seL4's evolution is deliberately conservative [3].

## 7 Conclusion

The seL4 information-flow proof is a landmark not because it achieved the impossible — it did not eliminate timing channels, and it cannot bless a misconfigured policy — but because it redrew the boundary of the possible. It showed that *intransitive noninterference*, the policy shape that real systems actually need, can be stated precisely (`nonleakage`), reduced to checkable local obligations (`confidentiality-u`), discharged at scale by a purpose-built proof calculus over the kernel's own specification monad, and carried to the C implementation by refinement. It showed that *integrity* — the capability derivation discipline — is not a separate concern but the load-bearing precondition of the confidentiality argument. And it was honest about the residual: timing channels mitigated by flushing rather than proven absent, hardware trusted rather than verified.

For the verification community, the template is now clear: formulate the policy as nonleakage over a step-indexed `sources` function, prove an unwinding theorem once, build the monadic proof calculus, and let refinement do the rest. For system builders, the lesson is sharper: a kernel can now come with a *mathematical certificate* that its isolation mechanism is airtight on storage channels — and the remaining work, timing mitigation and correct configuration, is at least precisely delimited. Thirty years after Goguen and Meseguer, the unwinding technique finally scaled to a real kernel. The next thirty will decide whether the timing-channel remainder can be closed with equal rigour.
