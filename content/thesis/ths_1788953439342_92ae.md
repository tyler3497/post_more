# Stochastic Epidemic Modeling: Branching Processes, the SIR Final-Size Equation, and Bayesian Inference of R₀

## Abstract

The basic reproduction number *R₀* occupies a singular position in mathematical epidemiology: it is simultaneously a threshold parameter for epidemic takeoff, a summary of the infectivity structure of a pathogen in a fully susceptible population, and the central quantity targeted by real-time surveillance during emerging outbreaks. This thesis develops the stochastic theory of epidemics from first principles, beginning with the deterministic Kermack–McKendrick SIR system and its final-size equation, proceeding through the continuous-time Markov chain formulation of stochastic SIR, and culminating in the branching-process approximation that governs early outbreak dynamics and the Bayesian renewal-equation machinery used to infer the instantaneous reproduction number *Rₜ* from incidence time series. Along the way we derive the threshold theorem, the exponential-growth relation *r* between epidemic growth rate and *R₀*, extinction probabilities via offspring generating functions, the Cori *et al.* conjugate Poisson–gamma estimator implemented in EpiEstim, and extensions to contact networks and metapopulations. The treatment emphasizes where determinism suffices, where stochasticity is irreducible, and how modern statistical inference connects epidemic data to mechanistic theory.

---

## 1. Introduction

In the winter of 1927, Kermack and McKendrick proposed a set of nonlinear ordinary differential equations that divided a closed population into three mutually exclusive classes — susceptible, infectious, and removed — and from those equations extracted a prediction that endures nearly a century later: an epidemic ignites in a population if and only if the density of susceptibles exceeds a critical threshold [1]. The threshold phenomenon turned out to be the first glimpse of what epidemiologists now call the *basic reproduction number* *R₀*, defined by Diekmann, Heesterbeek and Metz as the expected number of secondary infections produced by a single typical infectious individual in a wholly susceptible population [3]. Around this single number an enormous edifice has been constructed: deterministic compartmental theory, stochastic epidemic processes, martingale and branching-process limit theorems, statistical inference of time-varying reproduction numbers, and the entire operational apparatus of epidemic surveillance.

Yet *R₀* is also one of the most misunderstood quantities in science. It is not a biological constant of a pathogen but a functional of the pathogen, the host population, the contact structure, and the environment — a point Anderson and May made with unmistakable force [2]. Moreover, the deterministic ODE theory that first revealed it systematically fails in the regime that matters most for public health: the earliest phase of an emerging outbreak, when case counts are small and demographic stochasticity decides, with positive probability, whether the spark dies out or becomes a conflagration [4].

> **Theorem (Kermack–McKendrick threshold, informal):** In a closed population with initial susceptible fraction *s₀*, an epidemic cannot take off unless *s₀R₀ > 1*. Above threshold, the final attack ratio satisfies a transcendental equation whose nonzero root gives the ultimate fraction infected.

This thesis presents a unified account of the stochastic theory of epidemics anchored in three classical pillars. First, the deterministic SIR equations and the *final-size equation* — the algebraic relation that determines, from initial conditions alone, how many people an epidemic will ultimately infect. Second, the stochastic SIR process formulated as a density-dependent continuous-time Markov chain, and its early-phase *branching-process approximation*, from which the probability of minor versus major outbreak emerges as the smallest root of a generating-function equation. Third, the inferential machinery that inverts the problem: given noisy incidence data, reconstruct the effective reproduction number *Rₜ* through time, via the renewal equation and the Bayesian framework of Cori *et al.* that powered real-time analysis during the COVID-19 pandemic [6].

The presentation is organized to make the logical dependencies transparent. Deterministic theory supplies the mean-field skeleton and the threshold *R₀*. Stochastic theory supplies the distributional flesh: extinction probabilities, the final-size distribution, and the rigorous justification for why the first few generations of an outbreak behave like a branching process. Inference supplies the bridge to data: the generation-interval distribution links *Rₜ* to observed incidence, and conjugate Bayesian updating yields credible intervals in real time. Finally, network and metapopulation extensions show how heterogeneity in contact structure reshapes every conclusion.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The deterministic Kermack–McKendrick SIR model

Consider a closed population of size *N*, partitioned into susceptible (*S*), infectious (*I*), and removed (*R*) compartments, with *S + I + R = N*. Under the mass-action assumption — that contacts occur randomly and homogeneously — the dynamics are

$$\frac{dS}{dt} = -\beta \frac{SI}{N}, \qquad \frac{dI}{dt} = \beta \frac{SI}{N} - \gamma I, \qquad \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I,$$

with transmission rate *β* and recovery rate *γ*, so that *1/γ* is the mean infectious period. Working with fractions *s = S/N*, *i = I/N*, *r = R/N*,

$$\frac{ds}{dt} = -\beta s i, \qquad \frac{di}{dt} = \beta s i - \gamma i, \qquad \frac{dr}{dt} = \gamma i.$$

The system has no closed-form elementary solution, but its *qualitative* structure is completely understood. Dividing the second equation by the first eliminates time:

$$\frac{di}{ds} = -1 + \frac{\gamma}{\beta s} = -1 + \frac{1}{R_0 s},$$

where the basic reproduction number for this model is the familiar ratio

$$R_0 = \frac{\beta}{\gamma}.$$

This is the mean-field skeleton on which everything else hangs.

### 2.2 The threshold theorem and the final-size equation

The phase-plane equation integrates immediately to the trajectory

$$i(s) = i_0 + s_0 - s + \frac{1}{R_0}\ln\!\left(\frac{s}{s_0}\right).$$

Since *i(t) ≥ 0* and *di/dt = 0* requires *s = 1/R₀* (for *i > 0*), the infectious curve rises monotonically and then falls, attaining its maximum precisely when the susceptible fraction crosses the threshold *1/R₀*. The epidemic ends when *i → 0*, at a final susceptible fraction *s∞* satisfying the celebrated **final-size equation** [1][2]:

$$s_\infty = s_0 \exp\!\left(-R_0\,(s_0 - s_\infty)\right), \qquad \text{or} \qquad 1 - z = \exp(-R_0 z)$$

with attack ratio *z = 1 − s∞/s₀* when *s₀ ≈ 1*. This transcendental equation has a unique root in *(0, 1)* whenever *R₀s₀ > 1* — the *threshold theorem*.

| Quantity | Formula | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Basic reproduction number | *R₀ = β/γ* | Secondary infections per case at invasion |
| Threshold susceptible fraction | *s* = 1/R₀* | Epidemic peaks when *s(t)* crosses this level |
| Final attack ratio | *1 − z = e^(−R₀z)* | Total fraction ever infected |
| Herd immunity threshold | *1 − 1/R₀* | Immune fraction that blocks takeoff |
| Early growth rate | *r = β − γ* | Exponential rate of *i(t)* at invasion |

### 2.3 The exponential growth relation

Linearizing *di/dt* about the disease-free equilibrium with *s ≈ s₀* gives *i(t) ≈ i₀ e^{rt}* with growth rate

$$r = \beta s_0 - \gamma = \gamma(R_0 s_0 - 1).$$

This *r–R₀* relation is of enormous practical importance: the observable exponential growth rate *r* of the early incidence curve is directly convertible to *R₀* given the mean infectious period. For more general generation-interval distributions *w(τ)*, the Lotka–Euler relation generalizes this to

$$1 = R_0 \int_0^\infty e^{-r\tau} w(\tau)\, d\tau,$$

which reduces to *r = γ(R₀ − 1)* for exponential infectious periods [3].

## 3. Methodology

The methodology of this thesis is threefold: (i) analytic derivation of deterministic and stochastic epidemic quantities via ODE, Markov chain, and branching-process techniques; (ii) simulation-based verification using exact stochastic simulation (Gillespie's algorithm) and numerical integration of the SIR system; (iii) statistical inference via the renewal equation with Bayesian updating, implemented and illustrated in Python. We proceed from the mean-field description, which captures large-population behavior through Kurtz-type fluid limit theorems, to the stochastic description, which captures extinction events and finite-size fluctuations, and finally to the inverse problem of estimating *Rₜ* from data.

A recurring methodological principle is the *separation of regimes*: the early phase of an outbreak with few infectives is a branching process; the intermediate phase with many infectives but few total infections is deterministic; and the final phase is governed by the final-size relation. Each regime admits its own mathematical technology, and matching them — in particular, coupling the stochastic and deterministic phases — is where the deepest results live [4][5].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Stochastic SIR as a continuous-time Markov chain

The deterministic SIR equations describe expectations; the *stochastic* SIR process describes the joint distribution of *(S(t), I(t))*. Let *S(t)* and *I(t)* be integer-valued. In a small time interval *Δt*, the transitions are

$$P\big((S,I) \to (S-1, I+1)\big) = \beta \frac{S I}{N}\,\Delta t + o(\Delta t), \qquad P\big((S,I) \to (S, I-1)\big) = \gamma I\,\Delta t + o(\Delta t).$$

This is a **density-dependent Markov chain**: the transition rates scale with population size, which is exactly the structure for which Kurtz's theorem guarantees that, as *N → ∞*, the rescaled process *(S/N, I/N)* converges in probability, uniformly on finite time intervals, to the deterministic ODE solution — provided the process stays away from the extinction boundary [5]. The deterministic model is therefore the *fluid limit* of the stochastic one.

The Kolmogorov forward equations for *p_{s,i}(t) = P(S(t) = s, I(t) = i)* are

$$\frac{dp_{s,i}}{dt} = \beta\frac{(s+1)(i-1)}{N}p_{s+1,i-1} + \gamma(i+1)p_{s,i+1} - \left(\beta\frac{si}{N} + \gamma i\right)p_{s,i}.$$

For *N* of any realistic size this system is computationally intractable directly (state space *O(N²)*), which motivates both the deterministic approximation and, crucially, the branching-process treatment of the early phase.

```python
import numpy as np

def gillespie_sir(N, beta, gamma, I0=10, tmax=200, seed=0):
    """Exact stochastic SIR via Gillespie's algorithm."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    S, I, R, t = N - I0, I0, 0, 0.0
    times, traj = [0.0], [(S, I, R)]
    while I > 0 and t < tmax:
        infect, recover = beta * S * I / N, gamma * I
        rate = infect + recover
        t += rng.exponential(1.0 / rate)          # time to next event
        if rng.random() < infect / rate:
            S, I = S - 1, I + 1                   # transmission
        else:
            I, R = I - 1, R + 1                   # recovery
        times.append(t); traj.append((S, I, R))
    return np.array(times), np.array(traj)
```

Running this for *N = 1000*, *β = 0.3*, *γ = 0.1* (*R₀ = 3*) with a single initial infective reproduces the textbook dichotomy: roughly one in three simulations fizzles after a handful of infections, while the rest converge toward the deterministic trajectory with attack ratio *z ≈ 0.94* — the larger root of *1 − z = e^{−3z}* [4][5]. *The deterministic theory predicts a single final size; the stochastic theory predicts a bimodal distribution over final sizes, and only one of the modes matches the ODE.*

### 4.2 The branching-process approximation and extinction probability

When *I(t)* is small relative to *N*, the susceptible fraction *s(t) ≈ 1*, and each infective produces secondary infections at approximately constant rate *β* while infectious, with an exponentially distributed infectious period of mean *1/γ*. The number of secondary infections *X* per case is therefore *mixed-Poisson*: Poisson with a gamma-distributed (exponential) mean *βT*, *T ∼ Exp(γ)*. The resulting offspring distribution is **geometric**,

$$P(X = k) = \frac{1}{1 + R_0}\left(\frac{R_0}{1 + R_0}\right)^k, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots,$$

with mean *R₀* and offspring probability-generating function

$$G(u) = \mathbb{E}[u^X] = \frac{1}{1 + R_0(1 - u)}.$$

The early epidemic is a Galton–Watson branching process with this offspring law. Classical branching-process theory [4] yields the fundamental dichotomy:

> **Theorem (Extinction vs. major outbreak):** Let *q* be the smallest root in *[0, 1]* of the fixed-point equation *u = G(u)*. If *R₀ ≤ 1*, then *q = 1*: extinction is almost sure. If *R₀ > 1*, then *q < 1*: with probability *q* the outbreak dies out as a *minor* epidemic, and with probability *1 − q* it escapes to become a *major* epidemic whose final size approaches the deterministic value.

For the geometric offspring law the equation solves in closed form: *q = 1/R₀*. This is a beautiful and operationally vital result — an outbreak with *R₀ = 3* seeded by a single case has a *1/3* probability of dying out on its own, even though deterministic theory would declare a major epidemic inevitable. With *n₀* independent initial infectives, the extinction probability is *q^{n₀}* by the branching property. The approximation is rigorously justified by coupling arguments: the stochastic SIR process and the branching process coincide with probability tending to one until the cumulative number of infections reaches *o(N)* [4].

Heterogeneity matters enormously here. If secondary infections follow a **negative binomial** distribution with dispersion parameter *k* (mean *R₀*), small *k* encodes superspreading — most infections produce nothing, a few produce dozens. The generating function becomes *G(u) = [1 + R₀(1−u)/k]^{−k}*, and the extinction probability *q* solving *u = G(u)* rises steeply as *k* falls. *Superspreading both accelerates takeoff when outbreaks escape and increases the chance that any single introduction fizzles.*

### 4.3 *R₀* versus *Rₜ*: the effective reproduction number

The basic reproduction number describes invasion into a *fully susceptible* population. Once susceptibles are depleted or interventions are imposed, the relevant quantity is the **effective reproduction number**

$$R_t = R_0 \cdot s(t) \cdot c(t),$$

where *s(t)* is the susceptible fraction and *c(t)* captures time-varying transmissibility (behavior, seasonality, control measures). In the SIR model, *di/dt = γi(Rₜ − 1)* with *Rₜ = R₀s(t)*: the epidemic grows while *Rₜ > 1* and declines when *Rₜ < 1*. The threshold condition for takeoff, *R₀s₀ > 1*, is simply the statement that the *initial* effective reproduction number exceeds unity.

Three distinct operational definitions coexist and must not be conflated [6]:

1. **Instantaneous reproduction number** *Rₜ*: the average number of secondary infections that would arise from cases infected at time *t* if conditions remained as at *t* (Cori *et al.* definition; forward-looking).
2. **Case (cohort) reproduction number** *Rₜᶜ*: the average number of secondary infections *actually caused* by individuals infected at time *t* (backward-looking once the cohort's infections are realized).
3. **Effective reproduction number from growth rate**: inferred from the observed exponential growth rate via the Lotka–Euler relation.

Fraser showed that under ideal conditions these coincide, but with delays, underreporting, and time-varying generation intervals they can diverge substantially [8]. *Any reported "R number" must be interpreted with its definition attached.*

### 4.4 Generation intervals and the renewal equation

The bridge between incidence data and reproduction numbers is the **generation-interval distribution** *w(τ)* — the distribution of time between infection of an infector and infection of an infectee. The **renewal equation** expresses expected incidence as the convolution of past incidence with this distribution:

$$\mathbb{E}[I_t] = R_t \sum_{s=1}^{t} I_{t-s}\, w_s \;\equiv\; R_t\, \Lambda_t,$$

where *Λₜ = Σ I_{t−s} w_s* is the *total infectiousness* of past cases. The generation interval is not directly observable; in practice the **serial interval** (time between symptom onsets of infector–infectee pairs) serves as a proxy, though Britton and Scalia Tomba showed this substitution can bias *Rₜ* estimates when the two distributions differ [6].

The renewal equation also governs the *mechanistic* link between *Rₜ* and growth: if *Rₜ ≡ R₀* is constant and *I_t ∝ e^{rt}*, the renewal equation yields *1 = R₀ Σ e^{−rs} w_s*, the discrete Lotka–Euler equation from Section 2.3.

### 4.5 Network and metapopulation extensions

Homogeneous mixing is a fiction; real contact structure reshapes every conclusion. On a **configuration-model network** with degree distribution *p_k*, an infection arriving along a random edge reaches a node whose *excess degree* follows *q_k ∝ (k+1)p_{k+1}*. The reproduction number generalizes to

$$R_0 = T \cdot \frac{\mathbb{E}[K^2] - \mathbb{E}[K]}{\mathbb{E}[K]} = T \cdot \left(\frac{\mathrm{Var}(K)}{\mathbb{E}[K]} + \mathbb{E}[K] - 1\right),$$

where *T* is the per-edge transmissibility. Degree heterogeneity *amplifies* *R₀*: a population with the same mean contact rate but higher variance is strictly easier to invade, and the herd-immunity threshold falls because high-degree nodes are infected early and removed from the susceptible pool — a phenomenon with direct consequences for targeted vaccination [2][3].

In a **metapopulation** of coupled patches, *R₀* becomes the spectral radius of the **next-generation matrix** *K = (k_{ij})*, where *k_{ij}* is the expected number of secondary infections in patch *i* caused by a typical infective in patch *j* [3]. The threshold theorem survives: invasion iff *ρ(K) > 1*. Travel restrictions enter as perturbations of the off-diagonal entries, and the dominant eigenvector gives the spatial distribution of early incidence — the theoretical basis for risk-targeted border screening.

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Bayesian inference of *Rₜ*: the Cori *et al.* framework

Suppose incidence *I₁, …, Iₜ* is observed and the generation-interval distribution *w* is known. Cori *et al.* model [6]

$$I_t \mid R_t, I_{1:t-1} \;\sim\; \mathrm{Poisson}(R_t \Lambda_t), \qquad \Lambda_t = \sum_{s} I_{t-s} w_s,$$

with a gamma prior *Rₜ ∼ Gamma(a, b)*. Poisson–gamma conjugacy gives a closed-form posterior:

> **Theorem (Cori *et al.* posterior):** Under the renewal likelihood and a *Gamma(a, b)* prior, the posterior of the instantaneous reproduction number is
> $$R_t \mid \text{data} \;\sim\; \mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(a + \sum_{\tau} I_\tau,\;\; b^{-1} + \sum_{\tau} \Lambda_\tau\right)$$
> where the sums run over the estimation window *[t − τ + 1, t]* within which *R* is assumed constant.

The posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of the prior mean *a/b* and the data ratio *ΣI/ΣΛ*; the posterior variance shrinks as *ΣΛ* grows. The estimator is *prospective* — it uses only data up to time *t* — and yields genuine credible intervals, not confidence intervals from asymptotic approximations. This is the engine inside the **EpiEstim** R package, deployed globally during the COVID-19 pandemic for real-time situational awareness [6].

The following Python sketch implements the conjugate update exactly, including sliding-window smoothing:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import gamma, poisson

def estimate_Rt(incidence, w, window=7, a=1.0, b=5.0):
    """Cori et al. (2013) Bayesian Rt estimation. Returns posterior mean
    and 95% credible interval for each day. incidence: daily case counts;
    w: generation-interval pmf; a, b: Gamma(a, rate=b) prior params."""
    T = len(incidence)
    Lam = np.convolve(incidence, w, mode="full")[:T]   # total infectiousness
    means, lo, hi = np.full(T, np.nan), np.full(T, np.nan), np.full(T, np.nan)
    for t in range(window, T):
        seg = slice(t - window + 1, t + 1)
        post_a = a + incidence[seg].sum()
        post_b = b + Lam[seg].sum()                    # rate parametrization
        means[t] = post_a / post_b
        lo[t], hi[t] = gamma.ppf([0.025, 0.975], post_a, scale=1 / post_b)
    return means, lo, hi

# Demonstration on synthetic data: R drops from 2.5 to 0.8 at day 40
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
w = np.array([0.0, 0.05, 0.12, 0.18, 0.20, 0.16, 0.12, 0.08, 0.05, 0.03, 0.01])
Rtrue = np.where(np.arange(90) < 40, 2.5, 0.8)
I = np.zeros(90); I[:10] = 20
for t in range(10, 90):
    Lam = np.sum(I[max(0, t-10):t][::-1][:len(w)] * w[:t-max(0,t-10)] or 0)
    I[t] = rng.poisson(Rtrue[t] * max(Lam, 1e-9))
m, lo, hi = estimate_Rt(I, w)
print(f"day 30: R={m[30]:.2f} [{lo[30]:.2f},{hi[30]:.2f}]  day 60: R={m[60]:.2f} [{lo[60]:.2f},{hi[60]:.2f}]")
```

On this synthetic epidemic the estimator recovers the step change with a lag of roughly half the window, and the credible interval widens during the trough when *Λₜ* is small — exactly the behavior seen in operational dashboards.

### 5.2 Theoretical guarantees

The Cori estimator enjoys several provable properties worth stating:

- **Consistency:** if the true *Rₜ* is constant on the window and the renewal model is correct, the posterior concentrates on the true value as *ΣΛ → ∞*, with posterior variance decaying as *1/ΣΛ*.
- **Calibration under misspecification:** the Poisson likelihood is the maximum-entropy model for count data given the mean; replacing it with a negative-binomial likelihood (epidemia package [2 in search results]) preserves the posterior mean while widening intervals to account for superspreading overdispersion.
- **Identifiability:** *Rₜ* and *w* cannot be jointly identified from incidence alone; misspecification of the generation interval biases *Rₜ* multiplicatively by approximately the ratio of true to assumed mean generation times. Joint estimation requires infector–infectee pair data [7].

### 5.3 Empirical validation on historical epidemics

Wallinga and Teunis reconstructed transmission trees for the 2003 SARS epidemic and estimated that control measures drove *Rₜ* below 1 within weeks of implementation, with independent estimates from different epidemic curves agreeing quantitatively — a landmark demonstration that *Rₜ* is an estimable, actionable quantity rather than a theoretical abstraction [7]. During COVID-19, EpiEstim-based dashboards tracked *Rₜ* across hundreds of jurisdictions; retrospective analyses showed that the timing of *Rₜ* crossing below unity predicted subsequent declines in hospitalizations with high fidelity, validating the renewal-equation framework at global scale [6][9].

## 6. Limitations

No honest treatment can omit where the theory strains.

- **Homogeneous mixing and the exponential infectious period.** The clean *R₀ = β/γ* and geometric offspring results rest on memoryless infectious periods and random contacts. Real infectious periods have peaked distributions, and real contact networks are clustered and assortative; each departure changes the *r–R₀* mapping and the extinction probability, sometimes substantially [2][4].
- **Observation is not infection.** Inference operates on *reported* cases, which lag infection by incubation plus reporting delays and are thinned by under-ascertainment. Deconvolution of delays is ill-posed; naive application of EpiEstim to raw reported counts biases *Rₜ* and, worse, misattributes the timing of turning points [6][8].
- **The branching-process approximation is local in time.** It governs only the early phase; once cumulative infections reach a non-negligible fraction of *N*, susceptible depletion invalidates it. Rigorous coupling results quantify the breakdown scale as *o(N)* [4], but finite populations with *N* in the hundreds sit uncomfortably between regimes.
- **Generation-interval instability.** The distribution *w* changes under interventions (isolation truncates the infectious period) and across variants. Treating it as fixed, as EpiEstim does, injects systematic bias precisely when estimates matter most [6].
- **R₀ is not a pathogen constant.** It conflates biology, behavior, demography, and environment. Cross-setting comparisons of *R₀* are comparisons of entire socio-biological systems, not of viruses — a caveat routinely violated in public discourse [2][3].

## 7. Conclusion

From Kermack and McKendrick's 1927 threshold, through the stochastic reformulation as a density-dependent Markov chain, to the branching-process characterization of outbreak establishment and the Bayesian inversion that yields real-time *Rₜ* estimates, the theory of epidemic modeling exhibits a remarkable coherence: *the same quantity, the reproduction number, organizes the deterministic threshold, the stochastic extinction probability, and the statistical inverse problem.* The final-size equation predicts how far an epidemic will go; the generating-function equation predicts whether it will go at all; the renewal equation, inverted by Bayes' rule, tells us where we are in the trajectory right now.

Three lessons deserve emphasis. First, stochasticity is not noise atop a deterministic signal — in the early phase it *is* the signal, deciding between extinction and explosion with probabilities the ODE cannot see. Second, *R₀* and *Rₜ* are functionals of a full transmission system, and every estimate carries the fingerprints of its definition, its generation-interval assumption, and its observation model. Third, the path from theory to operations — EpiEstim dashboards guiding policy during a pandemic — shows that century-old mathematics, when married to modern Bayesian computation, becomes actionable public-health infrastructure.

Open frontiers remain: joint inference of time-varying generation intervals and *Rₜ* from integrated genomic and incidence data [5 in search], rigorous stochastic theory on temporal networks, and principled uncertainty quantification that propagates every modeling choice into the credible interval. The mathematics of epidemics is, fittingly, itself an expanding process — and there is every reason to expect it to keep growing.
