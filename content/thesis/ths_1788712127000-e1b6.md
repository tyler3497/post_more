# Coverage-Guided Fuzzing: AFL++ Mutation Engines, Sanitizer Instrumentation, and Whitebox Hybrid Fuzzing with Symbolic Execution

## Abstract

Coverage-guided fuzzing has become the dominant paradigm for automated vulnerability discovery in systems software. This thesis examines the architecture and theory of modern greybox fuzzers descended from Michal Zalewski's American Fuzzy Lop (AFL), with emphasis on the AFL++ mutation engine, sanitizer-based instrumentation, and the hybridization of fuzzing with symbolic execution. We formalize the coverage-guided fuzzing loop as a stochastic search over program paths, analyze the forkserver execution model and the edge-coverage bitmap that sustain tens of thousands of executions per second, and dissect the deterministic, havoc, and splice mutation stages together with power schedules that allocate fuzzing energy across seeds. We then examine whitebox techniques — Godefroid's SAGE generational search and the Driller system's selective concolic execution — that recover deep program paths where pure mutation stalls. Empirical evidence from the AFLFast, Driller, and SAGE evaluations is reviewed alongside methodological concerns in fuzzer benchmarking. We conclude with the limitations that keep coverage-guided fuzzing an open research problem.

## 1 Introduction

Fuzz testing — the automated generation of unexpected inputs to provoke failures — is among the oldest ideas in software testing, yet it remained a blunt instrument for decades. Classical blackbox fuzzers mutate seed inputs blindly: they know nothing about the program under test and therefore exercise, with overwhelming probability, only the shallowest parsing code. The then-branch of `if (x == 0xDEADBEEF)` is taken with probability 2^-32 per random 32-bit draw; deep logic guarded by such *magic values*, checksums, or length fields is effectively invisible to blind mutation [5].

The watershed came in 2013, when Michal Zalewski released American Fuzzy Lop (AFL) [1]. AFL's insight was to close the loop: instrument the target so that each execution reports the control-flow edges it traversed, and retain only those mutants that discover *new* edges. Inputs became individuals in an evolutionary process; edge coverage became fitness. Combined with a forkserver that reduced per-execution overhead from milliseconds to microseconds, AFL could evaluate tens of thousands of inputs per second and, famously, synthesize a valid JPEG from the string "hello" in hours [1].

This thesis studies the modern descendants of that design. Section 2 situates coverage-guided (greybox) fuzzing between blackbox and whitebox testing and introduces the instrumentation primitives — edge coverage, hit-count bucketing, sanitizers — on which everything else rests. Section 3 formalizes the fuzzing loop as a stochastic search and derives the role of power schedules from a Markov-chain model of path discovery [6]. Section 4 dives into five technical pillars: (4.1) compile-time instrumentation and the forkserver; (4.2) the AFL++ mutation engine's deterministic, havoc, and splice stages; (4.3) seed scheduling from round-robin to entropic power schedules; (4.4) sanitizer instrumentation and in-process fuzzing with libFuzzer; and (4.5) whitebox hybrid fuzzing, from SAGE's generational search [5] to Driller's selective concolic execution [4]. Section 5 reviews the empirical record; Section 6, the limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 A spectrum of program knowledge

Fuzzers are conventionally classified by how much program knowledge they exploit:

- **Blackbox fuzzers** treat the target as opaque. They mutate seeds or generate inputs from a grammar, observing only crashes. Peach and early file fuzzers belong here: cheap per execution, but coverage grows slowly.
- **Whitebox fuzzers** exploit full program semantics. SAGE [5] traces x86 instructions, builds symbolic constraints for every branch on attacker-controlled bytes, and asks an SMT solver (Z3) to negate constraints and produce inputs driving new paths. Coverage per execution is excellent; executions per second are not — SAGE processed on the order of thousands of instructions per second against AFL's near-native speed.
- **Greybox (coverage-guided) fuzzers** occupy the middle: lightweight compile-time or binary instrumentation reports *which* code ran, but the fuzzer reasons about inputs only statistically. AFL, AFL++, libFuzzer, and honggfuzz live here. Edge coverage proved to be the empirical sweet spot in the throughput-of-discovery tradeoff [6].

### 2.2 Coverage as feedback

The feedback signal must be cheap to compute, compact to store, and informative enough to distinguish interesting executions. AFL's answer is the **edge-coverage bitmap**: a 64 KiB shared-memory region in which each control-flow edge *(prev_block to cur_block)* maps to one byte [1]:

```c
/* AFL's edge instrumentation, conceptually */
cur_location = <compile-time random constant for this block>;
shared_mem[cur_location ^ prev_location]++;   /* record edge */
prev_location = cur_location >> 1;            /* shift preserves direction */
```

The right-shift of `prev_location` distinguishes the edge *A to B* from *B to A*; the XOR folds the pair into the 16-bit index space. Collisions are possible but rare enough in practice, and AFL++ later introduced collision-free instrumentation variants [2]. Raw hit counts are then **bucketed** into eight classes — 1, 2, 3, 4-7, 8-15, 16-31, 32-127, 128+ — so that only *logarithmically significant* changes in loop-trip counts register as "new" behavior. A tuple is interesting if it sets a bit in a virgin-bytes map no prior input set.

### 2.3 Sanitizers: turning silent corruption into crashes

Coverage finds paths; **sanitizers** turn the memory-safety violations lurking on those paths into observable crashes. AddressSanitizer (ASan), MemorySanitizer (MSan), and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer (UBSan) — developed by Serebryany and colleagues at Google and within LLVM [3][7] — instrument memory accesses and undefined operations at compile time, typically at 2-3x slowdown for ASan. The modern recipe, *"sanitize, fuzz, and harden,"* pairs a coverage-guided fuzzer with ASan+UBSan builds so that out-of-bounds reads, use-after-free, and integer-undefined behavior become fatal signals the fuzzer can harvest [7]. SanitizerCoverage, the `-fsanitize-coverage=` family of flags, additionally exposes edge, basic-block, and 8-bit-counter feedback to in-process fuzzers [3].

## 3 Methodology

We formalize coverage-guided fuzzing as a stochastic search. Let *P* be the program, *S* = {s1, ..., sn} the seed corpus, and *C(s)* the set of coverage tuples exercised by input *s*. The fuzzer iterates:

1. **Select** a seed *s* in *S* according to a scheduling policy.
2. **Assign energy** *e(s)* — the number of mutants to generate from *s* — per a power schedule.
3. **Mutate** *s* to *s'* via deterministic or random operators.
4. **Execute** *P(s')* in a forked child; collect *C(s')* and any crash.
5. **Retain** *s'* in *S* iff *C(s')* minus the union of *C(s)* over *S* is nonempty (new coverage), or *P(s')* crashes.

This is a *Markov chain* over corpus states: each seed is a state, and mutating seed *i* yields seed *j*'s coverage with some transition probability *p_ij* [6]. Bohme, Pham, and Roychoudhury showed that the expected time to discover new paths is minimized not by uniform energy, but by a schedule that favors seeds whose stationary probability is low — i.e., seeds exercising *rare* paths:

> **Theorem 1:** *Let pi_i be the stationary probability of the Markov chain induced by fuzzing seed i. Assigning energy e(i) proportional to 1/pi_i, with a monotone-increasing correction each time seed i is chosen, minimizes the expected number of executions to discover a previously unseen path relative to any schedule monotone in pi_i [6].*

In practice, pi_i is estimated by how often the fuzzer already exercises seed *i*'s paths: the exponential schedule of AFLFast [6] multiplies energy for seeds that keep yielding novelty and starves seeds stuck on high-frequency paths. AFL++ generalizes this into a family of schedules (`fast`, `explore`, `coe`, `exploit`, `rare`, `mmopt`) selectable per campaign [2].

Energy allocation is only half the battle; the other half is *what* mutations to apply, which is the subject of section 4.2.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Compile-Time Instrumentation and the Forkserver Architecture

Throughput is the binding constraint on everything in section 3: a fuzzer that executes 100 inputs per second explores two orders of magnitude less than one executing 10,000 per second. Two AFL innovations jointly removed the bottleneck [1].

**Instrumentation.** The `afl-gcc`/`afl-clang` wrappers inject the edge-recording snippet of section 2.2 at every basic block, plus a `__afl_maybe_log` guard that skips the shared-memory setup cost when the forkserver is absent. LLVM-mode instrumentation (`afl-clang-fast`) operates at the IR level and supports selective instrumentation, never-zero counters, and — in AFL++ — **cmplog**: logging the operands of comparison instructions and calls, which lets the fuzzer observe *how close* a mutant came to satisfying a magic-value check, in the spirit of REDQUEEN's input-to-state correspondence [2].

**The forkserver.** Process creation via `execve()` costs roughly a millisecond — dynamic linking, loader work, `main()` startup. AFL instead launches the target *once*; the instrumented binary, immediately after initialization, stops and waits on a pipe. For each test case, `afl-fuzz` requests a `fork()`: the child inherits the fully initialized address space copy-on-write, reads the test case, runs to completion, and reports its exit status and coverage bitmap through shared memory. Fork latency is tens of microseconds, yielding the characteristic 5,000-50,000 execs/sec on single binaries [1]. Persistent mode (in-process looping over `__AFL_LOOP`) and libFuzzer's fully in-process model [3] push throughput further for library harnesses, at the cost of requiring harness cooperation and tolerating in-process state pollution.

### 4.2 The Mutation Engine: Deterministic Stages, Havoc, and Splice

AFL++'s mutation engine applies operators in two phases [1][2]. **Deterministic stages** walk every offset of the seed systematically — bit flips of widths 1, 2, and 4; byte flips of 8, 16, 32; arithmetic addition and subtraction of small constants at 8/16/32-bit widths; substitution of *interesting values* (`-1`, `0`, `1`, `INT_MAX`, ...); and dictionary insertions from user-supplied or auto-discovered tokens. Deterministic mutation is exhaustive and reproducible: it finds the surgical single-byte bugs. **Havoc** then stacks randomly chosen operators at random positions for thousands of iterations — the source of serendipitous deep discoveries. **Splice** performs crossover between two seeds, recombining independently discovered features. AFL++ extends this core with:

- **MOpt**, which treats mutator selection as an optimization problem solved online with Particle Swarm Optimization, learning which operators pay off for the current target [2];
- **cmplog / RedQueen**, which replaces blind guessing of magic bytes with observed comparison operands;
- **custom mutators** (Python and C APIs) and grammar-aware plugins for structured formats.

The deterministic-havoc split encodes a deliberate bet: systematic local search first, then heavy-tailed random jumps — an arrangement repeatedly vindicated in FuzzBench-style evaluations.

### 4.3 Seed Scheduling and Power Schedules

Which seed to fuzz next, and how much energy to spend on it, determines the *order* in which the path space is explored. Classical AFL cycled seeds round-robin with a crude favoring heuristic (fast, small, novel seeds preferred). AFLFast [6] replaced this with the Markov-chain-derived exponential schedule of Theorem 1: seeds receive energy inversely proportional to the estimated density of their stationary distribution, so rare-path seeds get exponentially more mutations. AFL++ ships a portfolio — `fast` (AFLFast++), `explore`, `coe` (cut-off exponential), `lin`, `quad`, `exploit`, `rare`, `mmopt` — because no single schedule dominates across targets; *explore*-heavy schedules win early in a campaign, *exploit*-heavy schedules win once the easy frontier is exhausted [2]. More recent work derives **Entropic**, an information-theoretic schedule that maximizes the Shannon entropy of exercised behaviors per execution. The practical lesson is robust across all variants: *most executions exercise the same few high-frequency paths, and energy must be actively steered away from them*.

```python
# Sketch of a cut-off exponential power schedule (after AFLFast)
def energy(seed, total_paths_through_seed, alpha=5, max_energy=10000):
    # rare paths -> high energy; cap runaway allocation
    raw = alpha * (1.0 / max(1, total_paths_through_seed))
    return int(min(raw, max_energy))
```

### 4.4 Sanitizer Instrumentation and In-Process Fuzzing

Where AFL++ is process-per-execution by default, **libFuzzer** [3] takes the in-process route: the user writes a harness

```c
int LLVMFuzzerTestOneInput(const uint8_t *data, size_t size) {
    parse(data, size);   /* library entry point under test */
    return 0;
}
```

compiled with `-fsanitize=fuzzer,address,undefined`. SanitizerCoverage instruments every edge (or basic block, or with 8-bit counters a la AFL) and calls back into the libFuzzer runtime, which maintains its own corpus in memory, mutates with its internal engine, and writes only coverage-increasing inputs to the corpus directory. Because there is no fork and no IPC, libFuzzer sustains hundreds of thousands of executions per second on small harnesses, and the ASan/UBSan instrumentation converts silent corruption into immediate, attributable crashes [7]. The tradeoff is *statefulness*: in-process iteration can leak state between test cases, and harness quality dominates results — a lesson the OSS-Fuzz project operationalized by pairing libFuzzer and AFL++ harnesses with continuous cloud execution across hundreds of open-source projects [3]. Honggfuzz occupies a middle ground, supporting both in-process (persistent) and fork-based modes with hardware feedback extensions.

| Fuzzer | Instrumentation | Execution model | Feedback signal | Typical throughput |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AFL (2013) [1] | compile-time edge bitmap | forkserver | edge + hit-count buckets | 1-50k exec/s |
| AFL++ [2] | LLVM/QEMU/unicorn, cmplog | forkserver / persistent | edge, cmplog operands | 1-100k exec/s |
| libFuzzer [3] | SanitizerCoverage | in-process | edge / 8-bit counters | 100k-1M exec/s |
| SAGE [5] | x86 instruction tracing | concolic re-execution | path constraints to Z3 | thousands instr/s |
| Driller [4] | AFL bitmap + angr VEX | fork + selective concolic | edges; solver-generated | hybrid |

### 4.5 Whitebox Hybrid Fuzzing: SAGE and Driller

Pure mutation stalls at *coverage walls*: checksums, magic numbers, and deeply nested guards whose satisfaction probability under random mutation is negligible. Two landmark systems attack the wall with symbolic reasoning.

**SAGE** (Scalable Automated Guided Execution) [5] pioneered *whitebox fuzzing* at Microsoft. From a well-formed seed input, SAGE records an x86 instruction trace, symbolically evaluates the trace to gather a *path constraint* — a conjunction of branch conditions over input bytes — then negates constraints one by one and asks Z3 for satisfying assignments, producing a *generational* family of children per parent run. A coverage-maximizing search heuristic prioritizes the frontier. Without any format knowledge, SAGE reproduced the MS07-017 ANI vulnerability missed by extensive blackbox fuzzing and static analysis, and during Windows 7 development found roughly one-third of all bugs discovered by file fuzzing [5]. Its cost is throughput: symbolic emulation runs at thousands of instructions per second, so SAGE is a scalpel, not a shovel.

**Driller** [4] (Stephens et al., NDSS 2016) proposed the hybrid division of labor that now dominates: let the fast greybox fuzzer do the bulk exploration, and invoke *selective* concolic execution only when the fuzzer gets stuck. Driller partitions the program into *compartments* separated by *specific* inputs — inputs the fuzzer is unlikely to guess (magic values, checksums) — versus *generic* inputs it mutates freely. When AFL's discovery rate flatlines, Driller lifts the stuck seed into angr, symbolically executes with the concrete input as guidance, solves the negated branch constraints blocking new compartments, and feeds the solver-produced inputs back to the fuzzer, which resumes high-throughput mutation from the newly unlocked frontier. Evaluated on 126 binaries from the DARPA Cyber Grand Challenge qualifying event, Driller found as many vulnerabilities as the top-scoring fully autonomous team and substantially more basic-block coverage than AFL or pure concolic execution alone [4]. Modern hybrids (QSYM, SymCC) refine the same loop with faster concolic engines, but Driller's *selective* insight — symbolic execution as a stuck-detector's assistant rather than the main search — remains the field's working consensus.

## 5 Empirical Evidence

The empirical record supports three central claims, each with methodological caveats worth stating plainly.

**Power schedules work.** In the AFLFast evaluation [6], the exponential schedule exposed 3 previously unreported CVEs in 24 hours that baseline AFL missed entirely, found 6 shared CVEs on average 7x faster, and produced at least an order of magnitude more unique crashes. The Markov-chain model predicted, and the data confirmed, that steering energy toward low-frequency paths dominates uniform allocation.

**Hybridization breaks coverage walls.** Driller's evaluation on 126 CGC binaries [4] showed the hybrid discovering vulnerabilities at the level of the best autonomous CGC competitor, while neither AFL alone nor concolic execution alone reached that mark — direct evidence that the fuzzer/concolic alternation is super-additive rather than merely additive.

**Whitebox fuzzing finds real bugs at scale.** SAGE's deployment inside Microsoft [5] attributed roughly a third of Windows 7-era file-fuzzing bugs to whitebox runs, including the MS07-017 ANI case that blackbox campaigns had missed — the canonical demonstration that constraint solving pays for itself on hard targets.

Two caveats temper these results. First, fuzzer evaluations are notoriously sensitive to benchmark choice: Klees et al.'s "Evaluating Fuzz Testing" showed that AFL-vs-AFLFast conclusions flip between programs like FFmpeg and binutils, and the community now recommends Mann-Whitney U tests over multiple trials rather than single-run crash counts [6]. Second, coverage correlates with bug-finding but imperfectly — the marginal cost of each new vulnerability grows exponentially with campaign length, which is why continuous services like OSS-Fuzz emphasize fixing found bugs so the campaign can progress to deeper code [3].

## 6 Limitations

Coverage-guided fuzzing remains, in Bohme, Cadar, and Roychoudhury's phrase, a field of *challenges as much as solved problems* [6]:

1. **Coverage walls persist.** Checksums, cryptographic checks, and multi-byte magic values still defeat pure mutation; cmplog and RedQueen-style input-to-state correspondence mitigate but do not eliminate the problem, and solver-backed hybrids pay steep throughput costs.
2. **The oracle problem.** Fuzzers detect crashes, hangs, and sanitizer violations — not logic bugs, information leaks through legitimate outputs, or specification violations. Coverage of code is not coverage of *behaviors*.
3. **Throughput versus precision.** Every improvement in feedback fidelity (finer-grained coverage, heavier sanitizers, symbolic reasoning) costs executions per second. The optimal point on this frontier is target-dependent and poorly characterized.
4. **Evaluation validity.** Small benchmark suites, single-trial results, and crash-count metrics without statistical testing continue to plague the literature; the community's benchmarking discipline (FuzzBench, Magma) is improving but incomplete [6].
5. **Seed and harness dependence.** Campaign outcomes hinge on seed quality and, for in-process fuzzers, harness fidelity — factors rarely controlled in comparisons and difficult to automate.
6. **Statefulness and environment.** Forkserver and persistent modes assume hermetic, deterministic targets; networked, multithreaded, or time-dependent programs violate these assumptions and produce flaky coverage signals.

## 7 Conclusion

Coverage-guided fuzzing succeeded because it made a pragmatic trade: *just enough* program knowledge — edge coverage in a 64 KiB bitmap — to steer a *very fast* random search. The AFL lineage refined every component of that loop: the forkserver removed the process-spawning bottleneck, the deterministic/havoc/splice mutation engine balanced systematic and serendipitous search, power schedules allocated energy where the Markov-chain model said it mattered, and sanitizer instrumentation turned silent corruption into signal. Where mutation alone stalls, whitebox hybrids — SAGE's generational constraint solving and Driller's selective concolic execution — supply the precision that speed cannot. The empirical record, from AFLFast's CVEs to Driller's CGC performance to SAGE's Windows 7 bug haul, shows each refinement compounding rather than merely adding. The open problems are equally clear: principled handling of coverage walls, honest benchmarking, richer oracles, and the throughput-precision frontier. Fuzzing remains equal parts hack, art, and science — and its scientific half is still being written.
