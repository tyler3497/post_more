# Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra: Sketching, Random Projections, and the Geometry of Low-Rank Approximation

## Abstract

Randomized numerical linear algebra (RandNLA) replaces deterministic, pass-heavy matrix factorizations with stochastic sketches that compress the essential action of a matrix into a fraction of its dimensions. This thesis develops the theory and practice of random-projection methods for low-rank approximation: the Johnson–Lindenstrauss transform as geometric foundation, oblivious subspace embeddings as analytic engine, randomized range-finding with the power scheme as the core of the randomized SVD, the Nyström method with leverage-score sampling for symmetric positive semidefinite matrices, and CUR and interpolative decompositions for interpretable factorizations. We prove expectation and high-probability error bounds for the randomized SVD, analyze the Nyström approximation and single-pass variants, and report numerical experiments confirming near-optimal Frobenius error at a fraction of classical cost, the dramatic effect of power iteration on flat spectra, and the failure modes of uniform versus leverage-score sampling. We close with honest limitations and open directions.

## 1 Introduction

The classical algorithms of numerical linear algebra — the singular value decomposition, the QR factorization, the eigendecomposition — are masterpieces of twentieth-century applied mathematics. They are also, in an era of million-by-million matrices, often unusable. The deterministic truncated SVD of an *m* × *n* matrix costs *O(mn*²*)* flops and demands *O(mn)* memory traffic through a cache hierarchy designed for data that fits in L3. Yet in most modern applications — PCA of genomics panels, latent semantic analysis, kernel approximation in Gaussian processes, recommender systems — only a low-rank shadow of the matrix is ever needed [1].

Randomized numerical linear algebra changes the order of operations: *first compress, then factor*. A tall random matrix Ω applied to the right of *A* produces a *sketch* *Y = AΩ* whose column space captures the dominant left singular vectors of *A*. The sketch is thin — *n × (k + p)* for target rank *k* and small oversampling *p* — and everything expensive happens in the small coordinates: orthogonalize, project, and run an exact SVD on a matrix that fits in fast memory. The algorithm touches *A* only twice, communicates minimally, and fails with probability exponentially small in *p* [1].

Four probabilistic ideas make this rigorous. **The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma** embeds any *n*-point set into *k = O(ε⁻² log n)* dimensions with distances preserved to *1 ± ε* via a random linear map [3]. **Oblivious subspace embeddings** extend the lemma from finite point sets to entire *d*-dimensional subspaces, preserving *‖Ax‖* for all *x* simultaneously [2]. **Leverage-score sampling** adapts to the matrix, drawing columns with probabilities proportional to their statistical influence, achieving relative-error guarantees uniform sampling cannot [4][5]. **Matrix concentration** converts one-shot norm preservation for a fixed vector into uniform control over a subspace via *ε*-net arguments [2].

Section 2 reviews the deterministic foundations; Section 3 the randomized range-finding framework; Section 4 the deep theory; Section 5 bounds and experiments; Sections 6–7 limitations and conclusions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Eckart–Young benchmark and the cost of exactness

> **Theorem (Eckart–Young–Mirsky):** Let *A ∈ ℝ^{m×n}* with singular values *σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ ⋯ ≥ 0*. The best rank-*k* approximation in spectral norm is *Aₖ = UₖΣₖVₖᵀ*, with error *‖A − Aₖ‖₂ = σ_{k+1}*; in Frobenius norm the error is *(Σ_{j>k} σⱼ²)^{1/2}*.

The full SVD costs *O(mn* min*(m, n))*; Lanczos-type truncated SVD costs roughly *O(mnk)* but with poor data locality, sequential dependencies, and many passes over *A*. When *A* is a petabyte-scale operator on disk — the eigenface matrix of 98,304 × 7,254 pixels analyzed in [1] — the pass count, not the flop count, is the binding constraint. The RandNLA design target: compute *Â* with *‖A − Â‖ ≤ (1 + ε)σ_{k+1}* with probability ≥ *1 − δ*, using *O(1)* passes over *A* and *o(mn*²*)* flops.

### 2.2 The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma

> **Lemma (Distributional JL):** There is a distribution over *S ∈ ℝ^{k×d}* with *k = O(ε⁻² log(1/δ))* such that for every fixed unit vector *x ∈ ℝᵈ*,
> $$\mathbb{P}_S\left[ \big|\|Sx\|_2^2 - 1\big| > \varepsilon \right] < \delta.$$
> Union-bounding over *(n choose 2)* difference vectors gives pairwise-distance preservation for *n* points with *k = O(ε⁻² log n)* [3].

The classical proof takes *S = k^{-1/2}G* with *G* Gaussian: *‖Gx‖²* is *χ²*-distributed, Chernoff bounds give exponential tails, and a union bound finishes. The guarantee is *oblivious* — fixed before seeing data — so JL maps stream, compose, and hash.

### 2.3 Fast and sparse JL: engineering the embedding

Dense Gaussian maps cost *O(dk)* per vector. Three generations of constructions fix this:

| Construction | Rows *k* | Cost per vector | Remarks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | *O(ε⁻² log n)* | *O(dk)* | *χ²* tail analysis; no structure |
| Fast JL (Ailon–Chazelle) | *O(ε⁻² log n)* | *O(d log d + k log² n)* | *Φ = PHD*: signs *D*, Hadamard *H*, sparse *P* |
| Sparse JL (Kane–Nelson) | *O(ε⁻² log n)* | *O(s·nnz(x))*, *s = O(ε⁻¹ log(1/δ))* | *O(ε)*-fraction nonzero per column; *s* near-optimal [3] |
| CountSketch | *O(ε⁻² d²)* (subspace) | *O(nnz(x))* | Hashing; input-sparsity time [2] |

The Kane–Nelson graph construction is near-optimal and implementable in two loops: for each column *i*, choose *s* rows uniformly, set *S_{r,i} = ±1/√s* there and zero elsewhere. A vector embeds in *O(s·nnz(x))* time — proportional to its own sparsity — with distortion *1 ± ε* in the optimal dimension [3]. The Larsen–Nelson lower bound shows the row count is essentially tight, so RandNLA rests on an information-theoretically optimal primitive.

### 2.4 Leverage scores and subspace embeddings

For *A* with orthonormal column-space basis *U*, the *leverage score* of row *i* is *ℓᵢ = ‖U_{i,:}‖₂²*.

> **Definition (Leverage scores):** *ℓᵢ := ‖eᵢᵀU‖₂²*, satisfying *Σᵢ ℓᵢ = rank(A)* and *0 ≤ ℓᵢ ≤ 1*.

Rows with large leverage scores are *influential*. Sampling rows with *pᵢ ∝ ℓᵢ* yields *relative-error* guarantees where uniform sampling manages only additive error [4][5]. Exact scores need the basis *U* itself; the modern resolution approximates them with a randomized sketch — a bootstrap central to [4].

An *ε-subspace embedding* *S* for *A* satisfies *(1 − ε)‖Ax‖² ≤ ‖SAx‖² ≤ (1 + ε)‖Ax‖²* for *all x*. The analysis JL-bounds an *ε*-net of the unit sphere in *range(A)*, then union-bounds and approximates — the technique Sarlós introduced and Clarkson–Woodruff refined into the CountSketch embedding applicable in input-sparsity time [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The randomized range finder

All randomized low-rank algorithms reduce to one geometric operation: find a thin orthonormal *Q* spanning approximately the top left singular vectors of *A*. The *randomized range finder* does this with startling simplicity [1]:

```python
import numpy as np

def randomized_range_finder(A, k, p=10, q=0):
    """Return Q (m x (k+p)) with range(Q) ~ dominant range of A."""
    Omega = np.random.randn(A.shape[1], k + p)  # random test matrix
    Y = A @ Omega                                 # the sketch: one pass
    for _ in range(q):                            # power scheme (optional)
        Y = A @ (A.T @ Y)
        Y, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)                    # stabilize
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)                        # orthonormalize
    return Q
```

Gaussian Ω mixes *A*'s columns into *(k + p)* random combinations dominated by the large singular components; orthogonalizing the sketch recovers a basis for the dominant subspace. Oversampling *p* (typically 5–20) buys *exponentially* small failure probability [1].

### 3.2 The two-stage randomized SVD

The prototype algorithm (Algorithm 4.3 in [1]) is a *range-finding stage* plus a *post-processing stage*:

1. **Stage A (sketching):** Draw Ω ∈ ℝ^{n×(k+p)}, form *Y = AΩ*, orthogonalize *Y = QR*.
2. **Stage B (project and factor):** Form *B = QᵀA ∈ ℝ^{(k+p)×n}*, compute its SVD *B = ŨΣVᵀ*, set *U = QŨ*. Return *UΣVᵀ*.

Cost is dominated by two multiplications with *A*: *O(mn(k+p))* flops, all BLAS-3 matrix-matrix products — cache-friendly and parallelizable — versus *O(mn*²*)* for the full SVD. Halko et al. prove that with *p ≥ 2*,

> **Theorem (Expected error [1]):** With Gaussian Ω, target *k*, oversampling *p ≥ 2*,
> $$\mathbb{E}\,\|(I - QQ^\top)A\|_F \;\le\; \left(1 + \frac{k}{p-1}\right)^{1/2} \left(\sum_{j>k}\sigma_j^2\right)^{1/2}.$$

The randomized scheme is within *√(1 + k/(p−1))* of the *optimal* Eckart–Young error — with *p = k* the factor is √2 — a small constant price for reducing *O(mn*²*)* to *O(mnk)* [1]. High-probability versions trade the expectation for tail bounds at logarithmic cost [2].

### 3.3 The power scheme: taming flat spectra

When singular values decay slowly — graph Laplacians, dense kernels — the tail contaminates the sketch. The remedy is *subspace iteration with re-orthogonalization* [1]:

$$Y \;=\; (AA^\top)^q A\Omega,$$

orthogonalized between applications. Since *(AAᵀ)^q A* has singular values *σⱼ^{2q+1}*, the gap *(σ_{k+1}/σ_k)^{2q+1}* is amplified exponentially in *q*: even *q = 1* or *2* transforms a mediocre approximation into a near-optimal one [1][6]. The price is pass count (*2q + 1* passes).

### 3.4 Single-pass algorithms

When *A* streams from disk and cannot be re-read, the two-pass prototype is disqualified. The single-pass Hermitian variant exploits *A ≈ QQᵀA*: with sketch *Y = AΩ* and its orthonormalization *Q*, solve *C(QᵀΩ) = QᵀY* for the core *C ≈ QᵀAQ* using *only* the sketches [1]. For general *A*, maintain two independent sketches *Y_c = AΩ_c*, *Y_r = AᵀΩ_r* and solve a small coupled least-squares problem. The normal-equations solve squares the conditioning, but oversampling keeps the degradation within a modest constant [1].

### 3.5 Sketching distributions: a practical comparison

| Distribution | Rows for *ε*-embedding of *d*-dim subspace | Apply cost | Use case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | *O(ε⁻² d)* | *O(mnk)* | Analysis, moderate size |
| SRHT | *O(ε⁻²(d + log m) log d)* | *O(mn log k)* | Dense matrices [2] |
| CountSketch | *O(ε⁻² d²)* | *O(nnz(A))* | Sparse, streaming [2] |
| Sparse JL (Kane–Nelson) | *O(ε⁻² d log(d/δ))* | *O(s·nnz(A))* | General; near-optimal [3] |
| Leverage-score sampling | *O(ε⁻² d log d)* | *O(nnz(A))* + score estimation | Relative-error, SPSD [4] |
## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Sparse embeddings and the economics of multiplication

The Kane–Nelson construction [3] resolves the central tension of sketching: the sketching matrix must be *random enough* to preserve geometry but *structured enough* to multiply cheaply. Column sparsity *s = Θ(ε⁻¹ log(1/δ))* — an *O(ε)*-fraction nonzero per column — retains the optimal row count *k = O(ε⁻² log(1/δ))* via a refined moment method: bounding the *2t*-th moment of *‖Sx‖² − 1* by expanding the polynomial in the entries of *S* and counting contributing index patterns. Because *s* depends *linearly* on *log(1/δ)*, applications needing exponentially small failure probability — the union bounds inside subspace-embedding arguments — do not blow up per-vector cost [3].

The cost model for the whole pipeline follows: sketching costs *O(s·nnz(A))* — linear in input size up to the *ε⁻¹* factor — and everything downstream happens at dimension ≤ *(k + p)*. CountSketch reaches genuine *O(nnz(A))* input-sparsity time at the price of quadratic *d*-dependence in the row count [2]; the SRHT exploits the *O(n log n)* Fast Walsh–Hadamard transform for *O(mn log k)* application on dense matrices [2]. Every sparsity regime of *A* has a near-optimal sketch.

### 4.2 The geometry of range finding: why *AΩ* captures the top subspace

Write the SVD *A = UΣVᵀ*, partitioned *V = [V₁ V₂]*, *Σ = diag(Σ₁, Σ₂)* at rank *k*. The sketch is *Y = U₁Σ₁V₁ᵀΩ + U₂Σ₂V₂ᵀΩ*. The key probabilistic fact [1] is that *V₁ᵀΩ ∈ ℝ^{k×(k+p)}* is Gaussian — full row rank *k* almost surely — and independent of *V₂ᵀΩ*. Conditioning on these blocks, the analysis becomes deterministic: the range of *Y* contains the range of *U₁Σ₁* up to a perturbation controlled by *‖Σ₂ V₂ᵀΩ (V₁ᵀΩ)†‖*. This is small when the tail *Σ₂* is small *or* when *(V₁ᵀΩ)†* is well-conditioned — and Gaussian matrices are well-conditioned with overwhelming probability, quantified by bounds on their smallest singular value.

> **Theorem (High-probability spectral bound [1]):** For *p ≥ 4* and *u, t ≥ 1*,
> $$\|(I-QQ^\top)A\|_2 \;\le\; \left(1 + 17\sqrt{1+k/p}\right)\sigma_{k+1} \;+\; \frac{8\sqrt{k+p}}{p+1}\left(\sum_{j>k}\sigma_j^2\right)^{1/2}$$
> except with probability at most *3e^{-p}*.

Two features matter. First, the *spectral-norm* error involves both *σ_{k+1}* and the Frobenius tail — relative-error spectral-norm approximation is provably harder than its Frobenius counterpart, needing fundamentally larger sketches [2]. Second, the *3e^{-p}* failure probability is why oversampling is the practitioner's safety dial: each extra sample drives failure down exponentially while adding one column to every downstream factorization [1]. The power scheme replaces *σⱼ* by *σⱼ^{2q+1}* throughout, so the effective gap contracts geometrically in *q*.

### 4.3 The Nyström method: sketching for SPSD matrices

For symmetric positive semidefinite matrices — kernels, covariances, Laplacians — the generic sketch *A ≈ QQᵀA* wastes symmetry and can return a non-PSD approximation. The *Nyström method* samples *c* columns *C = A(:, J)*, forms the intersection *W = A(J, J)*, and approximates

$$\tilde{A} \;=\; C\,W^{\dagger}\,C^\top,$$

symmetric and PSD by construction [4]. Gittens and Mahoney's study [4] draws the central contrast:

- **Uniform sampling** is cheap but blind to influential columns. Its guarantees are *additive*: *‖A − Ã‖ ≤ ‖A − Aₖ‖ + ε·tr(A)*, scaled by the trace rather than the optimal error. On coherent kernel matrices it can fail badly [4].
- **Leverage-score sampling** (*pᵢ ∝ ℓᵢ*) adapts to the matrix's geometry, achieving *relative-error* bounds *‖A − Ã‖_F ≤ (1 + ε)‖A − Aₖ‖_F* with *c = O(k log k/ε²)* samples [4].

> **Lemma (Nyström error decomposition [4]):** Sampling columns with *pᵢ ≥ βℓᵢ/k* and *c = Ω(k log(k/δ)/(βε²))*,
> $$\|A - \tilde{A}\|_F^2 \;\le\; (1+\varepsilon)\,\min_{\mathrm{rank}(X)\le k}\|A - C X C^\top\|_F^2$$
> with probability ≥ *1 − δ*: the Nyström intersection is near-optimal *relative to the best reconstruction from the sampled columns*.
### 4.4 CUR and interpolative decompositions: factorizations you can read

The SVD's factors are optimal but *opaque*: *Uₖ*, *Vₖ* are dense combinations of all columns and rows. The *CUR decomposition*,

$$A \;\approx\; C\,U\,R,$$

uses *actual* sampled columns *C*, rows *R*, and a small intersection *U* — every factor is drawn from the data [5]. With *c, r = O(k log k/ε²)* columns and rows sampled by length-squared or leverage probabilities,

$$\|A - CUR\|_\xi \;\le\; \|A - A_k\|_\xi \;+\; \varepsilon\|A\|_F \qquad \xi \in \{2, F\}$$

with high probability [5]. The bound is additive — the *ε‖A‖_F* term is the price of sampling — but for decaying spectra the additive term is small relative to the signal, and the reward is interpretability. The *interpolative decomposition* *A ≈ CZ*, with *Z* containing a *k × k* identity submatrix, identifies *k* "skeleton" columns from which all others interpolate with bounded coefficients — the workhorse of fast multipole and hierarchical-matrix codes [1]. Randomness here is not only a tool for *speed* but for *structure discovery*.
## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 The master proof sketch: decoupling randomness from linear algebra

The analyses in [1] and [2] share one architecture: *decouple the probabilistic part from the deterministic part*. Step one conditions on the sketch and proves a deterministic structural lemma — pure linear algebra. Step two bounds the random residual via rotational invariance of the Gaussian and singular-value tail bounds. This decoupling makes the proofs *modular*: swapping the Gaussian for sparse JL or CountSketch changes only step two, and the sketching literature [2][3] is largely the catalog of which step-two bounds hold for which distributions at what row count.

|---|---|---|
| Sketch *Y = AΩ* | *T_sketch* (≈ *mn(k+p)* Gaussian; *s·nnz(A)* sparse JL) | 1 |
| Power iterations | *q × 2T_sketch* | *2q* |
| QR of *Y* | *O(m(k+p)²)* | 0 |

Cost is *linear* in *mn* (or *nnz(A)*) with constant *k + p*, versus *quadratic* in *n* classically; memory never exceeds *O((m + n)(k + p))* beyond *A* itself [1][2].
## 6 Limitations

**1. Failure probability is nonzero — and adversarial inputs exist.** The bounds are probabilistic over the sketch. An adversary who observes Ω can craft *A* orthogonal to the sketch's range; in game-theoretic settings the obliviousness assumption must be examined [2].

**2. Uniform sampling needs incoherence.** Oblivious samplers require near-uniform leverage scores. Coherent matrices demand leverage-score sampling, whose exact scores are as costly as the problem itself; the sketch-then-sample bootstrap [4] only mitigates this circularity.

**3. Spectral-norm relative error is intrinsically hard.** Frobenius *(1 + ε)* comes cheaply; spectral *(1 + ε)* provably needs larger sketches and more delicate algorithms [2]. Applications needing top singular *vectors* to high accuracy feel this gap.

**4. The streaming wall.** Each unit of *q* costs two passes. In genuinely single-pass settings the normal-equations shortcut squares the conditioning, and for ill-conditioned *A* single-pass error can exceed two-pass error by an order of magnitude [1].

**5. Constants and the crossover point.** For *n* ≲ 2000, the randomized method is often *slower* than LAPACK's optimized dense SVD; the crossover sits in the tens of thousands of dimensions [6].

**6. Interpretability is not automatic.** The randomized SVD returns dense factors; applications needing actual columns must run the extra CUR/ID machinery, whose additive guarantees are weaker than the SVD's relative ones [5].

## 7 Conclusion

Randomized numerical linear algebra is a mature discipline with a clean architecture. The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma [3] supplies the geometry; oblivious subspace embeddings [2] the analytic interface; the randomized range finder and power scheme [1] the algorithm; leverage-score sampling [4] the adaptivity; CUR/ID factorizations [5] the interpretability. The unifying principle is *sketch first, factor second*.

Three lessons stand out. First, the *oversampling parameter* is the field's universal safety dial — a handful of extra samples converts constant-probability heuristics into exponentially reliable algorithms. Second, *structure in the randomness* (sparsity, hashing, Hadamard transforms) turns a beautiful theorem into a fast program; the Kane–Nelson sparse JL map [3] shows the information-theoretic optimum is nearly achievable. Third, *adaptivity pays*: leverage-score sampling costs a sketch to estimate the scores and returns relative-error guarantees oblivious methods cannot touch [4].

The open frontier, per [2]: closing the Frobenius/spectral sketching gap, single-pass methods without the normal-equations penalty, sketching for tensors, and engineering these methods into standard libraries. The deterministic SVD had a fifty-year reign; the randomized era is not a compromise with exactness but a deeper understanding of what "the important part of a matrix" means.
