# Quantum Error Mitigation on Near-Term Quantum Devices: A Unified Analysis of Zero-Noise Extrapolation, Probabilistic Error Cancellation, and Learning-Based Techniques

## Abstract

Quantum error mitigation (QEM) designates a family of software-level protocols that suppress the effect of physical noise on estimated expectation values without the substantial qubit and gate overhead demanded by full quantum error correction [1][2]. This thesis develops a unified mathematical framework for the principal mitigation protocols: zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE), probabilistic error cancellation (PEC), Clifford data regression (CDR), purification-based virtual distillation and error suppression by derangement (ESD), and scalable measurement error mitigation. We derive the Richardson extrapolation estimator and its variance amplification, characterize the quasi-probability decomposition underlying PEC together with its one-norm sampling overhead $\gamma^{2n}$, and analyze the regression ansatz of CDR, which exploits classical simulability of near-Clifford circuits. We examine purification approaches estimating expectation values against $\rho^M/\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)$, prove their exponential suppression of incoherent errors, and we treat readout mitigation through reduced assignment-matrix inversion. A formal bias–variance–overhead comparison establishes the fundamental distinction between mitigation and correction: mitigation cannot reduce the logical failure rate of arbitrary-depth computation, and sampling costs are lower-bounded exponentially in the error volume [12]. We close with decision guidance for protocol selection in the NISQ regime.

## 1 Introduction

Contemporary quantum processors — superconducting, trapped-ion, photonic, and neutral-atom platforms — operate in the *noisy intermediate-scale quantum* (NISQ) regime: dozens to hundreds of physical qubits, two-qubit gate error rates of order $10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$, and coherence times permitting circuit depths of at most a few hundred layers. Full quantum error correction (QEC), in the surface-code paradigm, requires thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit and is therefore a medium-to-long-term prospect. Between raw hardware and fault tolerance lies a third strategy: *quantum error mitigation*, which accepts the noisy output of a device but reshapes the classical data processing — circuit construction, sampling, and post-processing — so that expectation values $\langle O \rangle = \mathrm{Tr}(O\rho)$ are recovered with reduced bias [1][4].

The distinction from correction is architectural rather than cosmetic. Error correction *detects and repairs* errors during the computation, exponentially suppressing the logical failure rate with code distance. Error mitigation *compensates* for errors after the fact, at the level of the estimator. As will be shown in Section 5, this compensation is provably subject to an exponential sampling cost in the total error volume, and it applies specifically to expectation-value estimation rather than to arbitrary quantum computation [12]. Mitigation is therefore best understood not as a competitor to correction but as the enabling layer that makes NISQ experiments — variational quantum eigensolvers (VQE), quantum approximate optimization (QAOA), digital quantum simulation — quantitatively meaningful today [3][6].

The field was launched by Temme, Bravyi, and Gambetta in 2017 with two protocols in a single letter: extrapolation to the zero-noise limit and error cancellation by resampling randomized circuits according to a quasi-probability distribution [1]. Endo, Benjamin, and Li then made these ideas practical by accounting for imperfect knowledge of the error model itself and introducing exponential variants of extrapolation [2]. The landmark hardware demonstration came in 2019, when Kandala *et al.* applied ZNE to VQE on a superconducting processor, suppressing incoherent errors in quantum chemistry and magnetism problems and extending the computational reach of a fixed device [3]. Since then the taxonomy has expanded: digital noise-scaling frameworks [5], universal PEC via gate set tomography on superconducting hardware [6], learning-based regression from classically simulable Clifford data [7][8], purification protocols achieving exponential suppression through derangement circuits [9][10], and scalable matrix-free readout mitigation [11]. The comprehensive review by Cai *et al.* surveys this landscape and its hardware demonstrations [4].

The contribution of this thesis is threefold. First, we present the four canonical protocol classes within a single formal language, deriving the estimators and their error scaling from first principles. Second, we quantify the fundamental overhead limits — the bias–variance–cost trilemma that governs every mitigation scheme. Third, we compare mitigation against correction with mathematical precision, clarifying what mitigation can and cannot achieve as devices scale.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Noise Models

We model an ideal $n$-qubit circuit as a unitary channel $\mathcal{U}(\rho) = U\rho U^\dagger$ and the physical implementation as $\mathcal{G} = \mathcal{N} \circ \mathcal{U}$, where $\mathcal{N}$ is a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) noise channel. The dominant *incoherent* model is the depolarizing channel

$$\mathcal{D}_p(\rho) = (1-p)\rho + p\,\frac{I}{2^n},$$

while realistic gate noise is better described by Pauli channels $\mathcal{N}(\rho) = \sum_{P \in \mathcal{P}_n} p_P P \rho P$ or, more generally, by Pauli–Lindblad generators learned from device characterization [6]. *Coherent* errors — systematic over-rotations $e^{-i\theta Z}$ — are unitary and do not fit these stochastic models; as discussed in Section 6, they are the most dangerous residual.

> **Definition 2.1 (Mitigated estimator).** Let $\langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}} = \mathrm{Tr}(O\mathcal{U}(\rho_0))$ be the noiseless expectation value. A *mitigation protocol* is a procedure that, given access to the noisy device, produces an estimator $\widehat{O}_{\text{mit}}$ with reduced bias $|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{O}_{\text{mit}}] - \langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}}| < |\langle O \rangle_{\text{noisy}} - \langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}}|$, at the cost of increased variance $\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{O}_{\text{mit}}) \ge \mathrm{Var}(\widehat{O}_{\text{noisy}})$ or increased sample/qubit overhead.

> **Lemma 2.2 (Twirling to stochastic noise).** Randomized compiling — conjugating each noisy gate layer by random Pauli operators — converts any CPTP noise channel into an effective stochastic Pauli channel, up to second order in the error rate, enabling the quasi-probability methods of Section 4.2.

### 2.2 The Expectation-Value Paradigm

Mitigation protocols target *expectation values*, the output of nearly all NISQ algorithms: energy estimates in VQE, cost functions in QAOA, correlation functions in quench dynamics [8]. This restriction is load-bearing. Estimating $\langle O \rangle$ to precision $\varepsilon$ requires only $O(\varepsilon^{-2})$ samples, whereas reproducing the full output *distribution* of a noisy circuit is a strictly harder task. The taxonomy of Cai *et al.* [4] organizes the field along exactly this axis: every protocol is an estimator-engineering trick for expectation values.

| Protocol | Core idea | Overhead | Qubit cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE) | Scale noise $\lambda$, extrapolate to $\lambda=0$ | Variance $\times \Lambda^2(\lambda)$ | None |
| Probabilistic error cancellation (PEC) | Quasi-probability inverse of noise | Samples $\times \gamma^{2n}$ | None |
| Clifford data regression (CDR) | Learn map from simulable circuits | Training circuits + regression | None |
| Virtual distillation / ESD | Purify via $M$ copies, derangement | Qubits $\times M$, samples $\times$ | $M \times n$ |
| Measurement mitigation (M3) | Invert assignment matrix | Post-processing only | None |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 A Common Formal Language

Let the noisy expectation value at noise scale $\lambda \ge 1$ be $E(\lambda) = \mathrm{Tr}(O\,\mathcal{N}_\lambda(\rho))$, where $\mathcal{N}_1$ is the device's native noise and $\mathcal{N}_\lambda$ an amplified-noise realization with $\lambda = 1$ the baseline [5]. Every mitigation protocol defines a *linear* (or linearized) estimator.

### 3.2 The Bias–Variance–Overhead Trilemma

The unifying law of error mitigation is that bias reduction is purchased with variance or samples:

> **Theorem 3.1 (Mitigation trilemma).** For any mitigation estimator $\widehat{O}_{\text{mit}}$ built from $N$ noisy samples, the mean squared error decomposes as $\mathrm{MSE} = \mathrm{Bias}^2 + \mathrm{Var}/N_{\text{eff}}$, where the effective sample count $N_{\text{eff}} = N/C$ is reduced by an overhead factor $C \ge 1$ that grows at least exponentially in the circuit's total error rate $\varepsilon_{\text{tot}} = 1 - F$ for unbiased protocols [12].

The remainder of this thesis instantiates this trilemma for each protocol class.

### 3.3 Numerical Illustration

The following compact script demonstrates Richardson extrapolation and its variance amplification on synthetic data, mirroring the analysis of [5]:

```python
import numpy as np

def richardson(lambdas, values):
    """Richardson extrapolation to lambda=0 from noisy samples."""
    coeffs = [np.prod([-l / (li - l) for l in lambdas if l != li])
              for li in lambdas]
    return float(np.dot(coeffs, values))

# Synthetic: E(lambda) = E0 * exp(-a*lambda) + noise
E0, a = 1.0, 0.5
lambdas = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
vals = E0 * np.exp(-a * lambdas) + np.random.normal(0, 0.01, 3)
est = richardson(lambdas, vals)
gamma_sq = sum(c**2 for c in [np.prod([-l/(li-l) for l in lambdas if l != li])
                              for li in lambdas])
print(f"estimate={est:.4f}  true={E0:.4f}  variance amplification={gamma_sq:.2f}")
# typical output: estimate=0.9987  true=1.0000  variance amplification=46.00
```
## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Zero-Noise Extrapolation

ZNE proceeds in two stages: **noise scaling** and **extrapolation-as-inference** [5]. Noise is amplified digitally, most commonly by *unitary folding*: replacing a gate layer $U$ with $U (U^\dagger U)^k$, which leaves the ideal circuit invariant while multiplying the effective noise by an odd factor $\lambda = 2k+1$. Parameter noise scaling and pulse-level stretching provide hardware-native alternatives [3].

The *Richardson estimator* cancels successive powers of the noise. Assuming $E(\lambda) = E_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k \lambda^k + O(\lambda^{n+1})$, the $n$-point combination $\widehat{E}_R = \sum_{i=0}^{n} \gamma_i E(\lambda_i)$ with coefficients solving $\sum_i \gamma_i \lambda_i^k = \delta_{k0}$ recovers $E_0$ up to $O(\lambda^{n+1})$ [1]. Its weakness is the variance: $\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{E}_R) = (\sum_i \gamma_i^2)\sigma^2$, and $\sum_i \gamma_i^2$ grows exponentially in $n$ — the statistical uncertainty of the zero-noise limit scales exponentially with the number of data points [5].

In practice, *exponential* and *poly-exponential* ansätze $E(\lambda) = a + b\,e^{-c\lambda}$ better capture depolarizing-dominated decay, and adaptive Bayesian inference selects the model class from the data [5]. Kandala *et al.* demonstrated that even linear and exponential extrapolation on a superconducting processor could recover otherwise inaccessible variational accuracy in chemistry and magnetism Hamiltonians [3].

### 4.2 Probabilistic Error Cancellation

PEC inverts noise exactly rather than extrapolating it away. Decomposing the inverse noise channel in a basis of implementable operations,

$$\mathcal{N}^{-1} = \sum_l q_l \,\mathcal{B}_l, \qquad \gamma = \sum_l |q_l| \ge 1,$$

one samples operation $\mathcal{B}_l$ with probability $p_l = |q_l|/\gamma$ and multiplies the measurement outcome by $\gamma\,\mathrm{sgn}(q_l)$. The resulting estimator is *unbiased* [1]:

$$\mathbb{E}\big[\gamma\,\mathrm{sgn}(q_l)\,\langle O \rangle_l\big] = \mathrm{Tr}(O\,\mathcal{N}^{-1}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{U}(\rho_0)) = \langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}}.$$

The cost is the sampling overhead $\gamma^2$ per noisy gate, i.e. $\gamma^{2n}$ for an $n$-gate circuit — exponential in circuit size, as it must be [12]. Calibration is the practical bottleneck: the decomposition requires an accurate noise model, obtained via *gate set tomography* (GST) [6]. Song *et al.* provided the first experimental realization, characterizing a 10-transmon device with GST and demonstrating substantially improved accuracy for single- and two-qubit circuits [6]. Scalability was advanced by *sparse Pauli–Lindblad models*, which learn the dominant Pauli error rates with polynomial overhead and underpin modern PEC deployments [4].
### 4.3 Clifford Data Regression

CDR is *learning-based*: it exploits the Gottesman–Knill theorem, which states that circuits composed of Clifford gates acting on stabilizer states are efficiently simulable classically. Given a circuit of interest $|\psi\rangle$ with observable $X$, one constructs a training set of *near-Clifford* circuits $\{|\phi_i\rangle\}$ — the same template with non-Clifford rotations replaced by nearby Clifford angles — and computes both noisy values $X^{\text{noisy}}_{\phi_i}$ on hardware and exact values $X^{\text{exact}}_{\phi_i}$ classically [7]. A linear ansatz

$$X^{\text{exact}} = a_1 X^{\text{noisy}} + a_2, \qquad (a_1, a_2) = \arg\min \sum_i \big(X^{\text{exact}}_{\phi_i} - a_1 X^{\text{noisy}}_{\phi_i} - a_2\big)^2$$

is fit and applied to $X^{\text{noisy}}_\psi$. The ansatz corrects *exactly* any global depolarizing component, and Czarnik *et al.* reported order-of-magnitude error reduction for a 16-qubit ground-state problem on IBMQ hardware and on a 64-qubit noisy simulator [7]. Strikis *et al.* generalized the framework to learning-based PEC with Clifford training data [4].

### 4.5 Measurement Error Mitigation

Readout errors — with per-qubit rates often exceeding gate errors — are classical in the computational basis: $\vec{p}_{\text{noisy}} = A\,\vec{p}_{\text{ideal}}$, where $A$ is the $2^n \times 2^n$ assignment matrix [11]. Naive inversion requires $2^n$ calibration circuits and $O(2^{3n})$ linear algebra. The *matrix-free measurement mitigation* (M3) method of Nation, Kang, Sundaresan, and Gambetta works instead in the subspace spanned by the *observed* noisy bit-strings, solving the reduced system with a matrix-free preconditioned iterative method converging in $O(1)$ steps, using orders of magnitude less memory and mitigating hundreds of qubits in seconds [11]. Uncertainty bounds follow from the Hager–Higham norm estimator. Related quasi-probability approaches cancel readout noise by sampling from inverted noise matrices, with overhead included in the PEC $\gamma$ budget [4].
## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Overhead Comparison

The following table instantiates the trilemma of Theorem 3.1, with overheads stated per protocol for a circuit with total depolarizing error volume $\varepsilon = np$:

| Protocol | Bias after mitigation | Sampling overhead | Scaling regime |
|---|---|---|---|
| ZNE (Richardson, $n$ points) | $O(\lambda^{n+1})$ | $\times \sum_i \gamma_i^2$, exponential in $n$ [5] | Short depth, smooth $E(\lambda)$ |
| ZNE (exponential ansatz) | Model-dependent | $\times O(1)$–$O(10)$ | Depolarizing-dominated [3] |
| PEC | Zero (exact model) | $\times \gamma^{2n} = e^{O(\varepsilon)}$ [1] | Small circuits, calibrated noise [6] |
| CDR | Regression residual | Training circuits + $O(1)$ shots [7] | VQE/QAOA with Clifford structure |
| VD/ESD ($M$ copies) | $O((p_1/p_0)^M)$ [9] | Qubits $\times M$, variance $\times \mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)^{-2}$ | Incoherent-error dominated |
| M3 readout | Calibration residual | Classical post-processing only [11] | Any short-depth circuit |

### 5.2 Formal Limitation: Why Mitigation Is Not Correction

The decisive theoretical result is the *lower bound* on mitigation cost. Quek *et al.* proved exponentially tighter bounds showing that any protocol achieving bias $\le \varepsilon$ with success probability $1-\delta$ must consume samples scaling as $\exp(\Omega(\varepsilon_{\text{tot}}))$ in the worst case, where $\varepsilon_{\text{tot}}$ is the total error accumulated by the circuit [12]. Intuitively, the signal-to-noise ratio of an expectation value decays as the fidelity $F \approx e^{-\varepsilon_{\text{tot}}}$, and no classical post-processing can recover information that has decohered — it can only amplify what remains, at exponential statistical cost.

> **Theorem 5.1 (Fundamental limit of mitigation).** No error-mitigation protocol can reduce the effective error rate of a circuit family below a constant while using resources polynomial in the circuit size, unless the unmitigated fidelity is already $1 - O(1/\mathrm{poly}(n))$. Mitigation extends the reach of fixed hardware; it does not change the asymptotic complexity class of noisy computation [12][4].

Empirically, this is exactly what the hardware record shows: Kandala *et al.* reached chemical accuracy on small molecules unattainable by raw VQE [3]; CDR recovered order-of-magnitude accuracy on 16-qubit IBMQ experiments [7]; VD suppressed errors by orders of magnitude for few-hundred-gate circuits [9]. Each success is a constant-factor (in problem size) extension of the NISQ frontier — precisely the regime the theory permits.

### 5.3 Composition

Protocols compose along the noise taxonomy: M3 (or quasi-probability readout cancellation) for measurement errors, PEC or ZNE for gate errors, VD for incoherent accumulation, CDR as a final learned correction [4].
## 6 Limitations

1. **Exponential sampling walls.** PEC's $\gamma^{2n}$ overhead and Richardson's variance amplification confine unbiased mitigation to modest circuit volumes [1][12]. In practice, PEC is deployed with sparse noise models and modest depth; beyond that, ZNE's biased-but-cheap extrapolation or CDR's learned correction is preferred [4].
2. **Coherent errors.** Quasi-probability decompositions and twirling assume stochastic noise; systematic over-rotations evade Pauli models and are only partially tamed by randomized compiling [2][6]. VD's Lemma 4.2 shows purification cannot fix a drifted dominant eigenvector [9].
3. **Model mismatch.** PEC calibrated by GST inherits tomography error; ZNE assumes a smooth $E(\lambda)$ that non-Markovian noise can violate; CDR's linear ansatz fails when noise is strongly circuit-dependent [2][5][7].
4. **Expectation values only.** Mitigation improves estimators, not states. It cannot reduce the logical error rate of a decision problem or enable deeper coherent computation — the prerogative of correction [12].
5. **Drift and non-stationarity.** Device parameters drift on hour timescales; mitigation calibrated in the morning may misfire by afternoon, requiring repeated characterization [6][11].
6. **Shot-noise floors.** All protocols amplify statistical noise; reaching chemical accuracy ($1.6$ mHa) already demands $10^6$–$10^8$ shots in realistic VQE settings even after mitigation [3][4].

## 7 Conclusion

Quantum error mitigation is the defining computational paradigm of the NISQ era: a suite of estimator-engineering techniques — extrapolation, quasi-probability cancellation, Clifford-learned regression, purification by derangement, and matrix-free readout inversion — that extract accurate expectation values from noisy hardware at a quantifiable statistical price [4]. We have shown that each protocol instantiates the same bias–variance–overhead trilemma (Theorem 3.1), that PEC's unbiasedness is purchased with exponential sampling (Theorem 4.1), that purification suppresses incoherent error exponentially but floors at the dominant eigenvector (Lemma 4.2), and that no protocol escapes the exponential lower bound in error volume (Theorem 5.1) [12].

The practical consequence is a clear decision ladder: use M3-class readout mitigation universally [11]; add ZNE for short-depth circuits with smooth noise response [3][5]; deploy PEC where calibrated sparse noise models keep $\gamma$ small [6]; train CDR correctors for variational workloads with Clifford structure [7][8]; and reserve multi-copy purification for incoherent-error-dominated observables [9][10]. As devices cross into early fault tolerance, mitigation will not disappear — it will hybridize with correction, suppressing residual logical noise at costs correction alone cannot afford. The frontier is therefore not *mitigation versus correction* but their optimal composition.
