# Oceanic Internal Waves and Abyssal Mixing: Internal Tides, Lee Waves, and the Finescale Parameterization of Diapycnal Diffusivity

## Abstract

The maintenance of the ocean's abyssal stratification against the continuous formation of dense deep water demands a mechanical energy supply of order two terawatts, a constraint first quantified by Munk's "abyssal recipes" and refined by Munk and Wunsch into an explicit energy budget. This thesis develops the modern account of that budget: barotropic tides and winds inject energy into the oceanic internal wave field; internal tides generated at rough topography, near-inertial waves radiated from the surface mixed layer, and stationary lee waves excited by geostrophic currents — above all the Antarctic Circumpolar Current — propagate, interact through weakly nonlinear wave–wave resonances, and break into the small-scale turbulence that performs diapycnal mixing. We derive the Osborn relation linking turbulent dissipation to diapycnal diffusivity, present the Gregg–Henyey–Polzin finescale parameterization that infers dissipation from observed shear and strain spectra, and confront the global energy accounts with microstructure observations and tracer-release experiments. The emerging synthesis — approximately 3.7 TW of tidal dissipation, of which roughly 1 TW enters the internal tide field, supplemented by ~0.2 TW of lee-wave generation and wind-driven near-inertial input — closes the abyssal energy budget within observational uncertainty, while revealing that the geography of mixing, not merely its global mean, controls the strength and structure of the meridional overturning circulation.

## 1 Introduction

The deep ocean is cold, dark, and — to a first approximation — stably stratified. That stratification is not a passive relic. Roughly 30 Sverdrups (1 Sv = 10⁶ m³ s⁻¹) of dense water formed at high latitudes sink into the abyss each year, and something must return that water upward across density surfaces, because the abyss does not fill without bound [2]. In the absence of interior mixing, Sandström's theorem implies the ocean would relax within a few millennia into a stagnant pool of cold, salty water, its circulation confined to a thin, convectively driven surface skin. The agent that prevents this fate is small-scale turbulence: diapycnal mixing that fluxes buoyancy downward (equivalently, heat upward) and allows deep water to upwell diffusively through the stratified interior.

Munk's 1966 "Abyssal recipes" made this argument quantitative [1]. Balancing a one-dimensional advection–diffusion equation for the abyssal density profile against 25 Sv of deep-water formation, Munk found that a depth-independent diapycnal diffusivity of κ ≈ 10⁻⁴ m² s⁻¹ — one cgs unit — was required to hold the observed stratification in steady state. Three decades later, Munk and Wunsch revisited the problem energetically and found that the required mechanical power was approximately 2.1 TW to maintain the abyssal density distribution against 30 Sv of deep-water formation [2]. The winds and the tides, they argued, are the only plausible mechanical energy sources: tidal dissipation is known from astronomy to equal 3.7 TW (2.50 ± 0.05 TW from the M₂ constituent alone), and the large-scale wind field supplies of order 1 TW to the general circulation.

This framing created two enduring puzzles. First, direct microstructure measurements of turbulence in the pelagic ocean — far from topography — yielded diffusivities near 10⁻⁵ m² s⁻¹, an order of magnitude below Munk's requirement, a low value corroborated by deliberate tracer-release experiments [2]. Second, nearly all of the 3.7 TW of tidal dissipation had traditionally been assigned to friction in shallow seas and bottom boundary layers, leaving essentially nothing for the interior. Munk and Wunsch's resolution was geographic: the computed diffusivity should be reinterpreted as a surrogate for a small number of concentrated sources of buoyancy flux — regions of intense mixing, principally over rough topography — from which water masses (but not the turbulence itself) are exported into the interior [2]. The modern program of abyssal mixing research is, in large part, the working-out of this hypothesis: identifying the wave motions that carry mechanical energy from its large-scale sources to its small-scale sinks, and quantifying the resulting geography of mixing. The comprehensive energetics review of Wunsch and Ferrari provides the canonical accounting framework [4].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the internal wave field and its canonical spectral description. Section 3 develops the theoretical and observational methodology — the Osborn relation and the finescale inference of dissipation. Section 4 treats the three principal generation pathways and the downscale cascade in depth. Section 5 confronts the global energy budget with observations. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background: The Oceanic Internal Wave Field

Internal gravity waves are the ocean's principal mechanism for transporting mechanical energy from the scales of forcing — thousands of kilometers for tides and winds — to the centimeter scales at which molecular dissipation acts. Restoring forces are gravity (through stratification, quantified by the buoyancy frequency N) and planetary rotation (through the Coriolis parameter f), so that free internal waves occupy the frequency band f < ω < N. Their dispersion relation,

> **Theorem (Internal-wave dispersion):** For a plane internal wave of horizontal wavenumber k_h and vertical wavenumber m in a uniformly stratified, rotating fluid, the intrinsic frequency satisfies ω² = (N²k_h² + f²m²)/(k_h² + m²), so that phase propagates obliquely while energy propagates along characteristics inclined at an angle θ to the horizontal with tan θ = √[(ω² − f²)/(N² − ω²)].

The observed internal wave field is remarkably universal. Garrett and Munk synthesized the available moored and towed observations into a canonical spectral model — the GM spectrum — describing the distribution of wave energy across frequency and vertical wavenumber as a smooth, separable function with a red frequency spectrum rolling off above f and a characteristic vertical-wavenumber bandwidth [3]. The GM spectrum's energy level, E₀, remains the reference standard against which observed spectral levels are normalized; departures from GM by factors of a few are the raw material of the finescale parameterization (Section 4.4). Its empirical success is itself a physical clue: some combination of generation, propagation, and nonlinear interaction maintains the wave field near a quasi-universal shape across much of the ocean [15].

Three generation mechanisms dominate the energy input to the internal wave field:

1. **Barotropic-to-baroclinic tidal conversion.** Oscillatory tidal currents flowing over topographic slopes displace isopycnals and radiate internal waves at tidal frequencies — the *internal tide*. Roughly 1 TW of the 3.7 TW of global tidal dissipation is converted into internal tides in the deep ocean, concentrated at mid-ocean ridges, island arcs, and continental slopes.
2. **Wind-generated near-inertial waves.** Time-varying wind stress excites inertial oscillations in the surface mixed layer, which radiate downward and equatorward as near-inertial internal waves, carrying an estimated 0.3–0.5 TW into the interior.
3. **Lee waves.** Steady geostrophic currents flowing over small-scale topography excite stationary internal waves — lee waves — whose intrinsic frequency is set by the Doppler shift |Uk|. In the Southern Ocean, where the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC) flows over some of the roughest topography on Earth, this pathway is a first-order energy sink for the eddy field.

Once generated, internal waves propagate, refract in the slowly varying stratification and shear of the large-scale circulation, reflect and scatter at topography and the surface, and interact nonlinearly with one another. The terminal step is *breaking*: when wave shear drives the local gradient Richardson number below criticality, or when wave-induced isopycnal displacements overturn, the organized wave motion collapses into three-dimensional turbulence, and the turbulent kinetic energy cascades to the Kolmogorov scale where viscosity converts it to heat. A fraction of the turbulent production performs work against stratification — the buoyancy flux — and it is this fraction that mixes the abyss.

## 3 Theoretical and Observational Methodology

### 3.1 The Osborn relation

The bridge between measured turbulence and diapycnal diffusivity is the Osborn relation [5]. Consider the turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) equation in a stratified shear flow. In a statistically steady state, turbulent production P is partitioned between the buoyancy flux B (the rate at which TKE is converted to potential energy by mixing dense fluid upward) and viscous dissipation ε:

> **Theorem (Steady TKE balance):** For stationary, homogeneous stratified turbulence, P = B + ε, and the flux Richardson number R_f ≡ B/P ≤ 0.15–0.2 bounds the fraction of production that performs mixing.

### 3.2 Finescale inference: from spectra to dissipation

> **Theorem (Finescale scaling):** If the downscale energy transfer through the internal wave spectrum scales as E², where E is the spectral energy level, then in steady state ε = ε₀ (N²/N₀²) · F(R_ω, f) · (E/E₀)², with ε₀ a reference dissipation rate, N₀ a reference stratification, R_ω the shear-to-strain ratio diagnosing the frequency content of the wave field, and F a function encoding the latitude dependence of the transfer rate.

## 5 Empirical Results and Energy-Budget Closure

The decisive test of the concentrated-sources hypothesis came from the Brazil Basin. Microstructure surveys across the Mid-Atlantic Ridge found diffusivities exceeding 5 × 10⁻⁴ m² s⁻¹ in the bottom 150 m over rough ridge-crest topography, while profiles over the smooth abyssal plains to the west gave κ_ρ ≲ 10⁻⁵ m² s⁻¹ — a contrast of nearly two orders of magnitude controlled entirely by the underlying bathymetry [8]. The deliberate SF₆ tracer release of Ledwell and colleagues provided the independent confirmation: the tracer spread diapycnally at rates matching the microstructure inference near the ridge and at background rates over the plains, ruling out instrumental artifact [9]. Internal-wave generation by the tides, radiating upward from the ridge into the stratified water above, was identified as the sustaining mechanism — exactly the physics Munk and Wunsch had invoked in the abstract [2].

Globally, the Waterhouse et al. microstructure compilation shows the same pattern writ large: dissipation rates elevated by one to two orders of magnitude within roughly a kilometer of rough topography, decaying upward into a quiescent interior, with the Southern Ocean standing out as the most energetic basin [13]. The emerging quantitative budget is summarized below.

| Energy pathway | Power (TW) | Disposition |
|---|---|---|
| Astronomical tidal dissipation | 3.7 (2.5 from M₂) | ~1 TW → internal tides; remainder in shallow seas and boundary layers [2] |
| Barotropic → baroclinic (internal tide) conversion | ~1.0 | q ≈ 0.3 dissipates locally; low modes radiate basin-scale [10]; small-scale tides dominate generation and breaking [16] |
| Wind work on the circulation | ~1.0 | Near-inertial waves (~0.3–0.5 TW) plus eddy field |
| Geostrophic → lee-wave conversion | ~0.2 | Half in the Southern Ocean; ~50% dissipates in bottom km [11][12] |
| Turbulent production sustaining observed mixing | ~2–3 | Consistent with the 2.1 TW abyssal requirement [2][4] |

When St. Laurent, Simmons, and Jayne implemented the tidal-mixing parameterization in a coarse-resolution ocean model — 30% local dissipation decaying over 500 m above bottom, the rest distributed in the far field — the equilibrium diffusivity profile ran from 0.3 cm² s⁻¹ in the thermocline to 7.7 cm² s⁻¹ in the abyss, with a depth mean of 0.9 cm² s⁻¹: essentially Munk's recipe, recovered from an energetically consistent, spatially varying scheme [10]. Models with this geography of mixing show substantially reduced temperature–salinity biases relative to uniform-diffusivity controls, demonstrating that *where* mixing happens matters as much as *how much* [14]. The Climate Process Team's five-year synthesis concluded that internal-wave-driven mixing is now representable in climate models with credible energetics, though the far-field dissipation problem — where exactly the radiated low-mode energy breaks — remains the largest structural uncertainty [14].

## 6 Limitations and Open Questions

The synthesis above is powerful but incomplete, and its weak points deserve explicit statement.

*The finescale parameterization's domain of validity.* GHP assumes a quasi-stationary, GM-like wave field cascading through weakly nonlinear interactions to a single dissipative sink. It degrades near the equator (where f → 0 invalidates the latitude scaling), near topography and boundaries (where the spectrum is non-GM and non-stationary), in the presence of strong mean flows, and where double diffusion rather than shear instability dominates. Factor-of-2–3 errors are typical; order-of-magnitude failures occur [13][14].

*The far-field problem.* Of the ~0.7 TW of internal-tide energy that escapes the near field, the geography of its dissipation is only crudely known. Candidate sinks — PSI at critical latitudes, scattering at rough topography and continental slopes, interaction with mesoscale eddies — are all observed, but their integrated contributions do not yet close the budget, and climate models distribute far-field dissipation by heuristic rules [14][16].

*Lee-wave fate and suppression.* Microstructure shortfalls relative to linear lee-wave generation, reabsorption by bottom-intensified flows, and the artificial diffusivities of wave-resolving models all cloud the lee-wave contribution; the 0.2 TW figure is a generation estimate, not a measured dissipation [12].

*Mixing efficiency.* As discussed in Section 4.5, Γ's variability propagates directly into every diffusivity estimate and into the power requirement itself [5][14].

*Observational sparsity.* The deep Pacific, Indian, and much of the Southern Ocean remain sparsely sampled by microstructure; the global maps are interpolations anchored by a few thousand profiles [13].

*Climate feedbacks.* Warming intensifies upper-ocean stratification, accelerating internal tides and altering generation; whether abyssal mixing strengthens or weakens in a warmer climate — and with what consequences for the overturning circulation and carbon storage — is an open, policy-relevant question [15].

## 7 Conclusion

Sixty years after Munk's "abyssal recipes," the problem he posed has been transformed from a one-dimensional diffusion balance into a three-dimensional, energetically closed account of the ocean's interior [1][2]. The winds and tides supply the power — 3.7 TW from the tides, ~1 TW from the winds — and the internal wave field is the transmission: internal tides radiating from mid-ocean ridges, near-inertial waves descending from storm tracks, and lee waves trapped above the rough topography of the Southern Ocean [4][11]. Weakly nonlinear wave–wave interactions cascade this energy downscale at a rate scaling with the square of the spectral level, and the Gregg–Henyey–Polzin parameterization converts observed finescale shear and strain into dissipation rates that match direct microstructure measurements within factors of a few [6][7][8]. The resulting geography — mixing hotspots over rough topography against a quiescent pelagic background — is precisely the "small number of concentrated sources" Munk and Wunsch inferred from energetics alone [2][9][13].

Two lessons carry beyond oceanography. First, global budgets constrain but do not determine: the overturning circulation responds to the *geography* of mixing, so the St. Laurent et al. demonstration that spatially varying, energetically consistent mixing repairs model biases is as important as the closure of the terawatt accounts [10][14]. Second, the chain from astronomical forcing to molecular dissipation spans twelve orders of magnitude in scale, and every link — generation, propagation, cascade, breaking, efficiency — remains an active research frontier [15]. The abyssal recipes are no longer recipes; they are a measurement program.
