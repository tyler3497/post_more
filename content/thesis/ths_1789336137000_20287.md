# The Mathematics of Gerrymandering: Markov Chain Monte Carlo on Districting Plans, Ensemble Analysis, and the Efficiency Gap

## Abstract

Partisan gerrymandering — the deliberate manipulation of district boundaries to entrench political advantage — has migrated from the back room to the courtroom, and with it, from rhetoric to mathematics. This article develops the modern quantitative theory of redistricting. Districting plans are formalized as connected, population-balanced partitions of a precinct dual graph; the vast space of such plans is explored with Markov chain Monte Carlo, above all the recombination (ReCom) proposal of DeFord, Duchin and Solomon; and a challenged plan is judged by ensemble-based outlier analysis, which asks whether its partisan statistics are extreme relative to thousands of neutrally drawn alternatives. Alongside these sampling methods we examine the efficiency gap of Stephanopoulos and McGhee, a wasted-vote measure of partisan symmetry that compresses the mechanics of packing and cracking into a single number with a proposed legal threshold. Case studies from North Carolina and Wisconsin, a rigorous significance test that requires no mixing proof, and the honest limits of the machinery — including exponential slow mixing on adversarial graphs — complete the picture.

## 1 Intro

In single-member plurality electoral systems, the district map is the election before the election. Whoever draws the lines chooses, to a remarkable degree, which votes will matter. The classical repertoire of the gerrymanderer has exactly two moves. *Packing* concentrates the opposing party's supporters into a small number of districts they win by landslide margins, wasting their surplus votes. *Cracking* disperses the remaining opposition voters across many districts where they form persistent, futile minorities. Both tactics convert votes into seats inefficiently for the victim and efficiently for the perpetrator — and both leave a statistical fingerprint.

For most of American history that fingerprint was evaluated by eye: districts shaped like salamanders, earmuffs, or river-hugging ribbons were Exhibit A. But geometry is a treacherous witness. Compactness requirements can be satisfied by a skilled gerrymanderer, and bizarre shapes are sometimes compelled by legitimate constraints such as rivers, county lines, or the Voting Rights Act. As one line of research puts it, stopping strange geometries will not stop gerrymandering [9].

The quantitative turn of the last decade replaced the eyeball test with a sampling paradigm. If we can draw a large, representative collection — an *ensemble* — of districting plans that satisfy all legal constraints but are otherwise drawn without partisan intent, then a challenged plan can be placed in context: is it a typical member of the ensemble, or an extreme outlier? This idea has now been admitted as expert evidence in American courts, forming the core of testimony in cases from North Carolina to Pennsylvania [3]. Its mathematical substrate is the theory of Markov chains on graph partitions, the subject of this article.

The legal stakes clarify why the mathematics matters. In *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019), the Supreme Court held that federal courts cannot adjudicate partisan-gerrymandering claims at all — they present nonjusticiable political questions. But the decision explicitly left state courts and state constitutions as live venues, and several state supreme courts have since struck down maps on state-law grounds. In those courtrooms, the question is no longer philosophical but statistical: *how unlikely is this map?* Answering it requires a probability distribution over maps, a sampler that explores it, a test statistic that captures partisan effect, and a significance framework that survives cross-examination. This article builds each of those components.

## 2 Background

**Districting as graph partitioning.**

- **Contiguity**: each district must be a single connected piece of territory.
- **Population balance**: districts must have nearly equal population, following the one-person-one-vote doctrine of *Reynolds v. Sims*.
- **Compactness**: districts should not be grotesquely shaped; measured by scores such as Polsby–Popper (four pi times area divided by squared perimeter) or Reock (district area divided by the area of its minimum bounding circle) [7].
- **Political-subdivision integrity**: counties and municipalities should be split as rarely as possible.
- **Voting Rights Act compliance**: Section 2 may require the creation of districts in which minority voters can elect candidates of choice, subject to the *Gingles* preconditions.

## 3 Methodology

**The ReCom proposal.**

1. Select two adjacent districts sharing a boundary.
2. Merge them into a single connected subgraph.
3. Draw a *uniform random spanning tree* of the merged subgraph, for instance by Wilson's algorithm (loop-erased random walk).
4. Cut a tree edge whose removal splits the tree into two components, each within the allowed population tolerance, and reassign the components as the two new districts.
5. Accept or reject the move under a Metropolis–Hastings rule targeting the desired distribution over plans.

> **Definition.** EG = (wasted votes for Democrats − wasted votes for Republicans) / (total votes cast),

> **Proposition.** EG = 2(V − 1/2) − (S − 1/2), where V is the Democratic statewide vote share and S the Democratic seat share.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The graph-partition formulation and why sampling is unavoidable

A skeptic might ask why we sample plans at all, rather than optimizing: simply find the most compact, most county-respecting plan and compare the enacted map to the optimum. The answer is computational complexity. Even for the unit-weight case, finding a balanced partition of a planar graph into a fixed number of connected parts is NP-hard for three or more districts, and sampling connected bipartitions proportionally to natural weights is hard even for two parts without balance constraints [6]. Optimization over plan space is therefore intractable in the worst case, and in any event a single optimum would be a brittle benchmark — it answers which plan is best by one criterion, not what a typical neutral plan looks like.

### 4.2 Inside the ReCom proposal: trees, weights, and geometry

The mathematical heart of ReCom is the interplay between spanning trees and district shape. For a graph H, Kirchhoff's matrix-tree theorem counts its spanning trees τ(H) via any cofactor of the Laplacian — a number that grows extremely fast with the internal connectivity of H. When ReCom merges two districts and cuts a uniform spanning tree, the probability of generating a particular bipartition is proportional to the number of spanning trees consistent with it. Summing over trees, the chain's stationary weight for a plan becomes proportional to the product of τ(D) over districts D, times the usual Metropolis–Hastings score terms.

### 4.3 Ensemble outlier testing with rigorous p-values

The most delicate statistical question is also the most practical: the chain has not been *proved* to mix on any real precinct graph, so in what sense is the ensemble a valid null distribution? A beautiful result of Chikina, Frieze and Pegden dissolves much of the difficulty [5]. Run the chain *starting from the enacted plan itself*, and compare the plan's label against the labels seen along its own trajectory:

> **Theorem (Chikina–Frieze–Pegden, 2017).** Let X_0, X_1, …, X_k be a trajectory of a reversible Markov chain, and let ω be any real-valued label. Call X_0 an *ε-outlier* if at most an ε fraction of the trajectory's labels are at least as extreme as ω(X_0). If X_0 is drawn from the chain's stationary distribution, the probability of observing an ε-outlier is at most √(2ε).

### 4.5 From computation to the courtroom

Quantitative evidence has now survived adversarial legal process repeatedly. Jonathan Mattingly's ensemble analysis of North Carolina's congressional plans — showing the 2012 and 2016 enacted maps as extreme outliers among more than 24,000 neutral alternatives — formed the statistical core of expert testimony in *Common Cause v. Rucho* [3]. The signature boxplot graphic, with enacted districts lying conspicuously outside the ensemble boxes, proved legible to judges without mathematical training. In Pennsylvania, ensemble and symmetry evidence contributed to *League of Women Voters v. Commonwealth* (2018), in which the state supreme court struck down the congressional map under the state constitution's free-and-equal-elections clause.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The North Carolina studies remain the flagship demonstration. Herschlag, Mattingly and collaborators generated ensembles of over 24,000 congressional plans drawn under nonpartisan criteria — contiguity, population balance, compactness, county preservation, and Voting Rights Act compliance — and scored each under actual historical elections [3]. The enacted 2012 and 2016 plans were extreme outliers: under the 2012 vote data, essentially no ensemble plan awarded Republicans as many seats as the enacted map did, while a plan drawn by a bipartisan panel of retired judges landed squarely in the middle of the distribution. The firewall analysis sharpened the point: as the Democratic statewide vote share was swept upward, nearly all ensemble plans surrendered the Republican supermajority once Democrats passed roughly half the vote, yet the enacted plan clung to its majority until the Democratic share exceeded 55 percent — a structural signature of cracking that no neutral process reproduces.

## 6 Limitations

Honesty about limits is what separates science from advocacy, and the ensemble program has several.

First, **no mixing guarantees** exist for real precinct graphs, and the slow-mixing construction shows none should be expected in full generality [6]. The Chikina–Frieze–Pegden test sidesteps this elegantly, but most published ensemble histograms still implicitly assume the chain has explored well — an assumption that must be defended with diagnostics in every application.

Second, **the null distribution is a choice**. Population tolerance, compactness weights, county-split penalties, and the spanning-tree weighting itself all shape the ensemble. A gerrymanderer defending a map can always propose an alternative ensemble, built on a different but defensible set of criteria, under which the map looks typical. The method detects deviation from a specified neutral process, not deviation from fairness in the abstract.

Third, **geography constrains what neutrality can achieve**. Chen and Rodden's finding stands: in states where one party's voters cluster in cities, even the typical neutral plan awards that party fewer seats than its vote share [4]. An outlier test asks whether a plan is extreme among neutral plans; it does not ask whether neutral plans are themselves fair. Compactness, in particular, is not a cure — compact districts can be gerrymandered, and requiring compactness can even entrench a geographic disadvantage.

Fourth, **the efficiency gap has known failure modes**: it presumes two-party competition with comparable turnout, mishandles uncontested races, and can label landslide-heavy but innocent maps as gerrymanders. Its proposed thresholds are conventions, not theorems [2].

Finally, ensembles answer a *comparative* question — is this plan unusual? — while courts and commissions ultimately need a *constructive* one: which plan should we adopt? Sampling diagnoses the disease; it does not write the prescription.

## 7 Conclusion

The mathematics of gerrymandering has matured into a coherent pipeline: formalize plans as graph partitions, explore the space with recombination Markov chains whose stationary weights encode compactness through spanning-tree counts, test challenged plans as outliers against neutral ensembles with rigorous finite-sample p-values, and summarize partisan effect with symmetry metrics like the efficiency gap. Applied to North Carolina, Wisconsin, Pennsylvania and Virginia, this pipeline has repeatedly distinguished intentional manipulation from the background noise of political geography — and has done so in a form that judges, commissions and the public can understand.

The open problems are as sharp as the solved ones. Can we design samplers with *provable* guarantees on realistic graph families, perhaps via the multi-scale hierarchical constructions now emerging [8]? Can ensembles incorporate Voting Rights Act compliance and communities of interest without smuggling partisan intent back in through the choice of weights? And can the framework extend beyond single-member districts to the ranked-choice and multi-member systems gaining traction in American reform debates? Whatever the answers, the era in which gerrymandering could hide behind cartographic obscurity is over: the map is now a data point, the ensemble is its context, and the mathematics gets the last word.
