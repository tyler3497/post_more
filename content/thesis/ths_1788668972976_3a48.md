# Register Allocation as Graph Coloring: Chaitin–Briggs Coalescing, Linear Scan, PBQP, and SSA-Based Optimal Allocation

## Abstract

Register allocation—mapping an unbounded set of program temporaries onto a finite bank of machine registers—remains one of the most consequential phases of a modern optimizing compiler, directly governing spill traffic, instruction scheduling freedom, and ultimately wall-clock performance. This thesis traces the intellectual arc of the field from Chaitin's seminal 1981 reduction of allocation to graph coloring of the interference graph [1][2], through the Briggs conservative-coalescing and George aggressive-coalescing refinements that made coloring practical [3][5], to the linear-scan family that trades code quality for compilation speed in just-in-time compilers [4], the partitioned boolean quadratic programming (PBQP) formulation that achieves near-optimal allocation on irregular architectures [8], and the SSA-based line of work showing that strict static single assignment renders interference graphs chordal—and hence optimally colorable in polynomial time [6][7]. We formalize each framework, derive the key correctness and complexity arguments including the widely misquoted NP-completeness claim [10], compare empirical results from LLVM's greedy allocator, CompCert's verified allocator [9], and the JIT literature, and close with open problems in spill placement, irregular register classes, and verified allocation.

## 1. Introduction

A compiler's intermediate representation reasons about *temporaries*: an unbounded supply of symbolic values, each defined once and used at arbitrary program points. A physical processor offers a small, fixed set of *machine registers*—16 general-purpose registers on x86-64, 32 on AArch64, a handful of special-purpose ones reserved by the calling convention. *Register allocation* bridges this gap: it assigns temporaries to registers, inserting *spill* code (stores to and loads from the stack frame) wherever the demand for simultaneously live values exceeds supply, and *copy* instructions wherever allocation decisions disagree at control-flow merges.

The difficulty of the problem is deceptive. At first glance it resembles a scheduling or packing problem; in fact, as Chaitin and colleagues at IBM Yorktown Heights showed in 1981, global register allocation is profitably modeled as **graph coloring** [1]. Each temporary becomes a node; two nodes are joined by an edge when their *live ranges interfere*—that is, when both are simultaneously live at some program point, so they cannot share a register. Coloring the graph with *k* colors (one per register) yields a valid allocation. The reduction is elegant, implementable, and—because *k*-colorability of general graphs is NP-complete—suggestive of deep hardness [10].

Four decades of research have refined every word of that sentence. The *graph* is not quite the interference graph of classical SSA programs, which turn out to be *chordal* and hence easy [6][7]. *Coloring* is rarely performed directly; instead, allocators *simplify* the graph, *coalesce* copy-related nodes to eliminate moves, *spill* when simplification stalls, and *select* colors in reverse order [2][3]. The *NP-completeness* claim, repeated in countless papers, is subtler than folklore suggests: Bouchez, Darte, and Rastello demonstrated that what Chaitin actually proved does not imply that deciding whether spilling is necessary is NP-complete in the general case [10]. And for just-in-time compilation, where allocation time is user-visible latency, the entire graph-coloring edifice is frequently replaced by **linear scan**, a single-pass interval algorithm with linearithmic complexity [4].

This thesis presents a unified treatment of these developments. Our contributions are expository and synthetic:

- A **formal framework** (§3) defining interference, coloring, coalescing criteria, linear scan, PBQP, and SSA chordality with consistent notation.
- A **deep dive** (§4) into the five major allocator families, including the precise statements of the Briggs and George coalescing criteria and the PBQP cost model.
- An **empirical and proof-theoretic survey** (§5) covering LLVM's production greedy allocator, the verified allocator of CompCert [9], spill heuristics and rematerialization, and the optimality theorems for SSA programs.
- A discussion of **limitations and open problems** (§6), including the true complexity landscape, irregular register classes, and the gap between verified and optimizing allocators.

> **Thesis claim:** No single allocator dominates; the design space is structured by three tensions—*compile time vs. code quality*, *generality vs. architectural irregularity*, and *optimality vs. verifiability*—and the SSA-based chordal results reframe, rather than dissolve, the classical NP-hardness narrative.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 From ad hoc allocation to Chaitin

Before 1981, global register allocation was a craft of heuristics: the original FORTRAN compiler reused temporaries with crude rules, and production compilers relied on local, basic-block-scoped allocation plus hand-tuned spill heuristics [1]. The observation that allocation *is* coloring had been mentioned in the literature (notably by Cocke and co-workers around 1971), but Chaitin et al. were the first to *implement* it, in an experimental PL/I compiler for the IBM System/370 with 17 registers (16 general-purpose plus the condition code) [1]. Their 1982 follow-up extended the framework to handle spilling natively: when the conflict graph cannot be colored with *k* colors, the allocator adds spill code, guided by cost estimates of keeping each value in a register versus memory [2].

### 2.2 The coalescing revolution

Chaitin's allocator suffered from a well-known pathology: *copy* instructions (register-to-register moves) generated by earlier phases could not be eliminated, and aggressive coalescing of their endpoints could render an otherwise colorable graph uncolorable. Briggs' 1992 Rice thesis introduced **optimistic coloring**—assuming high-degree nodes *can* be colored rather than preemptively spilling them—**conservative coalescing** (merge only when provably safe), **rematerialization**, and **live-range splitting**; the journal version with Cooper and Torczon remains the canonical reference [3]. George and Appel's **iterated register coalescing** (1996) simplified the architecture by interleaving simplification and coalescing in a single worklist algorithm and introduced the more aggressive **George criterion** [5]. Cooper, Dasgupta, and Eckhardt later revisited the Chaitin–Briggs design empirically, confirming its robustness [3].

### 2.3 Speed: linear scan and priority-based coloring

Chow and Hennessy's **priority-based coloring** (1984) offered an alternative philosophy: allocate registers to live ranges in priority order (estimated spill cost divided by size), splitting ranges that do not fit [3]. Poletto and Sarkar's **linear scan** (1999) went further, abandoning the interference graph entirely: live ranges are linearized into *intervals* over a total order of program points, and allocation proceeds in a single sweep with an *active* set of intervals, expiring those that end [4]. Linear scan is "up to several times faster than even a fast graph coloring allocator" while producing code "within 12% as fast" as aggressive coloring on most benchmarks [4]. Traub, Holloway, and Smith's **second-chance binpacking** added interval splitting and lifetime holes [4], and Wimmer and Mössenböck's improvements—spill-weight-driven splitting and SSA form—power the HotSpot client compiler and, in SSA-based form, the Graal compiler [12].

### 2.4 Optimality: PBQP and SSA chordality

For irregular architectures (DSPs with heterogeneous register banks, x86's overlapping `al`/`ax`/`eax`/`rax` aliasing), Scholz and Eckstein formulated allocation as **partitioned boolean quadratic programming**: each variable selects a register (a boolean vector), and costs—assignment costs plus pairwise interference costs—are minimized by a dedicated PBQP solver [8]. Meanwhile, a striking theoretical result emerged: the interference graphs of programs in *strict SSA form* are **chordal**, and chordal graphs are colored optimally by greedy elimination ordering [6][7]. Pereira and Palsberg gave a puzzle-solving allocator exploiting this structure [7]; Hack, Grund, and Goos built a production-quality SSA allocator for all of SPEC [6]. The catch, identified by Pereira and Palsberg, is that *spilling* and *coalescing* remain hard even under SSA, and classical SSA destruction reintroduces the general case.

### 2.5 Verified allocation

Leroy's CompCert project produced the first realistic, fully **verified** optimizing C compiler, including a verified register allocator based on iterated graph coloring with a validator architecture: an untrusted Caml implementation proposes an allocation, and a proved Coq checker validates it [9]. This established that sophisticated allocation need not be sacrificed for formal guarantees.

| Allocator family | Representative work | Compile-time complexity | Code quality | Production use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chaitin coloring | Chaitin et al. 1981/82 [1][2] | *O(n log n)*–*O(n²)* | High (for its era) | IBM PL/I prototype |
| Chaitin–Briggs | Briggs–Cooper–Torczon 1994 [3] | *O(n log n)* typical | High | GCC (historical), research |
| Iterated coalescing | George–Appel 1996 [5] | Worklist, near-linear | High | MLRISC, Jikes RVM |
| Linear scan | Poletto–Sarkar 1999 [4] | *O(n log n)*, small constant | Within ~12% of coloring [4] | HotSpot client, V8 (historical) |

| SSA chordal | Hack et al. 2006 [6] | Polynomial optimal coloring | High | Research, Graal-family |
| Greedy (LLVM) | LLVM 3.0+ | Near-linear, tunable | Production best-in-class | LLVM/Clang, Rust, Swift |
| Verified coloring | Leroy/CompCert 2009 [9] | Moderate | Conservative | CompCert |

## 3. Methodology / Formal Framework

We now fix notation used throughout. A program is a control-flow graph whose nodes are instructions; a *temporary* (variable) *v* is **live** at program point *p* if there exists a path from *p* to a use of *v* containing no definition of *v*. The **live range** of *v* is the set of points where *v* is live.

> **Definition 1 (Interference graph).** The interference graph *G = (V, E)* has one node per live range; *(u, v) ∈ E* iff *u* and *v* are simultaneously live at some program point (equivalently, their live ranges *interfere*). A **k-coloring** *c : V → {1, …, k}* with *c(u) ≠ c(v)* for all *(u,v) ∈ E* is a valid register assignment using *k* registers.

### 3.1 Chaitin's simplify–spill–select

The classical algorithm [2] proceeds in phases:

```
Simplify:   while ∃ node n with degree(n) < k:
                push n onto stack; remove n from G
Spill:      if G nonempty: choose spill candidate n
                (minimizing spill_cost(n) / degree(n)),
                push n; remove n            // optimistic: may still color it
Select:     while stack nonempty:
                pop n; assign a color not used by its (already colored) neighbors
                if none available: mark n spilled   // actual spill
Rebuild:    insert spill code; repeat until G is k-colorable
```

> **Theorem (Chaitin):** Any node removed during *Simplify* with degree *< k* is guaranteed a color during *Select*, because at most *k − 1* of its neighbors can already be colored when it is popped. [2]

*Spill cost* estimates the runtime penalty of spilling *n*: typically *Σ 10^depth* over uses and definitions, weighted by loop nesting depth. Chaitin's original heuristic spilled eagerly (pessimistic); Briggs' **optimistic coloring** defers the decision—spill candidates pushed during *Simplify* may still receive colors in *Select*, and empirically often do [3].

### 3.2 Coalescing criteria

A copy instruction *u := v* (or *u := v* with *u, v* simultaneously live only at the copy) invites **coalescing**: merging nodes *u* and *v* so the copy becomes a no-op. Merging adds the union of both nodes' edges, potentially raising degrees above *k − 1* and making the graph uncolorable. Two safety criteria govern the decision [3][5]:

> **Briggs criterion (conservative):** Let *t* be the node formed by merging *u* and *v*. Coalescing is *safe* iff the number of neighbors of *t* with degree *≥ k* is strictly less than *k*. Then *t* can always be simplified later: the high-degree neighbors are the only ones that could block coloring, and there are fewer than *k* of them.

> **George criterion (aggressive):** For copy *a := b*, coalescing *a* and *b* is safe iff for every neighbor *t* of *a*, either *t* is also a neighbor of *b* or *degree(t) < k*. Intuition: every neighbor of *a* that could threaten colorability either already interferes with *b* (no new constraint) or is low-degree (simplifiable).

The George criterion admits strictly more coalescing than Briggs'; George and Appel's **iterated** algorithm interleaves simplify and coalesce on worklists, re-simplifying after each successful merge, which eliminates most copies without the phase-ordering pathologies of earlier designs [5].

### 3.3 Linear scan

Linear scan replaces the graph with **intervals**: each live range is flattened to *[start, end]* over a linear numbering of instructions (lifetime holes optionally modeled) [4]:

```
active ← ∅
for each interval i in order of increasing start:
    ExpireOldIntervals(i)          // move ended intervals out of active, free registers
    if active is full (size = k):
        spill i or an active interval (by end point / spill weight)
    else:
        assign i a free register; insert i into active (sorted by end)
```

The algorithm is *O(n log n)* with tiny constants and single-pass over intervals; spilling is handled by splitting the interval at the current point or evicting the active interval with the farthest end [4][12].

### 3.4 PBQP formulation

Partitioned boolean quadratic programming [8] encodes allocation as optimization. For each variable *vᵢ*, a boolean vector **xᵢ** (exactly one entry 1) selects its register; cost matrices *Cᵢ* capture per-register assignment costs and *Cᵢⱼ* capture pairwise costs (∞ for interfering pairs assigned the same register, move costs for coalescing pairs). The objective:

$$\min \sum_i \mathbf{x}_i^T C_i + \sum_{(i,j) \in E} \mathbf{x}_i^T C_{ij} \mathbf{x}_j \quad 	ext{s.t. } \|\mathbf{x}_i\|_1 = 1$$

A branch-and-bound / reduction solver finds optimal or near-optimal solutions; register *classes*, aliasing, and irregular costs fall out of the cost matrices naturally [8].

### 3.5 SSA and chordality

In **strict SSA form** (each variable defined once, φ-functions at joins, and additionally the *dominance* property that definitions dominate uses), the interference graph has remarkable structure:

> **Theorem (Hack et al.; Pereira–Palsberg):** The interference graph of a program in strict SSA form is **chordal**: every cycle of length ≥ 4 has a chord. Chordal graphs admit a *perfect elimination ordering*, and greedy coloring along it uses exactly *ω(G)* colors (the clique number), which is optimal since *χ(G) ≥ ω(G)* always. [6][7]

Consequences: *coloring* SSA programs is polynomial and optimal; the remaining hardness lives in *spilling* (which live ranges to evict), *coalescing*, and SSA destruction (φ-elimination reintroduces general graphs) [6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Chaitin Simplification and the Spill Heuristic

Chaitin's central insight was that *degree < k* nodes are unconditionally safe to defer [2]. This yields a beautifully simple invariant, but the *spill heuristic*—which node to evict when simplification stalls—is where engineering judgment enters. The classical metric, *spill_cost(n)/degree(n)*, balances two forces: spilling a high-cost node (many hot uses) hurts runtime, while spilling a high-degree node unblocks many neighbors. Briggs observed that Chaitin's pessimism—assuming a pushed spill candidate is *doomed*—wasted opportunities: optimistic coloring, which attempts to color every node in *Select* regardless of how it was pushed, is "never worse" and often strictly better [3].

*Rematerialization* refines spilling further: some values (constants, addresses computable by a single `lea`) are cheaper to *recompute* than to spill and reload. Briggs propagated rematerialization information through the SSA graph using a variant of Wegman–Zadeck sparse conditional constant propagation, discovering a larger class of such values [3]. Modern allocators (notably LLVM's greedy) incorporate rematerialization as a first-class eviction alternative.

*Live-range splitting* attacks spills from the other side: rather than spilling a variable everywhere, split its range at selected points (loop boundaries, call sites) and spill only the high-pressure segment. Briggs incorporated Chow–Hennessy-style splitting into the coloring framework by splitting *before* coloring at chosen program points [3].

### 4.2 Coalescing: Conservative versus Aggressive

Copy instructions are the allocator's original sin: they exist to reconcile allocation decisions, yet each copy is a potential pipeline bubble. The tension is fundamental—**coalescing reduces moves but can increase spills** by raising node degrees. The Briggs criterion is *conservative*: it guarantees the merged node remains simplifiable, at the price of leaving many copies uncoalesced [3]. The George criterion is *aggressive*: it permits merges that Briggs would reject, relying on the iterated worklist to recover colorability [5].

Empirically, iterated coalescing with the George criterion eliminates the vast majority of copies introduced by SSA destruction and calling-convention lowering, with negligible spill increase [5]. A subtle point: coalescing interacts with *precolored* nodes (registers pinned by the ABI or by instructions with fixed register requirements). Precolored nodes are never simplified or spilled; coalescing a node with a precolored node is always safe and always desirable, since it can only reduce constraints.

> **Theorem (George–Appel invariant):** In iterated register coalescing, the simplify worklist always contains exactly the low-degree non-move-related nodes, and every coalescing step preserves the invariant that the current graph is a *conservative approximation* of the original: any *k*-coloring of the coalesced graph induces a valid allocation of the original program modulo the eliminated copies. [5]

### 4.3 Linear Scan and Its SSA Descendants

Linear scan's genius is representational: by numbering instructions and flattening live ranges to intervals, the *O(n²)*-flavored graph problem becomes a sweep-line problem [4]. The basic algorithm handles the common case; production variants add:

- **Lifetime holes**: intervals need not be contiguous; an interval inactive at the current point frees its register temporarily (Wimmer–Mössenböck) [12].
- **Interval splitting**: on register pressure, split the current interval and spill only the tail, or evict the active interval with the farthest end point (belady-like) [12].
- **SSA form**: Wimmer and Franz showed linear scan on SSA form simplifies interval construction (each SSA value's live range is a contiguous subtree of the dominator tree) and improves allocation quality to near-parity with coloring allocators [12].

The cost is real but bounded: Poletto and Sarkar measured code within 12% of aggressive graph coloring on nearly all benchmarks, at a fraction of the compile time [4]. For JIT compilers—HotSpot's client compiler, V8's historical full-codegen, Android's ART—this trade is decisive: allocation latency is user-visible jank.

### 4.4 PBQP: Optimality for Irregular Architectures

Graph coloring assumes registers are *interchangeable*: any color fits any node. Real machines violate this. x86-64 has overlapping sub-registers (`al`⊂`ax`⊂`eax`⊂`rax`), calling conventions pin arguments to specific registers, DSPs offer heterogeneous banks (address vs. data registers), and vector extensions impose alignment classes. Coloring handles these with *register classes* and precoloring—workable, but the heuristics degrade.

PBQP [8] dissolves the distinction: every architectural quirk becomes a number in a cost matrix. The solver (reduction rules R0/R1/R2 plus branch-and-bound) finds optimal solutions for function-sized instances and excellent approximations beyond. LLVM ships a PBQP allocator as an alternative backend; on irregular targets it measurably beats the greedy allocator's heuristics, at the cost of significantly higher compile time. PBQP's lesson is methodological: when the machine is irregular, *formulate allocation as explicit cost optimization* rather than stretching the coloring metaphor.

### 4.5 SSA, Chordality, and the Optimal Coloring Theorem

The deepest theoretical result in modern allocation is the **chordality of SSA interference graphs** [6][7]. The proof sketch: in strict SSA, each variable's live range is connected in the dominator tree; interference graphs of such "subtree" families are chordal. Chordal graphs are *perfect*: *χ(G) = ω(G)*, and greedy coloring in a perfect elimination order (computable by maximum-cardinality search in linear time) is optimal.

This reframes the entire field. The *coloring* subproblem—the historical source of NP-completeness anxiety—is easy for SSA programs. What remains hard:

1. **Spilling**: choosing a minimum-cost set of live ranges to evict so the remainder is *k*-colorable is NP-hard even under SSA (it generalizes knapsack-like packing on cliques).
2. **Coalescing**: optimal copy elimination remains NP-hard.
3. **SSA destruction**: conventional φ-elimination (replacing φ-functions with copies on predecessor edges, à la Sreedhar) can introduce interferences that break chordality; *SSA-based* allocators therefore color *before* destruction and repair afterward, or use *conventional* SSA (CSSA) with careful copy placement [6].

Hack, Grund, and Goos demonstrated the practical payoff: an SSA-based allocator with optimal coloring, spill-everywhere-then-repair spilling, and decoupled coalescing, competitive with the best coloring allocators on SPEC [6].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The complexity record, corrected

The most-quoted sentence in register allocation—"register allocation is NP-complete because graph coloring is NP-complete"—is, as Bouchez, Darte, and Rastello meticulously show, a *folk theorem* that outruns Chaitin's actual proof [10]. Chaitin showed that *given an arbitrary graph*, one can construct a program whose interference graph is that graph, so *k*-coloring reduces to allocation *in the worst case over programs*. This does **not** imply that deciding whether spilling is necessary for a *fixed* program is NP-complete; indeed, for several natural formulations (e.g., "can this program be allocated with *k* registers allowing splitting/moves?") the problem is polynomial or has different hardness [10]. The honest complexity map:

| Problem variant | Complexity |
|---|---|
| *k*-coloring a general interference graph | NP-complete (reduction from graph coloring) [1][10] |
| Coloring an SSA (chordal) interference graph | Polynomial, optimal via PEO [6][7] |
| Minimum-cost spilling (general) | NP-hard |
| Optimal coalescing | NP-hard |
| Allocation with live-range splitting allowed | Polynomial in several formulations [10] |
| Verified allocation (CompCert) | Decidable; proof-checked [9] |

### 5.2 Production evidence: LLVM's greedy allocator

LLVM's default **greedy allocator** descends from the linear-scan lineage but with coloring-grade sophistication: live intervals with spill weights, global eviction (spill a whole interval rather than split locally), live-range splitting at basic-block boundaries driven by a cost model, rematerialization, and iterative repair. It replaced both the earlier linear-scan ("fast") and PBQP paths as the default because it dominates on the metric that matters—*runtime performance per compile-time second*—across x86-64, AArch64, and RISC-V. The "fast" linear-scan allocator survives only for `-O0`, where compile time is paramount [4][12].

### 5.3 Verified allocation in CompCert

CompCert's allocator [9] is an *iterated register coalescing* implementation in OCaml whose output is validated by a Coq-proved checker: the validator confirms that the proposed coloring respects interferences, that coalesced copies are genuinely no-ops, and that spill code preserves semantics. The architecture—*unverified oracle + verified checker*—is the key methodological insight: it permits aggressive heuristics inside the trusted workflow without enlarging the trusted proof. CompCert's allocator is deliberately conservative (simpler heuristics, more spills than LLVM), quantifying the current *verifiability tax* on allocation quality.

### 5.4 Spill heuristics and rematerialization, quantified

The literature converges on several quantitative lessons:

- **Optimistic coloring** (Briggs) strictly dominates pessimistic spilling: spill code decreases, never increases [3].
- **Spill-everywhere** (spill the whole live range) versus **spill-at-use** (load before each use): the former simplifies the interference graph maximally but wastes bandwidth on cold segments; modern allocators split the difference via *splitting* [3][12].
- **Rematerialization** recovers 5–15% of spill traffic on constant-heavy code in Briggs' measurements [3]; LLVM's rematerializer is a standard late-allocation pass.
- **Priority-based coloring** (Chow–Hennessy) remains competitive on straight-line-heavy code but exhibits *O(n²)* behavior that Briggs measured against coloring's *O(n log n)* [3].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**The spilling gap.** Optimal coloring of SSA programs solved the easy half; *spill placement*—which ranges to evict, where to place loads/stores, how to split—remains heuristic everywhere, including LLVM and GCC. The PBQP formulation *can* express spill costs, but solver scalability limits its deployment [8].

**Irregular register classes.** Modern ISAs proliferate register classes: x86-64's `r8`–`r15` versus legacy byte-addressable registers, AArch64's scalar/vector/FP overlap, RISC-V's compressed-instruction constraints. Coloring allocators model these via class-constrained graphs; the heuristics interact poorly with coalescing. PBQP handles them natively but slowly [8]. A fast, near-optimal class-aware allocator remains open.

**Precoloring and fixed instructions.** Two-address instructions, ABI-fixed registers, and inline assembly constraints pin nodes to colors *before* allocation. Under SSA, precoloring destroys the chordality guarantee (pinned nodes create arbitrary constraints), dragging the problem back toward NP-hardness in practice [6].

**Verified optimizing allocation.** CompCert proves allocation *correct* but leaves performance on the table; LLVM is fast but unverified. Closing this gap—perhaps via verified validators for greedy-style allocators—is the most important open problem at the intersection of allocation and formal methods [9].

**Machine-learned heuristics.** Recent work explores learned spill weights and splitting policies. The risk is compile-time unpredictability; the promise is closing the last percents of the heuristic gap on irregular hardware.

---

## 7. Conclusion

From Chaitin's 1981 reduction of allocation to coloring [1], through the spill-aware extension of 1982 [2], Briggs' optimistic coloring and conservative coalescing [3], George and Appel's iterated aggressive coalescing [5], Poletto and Sarkar's linear scan for JITs [4], Scholz and Eckstein's PBQP optimum for irregular machines [8], the SSA chordality theorems of Hack, Pereira, and colleagues [6][7], and Leroy's verified allocator [9], register allocation has matured from black art to a structured engineering discipline with sharp theoretical boundaries. The NP-completeness folklore, properly understood [10], does not condemn us to heuristics everywhere: coloring itself is polynomial and optimal for SSA programs, and the residual hardness is concentrated in spilling, coalescing, and irregular architectures—precisely where the next decade of research belongs.
