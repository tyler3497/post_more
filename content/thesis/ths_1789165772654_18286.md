# Factor-Graph SLAM: Smoothing Formulations, Incremental Inference with iSAM2, and the Geometry of Robot Localization and Mapping

## Abstract

Simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) is the problem of estimating a robot's trajectory while simultaneously constructing a map of its environment. Modern SLAM is best understood not as a filtering problem but as a **smoothing** problem: the full posterior over the robot trajectory and the map is encoded as a *factor graph*, and the maximum-a-posteriori estimate is recovered by sparse nonlinear least squares. This thesis develops the complete pipeline, from the probabilistic formulation of the smoothing problem over **SE(2)** and **SE(3)** to the Bayes-tree machinery that makes *incremental* inference possible in real time. We present variable elimination as the graphical-model dual of sparse QR/Cholesky factorization, show how the resulting Bayes tree localizes the effect of new measurements to a small affected subtree, and derive the **iSAM2** algorithm — fluid relinearization plus incremental variable reordering — as its consequence. The geometry of robot localization is treated through the lens of Lie groups, tangent-space linearization, and manifold optimization, including the role of *loop-closure constraints* in bending drifted trajectories back onto consistent manifolds. Empirical evidence from standard benchmarks demonstrates that incremental smoothing attains batch accuracy at a fraction of the cost, while formal theorems characterize the exactness of elimination, the consistency of marginal recovery, and the convergence of relinearized Gauss–Newton on the product manifold.

## 1. Introduction

In 1986, Smith, Self, and Cheeseman introduced the stochastic map: a representation in which both robot pose and landmark positions are uncertain quantities, coupled by the measurements that relate them [1]. From this origin, SLAM matured through the Kalman-filter era — extended Kalman filter (EKF) SLAM, the thin junction tree filter, FastSLAM — and converged, over the 2000s, on a single dominant abstraction: **the factor graph**. The key realization, articulated forcefully by Dellaert and Kaess, is that the SLAM posterior factorizes into a product of local terms, each depending on a small subset of variables, and that this factorization is simultaneously the natural representation of the probability density *and* the natural substrate for the sparse linear algebra that solves it [2].

Filtering approaches (EKF SLAM, particle filters) estimate only the *current* pose and marginalize the past. They are online by construction but pay a price: marginalization introduces dense couplings, destroys sparsity, and — in the nonlinear case — permanently commits to linearization points, producing inconsistent estimates. Smoothing instead keeps the *entire trajectory* in the estimation problem. This is seemingly more expensive, but the factor graph of the full smoothing problem is sparse, and sparsity-aware inference is dramatically cheaper than dense covariance maintenance. The historical trajectory of the field can therefore be summarized: filtering was the right idea under the constraint of 1990s computation; smoothing became the right idea once sparse nonlinear least squares on factor graphs was made incremental.

The central technical obstacle is this: a robot operating online receives measurements one at a time, and must update its estimate *before* the next measurement arrives. Re-solving a batch nonlinear least-squares problem from scratch at every step is prohibitive. The iSAM family of algorithms — iSAM (2008) and iSAM2 (2012) — solved this by exploiting the structure of **incremental variable elimination**: each new measurement affects only a small portion of the problem, and the Bayes tree data structure [3] makes that locality explicit, computable, and maintainable under nonlinear relinearization.

> **Theorem (Informal):** *For a sparse factor-graph SLAM problem, the cost of an incremental Bayes-tree update is proportional to the size of the re-eliminated subtree, not to the size of the full trajectory — yielding near-constant-time updates on exploration problems and sublinear updates under loop closures.*

This thesis develops that theorem's full context. Section 2 reviews the probabilistic and algebraic background: factor graphs, MAP as nonlinear least squares, and optimization on manifolds. Section 3 develops the methodology: variable elimination, the Bayes tree, fluid relinearization, and the iSAM2 update cycle. Section 4 dives deep into five topics — the elimination/factorization duality, incremental ordering, relinearization geometry, loop-closure and manifold structure, and marginal covariance recovery. Section 5 presents empirical results and formal statements. Section 6 catalogues limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From filtering to smoothing

The original SLAM formulation of Smith, Self, and Cheeseman modeled the robot state and map as a single Gaussian random vector, propagated by an extended Kalman filter [1]. The EKF maintains the *filtered* density *p(xₖ | z₁:ₖ)* — the distribution over the current pose given all measurements so far. Marginalizing past poses is what makes the EKF online, but it also couples every landmark to every other landmark: the information matrix of the filtered problem is dense, and maintaining it costs *O(n²)* in the number of landmarks.

Smoothing instead targets *p(x₁:ₖ, L | z₁:ₖ)*, the density over the **full trajectory** *X = {x₁, …, xₖ}* and the map *L = {l₁, …, lₘ}*. Counter-intuitively, this larger state space yields a *sparser* problem: each measurement involves only the poses and landmarks it directly observes, so the information matrix of the smoothing problem has the sparsity of a banded-plus-arrowhead matrix — block-tridiagonal from odometry plus off-diagonal blocks from observations. Smoothing is where GTSAM got its name: "GT **Smoothing** and Mapping" [4].

### 2.2 Factor graphs and the MAP problem

A factor graph *G = (F, X, E)* is a bipartite graph with factor nodes *fᵢ ∈ F* and variable nodes *xⱼ ∈ X*, where an edge *(fᵢ, xⱼ)* indicates that factor *fᵢ* depends on variable *xⱼ* [2]. In SLAM, the factors are:

| Factor type | Variables | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Prior *f₀(x₁)* | *x₁* | Initial pose belief |
| Odometry *f(xᵢ, xᵢ₊₁; uᵢ)* | consecutive poses | Motion model with control *uᵢ* |
| Observation *f(xᵢ, lⱼ; zᵢⱼ)* | pose + landmark | Bearing/range measurement *zᵢⱼ* |
| Loop closure *f(xᵢ, xₖ)* | non-consecutive poses | Place-recognition constraint |

The joint density factorizes as *p(X, L | Z) ∝ ∏ᵢ fᵢ(Xᵢ)*. Assuming Gaussian noise models, the MAP estimate

$$\hat{X} = \arg\max_X \prod_i f_i(X_i) = \arg\min_X \sum_i \lVert h_i(X_i) - z_i \rVert^2_{\Sigma_i}$$

is a **nonlinear least-squares** problem. Linearizing each measurement function *hᵢ* about the current estimate *X⁰*,

$$h_i(X_i) \approx h_i(X_i^0) + H_i\,\delta_i,$$

yields the linear system *Aδ = b* (whitened: *AᵀA δ = Aᵀb*), solved by sparse QR or Cholesky factorization of the information matrix *Λ = AᵀA*. Iterating linearization and solve is **Gauss–Newton**; adding a trust region gives **Levenberg–Marquardt**.

### 2.3 Optimization on manifolds

Robot poses live on **Lie groups**, not vector spaces: planar poses on *SE(2)*, spatial poses on *SE(3)*. Linearization must therefore happen in the *tangent space*. For *T ∈ SE(3)*, a perturbation is expressed as *T ← T · Exp(ξ)* with *ξ ∈ 𝔰𝔢(3)*, and Jacobians are computed with respect to the **retraction**

$$R_T(\xi) = T\,\mathrm{Exp}(\xi), \qquad \xi \in \mathbb{R}^6.$$

The Gauss–Newton update becomes *δ\* = argmin_δ ‖Aδ − b‖²* with *δ* a stacked tangent vector, applied via *X ← X ⊕ δ\** using the retraction at each variable. This is the machinery introduced systematically in *Factor Graphs for Robot Perception* [2]: optimization *on* the manifold, not in a chart, avoids singularities (no Euler-angle gimbal lock, no quaternion norm constraints leaking into the linearization).

> **Theorem (Manifold Gauss–Newton):** *If the retraction R is a first-order retraction (DR(0) = id) and the linearized system has full column rank, then one Gauss–Newton step on the product manifold decreases the cost for sufficiently small steps, and the iteration converges quadratically in a neighborhood of a non-degenerate minimum.*

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Variable elimination as factorization

The methodological core of modern incremental SLAM is **variable elimination**, the graphical-model dual of sparse matrix factorization [3]. To eliminate variable *xⱼ* from the factor graph:

1. Collect all factors adjacent to *xⱼ* into the set *Fⱼ*.
2. Form the joint *ψ(xⱼ, Sⱼ) = ∏_{f∈Fⱼ} f*, where *Sⱼ* is the *separator* (neighbors of *xⱼ* excluding *xⱼ*).
3. Factor *ψ(xⱼ, Sⱼ) = p(xⱼ | Sⱼ)·τ(Sⱼ)* by conditioning on the separator (a Schur-complement operation in the Gaussian case).
4. Remove *xⱼ* and its factors; add *τ(Sⱼ)* as a new factor on *Sⱼ*; store the conditional *p(xⱼ | Sⱼ)*.

Repeating for an **elimination ordering** *π* converts the factor graph into a **Bayes net** — a directed acyclic graph encoding *p(X) = ∏ⱼ p(xⱼ | Sⱼ)*. Grouping each variable with its separator yields the **Bayes tree** (clique tree): a directed tree whose nodes are cliques *Cⱼ = {xⱼ} ∪ Sⱼ*, with edges from child to parent separators. Solving then proceeds *root-to-leaves* (back-substitution), while *adding* measurements corresponds to eliminating only the variables in the affected cliques.

The critical correspondence [3]:

| Graphical model operation | Linear algebra operation |
|---|---|
| Variable elimination | Sparse QR / Cholesky factorization |
| Separator | Nonzero pattern of a column of *R* |
| Bayes tree | Elimination tree of *AᵀA* |
| Conditional *p(xⱼ \| Sⱼ)* | Row of the square-root information matrix *R* |
| Back-substitution (root→leaves) | Solving *Rδ = d* |
| Re-eliminating a subtree | Partial refactorization of affected columns |

### 3.2 The iSAM2 update cycle

Given a Bayes tree from previous steps and a set of **new factors** Δ*F* (new odometry, new observations, loop closures), iSAM2 performs [3]:

1. **Identify the affected variables**: the union of variables in Δ*F* and their separators — the *top* of the Bayes tree (the most recently eliminated variables, which sit near the root under a good ordering).
2. **Detach** the affected subtree: remove those cliques from the tree, converting their conditionals back into factors.
3. **Re-eliminate**: eliminate the detached variables together with the new factors, using an **incremental variable ordering** (constrained COLAMD: recently accessed variables forced last, so they stay near the root).
4. **Reattach** the new subtree; back-substitute only along the changed path to update the estimate *incrementally*.

Crucially, the unaffected majority of the tree — often thousands of cliques — is *never touched*. The update cost scales with the subtree, not the trajectory length.

### 3.3 Fluid relinearization

Nonlinearity complicates incrementality: a Bayes tree encodes conditionals linearized at specific linearization points, and as the estimate drifts those points go stale. iSAM (2008) handled this with **periodic batch relinearization** — expensive, and a source of latency spikes [6]. iSAM2 replaces it with **fluid relinearization**: each variable tracks *Δ*, the deviation of its current estimate from its linearization point, and is marked for relinearization when *‖Δ‖ ≥ β* (default *β = 0.1*).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The elimination–factorization duality in detail

Consider the linearized SLAM system *Aδ = b* with *A ∈ ℝ^{m×n}*.

> **Theorem (Elimination–QR correspondence):** *Eliminating variables x₁, …, xₙ in order from a Gaussian factor graph produces the same upper-triangular factor R (up to sign) as the QR factorization of the whitened Jacobian A with columns ordered x₁, …, xₙ. The separator of xⱼ is precisely the set of variables with nonzeros in row j of R.*

The practical consequence is that *every sparse factorization algorithm is an inference algorithm on a graphical model*, and vice versa.

Fill-in has a graphical reading too: eliminating *xⱼ* adds edges between all pairs in its separator (the new factor *τ(Sⱼ)* couples them).

### 4.2 Incremental ordering and the locality of updates

Why do Bayes-tree updates stay local?

Loop closures break this locality: a constraint *f(xᵢ, xₖ)* with *i ≪ k* forces re-elimination of every clique on the path from *xᵢ*'s clique to the root.

There is a subtle failure mode worth naming: **ordering thrash**.

### 4.3 The geometry of relinearization

Fluid relinearization is, geometrically, a statement about **how far the tangent-space approximation can be trusted**.

A deeper view: the Bayes tree stores a *Gaussian belief in tangent coordinates anchored at linearization points*.

```python
import numpy as np

def fluid_relinearization_check(deltas, beta=0.1):
    """Mark variables whose estimate drifted beyond the trust threshold.

    deltas: dict var_id -> tangent-space deviation from linearization point
    Returns the set of variables to re-eliminate in the next iSAM2 update.
    """
    return {v for v, d in deltas.items() if np.linalg.norm(d) >= beta}

# Example: after a loop closure, recent poses moved substantially
deltas = {"x_%d" % i: np.random.randn(6) * (0.3 if i > 950 else 0.01)
          for i in range(1000)}
marked = fluid_relinearization_check(deltas)
print(f"{len(marked)} of {len(deltas)} variables marked for relinearization")
```

### 4.4 Loop closures and the geometry of localization

Odometry integrates motion: each step compounds noise, and pose uncertainty grows without bound — the trajectory *drifts*.

This is where the manifold structure bites hardest.

A notorious failure is the **false loop closure**: a wrong data association that adds a factor between poses that are not actually co-located.

### 4.5 Marginal covariances and consistency

MAP estimation gives the mode; many applications need the **marginals** *p(xᵢ | Z)* — the uncertainty ellipses.

Consistency — whether the estimated covariance matches the true error statistics — is the Achilles' heel of filtering SLAM: the EKF linearizes once and never revisits, so its covariance is systematically overconfident.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Formal results

We collect the key theoretical claims, with proof sketches.

> **Theorem 1 (Exactness of elimination):** *Variable elimination on a factor graph computes the exact MAP estimate (in the linear/Gaussian case) and the exact posterior factorization (in general). The Bayes tree stores exactly the conditional densities p(xⱼ | Sⱼ) of the chain-rule factorization induced by the elimination ordering.*

*Proof sketch.* Eliminating *xⱼ* replaces *∏_{f∈Fⱼ} f* by *p(xⱼ | Sⱼ)·τ(Sⱼ)*, an identity (conditioning). The product over all eliminations telescopes to *p(X) = ∏ⱼ p(xⱼ | Sⱼ)*, the chain rule. In the Gaussian case, each conditioning is a Schur complement, and the resulting *R* satisfies *RᵀR = Λ*; back-substitution then solves *Λδ = Aᵀb* exactly. ∎

> **Theorem 2 (Incremental correctness):** *Detaching the affected subtree of a Bayes tree, re-eliminating it with new factors, and reattaching yields the same Bayes tree (same ordering, same linearization points) as batch elimination of the full updated graph.*

*Proof sketch.* Elimination is a *local* operation: the conditional stored at each clique depends only on the factors in its subtree. Cliques outside the affected subtree have identical factor sets before and after the update, so their conditionals are unchanged; re-eliminating the detached variables with the new factors reproduces exactly the elimination the batch algorithm would perform on those variables. ∎

> **Theorem 3 (Convergence of relinearized Gauss–Newton on manifolds):** *Under standard smoothness and full-rank assumptions, the iSAM2 iteration — Gauss–Newton steps on the product manifold with fluid relinearization — converges to a stationary point of the MAP objective; near a non-degenerate minimum the convergence is quadratic.*

*Proof sketch.* Each update solves the linearized problem exactly (Theorem 1), producing a descent direction in the tangent space at the current estimate (manifold Gauss–Newton theorem, §2.3). Fluid relinearization guarantees linearization points track the estimate within *β*, so the model error is uniformly bounded; standard trust-region/line-search arguments then give global convergence to stationarity, and the Dennis–Moré characterization gives quadratic local convergence. ∎

### 5.2 Empirical results

Kaess et al. evaluate iSAM2 on seven datasets — Manhattan3500, City10000, Intel, Victoria Park, Sphere2500, and others — against batch Cholesky, iSAM1, HOG-Man, and SPA [3]:

| Dataset | Poses | iSAM2/update (ms) | Batch (ms) | Speedup |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Manhattan3500 | 3,500 | ~7 | ~40 | ~6× |
| City10000 | 10,000 | ~10 | ~120 | ~12× |
| Intel | 1,227 | ~2 | ~8 | ~4× |
| Victoria Park | 6,969 | ~15 | ~60 | ~4× |
| Sphere2500 | 2,500 | ~9 | ~55 | ~6× |

*(Timings are representative orders of magnitude from [3]; exact figures depend on hardware and GTSAM version.)*

Accuracy matches the batch solution to within *χ²* noise: the normalized *χ²* error of iSAM2 with *β = 0.1* is statistically indistinguishable from batch Levenberg–Marquardt, while iSAM1 with periodic relinearization shows transient error spikes at batch steps. The number of relinearized variables per update stays below 5% on exploration segments and spikes only at loop closures — the fluid mechanism concentrates computation exactly where nonlinearity demands it.

The broader empirical record since 2012 has been decisive: iSAM2 (via GTSAM) became the default back end for visual-inertial odometry, lidar SLAM, and multi-robot mapping systems [5, 7]. The Bayes tree has additionally enabled *covariance recovery*, *incremental variable reordering* research, and non-Gaussian extensions via nested sampling [7].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Gaussianity.** The entire edifice — elimination as Schur complement, the Bayes tree as *R* with probabilities — rests on Gaussian factors. Multimodal posteriors (ambiguous data association, symmetric environments) are collapsed to a single mode. Extensions exist (multi-hypothesis trees, non-Gaussian nested sampling [7]) but sacrifice the clean incremental structure.
2. **Data association is assumed.** The factor graph takes measurement-to-landmark associations as given; the back end cannot fix front-end association errors except through robust costs. A wrong association is a wrong factor, and no amount of incremental elegance repairs it.
3. **Worst-case update cost.** A loop closure to an ancient pose re-eliminates a large subtree — *O(n)* in the worst case. Real-time guarantees are therefore *amortized*, not hard. Systems with hard deadlines (e.g., 100 Hz visual-inertial odometry) typically run iSAM2 in a back-end thread decoupled from the front end.
4. **Ordering pathology.** Constrained COLAMD is a heuristic. Adversarial closure patterns can induce ordering thrash, and there exist graphs where the incremental ordering is far from the optimal batch ordering. Periodic full reordering is a pragmatic safety valve.
5. **Linearization-point staleness in the tree.** Fluid relinearization bounds staleness by *β*, but variables *below* the relinearized subtree keep old linearization points indefinitely. If the global estimate shifts substantially (a large loop closure), stale points in untouched cliques introduce small but nonzero inconsistency — the price of incrementality.
6. **Scalability of dense subproblems.** Landmarks seen from many poses (or dense photometric factors) create large cliques; elimination cost is cubic in clique size. Submapping and sparsification techniques address this at the cost of exactness.

## 7. Conclusion

Factor-graph SLAM reframes localization and mapping as *sparse nonlinear least squares on a graphical model*, and the Bayes tree reveals that sparse factorization *is* probabilistic inference. iSAM2's contribution — incremental variable reordering plus fluid relinearization on the Bayes tree — turned the batch smoothing formulation into a genuinely online algorithm without sacrificing the accuracy that makes smoothing superior to filtering. The geometry underlying it all is the geometry of Lie groups: poses on manifolds, linearization in tangent spaces, loop closures as projections onto constraint manifolds, and uncertainty as covariance propagated through conditional densities.

The trajectory of the field since iSAM2 confirms the paradigm: factor graphs now underlie visual-inertial navigation, lidar mapping, calibration, and planning, unified by libraries like GTSAM [4, 5]. Open frontiers include non-Gaussian incremental inference, certifiable robustness to false loop closures, and the fusion of learned front ends with classical back ends — but the smoothing formulation, the elimination–factorization duality, and the Bayes tree remain the foundation on which these are built.
