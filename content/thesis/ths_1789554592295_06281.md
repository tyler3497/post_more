# Indistinguishability Obfuscation and the Fall and Rise of Multilinear Maps: From Graded Encodings through Zeroizing Attacks to Well-Founded Assumptions

## Abstract

Indistinguishability obfuscation (iO) — often called the crown jewel of cryptography — promises to render any program unintelligible while preserving its behavior, formalized as the indistinguishability of obfuscations of any two equivalent circuits. The first plausible candidate, due to Garg et al. in 2013, rested on candidate multilinear maps: graded encoding schemes such as GGH13 over ideal lattices and CLT13 over the integers, later joined by graph-induced GGH15 [1][2]. A cryptanalytic wave followed — Cheon et al.'s zeroizing attacks totally breaking CLT [3], extensions of zeroizing to GGH13 [4], Miles–Sahai–Zhandry's annihilation attacks cryptanalyzing iO candidates themselves [5], and statistical leakage analyses [8] — seemingly destroying the foundations. Yet in 2020, Jain, Lin and Sahai proved iO from subexponential hardness of four well-studied assumptions (SXDH, LWE, LPN, NC0 PRGs), eliminating multilinear maps entirely [10]. This thesis reconstructs the saga: the graded-encoding formalism and branching-program obfuscators, the algebraic anatomy of each attack family, the structured-seed pseudorandom generator at the heart of the 2020 breakthrough, applications in functional and deniable encryption, and the open problems that remain.

##1 Intro

Program obfuscation is among the oldest dreams of cryptography: transform a program into an equivalent one that reveals nothing about its internals beyond its observable behavior. For decades the dream resisted formalization. Barak and coauthors showed in 2001 that the strongest formalization, *virtual black-box* (VBB) obfuscation, is impossible for general circuits [11], and proposed a weaker notion that might survive: **indistinguishability obfuscation**. An efficient algorithm *iO* is an indistinguishability obfuscator for a circuit class if it preserves functionality — the obfuscated circuit computes the same function — and if for every pair of functionally equivalent circuits *C0*, *C1* of comparable size, the distributions *iO(C0)* and *iO(C1)* are computationally indistinguishable.

> **Definition (Indistinguishability obfuscation).** An efficient uniform algorithm *O* is an *iO* obfuscator if (i) for every circuit *C*, the circuit *O(C)* computes the same function as *C*; (ii) *|O(C)|* is polynomial in *|C|*; and (iii) for all equivalent equal-size circuits *C0*, *C1*, the ensembles *{O(C0)}* and *{O(C1)}* are computationally indistinguishable.

For twelve years iO remained a definition in search of a construction. The breakthrough came in 2013, when Garg, Gentry, Halevi, Raykova, Sahai and Waters proposed the first candidate iO construction for all circuits, built atop *multilinear maps* instantiated through *multilinear jigsaw puzzles*, a simplified variant carrying just enough structure for obfuscation [2]. Security rested on a new algebraic hardness assumption, and the floodgates opened — iO was quickly shown to imply functional encryption, deniable encryption, witness encryption, and a zoo of other primitives [2][10].

The foundations, however, were poured on sand. The candidate multilinear maps of Garg–Gentry–Halevi (GGH13, over ideal lattices), Coron–Lepoint–Tibouchi (CLT13, over the integers), and later Gentry–Gorbunov–Halevi (GGH15, graph-induced from lattices) were heuristic constructions whose security was, in the words of the literature, "poorly understood" [9]. Beginning in 2014, a sustained cryptanalytic campaign — zeroizing attacks, annihilation attacks, statistical leakage — broke essentially every candidate map and, in 2016, the iO candidates built on them [3][4][5]. Skeptics asked whether iO existed at all outside idealized models.

The resolution arrived in August 2020. Jain, Lin and Sahai proved that subexponentially secure iO for all polynomial-size circuits follows from the subexponential hardness of four *well-founded* assumptions — SXDH on bilinear groups, LWE, LPN, and Boolean PRGs in NC0 — with no multilinear maps anywhere in the construction [10]. This thesis tells that story in full technical detail: what multilinear maps were, why they broke, how the attacks worked algebraically, how the 2020 construction bypassed them, and what remains open.

##2 Background

### From VBB impossibility to iO

The obfuscation program began with Barak, Goldreich, Impagliazzo, Rudich, Sahai, Vadhan and Yang (2001). Their devastating result: no efficient obfuscator can achieve virtual black-box security for all circuits — there exist function families learnable given code but not given black-box access [11]. Indistinguishability obfuscation was introduced as the natural retreat: rather than demanding that the obfuscated program leak nothing beyond input–output behavior, demand only that *which* of two equivalent implementations was obfuscated stay hidden. Remarkably, iO is *best-possible* obfuscation: anything hideable by any obfuscator is hidden by every iO obfuscator.

> **Theorem (Barak et al., informal).** If *P = NP*, then indistinguishability obfuscation for all polynomial-size circuits exists.

> **Proof sketch.** Given circuits *C0*, *C1*, a canonical-form reduction maps each circuit to the lexicographically first circuit computing the same function; under *P = NP* this reduction is efficient, and its output trivially satisfies indistinguishability. ∎

The theorem is a pure existence proof with no plausible instantiation — canonicalization is infeasible in the real world. Real progress required new mathematics.

### Multilinear maps

Boneh and Silverberg (2003) had proposed *multilinear maps* as a generalization of bilinear pairings: a map *e* from the direct product of cyclic groups *G1 × ⋯ × Gκ* to a target group *GT* that is linear in each argument and non-degenerate. For a decade no candidate existed. Then, within months of each other in 2013, two arrived.

**GGH13** (Garg–Gentry–Halevi, EUROCRYPT 2013) built a *graded encoding scheme* over the ring *R* equal to the integers adjoin *X* modulo *X^n + 1*, with plaintext space *R* modulo a secret prime ideal *I = (g)* [1]. An encoding of a ring element *m* at level *i* has the form *(r·g + m) / z^i* modulo *q*, for random *r* and secret denominator *z*; levels add under multiplication up to a maximum degree *κ*, and a public zero-testing parameter *p_zt* decides whether a top-level encoding hides zero. Noise grows with each operation, so *κ* is fixed at setup.

**CLT13** (Coron–Lepoint–Tibouchi, CRYPTO 2013) worked over the integers: the modulus *x0* is the product of secret primes *p_i*, and encodings use the Chinese Remainder Theorem across the prime slots [3]. **GGH15** (Gentry–Gorbunov–Halevi, TCC 2015) made the maps *graph-induced*: encodings are bound to paths in a directed acyclic graph rather than to integer levels, eliminating the low-level encodings of zero that powered the first attacks, and basing security on LWE [6].

GGH13, CLT13 and GGH15 were the *maps*; Garg et al.'s 2013 iO candidate was the *obfuscator* built on them [2]. It converts the circuit to a matrix branching program via Barrington's theorem — matrix pairs whose ordered product encodes the output — applies Kilian-style randomization with random invertible matrices, and encodes every entry with the multilinear map. Evaluation multiplies the encoded matrices within the degree bound and zero-tests the result; security was argued in idealized models where zero-testing reveals only a single bit [7].

| Year | Construction | Mathematical basis | Fate |
|------|-------------|-------------------|------|
| 2013 | GGH13 graded encodings | Ideal lattices, prime ideal *I = (g)* | Zeroizing attacks from 2015 [4] |
| 2013 | CLT13 integer maps | CRT over secret primes *p_i* | Total break by Cheon et al. [3] |
| 2013 | GGH+13 iO candidate | Barrington + Kilian over GGH13 | Annihilation attacks [5] |
| 2015 | GGH15 graph-induced maps | LWE, DAG-bound encodings | CLT15 variant fully broken [6] |
| 2015 | CLT15 integer variant | Revised CLT13 | Polynomial-time total break [6] |
| 2020 | Jain–Lin–Sahai iO | SXDH + LWE + LPN + NC0 PRG | Standing; no multilinear maps [10] |

##3 Methodology

This thesis proceeds by *algebraic reconstruction*: each multilinear-map candidate is presented through its encoding and zero-test equations, each attack is derived from those equations as a concrete linear-algebraic procedure, and the 2020 construction is presented as a deliberate inversion of the methodology — replacing heuristic graded encodings with reductions among well-studied assumptions.

Our analytical toolkit has three components. **First**, the graded-encoding formalism itself: we track, for every scheme, what the adversary legitimately learns (top-level zero-test outcomes) versus what the scheme inadvertently leaks (the *values* produced by zero-testing, noise structure, low-level encodings of zero). The attacks of 2014–2017 are unified by a single observation: every published zero-test leaked strictly more than one bit [5][8].

**Second**, the attack paradigm of *linearization*: zeroizing attacks convert products of encodings into matrices of small ring elements, then apply lattice or eigenvalue techniques to extract secrets. Annihilation attacks go further, using *nonlinear* polynomials in the published encodings that vanish at the top level without any encoding of zero existing at all [5].

**Third**, the constructive methodology of Jain–Lin–Sahai: rather than hardening graded encodings, eliminate them. Their proof introduces a new intermediate object, the *structured-seed pseudorandom generator* (sPRG) with polynomial stretch, whose seed splits into a public and a private part and which remains pseudorandom even given the public part; the sPRG is built from LPN over fields plus a Boolean NC0 PRG, and it plugs into a framework that had already reduced iO to LWE, SXDH, an NC0 PRG, and an sPRG [10]. The methodology throughout is *assumption minimalism*: every hardness claim must be a standard, long-studied one.

##4 Deep Dive

### The GGH13 graded encoding system

GGH13 is the archetype of lattice-based graded encodings, and its equations repay close study because every subsequent attack manipulates them [1]. Work in *R_q*, the ring *R = Z[X]/(X^n+1)* modulo a large *q*. Fix a secret small *g* generating the prime ideal *I*, a secret denominator *z*, and a multilinearity parameter *κ*. A level-*i* encoding of *m* in *R/I* is *u = [(r·g + m)·z^{-i}]_q*; levels add under multiplication up to *κ*. The zero-test parameter is *p_zt = [h·z^κ·g^{-1}]_q*; testing a top-level encoding computes *w = [p_zt·u]_q* and declares zero when the infinity norm of *w* is small, since *p_zt·u = h·(r + m·g^{-1})*, which is small exactly when *m = 0*.

Two features proved fatal. First, the zero-test outputs the *entire small ring element w*, not a single bit — a rich algebraic signal [5]. Second, the scheme publishes *low-level encodings of zero*, the raw material of the attacks. Re-randomization variants attempted to blunt a subtler leak — averaging many encodings of the same plaintext exposes secret-dependent components statistically — which Ducas and Pellet-Mary systematized across all GGH13 variants [8].

### CLT13 and the integer setting

CLT13 replaced lattices with elementary integer arithmetic [3]. Choose secret primes *p_i* and set *x0 = ∏ p_i*. An encoding of slot-messages *m_i* is *c = CRT(p_i; r_i·g_i + m_i) mod x0*; operations act slot-wise via the CRT. The zero-test parameter is *p_zt = Σ_i h_i·(z^κ·g_i^{-1} mod p_i)·(x0/p_i)*, and zero-testing computes *ω = [p_zt·c]_{x0}*, declaring zero when *ω* is small relative to *x0*. The integer setting made the scheme conceptually simpler — and cryptanalytically far more fragile, because the CRT slots interact through ordinary integer gcd computations rather than ideal-lattice geometry.

### GGH15: graph-induced maps and the LWE turn

Gentry, Gorbunov and Halevi (TCC 2015) redesigned graded encodings around *paths in a directed acyclic graph*: multiplication is permitted only along composable paths, with the graph's diameter replacing the degree bound [6]. Instantiated from LWE, GGH15 deliberately publishes no low-level encodings of zero, closing the exact door the first zeroizing attacks walked through. It represented the field's best defensive effort — new mathematics rather than patches — and it still fell: the CLT15 integer variant was totally broken in polynomial time by Cheon, Fouque, Lee, Minaud and Ryu [6].

### Zeroizing and annihilation: the cryptanalytic wave

The attacks form an escalation ladder. **Zeroizing** (Cheon–Han–Lee–Ryu–Stehlé, EUROCRYPT 2015) assumes the adversary holds many low-level encodings of zero *a_j = [(r_j·g)/z]* and some encodings *b_k* of non-zero values [3]. Multiplying *a_j·b_k* yields top-level encodings of zero; zero-testing produces small elements *ω_{jk}*; assembling these into matrices and exploiting their rank deficiency lets the attacker recover the secret factorization — for CLT13, *all* the secret primes *p_i*, a total break. Coron et al. extended zeroizing to settings with *no* low-level encodings of zero: CLT13 still falls completely, and GGH13 suffers at least a weak discrete-logarithm attack [4].

**Annihilation** (Miles–Sahai–Zhandry, CRYPTO 2016) removed the last refuge [5]. Instead of requiring encodings of zero, the attacker finds *nonlinear* polynomials in the published top-level encodings that evaluate to zero — annihilating polynomials — and combines them with the extra information leaked by GGH13's zero-test to distinguish obfuscations of two equivalent programs in polynomial time. This was the first polynomial-time cryptanalysis of *candidate iO schemes themselves* over GGH13. Garg, Miles, Mukherjee, Sahai, Srinivasan and Zhandry responded with an iO candidate provably secure in a new *weak multilinear map model* capturing all known polynomial-time attacks on GGH13, under a fresh assumption on the hardness of computing annihilating polynomials [7]; Garg, Mukherjee and Srinivasan gave a candidate avoiding the vulnerabilities altogether [9]. But the direction of travel was clear: each defense was narrower than the last attack.

```python
# Pedagogical sketch of the zeroizing attack's linear-algebraic core (CLT setting).
# Given: zero-test values w[j][k] from products (a_j * b_k) that all encode zero,
# each w[j][k] is a *small* integer vector across the secret prime slots.
def zeroizing_core(zero_test_matrix, x0):
    # Assemble small values into a matrix W over the integers
    W = integer_matrix(zero_test_matrix)          # rank-deficient: rank <= n
    # Find integer linear relations among the rows: W * v = 0 over the rationals
    relations = integer_kernel(W)                  # LLL / orthogonal lattice
    # Each relation exposes a multiple of a secret prime factor of x0
    for v in relations:
        g = gcd(dot(v, row) for row in W.rows())   # gcd with x0 splits x0
        p = gcd(g, x0)
        if 1 < p < x0:
            return p                               # secret prime recovered
    return None
```

```haskell
-- The iO security game, in the idealized single-bit zero-test model that
-- the 2013 candidates assumed and the 2015-2017 attacks refuted.
data Circuit = Circuit { size :: Int, eval :: [Bool] -> Bool }

equivalent :: Circuit -> Circuit -> Bool
equivalent c0 c1 = size c0 == size c1
                && and [eval c0 x == eval c1 x | x <- allInputs]

-- iO security demands: obfuscations of equivalent circuits are indistinguishable.
-- Annihilation attacks violate exactly this, using the *value* leaked by
-- zero-testing rather than the bit the model permits.
ioGame :: Obfuscator -> Adversary -> Circuit -> Circuit -> IO Bool
ioGame obf adv c0 c1 = do
  b  <- randomBit
  oc <- obf (if b then c0 else c1)
  b' <- adv oc
  return (b == b')
```

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The cryptanalytic results are constructive proofs with concrete complexity bounds. Cheon et al.'s attack on CLT13 runs in time polynomial in the security parameter and recovers *every* secret prime — the "total break" is an explicit eigenvalue-and-gcd computation, not asymptotic hand-waving [3]. The cryptanalysis of the CLT15 revision likewise recovers all secret parameters in polynomial time "for virtually all applications" [6]. Miles–Sahai–Zhandry exhibit two *explicit, simple* functionally equivalent programs whose GGH13 obfuscations are efficiently distinguishable — a direct, checkable refutation of the candidate's security claim [5]. Ducas and Pellet-Mary confirmed the statistical leak across GGH13 variants [8].

> **Theorem (Cheon–Han–Lee–Ryu–Stehlé, informal).** Given polynomially many low-level encodings of zero in CLT13, there is a polynomial-time algorithm recovering the complete secret factorization of the modulus *x0*.

> **Proof.** Form top-level zero encodings *a_j·b_k*, zero-test to obtain small slot vectors *ω_{jk}*, and assemble the matrix *W*. Because each row of *W* lies in a fixed low-dimensional lattice determined by the secret primes, integer linear algebra (orthogonal-lattice techniques) yields relations exposing multiples of individual primes; gcd computations against *x0* then split it completely. ∎

> **Theorem (Miles–Sahai–Zhandry, informal).** There exist functionally equivalent programs whose obfuscations under the Garg et al. candidate over GGH13 are distinguishable in polynomial time.

> **Proof.** Construct annihilating polynomials over the published top-level encodings that vanish without any low-level zero existing; evaluate them and exploit the full small ring element revealed by GGH13 zero-testing to extract a distinguishing signal between the two obfuscations. ∎

On the constructive side, the Jain–Lin–Sahai theorem is the capstone [10]:

> **Theorem (Jain–Lin–Sahai 2020, informal).** Assume subexponential hardness of SXDH on prime-order asymmetric bilinear groups, LWE over *Z_p* with subexponential modulus-to-noise ratio, LPN over *Z_p* with polynomially many samples and inverse-polynomial error rate, and a Boolean NC0 PRG with superlinear stretch. Then subexponentially secure iO for all polynomial-size circuits exists; with only polynomial hardness, collusion-resistant public-key functional encryption for all circuits exists.

The proof's centerpiece is the *structured-seed PRG*: a generator whose seed has a public part visible to the adversary and a private part, with polynomial stretch and pseudorandomness holding despite the exposure. Jain, Lin and Sahai build it from LPN over fields combined with an NC0 PRG — the one genuinely new cryptographic object in the paper — and feed it into the existing LWE-plus-SXDH framework that had already reduced iO to exactly this missing piece [10]. Press coverage quoted the field's reaction: the result, one researcher said, should "definitively silence the iO skeptics," since the assumptions are ones "widely used and studied in the past," in contrast to the "untested and ultimately shaky foundations" of the multilinear-map era.

##6 Limitations

The 2020 breakthrough settled *existence* under standard assumptions, not *efficiency*, *minimality*, or *quantum resistance*. Three limitations dominate.

**Efficiency.** Every known iO construction — multilinear-map-based or assumption-based — incurs astronomical overhead: the 2020 construction layers functional encryption, structured-seed PRGs, and perturbation-resilient machinery with polynomial blowups composed several deep, and its efficiency is, by its authors' own account, "very far from being practical" [10]. No implementation obfuscates non-trivial programs today, and none is on the horizon.

**Assumption strength.** The theorem needs *subexponential* hardness of all four assumptions to get iO (polynomial hardness buys only functional encryption) [10]. Whether iO follows from *polynomial* hardness of standard assumptions — or from LWE alone — is open. The exact minimal assumptions for iO are unknown: Komargodski et al. showed that iO implies one-way functions only under additional average-case hardness hypotheses, and unconditionally implies SZK arguments for NP, so iO sits in a strange intermediate zone — too strong to come for free, too weak to obviously imply much [11].

**Quantum vulnerability.** At least one of the four assumptions — SXDH on pairing groups — falls to quantum attacks, so the 2020 construction is not post-quantum [10]. Building iO from *quantum-safe* well-founded assumptions alone (ideally LWE by itself) remains a major open problem, as does determining whether multilinear maps might yet be salvaged in some restricted, provably-secure form — the weak multilinear map model keeps that door ajar without walking through it [7].

---

##7 Conclusion

Jain, Lin and Sahai's 2020 result closed the saga by changing the subject: iO needs no multilinear maps at all, following instead from four assumptions cryptographers have trusted for decades [10]. The lesson is twofold. Technically, the structured-seed PRG and the LWE-plus-SXDH framework show how to assemble powerful objects from modest, well-understood parts — a template for future assumption-minimal cryptography. Philosophically, the episode vindicates conservative methodology: the heuristic graded encodings collapsed exactly where they were least understood (the zero-test interface), while the assumptions that survived were the ones with the longest cryptanalytic track records.

What remains is the engineers' half of the problem: making iO efficient, weakening subexponential to polynomial hardness, and achieving quantum resistance. The crown jewel has been proven real; cutting it for practical wear is the work of the coming decade.
