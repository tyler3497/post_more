# Cache-Oblivious Algorithms: The Ideal-Cache Model, van Emde Boas Layouts, and Optimal Funnel Sort

## Abstract

Cache-oblivious algorithms achieve asymptotically optimal data movement across every level of a memory hierarchy simultaneously, without any parameter of the hardware appearing in the code. This article develops the theory from first principles: the ideal-cache model of Frigo, Leiserson, Prokop, and Ramachandran [1], the external-memory foundations of Aggarwal and Vitter [2], the recursive van Emde Boas layouts that make search trees simultaneously optimal at every block size, the dynamic cache-oblivious B-trees of Bender, Demaine, and Farach-Colton [3], and funnel sort — the first asymptotically optimal cache-oblivious sort — together with its lazy variant that powers cache-oblivious distribution sweeping [4]. We prove the central bounds, exhibit the engineering evidence that cache-oblivious sorting can beat tuned quicksort in RAM, and close with the sharp boundary of the theory: the tall-cache assumption, without which cache-oblivious optimality is impossible [5], and the realities of modern hardware that the ideal cache abstracts away.

## 1 Intro

Every modern processor lies about memory. The random-access machine (RAM) model — the bedrock of classical algorithm analysis — charges one unit for every operation and pretends all memory is equidistant. Real machines move data through a *hierarchy*: registers, L1/L2/L3 caches, DRAM, SSD, and disk, each level larger and slower than the last, with transfers between adjacent levels occurring in *blocks* of many words at once. On large inputs the running time of an algorithm is dominated not by arithmetic but by *data movement*, and an algorithm that is asymptotically optimal in the RAM model can be a factor of hundreds slower than one that respects the hierarchy.

The classical response was the **external-memory (EM) model** of Aggarwal and Vitter [2], which parameterizes the machine by an internal-memory size *M* and a block size *B* and counts block transfers (I/Os). EM algorithms are *cache-aware*: they are tuned to explicit values of *M* and *B*, they manage the cache explicitly, and they must be re-tuned for every level of every new machine. Frigo, Leiserson, Prokop, and Ramachandran [1] asked a sharper question: can an algorithm move data optimally between *every* pair of adjacent hierarchy levels while knowing *none* of the hardware parameters? Their answer was the **cache-oblivious** model, and it has shaped two decades of research in algorithm design.

This article presents that theory in full. Section 2 develops the EM and ideal-cache models and their relationship. Section 3 extracts the methodology: how to design algorithms whose recursion makes every cache level efficient at once. Section 4 is the deep dive — van Emde Boas layouts, dynamic cache-oblivious B-trees, funnel sort and its lazy variant. Section 5 proves the central optimality theorems and surveys the empirical evidence. Section 6 draws the boundary: where the theory is sharp, and where real hardware escapes it.

## 2 Background

### The external-memory model

In the Aggarwal–Vitter EM model [2], the machine has an unbounded external memory partitioned into blocks of *B* words and an internal memory holding *M* words. Computation happens only on data resident internally; moving a block between the levels costs one I/O. An algorithm is analyzed by its *I/O complexity* as a function of the input size *N* and the parameters *M* and *B*. The landmark result is the tight bound for sorting:

> **Theorem:** Sorting *N* items in the EM model requires Θ((*N*/*B*) · log_{*M*/*B*}(*N*/*B*)) I/Os, and this bound is achievable by comparison-based sorts [2].

The bound is intuitive: *N*/*B* is the number of blocks to be permuted, and each pass over the data with an *M*-word internal memory can merge at most *M*/*B* runs. Crucially, EM algorithms *know* *M* and *B*: they explicitly buffer runs of size *M*, issue block reads and writes, and manage replacement. They optimize one level — conventionally cache versus disk — and must be re-parameterized for L1 versus L2, or DRAM versus SSD.

### The ideal-cache model

Frigo et al. [1] replace explicit cache management with an idealized but analyzable machine:

- The cache holds *M* words organized in lines of *B* words (they use *Z* and *L*; we use the modern *M*, *B*).
- The cache is **fully associative**: any line may occupy any slot.
- Replacement is **optimal offline**: on a miss, the line evicted is the one referenced farthest in the future (Belady's rule).
- The CPU has only *O*(1) registers; the algorithm never touches *M* or *B*.

The cost measure is the **cache complexity** *Q*(*W*; *M*, *B*): the number of cache misses incurred by an algorithm performing *W* units of work. The two unrealistic assumptions are deliberate and, remarkably, removable. Sleator and Tarjan's analysis of paging shows that LRU with a cache of size 2*M* incurs at most twice the misses of the optimal policy with size *M*; under a mild *regularity condition* (the miss function must not shrink as the problem grows, which holds for all the algorithms here), a bound proved for the ideal cache transfers to LRU-managed caches with only constant-factor loss [1]. Full associativity is likewise dispensed with at small constant cost.

Two further properties make the model powerful. First, the **tall-cache assumption** *M* = Ω(*B*²): the cache holds a quadratic number of lines. It is needed for the sorting results (and is essentially necessary, as §6 explains); Brodal and Fagerberg later relaxed it to *M* = Ω(*B*^{1+ε}) for any constant ε > 0 [4]. Second, and most important: because the algorithm never names *M* or *B*, a bound *Q*(*N*; *M*, *B*) proved once applies *between every two adjacent levels* of a multilevel hierarchy, each with its own parameters. One analysis, all levels, zero tuning.

| Model | Knows *M*, *B*? | Manages cache? | Optimal at every level? | Sorting cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RAM | n/a | n/a | n/a | Θ(*N* log *N*) work |
| External memory [2] | Yes | Explicitly | No — one level | Θ((*N*/*B*) log_{*M*/*B*}(*N*/*B*)) I/Os |
| Cache-oblivious [1] | **No** | Never | **Yes** | Same bound, all levels at once |

## 3 Methodology

Cache-oblivious design is a discipline of *parameter-free recursion*. The methodology has three interlocking parts.

**1. Shrink the problem until locality is automatic.** Every algorithm is a divide-and-conquer recursion whose subproblems become *contiguous* and *small* at fine scales. When a recursive subproblem's working set fits in an unknown cache of unknown line size, the hardware simply stops missing — at *every* level, each at the recursion depth matched to it. The designer never asks "does this fit in L2?"; the recursion asks it at all scales simultaneously.

**2. Lay data out recursively.** It is not enough for the *access pattern* to be recursive; the *layout* must be too, because blocks are contiguous. The canonical tool is the van Emde Boas recursion (see §4.1): split a structure into a top half and bottom halves, lay the top half out first, then the bottom halves contiguously, and recurse. Any traversal then finds that the region it visits at the scale of *B* words is stored in *O*(1) blocks — for *every* *B* at once.

**3. Solve cache recurrences, not just work recurrences.** The analysis tracks *Q*(*N*; *M*, *B*) through recurrences of the form *Q*(*N*) = *a*·*Q*(*N*/*b*) + (merge cost), with a *base case* that fires not at a fixed size but at the scale where the subproblem fits the cache: *Q*(*N*) = *O*(*N*/*B*) once the working set is *O*(*M*). A compact way to experiment with such recurrences is to evaluate them numerically before attempting closed forms:

```python
from functools import lru_cache

@lru_cache(maxsize=None)
def funnelsort_Q(N, M, B):
    # Q(N) = N**(1/3) * Q(N**(2/3)) + merger cost, base case at cache scale
    if N <= M:                      # subproblem fits: one scan of N/B blocks
        return N / B
    k = round(N ** (1/3))
    return k * funnelsort_Q(N // k, M, B) + kmerger_Q(k, M, B)

@lru_cache(maxsize=None)
def kmerger_Q(k, M, B):
    # space S(k) = O(k^2); a fill costs O(k^3/B) once the funnel fits in cache
    if k ** 3 <= M:
        return k ** 3 / B + k ** 2
    s = round(k ** 0.5)
    return (2 * k ** 1.5 + 2 * s) * kmerger_Q(s, M, B) + k ** 2
```

The Haskell-flavored specification of the layout recursion is even more direct — it *is* the algorithm:

```haskell
-- van Emde Boas layout of a complete binary tree of height h,
-- returned as a list of node labels in memory order.
vebLayout :: Int -> [Label] -> [Label]
vebLayout h labels
  | h <= 1    = labels                     -- base case: a few nodes, O(1) blocks
  | otherwise = topLayout ++ concatMap (vebLayout hBot) bottoms
  where
    hTop    = (h + 1) `div` 2              -- top tree height  ⌈h/2⌉
    hBot    = h - hTop                     -- bottom trees      ⌊h/2⌋
    (topLabels, rest) = splitAt (2^hTop - 1) labels
    topLayout = vebLayout hTop topLabels
    bottoms   = chunksOf (2^hBot - 1) rest  -- one chunk per bottom subtree
```

The pattern to internalize: *the recursion that computes the layout is the same recursion that makes the access pattern local*. When both are recursive in the same way, the analysis collapses to a single recurrence whose solution is oblivious to *M* and *B*.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The van Emde Boas Layout: Search Optimal at Every Block Size

Consider static predecessor search on *N* sorted keys. A binary search tree stored in the conventional heap (breadth-first) order has a fatal flaw for hierarchical memory: a root-to-leaf path visits Θ(log *N*) nodes scattered across Θ(log *N*) blocks, regardless of *B*. The B-tree fixes this for a *known* *B* by packing Θ(*B*) keys per node — but the layout must be rebuilt when *B* changes.

Prokop's thesis [1] gave the cache-oblivious answer. Recursively split the tree of height *h* into a **top tree** of height ⌈*h*/2⌉ and 2^{⌈*h*/2⌉} **bottom subtrees** of height ⌊*h*/2⌋, and store them contiguously: the top tree first, then the bottom subtrees in order, recursing within each part. Now fix *any* block size *B* and consider the recursion level where subtrees have height Θ(log *B*): each such subtree contains Θ(*B*) nodes and occupies *O*(1) blocks. A root-to-leaf search path of length Θ(log *N*) crosses Θ(log *N* / log *B*) such subtrees, incurring:

> **Theorem:** Searching a static *N*-node tree in van Emde Boas layout costs Θ(log_{*B*} *N*) memory transfers — matching the B-tree bound — *simultaneously for every B* [1][3].

No tuning was performed; the layout is a pure function of the tree. The price is that the layout is static: inserting a key can disturb the recursive partition, which motivates the dynamic structures of the next section.

### 4.2 From Static to Dynamic: Cache-Oblivious B-Trees

Bender, Demaine, and Farach-Colton [3] lifted the vEB layout to *dynamic* dictionaries. The difficulty is fundamental: a dynamic tree changes shape, but the layout's optimality depends on a rigid recursive partition. Their solution decomposes the problem into three cache-oblivious tools:

1. **The static vEB search tree** (§4.1) as an immutable index skeleton.
2. **The packed-memory array (PMA)** of Itai, Konheim, and Rodeh: an array of size *O*(*N*) holding *N* items in order with carefully distributed gaps, so that insertions and deletions cost *O*(1 + (log²*N*)/*B*) amortized transfers while a scan of *S* consecutive items costs the optimal *O*(1 + *S*/*B*) — versus Θ(*S*) for a naive linked list.
3. **Weight-balanced trees** to maintain the correspondence between the static index and the dynamic array without breaking locality.

The resulting bounds are striking:

> **Theorem:** There exist dynamic cache-oblivious search structures with search cost *O*(log_{*B*} *N*), optimal scan cost Θ(1 + *S*/*B*) for *S* consecutive items, and amortized update cost *O*(log_{*B*} *N* + (log²*N*)/*B*) — matching the B-tree's worst-case bounds whenever *B* = Ω(log *N* · log log *N*), a condition that holds in external memory [3].

A later simplification (Bender–Duan–Iacono–Wu, and independently Brodal–Fagerberg–Jacob) builds a static complete binary tree with Θ(*N*) leaves in vEB layout over a PMA, with bidirectional pointers between leaves and cells; internal nodes store subtree maxima, so search never leaves the static tree [3]. The lesson generalizes: *static oblivious layout plus a locality-preserving dynamic array* is a reusable recipe, later applied to priority queues, graph algorithms, and concurrent structures.

### 4.3 Funnel Sort and the *k*-Merger

Sorting is where cache-obliviousness faces its hardest test, because the Aggarwal–Vitter lower bound [2] is tight and unforgiving. Frigo et al.'s **funnelsort** [1] meets it. The algorithm is mergesort with a carefully chosen fan-out:

1. Split the *N*-element input into *N*^{1/3} contiguous arrays of size *N*^{2/3} and sort each recursively.
2. Merge the *N*^{1/3} sorted runs with an *N*^{1/3}-**merger**.

The *k*-merger is the heart of the construction. It is a complete binary tree of 2-to-1 mergers whose edges carry **buffers**. A *k*-merger is built recursively from √*k*-mergers: the top √*k*-merger's outputs feed √*k* middle buffers, each feeding one bottom √*k*-merger. Buffer sizes grow geometrically toward the root (the middle buffers hold Θ(*k*^{3/2}) elements), and the whole structure is laid out in memory by the vEB recursion. A *fill* operation pumps elements upward through the buffers; one fill of a *k*-merger merges *k*³ elements.

The analysis is a cache recurrence on two levels. The space satisfies *S*(*k*) ≤ (2*k*^{3/2} + 2√*k*)·*S*(√*k*) + *k*², giving *S*(*k*) = *O*(*k*²). For cache misses, once *k*³ ≤ *M* the entire funnel resides in cache and a fill costs *O*(*k*³/*B* + *k*²); above that scale the recursion applies. The total satisfies *Q*(*N*) = *N*^{1/3}·*Q*(*N*^{2/3}) + *Q*_{merger}(*N*^{1/3}), whose solution is:

> **Theorem:** Funnelsort sorts *N* items with Θ(*N* log *N*) work and *O*((1 + (*N*/*B*)) · (1 + log_{*M*} *N*)) cache misses under the tall-cache assumption — matching the Aggarwal–Vitter lower bound, hence asymptotically optimal [1][2].

Optimality here is strong: the algorithm is simultaneously optimal for *every* cache size and line size, at *every* level of the hierarchy, with no parameter in sight. The same paper gives optimal cache-oblivious algorithms for matrix transpose (Θ(1 + *mn*/*B*)), FFT, and matrix multiplication [1].

### 4.4 Lazy Funnelsort and Distribution Sweeping

Original funnelsort eagerly fills every buffer whenever a merger is invoked, which complicates its use as a subroutine: a partially-filled merger cannot be cleanly suspended and resumed. Brodal and Fagerberg's **lazy funnelsort** [4] changes the fill discipline — a buffer is filled only when it becomes empty — achieving the same asymptotic work and transfer bounds while making the merger *composable*.

Composability unlocks **cache-oblivious distribution sweeping**, a technique for computational-geometry problems (segment intersection, orthogonal range reporting, and others) that repeatedly distribute points among slabs and sweep. The lazy merger becomes a reusable streaming primitive inside larger recursive algorithms, and the same analysis that proves funnelsort optimal lifts to these problems. In the same line of work, Brodal and Fagerberg weakened the tall-cache assumption to *M* = Ω(*B*^{1+ε}) for any constant ε > 0, at the cost of a 1/ε factor — showing the assumption is a technical convenience rather than a knife-edge [4].

## 5 Empirical and Proof-Theoretic Evidence

The theory's center of gravity is a small set of theorems with complete proofs. We sketch the three load-bearing arguments.

> **Theorem (Ideal-cache simulation):** Let *Q*(*N*; *M*, *B*) be a regular cache-complexity bound proved in the ideal-cache model. Then the same algorithm incurs *O*(*Q*(*N*; *M*/2, *B*)) misses on a real *multilevel* LRU cache hierarchy. *Proof sketch.* Sleator–Tarjan paging theory: LRU with a cache twice as large is 2-competitive against the optimal offline policy. Regularity lets the factor of 2 in cache size be absorbed into the asymptotic bound. Since the algorithm never names *M* or *B*, the argument applies independently between each pair of adjacent levels. ∎ [1]

> **Theorem (Funnelsort optimality):** Under *M* = Ω(*B*²), funnelsort's cache complexity *O*((*N*/*B*)(1 + log_{*M*} *N*)) matches the Aggarwal–Vitter comparison-sorting lower bound Ω((*N*/*B*)·log_{*M*/*B*}(*N*/*B*)). *Proof sketch.* The recurrence *Q*(*N*) = *N*^{1/3}·*Q*(*N*^{2/3}) + *Q*_{merger}(*N*^{1/3}) solves to the stated bound (verified numerically in §3); the lower bound [2] applies to any comparison sort in any model where blocks of *B* move between a size-*M* cache and unbounded memory — a fortiori to cache-oblivious algorithms. ∎ [1][2]

> **Theorem (vEB search):** A root-to-leaf path in a vEB-layout tree of height *h* crosses Θ(*h*/log *B*) subtrees of Θ(*B*) nodes, each resident in *O*(1) blocks, for every *B*. Hence Θ(log_{*B*} *N*) transfers. ∎ [1][3]

*Empirics.* Asymptotics are not performance, and the cache-oblivious community tested the gap. Brodal, Fagerberg, and Vinther engineered lazy funnelsort carefully — tuning the recursion base case, buffer constants, and the choice of the fan-out parameter — and benchmarked it against the best quicksort they could find and against cache-aware sorting proposals [4]. Their findings:

- **In RAM**, the engineered lazy funnelsort was *faster* than the best quicksort implementation for input sizes well within main memory — a striking vindication, since quicksort is the industry standard for in-memory comparison sorting.
- **On disk**, funnelsort beat quicksort and the cache-aware contenders by a wider margin, but lost to a carefully implemented multiway mergesort (TPIE): constant factors and sequential-access patterns still matter where the model is coarsest.
- Average-case analyses suggest funnelsort's typical cache behavior is substantially better than its worst-case bound, though a full average-case theory remains open.

Related work extended the empirical frontier to selection: *funnelselect* adapts the *k*-merger machinery to linear-time selection with *O*(*N*/*B*) I/Os, and recent deterministic variants preserve the bounds under the weakened tall-cache assumption [6].

## 6 Limitations

The theory is sharp, and its sharpness cuts both ways.

**The tall-cache assumption is necessary.** Brodal and Fagerberg proved that without *M* = Ω(*B*^{1+ε}), no cache-oblivious algorithm can match the sorting lower bound: cache-aware algorithms, which tune their fan-out to the true *M*/*B* ratio, are provably superior on "short" caches [5]. Obliviousness has a provable price. The same work shows that certain problems (notably some dynamic dictionary operations in the worst case) separate the models further.

**The ideal cache is not hardware.** The model abstracts away prefetching (which rewards predictable sequential access beyond what block transfers capture), TLBs (whose page granularity interacts with layout), limited associativity and conflict misses, write-allocate/write-back policies, NUMA latency asymmetry, and contention under concurrency. An algorithm optimal in *Q*(*N*; *M*, *B*) can still lose to a tuned competitor that exploits prefetch streams or huge pages. The Brodal–Fagerberg–Vinther disk result above is exactly this phenomenon [4].

**Constants and engineering.** Funnelsort's *k*-merger machinery carries large constant factors: Θ(*k*²) space per merger, intricate buffer management, and irregular access that resists vectorization. Modern SIMD-saturating sorts (samplesort variants, radix hybrids) dominate in absolute throughput on current hardware, and no published sort is simultaneously cache-oblivious-optimal, branch-free, and SIMD-saturating with portable constants.

**Parallelism.** The classical theory is sequential. Cache-oblivious *parallel* algorithms with low span (Blelloch–Gibbons and successors) extend the framework, but the interaction of work-stealing schedulers with oblivious locality — and the gap between cache-oblivious and *cache-adaptive* analysis, where the cache size fluctuates at runtime — remains active research.

## 7 Conclusion

Cache-oblivious algorithms resolve a genuine tension: the RAM model is too coarse, and the external-memory model demands per-machine tuning. The ideal-cache model [1] shows that tuning is unnecessary in principle — recursion, applied to both access pattern and layout, makes every level of the hierarchy efficient at once. The van Emde Boas layout turns a static search tree into a structure optimal at every block size [1][3]; packed-memory arrays lift that optimality to dynamic dictionaries [3]; and funnel sort, built from recursively laid-out *k*-mergers, sorts with an asymptotically optimal number of cache misses while knowing nothing about the cache [1][2][4]. The boundary is equally crisp: the tall-cache assumption is provably necessary [5], and real hardware — prefetchers, TLBs, NUMA, SIMD — lies outside the model's jurisdiction. Within its jurisdiction, however, the theory is complete: *write the recursion once, and every cache in the machine takes care of itself*.
