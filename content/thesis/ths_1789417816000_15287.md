# Differential Privacy in Machine Learning: Definitions, Composition, the Moments Accountant, and DP-SGD

## Abstract

Machine learning models trained on sensitive data can leak membership and attributes through their parameters, motivating rigorous privacy guarantees for the training process itself. This thesis develops the theory and practice of differential privacy (DP) in machine learning, from the foundational definitions of pure and approximate DP to the scalable algorithms that train modern neural networks with provable privacy budgets. We formalize sensitivity-based mechanisms, the Gaussian mechanism, and the basic and advanced composition theorems that govern how privacy degrades under adaptive iteration. We then develop the Rényi differential privacy framework that underpins the moments accountant of Abadi et al., showing how tracking the cumulant generating function of the privacy loss random variable yields dramatically tighter end-to-end bounds than generic composition. We analyze privacy amplification by subsampling, the Poisson and fixed-size sampling schemes that let DP-SGD treat each training step as a weak privacy mechanism, and the per-example gradient clipping that bounds sensitivity in non-convex optimization. A concrete implementation sketch, complexity analysis, and empirical results on MNIST and CIFAR-10 illustrate the utility-privacy tradeoff in practice.

---

## 1 Intro

Modern machine learning pipelines ingest data that is personal by construction: clinical records, keystroke histories, genomic sequences, and behavioral traces. When a model is trained on such data and subsequently released, the model itself becomes a disclosure channel. *Membership inference attacks* determine whether a particular record participated in training; *model inversion* and *attribute inference* reconstruct sensitive features of training examples from parameters or confidence scores. Heuristic defenses — output rounding, confidence masking, ad hoc noise — provide no worst-case guarantee, and they have been repeatedly broken by adaptive adversaries. What is needed is a *mathematical* guarantee that quantifies, in advance, how much any single individual's data can influence what the adversary observes.

*Differential privacy* (DP), introduced by Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, and Smith [1], supplies exactly such a guarantee. A randomized algorithm is differentially private when its output distribution is nearly indistinguishable on any pair of datasets differing in a single record. The guarantee is *worst-case* over datasets and *information-theoretic* over adversaries of unbounded computational power, which makes it one of the strongest and most studied notions in privacy-preserving computation.

Applying differential privacy to machine learning is, however, far from a direct translation of the classical theory. Deep learning requires *thousands* of adaptive, data-dependent steps, each touching the sensitive dataset; naively composing per-step guarantees yields vacuous end-to-end bounds. Gradients in non-convex optimization have no a priori bounded sensitivity, so the sensitivity-calibrated noise mechanisms of the early DP literature do not apply out of the box. This thesis traces the complete arc from the definitions of pure and approximate DP through the composition theorems, the Rényi DP refinement, the *moments accountant* technique, privacy amplification by subsampling, and finally the *DP-SGD* algorithm of Abadi et al. [3] — the workhorse of private deep learning — with its signature device of *per-example gradient clipping*.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Neighboring Datasets and the Definitions

Let a *dataset* be a multiset of records drawn from a universe X. Two datasets D and D' are *neighboring* (written D ~ D') when one can be obtained from the other by adding or removing a single record. A *randomized mechanism* M maps datasets to distributions over an output space R.

> **Theorem:** *Pure differential privacy [1].* A mechanism M satisfies ε-differential privacy (pure DP) if for all neighboring D, D' and all measurable S ⊆ R,
> Pr[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ e^ε · Pr[M(D') ∈ S].

Pure DP is strong but often too brittle: the Gaussian mechanism, central to machine learning, does not satisfy it for any finite ε. The relaxation that admits a small probability δ of catastrophic failure is *approximate DP*.

> **Theorem:** *Approximate differential privacy [2].* A mechanism M satisfies (ε, δ)-DP if for all neighboring D, D' and all measurable S ⊆ R,
> Pr[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ e^ε · Pr[M(D') ∈ S] + δ.

The parameter δ is conventionally taken to be cryptographically small, on the order of 1/n² for a dataset of n records. Approximate DP is the operative definition for essentially all private machine learning.

### 2.2 Sensitivity and the Classical Mechanisms

The bridge from a deterministic query f to a private mechanism is *sensitivity*: how much can one record move the answer? The *ℓ1-sensitivity* Δ₁(f) = max_{D~D'} ‖f(D) − f(D')‖₁ and the *ℓ2-sensitivity* Δ₂(f) calibrate the classical mechanisms.

> **Theorem:** *Laplace mechanism [1].* Releasing f(D) + Lap(Δ₁(f)/ε), where Lap(b) denotes Laplace noise of scale b, satisfies ε-DP.

> **Theorem:** *Gaussian mechanism [2].* Releasing f(D) + N(0, σ²I) with σ ≥ c·Δ₂(f)/ε, for c² > 2 ln(1.25/δ), satisfies (ε, δ)-DP.

Two structural properties make DP composable into larger systems. First, *post-processing*: any data-independent function of the output of a DP mechanism is itself DP with the same parameters — privacy cannot be "processed away," which is why releasing model parameters computed by a DP training procedure is safe [2]. Second, *group privacy*: an (ε, δ)-DP mechanism is automatically (kε, k·e^{(k−1)ε}δ)-DP with respect to groups of k records, at the price of a linearly degrading guarantee.

### 2.3 The Privacy Loss Random Variable

Much of the modern analysis is organized around the *privacy loss random variable* (PLRV). For neighboring D, D' and an outcome o drawn from M(D), define

L(o) = ln( Pr[M(D) = o] / Pr[M(D') = o] ).

A mechanism is ε-DP exactly when |L| ≤ ε almost surely, and (ε, δ)-DP exactly when Pr[L > ε] ≤ δ (up to the symmetric tail). The moments accountant and Rényi DP both amount to controlling the moment generating function of L rather than its worst case, which is the key to tight composition.

## 3 Methodology

Our analytical methodology treats *privacy accounting* as a formal calculus with three ingredients:

1. **Mechanism-level bounds.** For each primitive (clipped gradient release, noisy aggregation), derive a per-step privacy guarantee in the tightest available currency — typically Rényi DP of order α.
2. **Composition.** Combine per-step guarantees across T adaptive steps using composition theorems that exploit the structure of the mechanism rather than generic worst-case bounds.
3. **Conversion.** Translate the composed guarantee back into the interpretable (ε, δ)-DP language at the end, optimizing over any free parameters (Rényi order, moment order λ).

The accountant pattern is implemented as a small stateful object that accumulates per-step costs and converts on demand:

```python
import numpy as np

class RDPAccountant:
    """Track Rényi-DP costs across adaptive training steps."""
    def __init__(self, orders):
        self.orders = np.asarray(orders, dtype=float)
        self.costs = np.zeros_like(self.orders)

    def step(self, rdp_fn):
        # rdp_fn(alpha) -> per-step RDP epsilon at order alpha
        self.costs += np.array([rdp_fn(a) for a in self.orders])

    def get_epsilon(self, delta):
        # optimal conversion: eps = min_alpha cost(alpha) - ln(delta)/(alpha-1)
        vals = self.costs - np.log(delta) / (self.orders - 1.0)
        return float(np.min(vals))
```

This pattern — accumulate in a composable currency, convert once at the end — is the methodological spine of the entire thesis. The moments accountant of [3] is an instance with the currency being the cumulant generating function of the privacy loss; RDP accounting [4][5] is the cleanest modern formulation.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Advanced Composition Theorems

Training a model for T iterations applies a DP mechanism T times, adaptively. *Basic composition* states that T mechanisms with parameters (εᵢ, δᵢ) compose to (Σᵢ εᵢ, Σᵢ δᵢ)-DP [2] — linear degradation that is tight in the worst case but far too pessimistic for mechanisms whose privacy loss concentrates, such as the Gaussian mechanism.

> **Theorem:** *Advanced composition [2].* For ε in (0, 1), the T-fold adaptive composition of (ε, δ)-DP mechanisms satisfies (ε', Tδ + δ')-DP with ε' = √(2T ln(1/δ'))·ε + Tε(e^ε − 1).

The proof controls the sum of T independent (or adaptively chosen) privacy loss variables with a Chernoff/Hoeffding-style tail bound: while the worst case adds linearly, typical privacy loss concentrates around its mean, so the ε parameter need only grow as √T. For heterogeneous mechanisms the optimal composition problem is substantially harder, and the landscape of exact and approximate composition bounds is surveyed comprehensively in [7].

| Composition rule | ε degradation (k mechanisms) | δ degradation | Tightness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basic composition [2] | Σᵢ εᵢ (linear) | Σᵢ δᵢ | Tight in worst case |
| Advanced composition [2] | O(√(k ln(1/δ'))·ε + kε²) | kδ + δ' | Near-optimal for small ε |
| Optimal composition [7] | Exact via PLRV convolution | Exact | Computationally intensive |
| RDP composition [4] | Σᵢ εᵢ(α) per order α | Via conversion | Exact in RDP currency |

The crucial lesson is that *generic* composition theorems treat the mechanism as a black box. The moments accountant improves on them precisely by opening the box: for the subsampled Gaussian mechanism, the privacy loss distribution has a known, analyzable form, and tracking its moments beats any black-box bound [3][5].

### 4.2 Rényi Differential Privacy and the Moments Accountant

*Rényi differential privacy* (RDP), introduced by Mironov [4], reparameterizes privacy in terms of the Rényi divergence of order α > 1 between the output distributions on neighboring datasets:

D_α(M(D) ‖ M(D')) = (1/(α−1)) · ln E_{o~M(D')}[(M(D)(o)/M(D')(o))^α] ≤ ε(α).

A mechanism satisfying this for a range of orders is (α, ε(α))-RDP. RDP enjoys *exact, lossless* composition: the RDP parameters of composed mechanisms simply add, ε(α) = Σᵢ εᵢ(α), with no √k slack and no δ' to manage during composition. Conversion back to approximate DP is a one-line Chernoff argument: (α, ε(α))-RDP implies (ε(α) + ln(1/δ)/(α−1), δ)-DP, optimized over α [4].

The *moments accountant* of Abadi et al. [3] predates the RDP formalism but is now understood as its operational twin: it tracks the cumulant generating function K(λ) = ln E[e^{λL}] of the privacy loss random variable, which is exactly (λ+1)·D_{λ+1} up to the change of measure [5]. Its core theorem is composability of moments:

> **Theorem:** *Composability of moments [3].* If M is the adaptive composition of M₁, …, M_T, then for every λ > 0, α_M(λ) ≤ Σᵢ α_{Mᵢ}(λ), where α(λ) is the worst-case log-moment of the privacy loss.

The connection was made explicit afterward: the moments accountant *is* RDP accounting specialized to the subsampled Gaussian mechanism [4][5][7]. This unification matters because it lets practitioners swap in any mechanism with a known RDP curve — Laplace, subsampled shuffles, randomized response — into the same accountant machinery.

In a functional language the RDP composition law is nearly a one-liner, which exhibits why the framework is so convenient:

```haskell
-- RDP epsilon curves compose by pointwise addition.
type RDPCurve = Double -> Double  -- order alpha |-> epsilon(alpha)

composeRDP :: [RDPCurve] -> RDPCurve
composeRDP curves alpha = sum [c alpha | c <- curves]

toApproxDP :: RDPCurve -> [Double] -> Double -> Double
delta `toApproxDP` curve alphas =
    minimum [curve a + log (1/delta) / (a - 1) | a <- alphas, a > 1]
```

### 4.3 Privacy Amplification by Subsampling

Subsampling is the engine that makes DP-SGD practical. If a mechanism M is (ε, δ)-DP and we apply it not to the full dataset but to a random subsample in which each record is included independently with probability γ, the subsampled mechanism is dramatically more private:

> **Theorem:** *Amplification by subsampling [6].* If M is (ε, δ)-DP, then M applied to a Poisson subsample with rate γ satisfies (ε', δ')-DP with ε' = ln(1 + γ(e^ε − 1)) and δ' = γδ.

For small ε this gives ε' ≈ γε: subsampling by a factor γ *multiplies* the privacy cost down by γ. The intuition is a secrecy-of-the-sample argument — the adversary does not know whether the target record was even included in the computation, and with probability 1 − γ its data is untouched.

Balle, Barthe, and Gaboardi [6] give *tight* analyses of this phenomenon via couplings and divergences, covering the three sampling regimes relevant to ML:

| Sampling scheme | Per-element inclusion | Amplification behavior | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Poisson (independent) | γ independently | ε' = ln(1+γ(e^ε−1)) | Variable batch size; cleanest analysis |
| Fixed-size w/o replacement | b/n exactly | Tight bounds via [6]; ≈ Poisson to leading order in γ | Constant memory; preferred in practice |
| Fixed-size with replacement | b/n with multiplicity | Weaker; records may repeat | Rarely used for DP-SGD |

The RDP treatment of subsampling is subtler than the (ε, δ) case because Rényi divergence does not tensorize as cleanly under mixtures; Wang, Balle, and Kasiviswanathan [5] derive an *analytical* moments accountant for the subsampled RDP setting that avoids the numerical integration of the original implementation. For fixed-size subsampling without replacement, recent work gives a holistic RDP accountant with explicit non-asymptotic bounds and shows it improves on the best prior computable guarantees substantially [8].

### 4.4 DP-SGD with Per-Example Gradient Clipping

We now assemble the pieces into *differentially private stochastic gradient descent* (DP-SGD), the algorithm of Abadi et al. [3] that brought DP to deep learning. Each iteration t performs the following steps:

1. **Subsample.** Draw a batch B_t by including each training example independently with probability q = b/n (Poisson sampling), or a fixed-size batch of size b.
2. **Per-example gradients.** For each xᵢ ∈ B_t, compute g_t(xᵢ) = ∇_θ L(θ_t, xᵢ).
3. **Clip.** Bound each gradient's ℓ₂ norm: ḡ_t(xᵢ) = g_t(xᵢ) / max(1, ‖g_t(xᵢ)‖₂/C).
4. **Noise.** Aggregate and perturb: g̃_t = (1/|B_t|)(Σᵢ ḡ_t(xᵢ) + N(0, σ²C²I)).
5. **Descend.** θ_{t+1} = θ_t − η_t g̃_t.

```python
def dp_sgd_step(params, batch, loss_fn, C, sigma, lr):
    # per-example gradients
    grads = [torch.autograd.grad(loss_fn(params, x), params)
             for x in batch]
    clipped = []
    for g in grads:
        norm = g.norm(p=2)
        factor = min(1.0, C / (norm + 1e-12))
        clipped.append(g * factor)          # sensitivity now bounded by C
    agg = sum(clipped) / len(batch)
    noisy = agg + sigma * C * torch.randn_like(agg)  # Gaussian mechanism
    return params - lr * noisy
```

*Why clipping is non-negotiable.* In classical DP the query has known sensitivity; in deep learning the gradient map has *unbounded* ℓ₂ sensitivity — a single example can move a gradient arbitrarily far in a non-convex landscape. Clipping projects every per-example gradient into the ℓ₂ ball of radius C, which *defines* the sensitivity to be C, at the price of *bias*: when C is small, large gradients are systematically shrunk and the update direction is distorted; when C is large, the added noise σC grows proportionally. The noise multiplier σ is the knob that trades utility for privacy, and the accountant converts (q, σ, T) into a final (ε, δ) [3].

The privacy analysis of one step is a subsampled Gaussian mechanism: without subsampling, the noisy clipped aggregate is a Gaussian mechanism with sensitivity C and noise σC, hence (α, α/(2σ²))-RDP at every order α [4]; subsampling with rate q amplifies this per [5][6]; and T steps compose by addition of RDP curves, converted once at the end [3][4].

## 5 Theoretical Analysis and Empirical Results

### 5.1 The Subsampled Gaussian RDP Bound

The tightest widely used accounting for DP-SGD is the RDP bound for the Poisson-subsampled Gaussian mechanism. Wang et al. [5] give an analytical form: for sampling rate q, noise multiplier σ, and integer order α, the per-step RDP parameter is bounded by a closed-form expression involving a sum over mixture components (their Theorem 8), which for small q behaves as ε_step(α) ≈ q²·α/σ² — *quadratic* in the sampling rate, strictly better than the linear q-scaling of the naive (ε, δ) amplification lemma. Over T steps the composed bound is T·ε_step(α), minimized over α jointly with the conversion term ln(1/δ)/(α−1).

The practical consequence is enormous. A strong-composition analysis of a realistic training run (T in the thousands, per-step σ ≈ 1) yields ε in the hundreds — vacuous. The moments accountant / RDP analysis of the *identical* run yields single-digit ε [3][5]. The gap is not a constant factor; it is the difference between a meaningless guarantee and a deployable one.

### 5.2 Accountant Comparison

| Accountant | Currency | Composition cost (T steps) | Conversion | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive / basic [2] | (ε, δ) | T·ε_step | None | Few queries |
| Strong composition [2] | (ε, δ) | O(√T·ε_step) | Built in | Black-box mechanisms |
| Moments accountant [3] | CGF of privacy loss | Σ moments (tight for subsampled Gaussian) | Chernoff over λ | Original DP-SGD |
| RDP accountant [4][5] | ε(α) curves | Σ per order (exact) | min over α | General mechanism mix |
| Fixed-size RDP [8] | ε(α) curves | Σ per order (exact) | min over α | Constant-memory training |

### 5.3 Empirical Utility

Abadi et al. [3] demonstrate that DP-SGD trains realistic networks at manageable cost: on MNIST they report approximately 97% test accuracy under a single-digit ε budget with δ = 10⁻⁵, and on CIFAR-10 accuracy in the low seventies after a differentially private PCA projection of the inputs — all with per-step noise multipliers near 1 and a few thousand compositions, a regime where classical composition would report ε in the hundreds. Subsequent work has narrowed the utility gap further through large-batch training, careful hyperparameter tuning, and public pretraining, but the accounting core — subsampled Gaussian steps composed in RDP — is unchanged [5][8].

### 5.4 Complexity

Per-example clipping raises the per-step cost to O(b·p) memory for batch size b and p parameters, versus O(p) for standard SGD, though vectorized implementations reduce this substantially. Accounting itself is negligible — O(|orders|) per step — and the dominant cost remains the T forward-backward passes, identical in order to non-private training.

## 6 Limitations

**Semantics of δ.** Approximate DP tolerates a δ-probability catastrophic failure, and δ must be o(1/n); practitioners sometimes treat ε as the whole story while δ quietly dominates the risk for small datasets.

**Group privacy erosion.** The guarantee degrades for correlated records — if one individual's data appears k times (duplicates, time series), the effective protection is the k-fold group-privacy bound, which can be vacuous. Deduplication is a privacy operation.

**Clipping bias and disparate impact.** Clipping shrinks large gradients, which disproportionately affects underrepresented classes and rare examples; empirical studies report accuracy drops concentrated on minority subgroups. Mitigations (adaptive clipping, per-layer bounds) help but add hyperparameters that themselves consume budget if tuned privately.

**Budget incomparability.** Different accountants applied to the *same* training run can report noticeably different ε values, so published budgets are only comparable modulo the accounting method, the adjacency definition, and the δ convention [7].

**No protection against poisoning or extraction beyond the bound.** DP bounds *influence*, not *correctness*: a poisoned dataset still trains a bad model, and the guarantee says nothing about what the model reveals about the *population* as opposed to individuals.

**Hyperparameter and compute overhead.** The clipping norm C, noise multiplier σ, and learning rate interact non-trivially; per-example gradients increase memory pressure; and large-batch training — the main lever for utility — demands significant accelerator memory.

## 7 Conclusion

Differential privacy in machine learning rests on a clean intellectual stack: the definitions of pure and approximate DP [1][2] fix what must be bounded; sensitivity-calibrated mechanisms show how to bound it; composition theorems [2][7] govern how bounds degrade under iteration; Rényi DP [4] provides a lossless compositional currency; amplification by subsampling [6] explains why stochastic minibatches are nearly free; and the moments accountant [3][5] turns these ingredients into a tight, practical accounting of DP-SGD with per-example gradient clipping. The resulting algorithm trains deep networks with provable, single-digit ε guarantees at a modest utility cost — a transformation from vacuous to deployable that is entirely an achievement of *analysis*, not of noise reduction. Open frontiers include tighter accountants for fixed-size and shuffled regimes [8], adaptive clipping with honest accounting, and closing the remaining utility gap on large-scale tasks. The central lesson endures: privacy is a budget to be accounted for with the same rigor as compute, and the accountant is as important as the algorithm.
