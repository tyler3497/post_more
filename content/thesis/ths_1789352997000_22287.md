# Market Microstructure: Limit Order Books, Kyle's Model, and the Dynamics of High-Frequency Trading

## Abstract

Market microstructure studies how the mechanics of trading transform latent supply and demand into observable prices. This thesis unifies three intellectual traditions: the information-theoretic models of Kyle (1985) and Glosten-Milgrom (1985), in which bid-ask spreads and price impact emerge from adverse selection against informed order flow; the econophysics of the limit order book pioneered by Bouchaud, Mezard and Potters, which derives the humped shape of the book, power-law order placement and quasi-permanent logarithmic price impact from zero-intelligence order flow; and the market-design critique of Budish, Cramton and Shim, who show that the continuous limit order book converts even symmetrically observed public information into latency-arbitrage rents, fueling a socially wasteful arms race for speed. We present Kyle's linear equilibrium with full derivations, formalize the propagator model of impact, survey millisecond-level evidence, and evaluate frequent batch auctions as a structural remedy.

## 1 Introduction

Prices do not descend from a Walrasian auctioneer. They emerge, tick by tick, from a concrete mechanism: the *limit order book*, a prioritized queue of buy and sell intentions processed by matching engines in microseconds. Market microstructure is the study of this mechanism — how order types, priority rules, latency, and information asymmetry jointly determine transaction prices, spreads, and volatility [1].

The field developed in three waves. The first, in the mid-1980s, was *information-theoretic*. Kyle (1985) modeled a single informed insider trading against noise traders, with competitive market makers setting prices as conditional expectations of value given aggregate order flow; the slope of that pricing rule, *Kyle's lambda*, became the canonical measure of illiquidity [2]. Glosten and Milgrom (1985) derived the bid-ask spread of a specialist as compensation for adverse selection when some traders know more than the dealer. Easley and O'Hara (1987) added the information content of trade *timing*.

The second wave was *empirical and econophysical*. Researchers decomposed the spread into order-processing, inventory, and adverse-selection components, estimated price impact functions from transactions data, and — beginning with Bouchaud, Mezard and Potters (2002) — treated the order book itself as a statistical-mechanical system with universal scaling laws [3][4][5].

The third wave is the *market-design* era of high-frequency trading. Budish, Cramton and Shim (2015) argued that the continuous limit order book is a flawed mechanism: by treating time as continuous and processing orders serially, it converts even symmetrically observed public information into arbitrage rents, inducing a socially wasteful arms race for speed [6][7]. This thesis integrates all three waves into one account of how modern markets work.

## 2 Background

### The limit order book

A limit order book (LOB) is a double-sided queue. A *limit order* specifies a side (buy/sell), a price, and a quantity, and rests in the book until executed, cancelled, or expired. A *market order* demands immediate execution against resting limit orders. Orders match under *price-time priority*: the best price executes first, and ties break by arrival time. Key state variables include:

- **Best bid / best ask**: the highest buy and lowest sell limit prices.
- **Spread**: ask minus bid, the cost of a round trip.
- **Mid-price**: the average of best bid and ask, the standard proxy for the efficient price.
- **Depth**: resting quantity at each price level; **gaps** are empty levels behind the best quotes.

Most modern equity, futures, and crypto venues run continuous limit order books, many with maker-taker fee schedules that pay rebates to liquidity providers and charge takers.

### Kyle (1985): the canonical adverse-selection model

Kyle's single-auction model has three actors. An *insider* observes the liquidation value v of the asset, drawn from a normal prior with mean v0 and variance σv². *Noise traders* submit an aggregate random quantity u, normally distributed with mean zero and variance σu², for exogenous liquidity reasons. A competitive, risk-neutral *market maker* observes only total order flow y = x + u, where x is the insider's trade, and sets the price as the conditional expectation p = E[v | y] — the semi-strong efficiency condition [2].

> **Theorem (Kyle, 1985 — linear equilibrium).** There exists a unique linear equilibrium in which the insider trades X(v) = β(v − v0) with β = σu/σv, and the market maker prices P(y) = v0 + λy with λ = σv/(2σu). The insider's ex ante expected profit equals σuσv/2.

The parameter λ — *Kyle's lambda* — measures price impact: how many dollars the price moves per unit of order flow. It rises with the insider's information advantage (σv) and falls with noise-trader camouflage (σu) [1][2].

### Glosten-Milgrom (1985): the spread as adverse selection

In the Glosten-Milgrom sequential-trade model, a specialist posts bid and ask quotes before each trade, facing a population in which a fraction are informed. The zero-profit quotes are conditional expectations: the ask equals E[v | a buy arrives], the bid equals E[v | a sell arrives]. Because buys are more likely when value is high, the ask exceeds the unconditional mean, and the spread compensates the dealer exactly for expected losses to informed traders. The model predicts that spreads widen with the probability of informed trading — a prediction that launched the empirical spread-decomposition literature.

| Model | Key mechanism | Central prediction |
|---|---|---|
| Kyle (1985) | Insider hides in noise-trader flow; market maker filters | Linear pricing P(y) = v0 + λy; λ = σv/(2σu) |
| Glosten-Milgrom (1985) | Specialist quotes against informed fraction | Spread compensates adverse selection exactly |
| Bouchaud et al. (2002) | Zero-intelligence order deposition/cancellation | Humped book; power-law order placement |
| Budish-Cramton-Shim (2015) | Continuous time + serial processing | Public information yields latency-arbitrage rents |

## 3 Methodology

Studying microstructure requires choosing the right clock. In *calendar time*, activity is bursty; in *event time* (indexed by order-book events), many regularities stabilize. The core empirical object is the *response function*

R(l) = E[(m(t+l) − m(t)) · ε(t)],

the expected mid-price move l steps after a trade of sign ε(t). If impact were purely mechanical and permanent, R would be flat; if purely transient, it would decay to zero. Reality is subtler [4][5].

The *propagator model* of Bouchaud et al. formalizes this. The mid-price is written as a linear filter of past signed trades plus noise:

m(t) = Σ G(t − s) ε(s) v(s) + noise,

where G is a decaying propagator kernel and the sum runs over past trades. Calibrating G against data reveals a striking fact: the market sits near a *critical point* where strongly autocorrelated market orders (which push toward super-diffusion) are almost exactly offset by mean-reverting limit-order replenishment (which pushes toward sub-diffusion), leaving prices nearly diffusive [5].

For the Kyle model, the empirical methodology is a regression of price changes on signed order flow:

And the Kyle equilibrium itself is compact enough to state as pure functions:

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Kyle equilibrium, in full

The equilibrium is a fixed point between two optimizations. Given a conjectured linear pricing rule P(y) = μ + λy, the insider chooses x to maximize E[(v − μ − λ(x + u))x | v] = (v − μ − λx)x, yielding the first-order condition x = (v − μ)/(2λ). Given a conjectured linear strategy X(v) = α + βv, the market maker observes y = α + βv + u and applies the normal projection theorem: E[v | y] = v0 + Cov(v,y)/Var(y) · (y − E[y]). Consistency of the two conjectures pins down μ = v0, β = σu/σv and λ = σv/(2σu) [2].

Three comparative statics matter economically. First, λ rises with σv: more private information means each unit of flow is more informative, so the market maker shades quotes more aggressively. Second, λ falls with σu: deeper noise-trader flow camouflages the insider. Third, the insider trades *less* aggressively when λ is high (β and λ are inversely linked through σu/σv), which is why λ is estimable from the regression of price changes on flow — the standard empirical proxy for illiquidity [1].

The multi-auction extension shows λ declining over the trading horizon as information is impounded into prices, while the continuous-time limit (Back, 1992) yields constant proportional impact with the insider smoothing trades over time. A crucial caveat, documented by Collin-Dufresne and Fos, is *selection*: informed traders trade more aggressively precisely when measured liquidity is good, so naive estimates of λ understate true adverse selection — an endogeneity the constant-λ Kyle model rules out by construction [1].

### 4.2 Adverse selection and the anatomy of the spread

The quoted spread bundles three economic costs:

1. **Order-processing costs** — the dealer's fixed cost of handling trades.
2. **Inventory costs** — compensation for holding undesired positions (Stoll, 1978; Ho and Stoll, 1981).
3. **Adverse selection** — expected losses to better-informed traders, the Glosten-Milgrom component.

Empiricists separate them with covariance and regression structure. Roll (1984) inferred the *effective* spread from the negative autocovariance of price changes under pure bid-ask bounce. Glosten (1987) and Glosten-Harris (1988) decomposed the spread into transitory and permanent (adverse-selection) parts via the regression of price changes on trade direction and size. Stoll (1989) estimated a three-way split. Hasbrouck (1991) used a vector autoregression of quote revisions and trade signs; the long-run cumulative impulse response of quotes to a trade innovation measures its information content [10].

| Component | Interpretation | Identification strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Order processing | Fixed handling cost | Transitory bounce; Roll implied spread |
| Inventory | Risk of carrying positions | Mean reversion in dealer quotes |
| Adverse selection | Losses to informed flow | Permanent quote revision; Glosten-Harris |

A robust empirical finding is that the adverse-selection share rises for small, volatile, analyst-neglected stocks — exactly where σv/σu is high, as Kyle predicts [1].

### 4.3 The statistical physics of the order book

Bouchaud, Mezard and Potters (2002) studied the Paris Bourse order book and found striking regularities. Incoming limit-order prices follow a power law in distance from the best quote with a diverging mean — traders place orders surprisingly far from the touch. The *average* book has a humped shape: depth is thin at the best quotes, rises to a maximum a few ticks away, then decays. A zero-intelligence model — limit orders, market orders and cancellations arriving as independent Poisson processes with empirically calibrated rates — reproduces the hump quantitatively, showing that much of the book's shape needs no strategic explanation [3].

Potters and Bouchaud (2003) then measured the price impact function on Nasdaq, French and British stocks and found a *logarithmic* dependence of price response on volume, with impact that is quasi-permanent: the response function barely decays with lag. Trading itself is interpreted by the market as information [4]. The propagator framework reconciles this with diffusive prices: because market-order signs are strongly autocorrelated (order splitting by large traders), impact must be transient at the single-trade level — otherwise prices would trend. The market sits at the critical point where transient impact and persistent flow cancel, and the fluctuation-response relation pins down the kernel [5].

> **Theorem (fluctuation-response, Bouchaud et al. 2003).** If prices are diffusive while trade signs are long-range correlated, the single-trade impact kernel G must decay as a power law tuned so that Σ G(l)C(l) converges, where C(l) is the sign autocorrelation. The market self-organizes to this critical tuning.

### 4.4 High-frequency trading and latency arbitrage

The third wave begins with an observation about mechanism design. The continuous limit order book processes orders *serially in continuous time*. Budish, Cramton and Shim (2015) show this combination is the root flaw: even when a piece of public information is observed by all participants at essentially the same instant, serial processing means someone is first, and the winner captures an arbitrage rent by sniping stale quotes on slower venues [6][8].

This *latency arbitrage* differs fundamentally from Kyle/Glosten-Milgrom adverse selection: the signal is symmetrically observed public information (e.g., an index future ticking in Chicago), not private information. Yet it functions as a tax on liquidity — any liquidity provider must bear the cost of being sniped — and it induces a socially wasteful arms race: microwave towers, hollow-core fiber, FPGA feed handlers, and exchange colocation, all to be microseconds faster at reacting to public news [7][9].

The empirical magnitudes are concrete. Budish, Cramton and Shim document, using millisecond-stamped direct-feed data, that short-horizon correlations between related securities (ES futures in Chicago and the SPY ETF in New York) break down completely, generating mechanical arbitrage opportunities worth roughly $75 million per year for that single pair — with the large majority initiated by price changes in Chicago, the center of price discovery [7]. Dedicated fiber networks achieve roughly 4-millisecond one-way latency between Chicago and New York, while ordinary investors face latency distributions an order of magnitude wider, making their split orders systematically exploitable [9]. Competition has not shrunk the rents; it has only raised the speed bar for capturing them [6][8].

The proposed remedy is *frequent batch auctions*: replace continuous trading with uniform-price double auctions run, say, every tenth of a second. Discrete time destroys the value of microsecond advantages, and the batch auction converts speed competition into price competition. The design preserves algorithmic liquidity provision while eliminating the mechanical rents at their source [6][7].

### 4.5 Execution, matching engines, and market design

Optimal execution (Bertsimas and Lo, 1998; Almgren and Chriss, 2000) treats a large parent order as a control problem: split it over time to balance market impact against timing risk, with Kyle's λ or the propagator kernel as the impact model [10]. At the engine level, matching is a deterministic state machine whose core invariant — best bid strictly below best ask, with price-time priority — can be specified formally:


```rust
pub struct Book {
    pub bids: BTreeMap<i64, u64>, // price -> resting quantity
    pub asks: BTreeMap<i64, u64>,

impl Book {
    pub fn best_bid(&self) -> Option<i64> {
        self.bids.keys().next_back().copied()
    }
    pub fn best_ask(&self) -> Option<i64> {
        self.asks.keys().next().copied()
    }
    pub fn spread_ticks(&self) -> Option<i64> {
        Some(self.best_ask()? - self.best_bid()?)
    }
    pub fn mid_ticks(&self) -> Option<i64> {
        Some((self.best_ask()? + self.best_bid()?) / 2)
    }
}
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Proof sketch of Kyle's Theorem 1.** Conjecture linear strategies. The market maker's problem: with y = α + βv + u jointly normal with v, the projection theorem gives E[v | y] = v0 + βσv²/(β²σv² + σu²) · (y − α − βv0), so consistency requires the pricing slope λ = βσv²/(β²σv² + σu²) and intercept μ = v0 − λα. The insider's problem: max_x E[(v − μ − λ(x+u))x | v] has first-order condition x = (v − μ)/(2λ), so consistency requires 1/(2λ) = β and μ = v0 − α/(2λ)... solving the system yields α = 0, β = σu/σv, λ = σv/(2σu), μ = v0. The second-order condition holds since λ > 0. Uniqueness within the linear class follows because the system of consistency equations has a unique positive solution [2].

Empirically, the model's core prediction — that price impact rises with the informed-to-noise ratio — is confirmed in the cross-section: λ estimated from price-change-on-flow regressions is higher for stocks with greater private-information intensity [1]. The order-book regularities are equally robust across venues and decades: power-law order placement, the humped average book, and logarithmic quasi-permanent impact appear in Paris, London, New York and Nasdaq data alike [3][4]. And at millisecond horizons, the Budish-Cramton-Shim stylized facts hold: correlations between economically linked securities collapse, mechanical arbitrage opportunities recur at undiminished size and frequency, and competition manifests only as ever-faster capture [6][7][8].

> **Theorem (Budish-Cramton-Shim, 2015 — market-design impossibility).** Under continuous-time serial processing, for any symmetrically observed public signal there exist latency-arbitrage rents bounded away from zero; no investment in speed by liquidity providers eliminates them. Frequent batch auctions with discrete clearing intervals drive these rents to zero while preserving gains from algorithmic market making.

## 6 Limitations

Every model here buys tractability with strong assumptions. Kyle (1985) assumes risk-neutral competitive market makers, a single insider with Gaussian information, and — critically — *exogenous* noise traders; in reality, liquidity demand responds to spreads and volatility, and informed traders strategically condition on measured liquidity, biasing λ estimates [1]. The Gaussian-linear structure delivers closed forms but cannot generate the fat-tailed returns and power-law order placement the econophysics literature documents [3].

The zero-intelligence order-book models reproduce average shapes but not the strategic layering, spoofing, and quote flickering of real high-frequency flow; their Poisson assumptions miss the long memory in order signs that the propagator framework must then reintroduce [5]. Latency-arbitrage estimates, while careful, rest on specific data windows and venue pairs — the $75 million figure covers one ES/SPY trade, and economy-wide extrapolation is speculative [7][9]. Frequent batch auctions face transition frictions: fragmented liquidity across venues, the political economy of incumbent exchanges, and open questions about optimal batch length and the treatment of large institutional crosses [6]. Finally, dark pools, internalization, and payment for order flow — where a large share of retail flow now executes — lie outside the lit-book models surveyed here.

## 7 Conclusion

Market microstructure has converged on a unified picture. *Adverse selection* — formalized by Kyle's λ and the Glosten-Milgrom spread — explains why trading moves prices and why liquidity is costly. *Order-book statistical mechanics* — humped books, power-law placement, propagator-critical impact — explains the fine structure of how that cost is distributed across price levels and time. And *market-design analysis* — the Budish-Cramton-Shim critique — explains why the continuous limit order book, for all its virtues, manufactures a speed arms race out of public information, and how discrete-time batch auctions could end it.

Open frontiers include cross-venue and cross-asset impact (how a trade in Chicago futures propagates to New York ETFs at microsecond horizons), the microstructure of crypto books that never close, machine-learned order-flow prediction and its feedback on λ itself, and the welfare analysis of batch-auction adoption. The deeper lesson endures: prices are not found; they are *made*, one matched order at a time, by mechanisms we can analyze, measure, and redesign.
