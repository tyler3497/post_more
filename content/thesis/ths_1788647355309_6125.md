# Neural Radiance Fields and 3D Gaussian Splatting: Volumetric Rendering, Differentiable Rasterization, and Real-Time Novel-View Synthesis

## Abstract

The problem of *novel-view synthesis*—reconstructing photorealistic images of a scene from arbitrary viewpoints given only a sparse set of calibrated photographs—has been transformed in the past five years by two complementary families of techniques. **Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)** [1] showed that a continuous 5D function mapping position and viewing direction to density and radiance, encoded by a neural network and optimized purely through differentiable volume rendering, could achieve unprecedented fidelity without any explicit geometry. Yet NeRF's reliance on hundreds of MLP evaluations per pixel made real-time rendering impossible. **3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)** [2] replaced the implicit MLP with an explicit collection of anisotropic 3D Gaussians and a tile-based differentiable rasterizer, reaching 1080p rendering above 100 FPS at quality rivaling the best NeRF variants. This thesis develops the mathematical foundations shared by both approaches—the volume rendering integral, positional encodings, and alpha compositing—and compares their training dynamics, anti-aliasing behavior, memory footprints, and failure modes, from Instant-NGP [3] through Mip-NeRF 360 [4] to Zip-NeRF [5].

## 1 Introduction

For decades, photorealistic image synthesis required explicit 3D geometry: meshes, textures, and physically based light transport. Novel-view synthesis from photographs—whether by image-based rendering, multi-view stereo, or proxy geometry—suffered from a chronic mismatch: continuous scenes forced into discrete, hand-engineered representations produced holes, ghosting, and texture stretching.

In 2020, Mildenhall *et al.* [1] demonstrated that a scene could instead be represented as a *continuous volumetric function* learned entirely from posed 2D images. Their insight was architectural as much as mathematical: volume rendering, the classical integral transport formulation of emission and absorption along a ray, is *naturally differentiable*. By querying a neural network at points along camera rays, integrating the returned densities and colors, and comparing the composite against ground-truth pixels, the entire network could be trained with gradient descent. No mesh, no depth supervision, no lighting model—only multi-view consistency.

The cost of this elegance was computational. A single NeRF scene required **12–72 hours** of training on a V100 GPU, and rendering a single 1080p frame took tens of seconds, since each of two million pixels demanded roughly 192 forward passes of the MLP. The field responded in waves:

1. **Acceleration via data structures** — voxel grids, tensor factorizations, and above all the multiresolution hash encoding of Instant-NGP [3], which reduced training from days to *seconds* while preserving quality.
2. **Aliasing and scale reasoning** — Mip-NeRF replaced rays with cones and introduced the integrated positional encoding; Mip-NeRF 360 [4] extended this to unbounded 360° scenes with scene contraction, proposal networks, and a distortion regularizer.
3. **The explicit turn** — 3D Gaussian Splatting [2] abandoned the neural network entirely at render time, optimizing an explicit set of millions of anisotropic Gaussians splatted by a hand-written differentiable rasterizer. Quality matched Mip-NeRF 360 while rendering exceeded **100 FPS**.

This thesis argues that these developments are not competing alternatives but a single trajectory: the gradual migration of complexity from the *representation* (implicit MLPs) into the *renderer* (differentiable rasterization), while the underlying mathematics—the transmittance-weighted volume integral—remains invariant.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Volume Rendering Integral

Long before neural representations, Kajiya and Von Herzen formalized rendering of participating media through the *volume rendering equation*. For a ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$, the radiance reaching the camera is:

$$C(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\,\sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t), \mathbf{d})\,dt, \quad T(t) = \exp\!\left(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\,ds\right)$$

where $\sigma$ is the volume density, $\mathbf{c}$ the view-dependent emitted radiance, and $T(t)$ the *transmittance*—the probability that light survives from $t$ back to the camera. NeRF's entire training signal derives from the fact that this integral is differentiable in $\sigma$ and $\mathbf{c}$, and hence in every parameter that produces them [1].

In practice the integral is approximated by quadrature. Sampling $N$ stratified points $t_1 < \dots < t_N$ along the ray and defining $\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i$, NeRF computes:

```python
def volume_render(colors, sigmas, deltas):
    """Differentiable discrete volume rendering (alpha compositing)."""
    alpha = 1.0 - np.exp(-sigmas * deltas)      # per-segment opacity
    transmittance = np.cumprod(np.concatenate([[1.0], 1.0 - alpha[:-1]]))
    weights = transmittance * alpha              # w_i: transmittance-weighted opacity
    color = np.sum(weights[:, None] * colors, axis=0)
    return color, weights
```

The weights $w_i$ form a probability distribution along the ray; their expectation $\sum_i w_i t_i$ is the expected termination depth. This simple observation underlies *hierarchical sampling*: NeRF trains a coarse network to produce a first weight distribution, then importance-samples a fine network's points where the coarse weights are large—concentrating the representational budget near surfaces [1].

### 2.2 Positional Encoding and Spectral Bias

Standard MLPs with ReLU activations exhibit a *spectral bias*: they learn low-frequency functions first and fit high frequencies only reluctantly. For view synthesis this is fatal, because radiance fields are rich in sharp edges and texture. NeRF's remedy was the **positional encoding**

$$\gamma(p) = \big(\sin(2^0\pi p), \cos(2^0\pi p), \dots, \sin(2^{L-1}\pi p), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi p)\big),$$

applied to each coordinate with $L=10$ for position and $L=4$ for direction [1]. The mapping lifts the input into a high-dimensional Fourier basis in which high-frequency detail becomes linearly separable. Mip-NeRF observed that point-sampling with this encoding causes *aliasing*: a pixel is a cone footprint, not a ray. Its **integrated positional encoding (IPE)** computes the expected encoding over a conical frustum approximated by a Gaussian, band-limiting the representation consistently with the pixel footprint [4]—the same low-pass principle that later resurfaced in 3DGS as *Mip-Splatting*.

### 2.3 The Acceleration Interlude: Instant-NGP

Müller *et al.* [3] asked a different question: if positional encoding is just a fixed basis, why not make the encoding *itself* trainable? Their multiresolution hash encoding maintains $L$ levels of hash tables of feature vectors over grids of geometrically increasing resolution. A query point is trilinearly interpolated in each level, the feature vectors are concatenated, and a *tiny* MLP decodes them. Hash collisions are disambiguated by the multiresolution structure, and fully-fused CUDA kernels keep everything on-chip. The result: NeRF-quality scenes after *seconds* of training [3]. Instant-NGP proved that the MLP was never the essential object; the essential object was the *encoding of space*.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Scene Representation as Anisotropic Gaussians

3D Gaussian Splatting [2] takes the lesson of Instant-NGP to its logical extreme: eliminate the network at render time altogether. The scene is an explicit set of $N$ (typically $10^5$–$10^7$) 3D Gaussians, each parameterized by:

- a mean (position) $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^3$,
- a covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^\top\mathbf{R}^\top$, decomposed into a rotation quaternion $\mathbf{q}$ and a per-axis scale vector $\mathbf{s}$ for stable, positive-definite optimization,
- an opacity $o \in [0,1]$,
- view-dependent color via **spherical harmonics (SH)** coefficients (degree 3, i.e., 16 coefficients per channel), which compactly capture specularities and anisotropic reflectance without per-view MLPs.

Initialization comes from the sparse point cloud produced by structure-from-motion (COLMAP), so optimization begins near a plausible geometry rather than from scratch.

### 3.2 Differentiable Tile-Based Rasterization

Rendering projects each 3D Gaussian to the image plane. Under the affine approximation of the perspective projection with viewing transform $\mathbf{W}$ and projective Jacobian $\mathbf{J}$, the projected 2D covariance is [2]:

$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}' = \mathbf{J}\,\mathbf{W}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{J}^\top.$$

The 2D Gaussian contributes to pixels in its support with opacity $\alpha = o \cdot \exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}')^\top {\boldsymbol{\Sigma}'}^{-1} (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}'))$. The renderer then executes the now-familiar alpha compositing in front-to-back order:

$$C = \sum_{i=1}^{K} \mathbf{c}_i \,\alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-\alpha_j),$$

which is precisely the discrete volume rendering quadrature of Section 2.1—the weights $w_i = \alpha_i \prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j)$ are the transmittance-weighted opacities of a sampled medium. **3DGS is, mathematically, NeRF with the integral's samples replaced by sorted splats.** This identity is the conceptual core of the comparison.

For performance, the screen is divided into $16 \times 16$ tiles. Each Gaussian is instantiated per tile it overlaps; thread blocks share depth-sorted Gaussian lists in fast on-chip memory, and per-pixel blending proceeds in sorted order. Every operation is differentiable CUDA, so gradients flow back to $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, $\mathbf{q}$, $\mathbf{s}$, $o$, and the SH coefficients through an $\mathcal{L}_1$ plus D-SSIM photometric objective.

### 3.3 Adaptive Density Control

Since the initial point cloud is sparse, 3DGS interleaves optimization with structural refinement every few hundred iterations [2]:

| Operation | Trigger | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Clone** | Small Gaussian, large view-space positional gradient | Duplicates the Gaussian along the gradient direction |
| **Split** | Large Gaussian, large view-space positional gradient | Replaces it with two smaller Gaussians with divided scale |
| **Prune** | Opacity below $\epsilon_\alpha$, or world-space scale larger than scene extent | Removes the Gaussian |

This *densification and pruning* is the explicit analog of NeRF's hierarchical sampling: representational budget migrates toward surfaces, while empty space and redundant primitives are discarded. No neural proposal network is needed—the view-space gradient magnitude itself is the saliency signal.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The NeRF Core: Encoding, Sampling, and the Integral

Three design decisions made NeRF work where earlier learned rendering failed. First, the **Fourier positional encoding** defeated spectral bias, letting an MLP memorize high-frequency texture [1]. Second, **hierarchical volume sampling** allocated the fine network's queries according to the coarse network's transmittance weights, learning an importance sampler over depth. Third, the **emission-absorption integral** provided the right inductive bias: density is shared across viewing directions while radiance is view-dependent, so consistent geometry is the only way to explain all images simultaneously.

The weaknesses followed from the strengths. Every pixel required 192 MLP evaluations (64 coarse + 128 fine); at 2M pixels per 1080p frame, a single image cost ~400M network evaluations. Training took **12–72 hours** [6]. View sparsity (fewer than ~20 images) produced the *shape–radiance ambiguity*: with few constraints, the optimizer explains each image with cloudy "floaters" near the camera rather than true surfaces.

### 4.2 The 3DGS Core: Splats, Tiles, and Spherical Harmonics

3DGS inherits NeRF's integral but changes *what is integrated*. Each Gaussian is simultaneously a density kernel and a radiance carrier: the opacity-weighted 2D splat is the quadrature sample, and the SH coefficients supply view dependence. SH degree 3 captures diffuse and low-frequency glossy behavior; higher frequencies (sharp mirror reflections) remain difficult, exactly as in NeRF where direction encoding used only $L=4$.

The engineering triumph is the **tile rasterizer**. Sorting is per-tile rather than global; a radix sort over 64-bit keys (tile ID, depth) runs in milliseconds; per-pixel loops touch only the Gaussians overlapping their tile. Memory is the price: a typical scene holds 3–5M Gaussians, each storing 59 floats (position, scale, quaternion, opacity, 48 SH coefficients), totaling **~700MB–1.2GB**. NeRF, by contrast, stores a scene in a few megabytes of MLP weights. **3DGS trades memory for speed at a ratio of roughly 100:1** relative to an implicit model.

### 4.3 Quality–Speed Tradeoffs and the Aliasing Frontier

No method dominates on all axes. The table below (values representative of the Mip-NeRF 360 benchmark suite as reported in the cited papers) tells the story:

| Method | Training time | Render speed (1080p) | Quality (PSNR) | Scene size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NeRF [1] | 12–72 h | ~0.05 FPS | ~25.0 | ~5 MB |
| Instant-NGP [3] | ~5 min (seconds to converge) | ~10–30 FPS | ~25.6 | ~50 MB |
| Mip-NeRF 360 [4] | ~48 h | ~0.1 FPS | **26.6** | ~15 MB |
| Zip-NeRF [5] | ~2 h (24× faster than [4]) | ~1 FPS | **27.0** | ~600 MB |
| 3D Gaussian Splatting [2] | ~30–50 min | **>100 FPS** | ~27.0 (comparable to [4]) | ~700 MB–1 GB |

> **Theorem (Informal):** *Any radiance-field renderer that achieves interactive frame rates must either (a) precompute and cache the field into an explicit structure, or (b) evaluate the field through a hardware-accelerated rasterization path. The MLP-evaluate-per-sample path cannot sustain 1080p real-time rendering on current hardware.*

The aliasing story completes the picture. Mip-NeRF 360 solved aliasing for MLPs via cone tracing and IPE plus a **distortion regularizer** that penalizes spread-out weight distributions, crushing floaters [4]. Zip-NeRF brought anti-aliasing to grid-based NeRFs, combining the multisampling of [4] with Instant-NGP's hash grids to cut error rates by 8–77% while training 24× faster than Mip-NeRF 360 [5]. 3DGS initially regressed on aliasing: splats are not band-limited, so zooming out produces shimmering and popping. **Mip-Splatting** answered with a 3D smoothing filter (bounding each Gaussian's screen-space extent from below, a low-pass over the representation) plus a 2D Mip filter during rasterization—restoring scale-consistent rendering with negligible cost. The pattern is unmistakable: *every* radiance-field family independently rediscovers that rendering is sampling, and sampling without filtering is aliasing.

### 4.4 Geometry Extraction and Editing

A radiance field is a rendering primitive, not a geometric one. Extracting surfaces requires an additional step: NeRF pipelines typically run **marching cubes** on a density grid sampled from the MLP, while 3DGS-based approaches like *SuGaR* align Gaussians to surfaces with a signed-distance regularizer and then extract meshes from a Poisson reconstruction of the Gaussian centers.

This is where the explicit representation shows its second advantage: *editability*. Gaussians can be selected, deleted, duplicated, and deformed with standard point-cloud tools; semantic segmentation of the Gaussian set enables object removal and scene composition. Editing a NeRF means retraining the MLP. Dynamic scenes sharpen the contrast—deforming Gaussian sets is dramatically cheaper than conditioning an MLP on time. The survey literature [6] identifies dynamic and editable radiance fields as the dominant application frontier, since downstream tasks—robotics, AR, digital twins—demand manipulable geometry, not just pretty pixels.

### 4.5 Failure Modes

1. **Floaters.** Semi-transparent density that explains one view but occludes others. NeRF's distortion loss [4] and 3DGS's opacity pruning both attack this, but sparse inputs still produce clouds near the camera in both families.
2. **View sparsity.** Below ~20 well-distributed views, both methods degrade; the shape–radiance ambiguity is a property of the *loss landscape*, not the representation. Few-shot variants add depth priors or diffusion-based regularization, trading generality for robustness.
3. **Reflective and refractive surfaces.** Mirrors, glass, and glossy paint violate the assumption that radiance varies smoothly with direction. SH of degree 3 cannot represent a sharp mirror lobe; NeRF's direction MLP does slightly better but still fails on true mirrors. Both families reconstruct reflections as *geometry behind the surface*—a known, unsolved pathology.
4. **Thin structures and transparency.** 3DGS struggles with sub-pixel geometry (fences, foliage) because splats have minimum screen-space extent; NeRF handles these better through the continuous density field.
5. **Temporal flicker.** In video rendering, per-frame-independent 3DGS optimization causes shimmering; joint spatiotemporal optimization remains an open problem.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The central quantitative claim of the field's recent history is due to Kerbl *et al.* [2]: with training times competitive with the fastest prior methods (~30–50 minutes), 3DGS reaches rendering quality *slightly better than Mip-NeRF 360* [4]—the previous quality champion—while rendering at well over **100 FPS** at 1080p. Concretely, on the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset, 3DGS reports average PSNR ≈ 27.0, SSIM ≈ 0.81, and LPIPS ≈ 0.22, matching or exceeding [4] (PSNR ≈ 26.6) at a **1000× rendering speedup**.

Zip-NeRF [5] provides the complementary data point on the NeRF side: by fusing anti-aliased cone sampling with hash-grid features, it reduces error rates by **8–77%** relative to either Mip-NeRF 360 or Instant-NGP alone, and trains **24× faster** than Mip-NeRF 360. Instant-NGP [3] anchors the speed extreme: NeRF-quality reconstructions in *seconds* of training, rendering in tens of milliseconds—though at quality below [4] and [5].

A formal observation connects the families. Consider the discrete rendering equation shared by both:

$$C = \sum_i w_i\,\mathbf{c}_i, \qquad w_i \ge 0,\;\; \sum_i w_i \le 1.$$

> **Theorem:** *Let a ray's transmittance-weighted sample weights $\{w_i\}$ be fixed. Then the rendered color $C$ is linear in the per-sample colors $\{\mathbf{c}_i\}$, and the photometric loss gradient with respect to any parameter $\theta$ factors as $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta = \sum_i (\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{c}_i)\cdot(\partial\mathbf{c}_i/\partial\theta) + (\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w_i)\cdot(\partial w_i/\partial\theta)$. NeRF and 3DGS differ only in how $\{w_i\}$ and $\{\mathbf{c}_i\}$ are parameterized—via an MLP's density head and radiance head in the former, via projected Gaussian opacities and SH coefficients in the latter—so both optimize the identical objective landscape restricted to different hypothesis classes.*

*Proof sketch.* Both render through the same alpha-compositing quadrature; differentiation follows the chain rule through the weights. In NeRF, $w_i$ derives from the MLP's density head and $\mathbf{c}_i$ from its radiance head; in 3DGS, $w_i$ derives from the projected Gaussian opacity and $\mathbf{c}_i$ from the SH expansion. The loss is the same photometric objective; the hypothesis classes differ in smoothness, locality, and capacity, explaining the divergent optimization dynamics and memory profiles while the gradients share a common algebraic form. ∎

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

- **Memory scaling.** 3DGS scenes of 1GB are impractical for web and mobile deployment; compression (vector quantization of SH, opacity-aware pruning) is active research but still lossy.
- **Unbounded scenes.** Mip-NeRF 360's scene contraction elegantly parameterizes all of $\mathbb{R}^3$ [4]; 3DGS handles backgrounds with large distant Gaussians, but sky and far-field geometry remain fragile.
- **Relighting.** Both families bake illumination into the representation. Decomposing into albedo, normals, and lighting (inverse rendering) is unsolved at 3DGS quality levels.
- **Generalization.** Every method discussed here is *per-scene optimized*. Feed-forward models that infer a radiance field from a few images in a single pass exist but lag far behind in quality; closing this gap is arguably the field's most important open problem [6].
- **Theoretical understanding.** Why does the distortion regularizer work so well? What is the sample complexity of radiance-field reconstruction? The field remains largely empirical; a statistical learning theory of neural and splat-based rendering does not yet exist.

## 7 Conclusion

NeRF [1] proved that *differentiable volume rendering* could conjure geometry from photographs alone. Instant-NGP [3] proved the MLP was incidental and the encoding essential. Mip-NeRF 360 [4] and Zip-NeRF [5] proved that scale-aware sampling and regularization tame aliasing and floaters. 3D Gaussian Splatting [2] proved that the entire apparatus could be made explicit—millions of anisotropic kernels, a tile rasterizer, spherical harmonics—and thereby made *real-time*.

The through-line is the volume rendering integral. Whether its samples are MLP queries along a ray or sorted splats in a tile, whether density lives in weights or in opacities, the mathematics of transmittance-weighted compositing governs optimization, aliasing, and failure alike. The destination is clear—a world in which any set of photographs becomes an interactive, editable, photorealistic 3D scene in minutes. The integral will still be there, quietly doing the compositing.
