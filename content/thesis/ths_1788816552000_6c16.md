# The NVIDIA Hopper H100 Architecture: Fourth-Generation Tensor Cores, the Transformer Engine, DPX Instructions, and Confidential Computing for AI Superclusters

## Abstract

The NVIDIA H100 Tensor Core GPU, built on the Hopper architecture, represents the most significant data-center GPU redesign since Tensor Cores debuted in 2017. Fabricated on TSMC's 4N process with 80 billion transistors, the GH100 die integrates 144 streaming multiprocessors (132 enabled in SXM), fourth-generation Tensor Cores with FP8 support, 80 GB of HBM3 at 3.35 TB/s, and 900 GB/s fourth-generation NVLink [1][6]. This paper analyzes Hopper's four defining innovations: the fourth-generation Tensor Core with a widened dense-matrix datapath; the Transformer Engine, which dynamically mixes FP8 E4M3/E5M2 precisions to deliver up to 9× faster training and 30× faster inference than the prior generation [1][4]; DPX instructions accelerating dynamic programming kernels such as Smith–Waterman by up to 7× over A100 and 40× over CPU [1][8]; and the first confidential-computing mode in a production GPU, combining encrypted CPU–GPU traffic with hardware attestation [5][9]. We formalize the throughput and roofline arguments behind these gains and evaluate the persisting trade-offs: power density, FP8 accuracy cliffs, and the utilization cost of secure partitioning.

## 1 Introduction

The cadence of accelerator improvement has not been set by transistor scaling alone for nearly a decade. From Volta's introduction of Tensor Cores through Ampere's sparsity support, each NVIDIA data-center generation has extracted the majority of its performance delta from *specialized datapaths* — hardware structures purpose-built for the dominant operation of the era's workloads. The Hopper architecture, unveiled at GTC 2022 and shipped as the H100 Tensor Core GPU, continues this strategy with unusual breadth: rather than a single headline feature, Hopper introduces four architectural subsystems that each target a distinct workload class, namely (i) fourth-generation Tensor Cores with an FP8 number system for transformer training, (ii) the Transformer Engine for automatic mixed-precision policy, (iii) DPX instructions for dynamic programming recurrences, and (iv) confidential-computing support for multi-tenant cloud AI.

The economic context matters. By 2022, transformer-based language models had become the dominant consumer of data-center GPU cycles, with models such as Megatron-530B straining both compute and memory bandwidth [4]. Simultaneously, the largest scientific computing problems — sequence alignment in genomics, shortest-path routing, and other dynamic programming workloads — remained stubbornly CPU- or FPGA-bound. Hopper is NVIDIA's argument that a single accelerator can serve all three markets without compromise.

This paper makes three contributions. **First**, we reconstruct the GH100 floorplan and streaming multiprocessor (SM) organization from the public architecture whitepaper and corroborating technical sources, deriving the arithmetic intensities at which Hopper's datapaths saturate [1][3]. **Second**, we formalize the numerical and scheduling arguments behind the Transformer Engine and DPX instructions, including the FP8 quantization dynamics that determine when reduced precision is free and when it is not [2][4]. **Third**, we analyze the confidential-computing architecture — the first of its kind on a GPU — and its implications for cloud tenancy, alongside a critical assessment of Hopper's limitations: 700 W power envelopes, FP8 accuracy cliffs on small vision transformers, and the utilization cost of secure partitioning [4][5].

> **Theorem:** *In the Hopper microarchitecture, no single datapath improvement dominates end-to-end workload speedup; rather, peak gains are attainable only when the Transformer Engine's precision policy, DPX recurrences, and memory-subsystem throughput are jointly saturated.*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Ampere to Hopper

The Ampere-generation A100, Hopper's direct predecessor, delivered 312 TFLOPS of dense TF32 Tensor Core throughput, 2 TB/s of HBM2e bandwidth, and third-generation Tensor Cores with fine-grained structured sparsity support. Its 54.2 billion transistors on Samsung 8N made it the workhorse of the 2020–2022 AI buildout. Hopper moves fabrication to TSMC's 4N node (a 4 nm-class process), nearly doubles the transistor budget to 80 billion, and — critically — restructures the SM to quadruple per-SM floating-point throughput and double per-SM throughput on all legacy Tensor Core and FP32/FP64 datapaths, clock-for-clock [1][5].

The full GH100 implementation contains 8 Graphics Processing Clusters (GPCs), 72 Texture Processing Clusters (TPCs) arranged as 9 per GPC, 2 SMs per TPC for 144 SMs total, 18,432 FP32 CUDA cores (128 per SM), 576 fourth-generation Tensor Cores (4 per SM), and 60 MB of L2 cache fed by 12 512-bit memory controllers serving up to 6 HBM3 stacks [1]. The shipping H100 SXM product enables 132 SMs (16,896 CUDA cores, 528 Tensor Cores), 50 MB of L2, and 5 HBM3 stacks totaling 80 GB, while the PCIe form factor enables 114 SMs [3][6].

### 2.2 The precision ladder

Deep-learning hardware has climbed a descending precision ladder for a decade: FP32 → TF32 → FP16/BF16 → INT8 → now FP8. Each rung roughly doubles throughput per unit silicon but narrows the representable dynamic range, and each rung has required algorithmic co-design (loss scaling, quantization-aware training) to avoid accuracy collapse. Hopper's distinctive move is not merely adding FP8 — it is institutionalizing the *policy* of precision selection inside the hardware/software boundary via the Transformer Engine, so that per-tensor, per-layer precision decisions are made automatically at runtime rather than by the model developer [2][4].

### 2.3 Interconnect and the scale-out problem

Training trillion-parameter models demands that *N* devices behave approximately as one. Hopper's fourth-generation NVLink delivers 900 GB/s of GPU-to-GPU bandwidth, and the NVLink Switch System extends all-reduce bandwidth across nodes, while PCIe Gen5 provides 128 GB/s of host bandwidth [1][6]. These figures are not ornamental: at 3,958 TFLOPS of FP8 throughput, the arithmetic intensity required to keep an H100 fed exceeds 1,000 FLOP/byte — a regime where even minor interconnect bottlenecks dominate step time.

## 3 Methodology

This analysis is a *synthetic architectural study*: we combine NVIDIA's published whitepaper figures [1], the Transformer Engine software documentation [2][10], independent technical reporting [4][5][9], and community-compiled specification references [3][6][7] to reconstruct Hopper's design rationale, then validate the internal consistency of the reported performance figures with first-principles roofline and arithmetic-intensity modeling.

**Verification discipline.** Every quantitative claim in this paper is traceable to at least one cited source. Specifications were cross-checked across the vendor whitepaper and at least one independent compilation (Together AI's specification table and Clarifai's H100 guide agree with the whitepaper on all headline figures [6][7]). Where NVIDIA reports speedups (9× training, 30× inference, 40× DPX), we reproduce the *comparison baselines* as stated by NVIDIA (A100 for transformer workloads; CPU for Floyd–Warshall) rather than re-baselining them, and we explicitly discuss the benchmark conditions in Section 5.

**Analytical method.** We model Tensor Core throughput with a matrix-multiply-accumulate (MMA) abstraction: for an $M \times N \times K$ GEMM at precision $p$ with per-cycle tensor throughput $T_p$,

$$t_{\mathrm{GEMM}} \approx \frac{2MNK}{T_p} + t_{\mathrm{mem}}(MNK, BW),$$

where $t_{\mathrm{mem}}$ captures the bandwidth term under a roofline model. This decomposition lets us separate *datapath* gains (fourth-generation Tensor Cores) from *policy* gains (Transformer Engine precision selection) and *system* gains (NVLink scale-out) — the central analytical contribution of Section 5.

> **Theorem:** *Under a roofline model, doubling per-SM Tensor Core throughput raises achievable GEMM performance only when arithmetic intensity exceeds the ridge point; below it, HBM3 bandwidth is the binding constraint, which is why Hopper pairs doubled compute with 3.35 TB/s of memory bandwidth rather than either alone [1][6].*

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 GH100 Organization and the Streaming Multiprocessor

The GH100 die is organized hierarchically: 8 GPCs → 72 TPCs → 144 SMs. Each Hopper SM contains 128 FP32 CUDA cores, 4 fourth-generation Tensor Cores, a restructured warp scheduler, and a new Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) unit that performs asynchronous, descriptor-driven transfers between global memory and shared memory [1][3]. The TMA is a quiet but consequential innovation: by offloading address generation and bulk movement from the warp schedulers, it enables *asynchronous producer–consumer* pipelining in which data movement and MMA computation overlap with near-zero scheduler involvement. This is the hardware substrate for the `wgmma` (warp-group matrix-multiply-accumulate) instruction family exposed at the SASS level for sm_90 targets [3].

Numerically, the SM reorganization yields: 67 TFLOPS FP32, 67 TFLOPS FP64 Tensor Core (34 TFLOPS dense FP64), 989 TFLOPS dense TF32, 1,979 TFLOPS dense FP16/BF16, 3,958 TFLOPS dense FP8, and 3,958 TOPS INT8 on the SXM product [6][7]. Fine-grained structured sparsity (2:4) doubles the dense figures in the sparse regime — 7,916 TFLOPS sparse FP8 — at the cost of a strict weight-sparsity pattern the compiler must honor [1].

| Precision | Dense throughput (SXM) | Sparse (2:4) throughput |
|---|---|---|
| FP64 (Tensor Core) | 67 TFLOPS | — |
| FP32 | 67 TFLOPS | — |
| TF32 | 989 TFLOPS | 1,979 TFLOPS |
| FP16 / BF16 | 1,979 TFLOPS | 3,958 TFLOPS |
| FP8 | 3,958 TFLOPS | 7,916 TFLOPS |
| INT8 | 3,958 TOPS | 7,916 TOPS |

*Table 1: H100 SXM peak Tensor Core throughputs. Dense figures per [6][7]; sparse figures per the 2:4 structured-sparsity support documented in the Hopper whitepaper [1].*

### 4.2 Fourth-Generation Tensor Cores, FP8, and the Transformer Engine

The fourth-generation Tensor Core widens the MMA datapath and, more importantly, introduces hardware support for the FP8 number system — two distinct 8-bit floating-point encodings:

- **E4M3** (1 sign, 4 exponent, 3 mantissa bits): maximum magnitude 448, fine relative precision; used for *forward* propagation, where activations and weights concentrate near zero.
- **E5M2** (1 sign, 5 exponent, 2 mantissa bits): maximum magnitude 57,344, matching FP16's dynamic range with coarser precision; used for *backward* propagation, where gradients span a wider dynamic range [4].

The **Transformer Engine (TE)** is the policy layer atop this hardware. Implemented as a drop-in PyTorch/JAX library, TE intercepts linear layers, LayerNorm, and attention projections, and per-tensor decides whether FP16/BF16 or FP8 is safe, applying delayed-scaling quantization with per-tensor scale factors maintained in FP32 [2]. The key design decisions are:

1. *Delayed scaling*: rather than computing exact per-tensor maxima every step (expensive), TE maintains a history of observed maxima and derives FP8 scale factors from it, amortizing the reduction cost [10].
2. *FP32 master weights and gradient accumulation*: optimizer state and gradient accumulation remain in FP32, so the reduced-precision matmuls never contaminate the optimization trajectory itself [2].
3. *Fused kernels*: TE ships fused attention, LayerNorm-MLP, and bias-dropout-GELU kernels so that the precision conversions do not materialize extra memory passes [10].

```python
import torch
import transformer_engine.pytorch as te
from transformer_engine.common import recipe

# Hopper FP8 recipe: E4M3 forward, E5M2 backward, delayed scaling
fp8_recipe = recipe.DelayedScaling(margin=0, fp8_format=recipe.Format.E4M3)
model = te.TransformerLayer(hidden_size=12288, ffn_hidden_size=49152,
                            num_attention_heads=96)

with te.fp8_autocast(enabled=True, fp8_recipe=fp8_recipe):
    out = model(x)          # matmuls execute at 3,958 TFLOPS FP8 on H100
loss = out.sum(); loss.backward()   # gradients accumulated in FP32
```

*Listing 1: Transformer Engine usage pattern on Hopper (after [2]). The same model code runs in FP16 on Ampere; only the recipe and hardware change.*

The headline results: up to **9× faster training** on mixture-of-experts transformer models versus A100, and up to **30× higher per-GPU inference throughput** on Megatron-530B at 1-second latency [1][4]. These are compound figures — FP8 doubles effective throughput *and* halves memory traffic, the fused kernels remove memory passes, and NVLink 4 keeps tensor-parallel communication off the critical path.

> **Theorem:** *The Transformer Engine's speedup is superlinear in the precision ratio: halving precision more than doubles step throughput because it simultaneously (a) doubles MMA throughput, (b) halves activation/weight memory traffic, and (c) shortens tensor-parallel communication payloads — three independent terms in the step-time model of Section 3.*

### 4.3 DPX Instructions for Dynamic Programming

Dynamic programming (DP) recurrences — $\mathrm{dp}[i][j] = \min$ or $\max$ over a small neighborhood plus a transition cost — are the computational core of sequence alignment (Smith–Waterman, Needleman–Wunsch), shortest paths (Floyd–Warshall), and large families of operations-research and reinforcement-learning algorithms. On prior GPUs these recurrences were awkward: the inner loop is a short sequence of integer min/max/add operations with a data-dependent clamp, exposing little of the parallelism that SIMT architectures reward, and they were consequently left to CPUs or FPGAs [8].

Hopper's **DPX instruction set** fuses the DP inner-loop idioms into single SM instructions. The canonical example is `__vimax3_s16x2_relu`, which computes $\max(\min(a+b, c), 0)$ packed two-per-32-bit-lane — precisely the recurrence step of Smith–Waterman with affine-gap clamping [3]. Related intrinsics (`__viaddmin_s16x2`, `__vimax_s16x2_relu`, and their unsigned variants) cover the Needleman–Wunsch and Floyd–Warshall inner loops [8]. By fusing 3–4 dependent integer operations into one issued instruction with ReLU-style clamping in hardware, DPX collapses the instruction count of the DP inner loop by roughly 3× and, combined with Hopper's higher clocks and SM count, yields the reported **7× Smith–Waterman speedup over A100** and **up to 40× over CPU implementations** for DP workloads such as Floyd–Warshall [1][8].

```c
// Smith-Waterman inner loop, Hopper DPX (sm_90), packed int16x2
// score = max(min(diag + subst, up + gap), 0) -- one instruction
int32_t s = __vimax3_s16x2_relu(diag_plus_subst, up_plus_gap, left_plus_gap);
// vs. ~4 scalar instructions + clamp on Ampere
```

*Listing 2: DPX fused recurrence step. The single instruction replaces a min, an add, a max, and a clamp-to-zero [3][8].*

The broader significance is architectural *reach*: DPX moves an entire algorithm family from "runs on CPU" to "runs 40× faster on the same GPU that trains your models," which matters for genomics pipelines and scientific computing centers that previously had to provision heterogeneous hardware [8].

### 4.4 Memory Hierarchy and Interconnect

Hopper's memory subsystem is engineered for the >1,000 FLOP/byte regime its Tensor Cores demand:

- **HBM3**: 80 GB across 5 stacks at 3.35 TB/s on SXM (up to ~3 TB/s class bandwidth), a ~65% bandwidth increase over A100's HBM2e [1][6]. The PCIe form factor ships 80 GB of HBM2e instead, trading bandwidth for slot compatibility [3].
- **L2**: 50 MB on the SXM product (60 MB on the full die), nearly 25% larger than A100's 40 MB, with higher bandwidth to absorb the TMA's asynchronous traffic [1].
- **NVLink 4**: 900 GB/s bidirectional GPU-to-GPU bandwidth — 1.5× NVLink 3 — with the NVLink Switch System extending full-bandwidth all-reduce across up to 256 GPUs in an HGX/DGX SuperPOD-class fabric [1].
- **PCIe Gen5**: 128 GB/s host bandwidth, doubling Gen4, which matters for checkpoint offload and disaggregated inference [6].

For an FP8 GEMM at 3,958 TFLOPS against 3.35 TB/s of bandwidth, the ridge point sits at approximately $1{,}181$ FLOP/byte, meaning only GEMMs with arithmetic intensity above ~1.2 kFLOP/byte — large-batch training and long-sequence attention — reach peak; everything else is bandwidth-bound. This is precisely why the Transformer Engine's traffic-halving matters as much as its throughput-doubling: it *lowers* the effective intensity requirement by moving the same model with half the bytes.

### 4.5 Confidential Computing and Multi-Instance GPU

The H100 is the first production GPU with a **confidential-computing (CC) mode**, addressing the trust problem of cloud AI: a tenant's model weights and data are valuable intellectual property, yet they execute on hardware owned by a cloud provider [5][9]. In CC mode, Hopper establishes a hardware root of trust in the GPU System Processor, performs remote attestation of the GPU's firmware and configuration against NVIDIA-signed reference measurements, encrypts all CPU↔GPU traffic over PCIe, and isolates GPU memory from the host — so that even a compromised hypervisor cannot observe or tamper with in-flight AI workloads [5][9].

This is complemented by **second-generation Multi-Instance GPU (MIG)**, which partitions the H100 into up to 7 hardware-isolated instances (10 GB each on the 80 GB product), each with dedicated SMs, memory, and now — in the CC context — secure multi-tenant guarantees [9]. The combination is aimed squarely at cloud providers: MIG gives the *partitioning*, confidential computing gives the *trust*, and together they allow a single H100 to serve multiple mutually distrusting tenants.

> **Theorem:** *Confidential computing on Hopper trades a small, measurable tax (attestation latency at session setup, PCIe encryption bandwidth overhead) for a qualitative change in the threat model: the GPU transitions from a trusted-device assumption to a verified-device guarantee, making hardware-enforced tenant isolation composable with MIG's physical partitioning [5][9].*

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

We now assemble the reported empirical results into a coherent performance account, checking each claim against the analytical model of Section 3.

**Training.** On a 395-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts transformer, NVIDIA reports H100 reducing time-to-train from 7 days (A100) to 20 hours — approximately a 9× speedup [9]. Decomposing via the step-time model: ~2× from FP8 datapath throughput, ~1.5–2× from halved memory traffic and fused TE kernels, and the remainder from higher SM count, clocks, and NVLink 4 keeping 8-GPU tensor parallelism efficient. The compound is consistent with the theorem in Section 4.2: each factor attacks a *different* term (compute, memory, communication), so they multiply rather than merely add.

**Inference.** On Megatron-530B, H100 delivers up to 30× higher per-GPU throughput than A100 at 1-second latency [1][4]. The outsized inference gain relative to training reflects inference's memory-bandwidth-bound character: FP8 halves the KV-cache and weight traffic per token, which is the binding constraint in autoregressive decoding, while the 900 GB/s NVLink fabric accelerates the tensor-parallel all-reduces that dominate large-model serving.

**Dynamic programming.** DPX delivers up to 7× on Smith–Waterman versus A100 and up to 40× versus CPU on Floyd–Warshall-class workloads [1][8]. These figures are plausible under instruction-count analysis: the DP inner loop collapses from ~4+ instructions to 1 fused DPX instruction, and the H100's 132 SMs at higher clocks provide the remaining factor.

**MLPerf.** H100-based systems swept MLPerf Training and Inference v3.0 benchmarks available at launch, with up to 4.5× inference gains over A100 on submitted workloads [9].

**Formal throughput bound.** Let $S$ be SM count, $t$ the per-SM FP8 tensor throughput, and $\eta$ the achieved efficiency. Peak dense FP8 throughput is $P = S \cdot t = 132 \times 30\ \text{TFLOPS/SM} \approx 3{,}958$ TFLOPS, matching the published figure [6][7]. For any GEMM of intensity $I$ FLOP/byte on bandwidth $BW$, achievable performance satisfies

$$P_{\mathrm{ach}}(I) \le \min(S \cdot t \cdot \eta,\ I \cdot BW),$$

which is exactly the roofline argument of Section 3, and it predicts the observed phenomenon that small-batch inference never approaches 3,958 TFLOPS while large MoE training does.

## 6 Limitations

Hopper's strengths are real, but a candid assessment must register its constraints.

**Power and thermals.** The SXM H100 carries a 700 W TDP — among the highest of any single-chip accelerator shipped to date — demanding liquid cooling or aggressive air-cooling infrastructure and raising total cost of ownership beyond the silicon price [6]. Performance-per-watt improves over A100 (NVIDIA claims substantial gains), but absolute power density is a data-center design constraint, not a footnote [3].

**FP8 accuracy cliffs.** The Transformer Engine's "free" precision reduction is not universally free. NVIDIA's own data show that small vision transformer models lose ~1% accuracy when trained in FP8 versus FP32, because narrow layers lack the statistical averaging that makes quantization noise benign in large models [4]. Practitioners must validate per-model; TE's automation reduces but does not eliminate this burden.

**DPX is integer-narrow.** DPX accelerates 16-bit integer DP recurrences; floating-point DP, 32-bit recurrences, and irregular DP with data-dependent control flow see little benefit [3][8]. It is a scalpel, not a hammer.

**Confidential computing friction.** CC mode requires attestation infrastructure, NVIDIA-signed firmware measurements, and currently imposes setup latency and some throughput overhead on host-device transfers; it also interacts nontrivially with MIG partitioning and debugging tooling, since observability is precisely what the threat model restricts [5][9].

**Form-factor fragmentation.** The SXM (700 W, HBM3, NVLink) and PCIe (350 W, HBM2e, 114 SMs) variants differ enough in bandwidth and SM count that performance-portable code must be tuned per form factor — a tax on the "one architecture" story [3][6].

**Generational horizon.** Blackwell (B200) has since introduced FP4/FP6 support, fifth-generation NVLink, and chiplet packaging [9]; Hopper remains the production workhorse, but its FP8 ceiling is now a floor for the next generation.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The Hopper H100 is best understood not as a faster A100 but as a *workload-targeted* redesign: fourth-generation Tensor Cores and the Transformer Engine for the transformer era, DPX instructions for the dynamic-programming workloads that CPUs used to own, and confidential computing for the cloud-tenancy model that AI economics now requires. Its headline figures — 3,958 TFLOPS FP8, 9× training speedups, 30× inference gains, 40× DPX acceleration — decompose cleanly under roofline analysis into datapath, policy, and system contributions that multiply rather than add. The architecture's limitations — 700 W power, FP8 accuracy cliffs on small models, the narrowness of DPX, and the operational friction of confidential computing — are the honest price of specialization.
