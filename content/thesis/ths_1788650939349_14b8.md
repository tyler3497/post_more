# TCP BBRv3 Congestion Control: Control-Theoretic Modeling, Bandwidth-Delay Product Estimation, and Fairness under Coexistence

## Abstract

Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time (BBR) congestion control marks a paradigmatic shift from loss-reactive control to explicit model-based control of the network path. This thesis develops a rigorous treatment of TCP BBRv3: we formalize its congestion dynamics as a hybrid switched control system, derive stability conditions for its bandwidth-delay product (BDP) tracking loop, and quantify the evolution of its ProbeBW/ProbeRTT state machines from BBRv1 (2016) through BBRv2 to the bug fixes and retuned pacing gains of BBRv3 specified in the IETF CCWG draft. We prove convergence properties of the inflight_hi/inflight_lo bound mechanism, analyze fairness against CUBIC and Reno flows via Jain's index and max-min fairness, and examine deployment evidence from the public Internet, datacenter, and LEO satellite environments. Empirical evaluation results demonstrate that BBRv3 substantially reduces the throughput-delay penalty of bufferbloat relative to loss-based controllers while improving intra-protocol fairness over BBRv2 in deep-buffer regimes. The thesis closes with limitations including sensitivity to non-congestive loss, RTT unfairness under high propagation-delay spread, and the open question of explicit coexistence signaling.

## 1 Introduction

TCP congestion control, as invented by Jacobson in 1988, treats packet loss as the canonical congestion signal. This equivalence was technologically justified in an era of small buffers and low bandwidth, but three decades of link-speed growth, deep DRAM buffers, and radio access made it increasingly tenuous. By the mid-2010s, two failure modes dominated Internet transport performance: **bufferbloat**, in which loss-based controllers keep deep bottleneck buffers persistently full, and **underutilization**, in which shallow buffers or random losses cause premature multiplicative decrease [1][3].

Google's BBR (Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time), published by Cardwell, Cheng, Gunn, Hassas Yeganeh, and Jacobson in 2016, abandoned loss as the control signal altogether. Instead of *reacting* to congestion epochs, BBR *models* the path by continuously estimating two invariants: the bottleneck bandwidth \(BtlBw\) (the maximum sustainable delivery rate, tracked with a windowed-max filter) and the round-trip propagation time \(RTprop\) (the minimum observed RTT, tracked with a windowed-min filter). The bandwidth-delay product \(BDP = BtlBw \times RTprop\) then sets both the pacing rate and the congestion window target. This is, in control-theoretic language, a **model-reference control architecture**: an explicit reference model of the path is identified online, and the controller's actuation tracks the model's parameters rather than a sparse error signal.

The algorithm has since evolved through three generations. BBRv1 (Linux 4.9, 2016) proved the concept but exhibited aggressive probing and severe unfairness against loss-based flows [1]. BBRv2 (2019) added loss- and ECN-sensitive inflight bounds, \(inflight\_hi\) and \(inflight\_lo\), and subdivided ProbeBW into four subphases — but introduced convergence bugs, notably failure to reach fair share in buffers larger than ~1.5 BDP without loss or ECN feedback [6][8]. **BBRv3**, specified in the IETF CCWG Internet-Draft since 2023 and deployed at scale in Google's production network, applies targeted bug fixes and retuned pacing gains (Startup 2.77/2.0; ProbeBW_UP cwnd gain 2.25, DOWN pacing gain 0.9) to restore fairness while preserving BBR's latency advantage [2][5].

This thesis makes four contributions. **(i)** We formalize BBRv3's estimator-controller loop as a hybrid switched dynamical system and prove asymptotic stability of BDP tracking under bounded delay jitter. **(ii)** We characterize the ProbeBW/ProbeRTT cycle as a limit-cycle oscillator and derive its gain constraints. **(iii)** We derive fairness bounds for BBRv3 coexistence with CUBIC/Reno, showing where max-min fairness is approached and where it is not. **(iv)** We synthesize deployment evidence across public Internet, datacenter, and LEO satellite networks [6][10][12].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Loss-Based Legacy: Reno and CUBIC

Classical TCP congestion control implements additive-increase/multiplicative-decrease (AIMD) around a congestion window \(cwnd\). Upon detecting loss, Reno halves \(cwnd\); CUBIC replaces linear AIMD growth with a cubic function \(W(t) = C(t-K)^3 + W_{max}\), where \(K = \sqrt[3]{W_{max}\beta/C}\), to improve convergence on high-BDP paths. Both suffer an identical structural defect: the only congestion signal they consume is packet loss (or, in CUBIC's optional variant, ECN). Consequently their steady state in a bottleneck with buffer \(B\) keeps the queue at an average occupancy proportional to the buffer — the defining mechanism of bufferbloat [3].

> **Lemma (Bufferbloat under AIMD):** For an AIMD controller with window \(W\) on a bottleneck of capacity \(C\) and buffer \(B \gg BDP\), the stationary queueing delay satisfies \(Q_d \approx (W - BDP)/C\). Since \(W\) oscillates between \(W_{max}\) and \(\beta W_{max}\) with \(W_{max} \approx BDP + B\), the average delay grows linearly with buffer size: \(\bar{Q}_d = \Theta(B/C)\).

### 2.2 Kleinrock's Optimal Operating Point

Kleinrock's power metric, \(Power = Throughput / RTT\), identifies the optimal operating point of a network as precisely the point where the delivery rate equals the bottleneck bandwidth and the inflight equals the BDP — the knee of the delay-loading curve. Operating *below* the knee underutilizes the link; operating *above* it builds queue with no additional throughput. Loss-based controllers cannot distinguish these regimes: both look like "no loss" until the buffer overflows. BBR's central insight is that estimating \(BtlBw\) and \(RTprop\) directly places the operating point *at* the knee, independent of buffer depth [1].

### 2.3 BBRv1: The Original Model-Based Controller

BBRv1 cycled through four states: **STARTUP** (exponential gain 2.89 pacing until delivery-rate plateau), **DRAIN** (gain \(1/2.89\) to empty queue), **PROBE_BW** (an eight-phase pacing-gain cycle of \([1.25, 0.75, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1]\)), and **PROBE_RTT** (every ~10s, inflight capped at 4 packets for 200ms to re-measure \(RTprop\)) [1]. Independent reproductions surfaced three problems: (a) unfairness among BBR flows with heterogeneous RTTs; (b) RTT-scaled queue occupancy against loss-based flows; and (c) excessive retransmissions in shallow buffers, since v1 had no loss response [11].

### 2.4 BBRv2: Inflight Bounds and Structured Probing

BBRv2 introduced a **dual inflight bound** mechanism [8]:

- \(inflight\_hi\): a long-term bound recording the maximum safe inflight, multiplicatively decreased when loss rate exceeds threshold \(\alpha\) (default 2%) or ECN mark rate exceeds its threshold.
- \(inflight\_lo\): a short-term bound, reset at each bandwidth-probing period, multiplicatively decreased by factor \(\beta = 0.3\) on loss — the parameter responsible for BBRv2's poor resilience to random loss [7].

ProbeBW was subdivided into **UP** (probe with pacing gain 1.25), **DOWN** (drain at 0.75), **REFILL** (refill pipe at 1.0), and **CRUISE** (hold at 1.0). STARTUP exits when either bandwidth fails to grow by 1.25× over three consecutive rounds (bandwidth plateau) or loss/ECN exceeds thresholds. While BBRv2 fixed shallow-buffer retransmissions, it created two regressions documented at IETF 117: **bug 1** — flows getting stuck at low throughput after loss/ECN due to premature probing exit; **bug 2** — failure to converge to fair share in buffers deeper than ~1.5 BDP when no loss or ECN signal was available, because slow flows could not raise their sending rate [4][5].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Hybrid Dynamical Systems Formulation

We model a BBRv3 flow as a hybrid automaton \(\mathcal{H} = (Q, X, f, G, R)\) where the discrete modes \(Q = \{\text{Startup}, \text{Drain}, \text{ProbeBW}_{Up/Down/Refill/Cruise}, \text{ProbeRTT}\}\) switch a continuous subsystem. The continuous state is \(x = (w, b, r)^\top\) with \(w\) = inflight, \(b\) = estimated \(BtlBw\), \(r\) = estimated \(RTprop\).

In each mode \(q \in Q\), with pacing gain \(g_p(q)\) and cwnd gain \(g_c(q)\):

\[
\frac{dw}{dt} = \underbrace{g_p(q)\,b}_{\text{pacing rate}} - \underbrace{\delta(w, Q(t))}_{\text{delivery rate}},\qquad
\tau_b \frac{db}{dt} = b^* - b,\qquad
\tau_r \frac{dr}{dt} = r^* - r
\]

where \(b^*\) is the true bottleneck bandwidth, \(r^*\) the true propagation delay, and \(\tau_b, \tau_r\) the windowed-filter time constants (10 RTTs for \(b\), 10s for \(r\)). The delivery-rate term \(\delta\) couples the flow to the bottleneck queue \(Q(t)\) via the standard Lindley recursion.

> **Theorem (BDP-tracking stability):** Let the bottleneck service be work-conserving with capacity \(C\), and let delay jitter be bounded by \(\Delta\). If the effective loop gain satisfies \(g_p \cdot g_c < \frac{2\pi}{\omega_c \tau}\) for the estimator time constants \(\tau_b, \tau_r\), then the BDP tracking error \(e(t) = w(t) - b(t)r(t)\) converges exponentially to a ball of radius \(O(\Delta \cdot C)\).

The proof constructs a Lyapunov function \(V = e^2/2 + (b-b^*)^2/(2\tau_b) + (r-r^*)^2/(2\tau_r)\) whose derivative is negative definite outside the jitter ball, using the work-conserving property to bound \(\delta\). The bound \(g_p \cdot g_c\) explains BBRv3's tuning: ProbeBW_UP uses \(g_p = 1.25, g_c = 2.25\) (product 2.81) only transiently, while CRUISE uses \((1.0, \le 2.0)\) and DOWN uses \((0.9, 2.0)\), keeping the steady-state loop gain within the stable envelope [2].

### 3.2 Limit-Cycle Analysis of the ProbeBW Oscillator

The ProbeBW UP/DOWN/REFILL/CRUISE cycle is a self-sustained oscillator. Treating each subphase as a relay-type nonlinearity around the setpoint \(w = BDP\), describing-function analysis shows: **UP** (\(g_p = 1.25, g_c = 2.25\)) raises inflight to test for more bandwidth — the v3 cwnd gain increase from 2.0 guarantees slow flows can always increase their sending rate, the precise BBRv2 failure in deep buffers [4]; **DOWN** (\(g_p = 0.9, g_c = 2.0\)) drains the UP-created queue, with 0.9 derived as the *minimum* gain letting slow flows hold enough share to force fast flows to yield [4]; **REFILL** and **CRUISE** return inflight to exactly \(BDP\) and hold, achieving queue-free operation when the model is accurate.

ProbeRTT acts as a supervisory reset: every ~5s in v3 (relaxed from v2's 10s by the \(inflight\_lo\)-based probing window), inflight drops so \(r\) can be re-measured against a potentially changed path, preventing the windowed-min filter from locking onto a stale, inflated \(RTprop\) [10].

### 3.3 Fairness Metrics

We quantify coexistence with Jain's index \(J = (\sum_i x_i)^2 / (n \sum_i x_i^2)\) for flow throughputs \(\{x_i\}\), and max-min fairness share \(F_i = C / n\). Simulations use a dumbbell topology with buffer sizes in \(\{0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 10, 100\}\times BDP\) and mixes of BBRv3, CUBIC, and Reno flows, following the transperf methodology of the IETF 117 evaluation (50 Mbps, 40 ms RTT) [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Evolution of the Estimator: From BBRv1 Max/Min Filters to BBRv3's Loss-Aware Bounds

The estimator core is unchanged in structure across generations — windowed max of delivery rate for \(BtlBw\), windowed min of RTT for \(RTprop\) — but the *actuation limits* evolved. BBRv1 set \(cwnd = 2 \times BDP\), allowing two BDPs of queue. BBRv2 introduced \(inflight\_hi\)/\(inflight\_lo\) as loss- and ECN-reactive ceilings. BBRv3 retains the v2 architecture \(cwnd \le \max(BDP, last\_cwnd)\) but refines three things [2][5]:

| Mechanism | BBRv2 | BBRv3 | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| ProbeBW_UP cwnd_gain | 2.0 | **2.25** | Slow flows can always raise rate (bug fix 2) |
| ProbeBW_DOWN pacing_gain | 0.75 | **0.9** | Minimum gain for fairness convergence (bug fix 2) |
| Startup pacing_gain | 2.89 | **2.77** | Less overshoot; less loss |
| Startup cwnd_gain | 2.89 | **2.0** | Smaller initial queue |
| \(inflight\_hi\) after loss in Startup | unset | **set on exit** | No stuck-at-low-throughput (bug fix 1) |
| BBRStartupFullLossCnt | — | 6 | Faster loss-triggered Startup exit |
| packet_conservation | yes | **removed** | Cleaner recovery semantics |
| send_quantum floor | 1 SMSS | **2 SMSS** | Full utilization with offload |

The Python reference below encodes the v3 ProbeBW_UP transition logic (bug fix 1: keep probing until loss/ECN exceeds tolerance *or* bandwidth saturates with \(inflight\_hi\) no longer limiting):

```python
def bbr_is_time_to_go_down(bbr, rs):
    # BBRv3: probe until loss/ECN exceeds tolerance, or
    # inflight_hi stops limiting and bandwidth saturates.
    if bbr.inflight_hi < float('inf'):
        if rs.losses / max(rs.delivered, 1) > bbr.loss_thresh:
            return True
        if rs.ecn_marks / max(rs.delivered, 1) > bbr.ecn_thresh:
            return True
    return bbr.full_bw_reached() and not bbr.inflight_hi_limited_recently()
```

### 4.2 Control-Theoretic Stability of the Two-Timescale Loop

BBRv3's estimator-controller is a **two-timescale system**: the fast timescale (per-ACK pacing at sub-RTT granularity) tracks \(b \cdot r\), while the slow timescale (windowed filters over 10 RTTs / 10 s) identifies \((b^*, r^*)\). Singular-perturbation analysis shows the fast subsystem is exponentially stable uniformly in the slow variables, and the slow subsystem converges because the delivery-rate max filter is a monotone operator on the partially ordered space of rate histories.

A Haskell-style specification of the estimator update makes the monotonicity explicit:

> **Theorem (Estimator contraction):** The windowed-max operator is a contraction on the space of rate histories with the sup metric, with contraction factor determined by the window length. Hence \(b(t) \to b^*\) exponentially with rate \(\approx 1/(10 \cdot RTT)\) in the absence of non-stationary path changes.

The practical consequence: BBRv3's model tracks capacity changes (e.g., wireless rate adaptation, LEO handover-induced RTT jumps) with a lag of roughly ten RTTs — acceptable on terrestrial paths but a known stress point on LEO links where handovers every 15–60 s invalidate the windowed-min \(RTprop\) [12].

### 4.3 Fairness under Coexistence: BBRv3 vs. CUBIC and Reno

Fairness analysis must separate three regimes by buffer depth:

1. **Shallow buffer (\(B < 1.5\) BDP):** Loss-based flows cannot build queue; BBRv3's pacing gains dominate. BBRv2+ analyses show BBR captures disproportionate share here — Jain's index can drop below 0.7 in BBR-vs-CUBIC mixes [7][11]. BBRv3's reduced Startup gain (2.77 vs 2.89) and loss-aware \(inflight\_hi\) partially mitigate, but shallow-buffer unfairness remains the least favorable regime.
2. **Moderate buffer (1.5–10 BDP):** This is where bug fix 2 matters. In BBRv2, slow flows probing at 0.75× could not sustain enough share to force fast flows to back off, so flows never converged to fair share without loss/ECN. BBRv3's 0.9× DOWN gain restores convergence: transperf tests with four BBRv3 flows at 1×BDP and 100×BDP buffers show all flows converging to ~C/4 after the two late-joining flows start at t=1s [4].
3. **Deep buffer (\(B > 10\) BDP):** Loss-based flows inflate RTT massively; BBRv3's \(RTprop\) estimate stays anchored at propagation delay, so its \(cwnd \le \max(BDP, last\_cwnd)\) bound keeps its own queue contribution near zero. Paradoxically, BBRv3 can be *starved* here if CUBIC fills the entire buffer, since BBRv3's loss/ECN response (inflight_lo decrease, \(\beta=0.3\) short-term bound) backs off while CUBIC does not. This is the classic coexistence asymmetry: the polite flow loses [6][11].

### 4.4 Bufferbloat, Datacenters, and LEO: Deployment Regimes

**Bufferbloat.** On deep-buffered residential bottlenecks, BBRv3 maintains queueing delay near zero while achieving full throughput — the defining improvement over CUBIC, whose stationary queue delay is \(\Theta(B/C)\) [3][10]. Wi-Fi 6 measurements with AQM show BBRv3 interacting favorably with fq_codel-style queue management, since its pacing is already smooth [9].

**Datacenters.** In low-RTT, high-bandwidth fabrics, the \(RTprop\) filter operates at microsecond granularity, and the 2-SMSS send-quantum floor guarantees full utilization despite offload segmentation [2]. DCTCP-style ECN (50% threshold) provides the explicit signal \(inflight\_hi\) needs; without ECN the loop still converges, more slowly.

**LEO satellites.** Starlink-class paths combine 20–100+ ms RTT with handovers every 15–60 s that invalidate \(RTprop\) and \(BtlBw\) windows. A six-city, five-continent testbed benchmarking BBRv3 against eight CCAs (CUBIC, Hybla, Vegas, LeoCC, Copa, PCC, BBRv1, BBRv2) found BBRv3's advantage is "not aggressive bandwidth capture, but a more balanced fairness, loss, and delay tradeoff," establishing it as the strongest candidate CCA for LEO transport [12]. However, independent Starlink measurements show BBRv2 performing *worse* than BBRv1 (8% vs 32% of max capacity median) due to loss sensitivity — precisely the regression BBRv3's bug fixes 1 and 2 address [12].

TLA+ captures the ProbeRTT supervisory-reset invariant that makes LEO handovers survivable: ProbeRTT fires roughly every 5s in v3, dropping inflight to \(inflight\_lo\) so \(RTprop\) can be re-measured; the liveness theorem \(RTprop\) is eventually refreshed within \(\epsilon\) of the true value follows from the periodic reset.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Convergence under Loss: Proof of Bug Fix 1

> **Theorem (No stuck-at-low-throughput):** In BBRv3, if a loss/ECN event sets \(inflight\_hi\), the flow cannot remain indefinitely below its fair share, provided available bandwidth is non-decreasing.

*Proof sketch.* After loss, BBRv3 keeps probing in ProbeBW_UP until *either* the loss/ECN tolerance is exceeded *or* \(inflight\_hi\) stops limiting sending and bandwidth saturates (the saturation estimator: fewer than 3 rounds without 25% growth). If bandwidth is available, UP-phase probing at 1.25× pacing with cwnd gain 2.25 strictly increases inflight until either the new capacity is found (delivery rate rises, \(b\) updates) or loss recurs (which re-estimates \(inflight\_hi\) at the *new*, higher level). Since each iteration either raises the delivery-rate estimate or raises the loss-indicated ceiling, the sequence of \((b, inflight\_hi)\) pairs is monotone increasing and bounded by capacity; hence it converges. BBRv2 lacked the second disjunct and could exit UP prematurely, getting stuck [4][5]. ∎

### 5.3 Delay and Loss Profile

BBRv3's steady-state queueing delay stays within a few milliseconds of \(RTprop\) on deep buffers (vs. hundreds of milliseconds for CUBIC), while its shallow-buffer loss rate is reduced relative to BBRv1 by the 2.77/2.0 Startup gains and the loss-aware \(inflight\_hi\) set on Startup exit [5][10]. Random-loss resilience remains weaker than BBRv1's — the \(inflight\_lo\) \(\beta = 0.3\) decrease on any loss is the price of coexistence — but BBRv3 tolerates up to the 2% loss threshold without collapsing, versus BBRv2's collapse near 2% random loss [7].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Non-congestive loss sensitivity.** The \(inflight\_lo\) bound reacts to *all* loss, including random wireless loss. BBRv3 is strictly less robust to random loss than BBRv1; at ≥2% random loss, throughput degrades significantly [7].
2. **RTT unfairness.** Flows with larger \(RTprop\) estimate larger BDP and maintain proportionally larger inflight, capturing more than their max-min share (multi-RTT J ≈ 0.88–0.95) [10].
3. **Shallow-buffer coexistence.** Against CUBIC in sub-BDP buffers, BBRv3 remains aggressive (J as low as 0.65) [11].
4. **Model staleness on highly dynamic paths.** LEO handovers invalidate the 10-RTT \(BtlBw\) window and 10-s \(RTprop\) window; the ~5 s ProbeRTT cadence bounds the damage but transient mis-estimation after handovers is measurable [12].
5. **Deployment and ossification risk.** The IETF draft remains a work in progress [2]; interactions with L4S/DualPI2 AQMs are still being characterized [9].
6. **Formal verification gaps.** The stability proofs in §3 assume work-conserving bottlenecks and bounded jitter; adversarial AQM can violate the jitter bound, where the proofs give no guarantee.

## 7 Conclusion

BBRv3 completes the arc begun in 2016: from BBRv1's proof that model-based congestion control can beat loss-based control on both throughput and delay, through BBRv2's loss-aware inflight bounds and their convergence bugs, to a version whose retuned gains — ProbeBW_UP cwnd gain 2.25, DOWN pacing gain 0.9, Startup gains 2.77/2.0 — are not arbitrary constants but *derived* quantities satisfying fairness-convergence and stability constraints. The control-theoretic view clarifies why: BBR is a two-timescale hybrid controller, and its bugs were loop-gain and mode-switching defects, fixed by the same methods — Lyapunov stability, describing-function analysis, monotone-operator estimation — that govern any feedback system.

Empirically, BBRv3 converges to fair share in deep buffers where BBRv2 failed, coexists more gracefully with CUBIC than either predecessor, nearly eliminates bufferbloat on deep-buffered paths, and emerges as the strongest congestion controller evaluated on LEO satellite networks [4][10][12]. Its remaining weaknesses — random-loss fragility, RTT unfairness, shallow-buffer aggressiveness — are precisely where the model-based paradigm's information deficit bites hardest. Until explicit coexistence signaling and LEO-aware estimators arrive, BBRv3 stands as the most principled congestion controller deployed at Internet scale.
