# The Neural Basis of Decision-Making: Drift-Diffusion Dynamics, Evidence Accumulation, and Dopaminergic Reward Prediction Errors

## Abstract

The brain must transform noisy, momentary sensory signals into categorical commitments and must learn, over trials, which commitments pay off. Two computational traditions have formalized these problems. The *drift-diffusion model* (DDM) describes how evidence accumulates over time toward decision bounds, jointly predicting the full distributions of choices and reaction times with a small set of interpretable parameters [1][2]. *Temporal-difference* (TD) learning describes how reward prediction errors update value estimates, and midbrain dopamine neurons appear to broadcast a physiological instantiation of the TD error [5][7]. Single-unit recordings in primate parietal and frontal cortex have revealed neural correlates of evidence accumulation during perceptual choices [3], while basal ganglia circuits implement the threshold mechanism that commits a choice [8]. This thesis synthesizes these traditions into a unified accumulate-and-learn framework: perceptual decisions are bounded accumulation processes whose bounds, biases, and learning rates are themselves tuned by dopaminergic prediction errors. We derive the core mathematics, map parameters to neural substrates, review decisive empirical results, and identify where the framework breaks down, including confidence, urgency, and the limits of the TD-dopamine equivalence.

## 1 Intro

Decision-making is the brain's fundamental currency. Every voluntary act — judging motion direction, choosing between gambles — requires converting graded, noisy signals into discrete, committed outcomes. For much of the twentieth century, psychology treated choice and reaction time as separate dependent variables. The modern synthesis, now dominant in computational neuroscience, treats them as two views of a single latent process: *sequential sampling*, in which momentary evidence is integrated over time until an internal decision variable crosses a threshold and triggers a response [1][2].

Two intellectual lineages converge on this idea. The first comes from mathematical statistics: Abraham Wald's sequential probability ratio test (SPRT), which proved that accumulating log-likelihood ratios until fixed bounds achieves optimal efficiency for binary decisions [4]. The second comes from systems neuroscience: recordings in the 1990s showing that neurons in parietal cortex ramp their firing rates during perceptual decisions in a manner strikingly consistent with an accumulator [3].

Parallel to this, a second tradition emerged from machine learning. Temporal-difference methods solve the *credit assignment* problem — how to learn from delayed reward — by propagating a reward prediction error backward through time [5]. The startling discovery that midbrain dopamine neurons encode precisely such an error signal transformed TD learning from an algorithm into a candidate neural mechanism [5][6][7].

These traditions are usually taught as separate chapters: decision models for *within-trial* dynamics, reinforcement learning for *across-trial* adaptation. This thesis argues they are facets of a single architecture. The decision bound is not a static constant; it is a control parameter adjusted by basal ganglia circuitry under dopaminergic modulation [8]. Confidence — the graded belief accompanying a choice — emerges from the same accumulated evidence that drives the decision itself [9]. And the dopamine signal, on closer inspection, encodes not just a scalar TD error but a *distribution* of predictions, suggesting the brain hedges its bets in ways classical TD theory never anticipated.

The remainder of this thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the historical and mathematical background of sequential sampling and reinforcement learning. Section 3 lays out the formal methodology: the DDM as a stochastic differential equation, its normative foundations, and the TD equations governing dopamine-mediated learning. Section 4 deep-dives into four topics: the neural implementation of accumulation, the SPRT optimality connection, the basal ganglia threshold, and the integration of value into perceptual decisions. Section 5 reviews decisive empirical and theoretical results. Section 6 confronts limitations, and Section 7 concludes with a synthesis and open directions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Random Walks to Diffusion

Early models of choice reaction time treated decisions as discrete random walks: each unit of time adds one step toward one of two absorbing boundaries (Laming, 1968; Link & Heath, 1975). Roger Ratcliff's 1978 theory of memory retrieval generalized this to continuous time, introducing the *drift-diffusion model*: a Wiener process with drift [1]. The move from discrete steps to continuous diffusion was conceptually significant — it meant the model could be written as a stochastic differential equation, placing decision-making inside the analytical machinery of stochastic processes.

The canonical form is:

$$dx_t = v\,dt + s\,dW_t, \quad x_0 = z, \quad 0 < z < a$$

where the decision variable $x_t$ evolves with drift rate $v$ (the quality of evidence) and diffusion coefficient $s$ (noise), starting at bias $z$, until it first hits either the upper boundary $a$ or the lower boundary $0$. The observed reaction time is the sum of the decision time (first-passage time) and a non-decision component $T_{er}$ capturing sensory encoding and motor execution.

The model's empirical power comes from a stringent prediction: the same four parameters must simultaneously fit the *entire joint distribution* of choices and reaction times — including error reaction times, which are not free data but constrained by the model's structure. Across decades, the DDM has fit perceptual discrimination, lexical decision, recognition memory, and value-based choice with remarkable quantitative accuracy [1][2].

### 2.2 The TD Tradition

Reinforcement learning faces a different problem: learning from *delayed* reward. The temporal-difference error, formalized by Sutton and Barto and linked to dopamine by Schultz, Dayan, and Montague [5], is:

$$\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$$

where $r_t$ is immediate reward, $V$ is the estimated value function, and $\gamma$ discounts the future. When a reward is unexpected, $\delta_t > 0$ and dopamine neurons burst; when an expected reward is omitted, $\delta_t < 0$ and they pause; when reward is fully predicted, they are silent — responding instead to the earliest predictive cue [5][6]. Bayer and Glimcher (2005) demonstrated that this relationship is *quantitative*: firing rate scales linearly with the magnitude of the prediction error [7].

The elegance of this scheme is its self-consistency. The same signal that *teaches* (by driving value updates) is generated from the *predictions being taught*, closing the loop without any external supervisor.

### 2.3 Neural Accumulators

The bridge from mathematics to neurons was built in the random-dot motion paradigm. Monkeys viewed a field of coherently moving dots and reported direction with a saccade. Shadlen and Newsome showed that neurons in the lateral intraparietal area (LIP) ramp their firing rates at a slope proportional to motion coherence, and the saccade is triggered when activity reaches a stereotyped level [3]. The ramp is not a clock: at fixed coherence, trial-to-trial variability in the *time* to threshold predicts the monkey's reaction time. Accumulation, it seemed, was not a metaphor but a firing-rate dynamics.

Subsequent work extended the accumulator story to the frontal eye fields, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and the superior colliculus, while the basal ganglia emerged as the locus of the threshold itself [8]: accumulation in cortical sensorimotor association areas, commitment in subcortical loops [2].

| DDM parameter | Psychological meaning | Candidate neural substrate |
|---|---|---|
| Drift rate $v$ | Quality/speed of evidence | MT/MST sensory drive to LIP |
| Boundary separation $a$ | Caution / speed–accuracy tradeoff | Basal ganglia output (STN, GPi) |
| Starting point $z$ | Prior bias / expectation | Pre-stimulus baseline in LIP, dlPFC |
| Non-decision time $T_{er}$ | Encoding + motor execution | Visual latencies, saccade preparation |
| Diffusion noise $s$ | Moment-to-moment variability | Poisson-like neural variability |

This mapping is the spine of the modern neurocomputational account of decision-making [2][8].
## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The DDM as a Stochastic Process

We formalize the decision variable $x_t$ as a diffusion process with constant drift:

$$dx_t = v\,dt + s\,dW_t, \qquad x_0 = z \in (0, a)$$

The decision time $T_d$ is the *first-passage time* $T_d = \inf\{t > 0 : x_t \notin (0,a)\}$, and the choice is determined by which boundary is hit. Observed reaction time is $RT = T_d + T_{er}$.

> Theorem: For the two-boundary diffusion process above, the probability of hitting the upper boundary (choice accuracy when drift points upward) is $P_{upper} = \frac{1 - e^{-2vz/s^2}}{1 - e^{-2va/s^2}}$, and the expected decision time admits a closed form in $v, a, z, s$. These expressions jointly constrain choices and reaction times with no additional free parameters.

Fitting proceeds by maximum likelihood over the *joint* density of choice and RT, or by hierarchical Bayesian methods that pool subjects while estimating individual parameters [2]. A critical methodological point: the model is falsifiable precisely because error RTs are *predicted*, not fit — slow errors require across-trial variability in drift ($\eta$), fast errors require variability in starting point ($s_z$).

### 3.2 The TD Update and Dopamine

On the learning side, the agent maintains value estimates updated by:

$$V(s_t) \leftarrow V(s_t) + \alpha\,\delta_t, \qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$$

Methodologically, the dopamine hypothesis makes three testable predictions, each confirmed in single-unit primate recordings [5][6][7]:

1. **Unexpected reward** elicits a phasic burst ($\delta_t > 0$).
2. **Omitted expected reward** elicits a phasic pause ($\delta_t < 0$).
3. **Fully predicted reward** elicits no response; the burst *transfers* to the earliest predictive cue.

The quantitative refinement matters: Bayer and Glimcher showed dopamine firing is not binary but grades continuously with the magnitude of the prediction error [7], matching TD theory's continuous-valued $\delta_t$.

### 3.3 Simulation Protocol

Our methodological exposition couples both traditions in simulation. The Python implementation below generates DDM sample paths and extracts choice and RT statistics:

```python
import numpy as np

def simulate_ddm(v=0.25, a=1.0, z=0.5, ter=0.30, s=0.10,
                 dt=0.001, n_trials=5000, rng=None):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(rng)
    choices, rts = [], []
    for _ in range(n_trials):
        # across-trial variability: the workhorse of error-RT fitting
        vi = rng.normal(v, 0.05)
        zi = rng.uniform(z - 0.03, z + 0.03)
        x, t = zi, 0.0
        while True:
            x += vi * dt + s * np.sqrt(dt) * rng.standard_normal()
            t += dt
            if x >= a:
                choices.append(1); rts.append(t + ter); break
            if x <= 0:
                choices.append(0); rts.append(t + ter); break
    return np.array(choices), np.array(rts)

ch, rt = simulate_ddm()
print(f"accuracy={ch.mean():.3f}  mean_rt={rt.mean():.3f}s")
```

And a companion TD learner demonstrates how prediction errors shrink as a cue becomes predictive — the computational analogue of the dopamine response transfer observed by Schultz and colleagues [5]:

```python
def td_pavlovian(gamma=0.95, alpha=0.10, episodes=60):
    V = {"cue": 0.0, "reward_state": 0.0}
    errors = []
    for ep in range(episodes):
        # cue -> reward state -> reward of 1.0
        d1 = 0 + gamma * V["reward_state"] - V["cue"]
        d2 = 1.0 + gamma * 0 - V["reward_state"]
        V["cue"] += alpha * d1
        V["reward_state"] += alpha * d2
        errors.append((d1, d2))
    return V, errors

V, errs = td_pavlovian()
print(f"learned V(cue)={V['cue']:.3f}; early d_reward={errs[0][1]:.2f}, late d_reward={errs[-1][1]:.2f}")
```

The first script reproduces the canonical DDM signatures — right-skewed RT distributions, faster mean RT at higher drift. The second reproduces the signature dopamine transfer: early in training the error fires at the *reward*, late in training it fires at the *cue* [5][6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Neural Implementation of Accumulation

Where, exactly, does the drift-diffusion computation live? The strongest evidence comes from the random-dot motion task in macaque. Area MT supplies direction-selective sensory evidence; LIP neurons integrate it, ramping toward a threshold that triggers the saccadic report [3]. Three properties license the accumulator interpretation:

- **Slope scales with stimulus strength**: motion coherence linearly modulates ramp slope, mirroring the DDM drift rate.
- **Threshold is stereotyped**: activity at saccade initiation is approximately constant across coherences, mirroring fixed boundaries.
- **Time-to-threshold predicts RT**: trial-to-trial variability in ramp duration predicts the monkey's reaction time, trial by trial.

Causal evidence followed: microstimulation of LIP biases choices toward the stimulated field, and inactivation slows contralateral decisions, consistent with perturbing an accumulator rather than a motor executor [2][3]. The accumulator is thus best understood as *distributed*: sensory drive in MT/MST, integration in LIP and frontal eye fields, with the decision variable readable as a population firing rate.

### 4.2 Optimality: The DDM as an SPRT

Bogacz, Brown, Moehlis, Holmes, and Cohen (2006) proved that for stationary two-alternative choice, the DDM implements Wald's *sequential probability ratio test* — and the SPRT minimizes expected decision time at any given error rate [4]. In other words, bounded accumulation is the *normatively correct* algorithm for trading speed against accuracy.

The intuition is compact.

> Theorem: Among all sequential tests of two simple hypotheses with bounded error probabilities, the SPRT — equivalently, the drift-diffusion process with absorbing boundaries — minimizes expected sample size. Bounded evidence accumulation is Bayes-optimal for speeded binary decisions.

This optimality result explains the DDM's uncanny empirical success: the brain may implement diffusion dynamics precisely because they are the optimal solution to the problem the organism faces [2][4].

This predicts — and experiments confirm — that disrupting STN activity (by lesion, inactivation, or deep-brain stimulation) produces impulsive, fast-but-inaccurate decisions: the threshold collapses [2][8].

The normative reading is equally sharp.

### 4.4 Value Enters the Accumulator

Perceptual decisions are rarely value-neutral.

Consider the unified picture.

- **$z$ (bias)** shifts with learned prior probability of reward.
- **$a$ (bound)** adjusts to the opportunity cost of deliberation.
- **$v$ (drift)** scales with learned attention to informative features.

The most striking recent refinement is *distributional* TD: Dabney and colleagues showed that dopamine neurons do not encode a single expected value but a *distribution* of possible values, with individual neurons specializing in optimistic or pessimistic quantiles.

### 4.5 Confidence as a Readout of the Same Evidence

Kiani and Shadlen (2009) demonstrated that LIP neurons carry not only the decision but also *confidence*: the probability the decision is correct, given the accumulated evidence and elapsed time [9].

## 5 Empirical and Theoretical Results

The accumulate-and-learn framework earns its keep through predictions that were confirmed only after being derived.

**1. The SPRT signature in behavior.** If the brain implements bounded accumulation, then choice accuracy and RT distributions must co-vary with stimulus strength in exactly the way the first-passage-time densities dictate: higher coherence yields faster *and* more accurate responses, with a specific quantitative relationship between the two. Ratcliff and colleagues verified this across dozens of paradigms [1]; the joint fits leave essentially no residual degrees of freedom — error RTs fall where the model says they must.

**2. Ramp-to-threshold in LIP.** Shadlen and Newsome's prediction — that a neural integrator's firing rate should ramp with slope proportional to evidence strength and trigger responses at a fixed threshold — was confirmed in area LIP during random-dot motion discrimination [3]. The quantitative match between ramp slope and DDM drift rate remains one of computational neuroscience's signature results.

**3. The dopamine transfer.** Schultz, Dayan, and Montague predicted from TD theory that the dopamine response should *transfer* from unexpected reward to the earliest predictive cue as learning proceeds [5]. Single-unit recordings confirmed the transfer with striking fidelity, including the counterintuitive pause on reward omission — a negative prediction error with no physical stimulus at all [6][7]. Bayer and Glimcher further showed the response scales *linearly* with prediction-error magnitude, licensing the interpretation of dopamine firing as a quantitative $\delta_t$ [7].

**4. The basal ganglia bound.** Lo and Wang's circuit model predicted that STN drive sets the decision threshold [8]. Subsequent causal manipulations — STN inactivation, deep-brain stimulation, pharmacological perturbation — produce precisely the impulsive, low-threshold behavior the model predicts, linking a DDM parameter to a manipulable circuit element [2][8].

**5. Confidence from the accumulator.** Kiani and Shadlen showed that the same diffusion process generating choices also predicts graded confidence, with neural correlates in LIP tracking the Bayesian posterior over correctness [9]. One latent process, three observables — a result that constrains all competing architectures.

**6. Optimality, not just fit.** The Bogacz et al. (2006) analysis [4] elevates the DDM to a *normative* account: bounded accumulation is the optimal policy for the speed–accuracy tradeoff. This reframes the empirical question from "does the brain approximate diffusion?" to "under what conditions would the brain *deviate* from the optimum — and what do those deviations reveal?"

Together, these results argue that evidence accumulation and TD prediction errors are operating principles of the circuits that implement them, not convenient fictions.

## 6 Limitations

No framework survives contact with the full data unscathed.

**Collapsing and time-varying bounds.** The canonical DDM assumes fixed boundaries, yet under deadline pressure subjects appear to implement *collapsing bounds*. Urgency-gating models, in which a growing urgency signal multiplies the evidence, fit some speed-pressured datasets better, though the neural implementation — bound collapse, gain increase, or both — remains contested.

**Non-stationary drift.** The DDM assumes constant within-trial drift, but attention fluctuates and evidence quality evolves. Leaky competing accumulator models capture recency effects the pure DDM misses: the brain's integrator is imperfect, and that imperfection is itself informative.

**Post-error and post-conflict adjustments.** After errors, subjects slow down and raise thresholds; after conflict, control adjusts drift. These across-trial dynamics require embedding the DDM in a slower control loop — the role our framework assigns to dopaminergic and basal ganglia modulation — but the mapping from $\delta_t$ to specific parameter updates ($a$, $z$, $v$) remains under-constrained empirically.

**The TD–dopamine equivalence is incomplete.** Dopamine responses show features TD theory does not predict: responses to novel non-rewarding stimuli, ramping activity approaching rewards, and the distributional code of Section 4.4. Distributional TD rescues much of this at the cost of a vector-valued error whose learning rules are still being worked out. Moreover, dopamine is *sufficient* to drive learning but not always *necessary* — lesions leave some reward learning intact, implicating parallel systems.

**Beyond binary choice.** The DDM is fundamentally two-alternative. Multi-alternative decisions require race architectures whose optimality properties are murkier and whose neural mappings are less crisp.

**Correlation versus causation.** Much of the neural evidence is correlational: ramps *resemble* accumulation. Perturbation studies strengthen the case but rarely achieve the clean parameter-specific dissociations — pure $v$ versus pure $a$ manipulations — that would constitute a decisive test.

## 7 Conclusion

This thesis has argued for a unified view of decision-making in which *bounded evidence accumulation* governs the within-trial dynamics of choice and *temporal-difference prediction errors* govern the across-trial tuning of the accumulator's parameters. The drift-diffusion model provides the formal core: a stochastic differential equation whose first-passage statistics predict choices, reaction times, and confidence from a single latent process [1][4]. Single-unit physiology locates the integrator in parietal and frontal circuits [3][9], the threshold in cortico–basal ganglia loops [8], and the teaching signal in midbrain dopamine neurons whose phasic activity quantitatively matches the TD error [5][6][7].

Three lessons stand out. **First**, normative analysis matters: the DDM is not just descriptive but optimal for the speed–accuracy tradeoff, which explains its empirical reach and tells us exactly where to look for deviations [4]. **Second**, the parameters are physiological: drift, bound, and bias map onto manipulable circuit elements, so the model makes contact with causal neuroscience rather than floating above it [2][8]. **Third**, learning and deciding are one loop: dopamine-mediated prediction errors adjust the very parameters — bias, caution, and evidence weighting — that the next decision will use, closing the circle from outcome back to deliberation [5][8].

The open frontier is *distributional* and *meta*-level: how the brain represents uncertainty over value, how it arbitrates between competing control systems under uncertainty, and how urgency, attention, and motivation continuously re-parameterize the accumulator in real time. The accumulate-and-learn architecture will not be the final word — collapsing bounds, leaky integration, and multi-alternative races already strain its simplest form. But as a first quantitative bridge from spikes to choices to learning, it remains the most successful synthesis computational neuroscience has produced, and the standard against which successors will be measured.
