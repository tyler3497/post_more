# Byzantine Agreement under Partial Synchrony: PBFT's Three-Phase Commit, HotStuff's Chained Linear Pipeline, and DAG-Structured Mempool Consensus

## Abstract

Partial synchrony is the timing model under which nearly all practical Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus protocols operate, yet its consequences for protocol design are frequently underappreciated. This thesis provides a rigorous, comparative treatment of three generations of partially synchronous Byzantine agreement: Castro and Liskov's PBFT (1999), Yin et al.'s HotStuff (2019), and the modern family of DAG-structured mempool-consensus protocols (DAG-Rider, Narwhal/Tusk, Bullshark, Mysticeti). We formalize the Dwork–Lynch–Stockmeyer partially synchronous model and its global stabilization time (GST) formulation, derive the quorum-intersection arguments that underpin safety at the optimal resilience bound n = 3f + 1, and analyze how each protocol family obtains liveness only after GST. We quantify the communication-complexity trajectory from PBFT's O(n²) three-phase multicast through HotStuff's linear chained pipeline to DAG-BFT's decoupled dissemination and consensus layers. We conclude with open limitations: leader-based DoS surfaces, the optimistic-responsiveness gap in DAG protocols, validator-set management, and the cost of threshold-cryptography pipelines in chained HotStuff variants.

## 1 Introduction

Distributed consensus is the art of achieving agreement among mutually distrusting machines. When failures are merely crashes, the problem is hard; when failures are *Byzantine* — nodes that lie, collude, and behave arbitrarily — it is among the deepest problems in computer science. The network timing model determines what is even possible. Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson showed that in a fully asynchronous network, deterministic consensus is impossible with even one crash fault [4]. Real networks, however, are rarely *fully* asynchronous forever: timeouts do eventually work. Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer formalized this middle ground as **partial synchrony**, in which there exists an unknown global stabilization time (GST) after which message delays are bounded by an unknown Δ [2]. This model is the sweet spot: safety holds even under asynchrony, while liveness is guaranteed only after GST — precisely what FLP permits.

This thesis traces how the field learned to build *practical* Byzantine fault tolerance inside this model, across three generations: **PBFT (1999)** — Castro and Liskov's landmark showed BFT state-machine replication could run nearly as fast as an unreplicated service, using a leader-driven three-phase protocol with O(n²) authenticator complexity [1]; **HotStuff (2019)** — Yin et al. reframed PBFT's phases as a *chain* in which each new phase's prepare doubles as the previous phase's vote, yielding linear O(n) complexity and a pipelined structure that powers modern proof-of-stake systems [3]; and **DAG-based BFT (2021–present)** — DAG-Rider, Narwhal/Tusk, Bullshark, and Mysticeti abandoned leader-sequencing of transactions entirely, separating high-throughput *data dissemination* (the DAG/mempool) from *consensus on ordering* [5,6,7,8].

Our contribution is a unified analytical framework: one quorum model, one safety argument, three generations of liveness machinery, and an honest accounting of what each generation sacrifices.

> **Thesis Statement:** *Partial synchrony is not merely a convenience for liveness proofs; it is the load-bearing assumption that makes Byzantine agreement practical, and each generational leap in BFT performance has been, at its core, a more efficient way to exploit the eventual-delay-bound structure of the partially synchronous model.*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Partially Synchronous Model

Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer defined two variants of partial synchrony [2]:

| Variant | Assumption | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **GST model** | Unknown GST exists; after GST, every message arrives within unknown bound Δ | Delays are bounded *eventually*; the protocol never learns Δ or GST |
| **Unknown Δ** | A fixed but unknown delay bound Δ holds from the start | Protocols must be Δ-independent |

The GST model dominates modern practice. Protocols must satisfy two distinct guarantees:

- **Safety** (agreement + validity): holds in *all* executions, even before GST — i.e., under full asynchrony. This is why FLP does not bite: agreement does not require termination.
- **Liveness** (termination): required only after GST. Exponentially backed-off timeouts guarantee that after GST, the protocol's assumed bound eventually exceeds Δ, and leader-based view changes then make progress.

> **Theorem:** *(Dwork–Lynch–Stockmeyer, 1988)* There exist protocols solving Byzantine consensus with authentication for any *n ≥ 3f + 1* under partial synchrony, tolerating *f* Byzantine faults [2].

The bound *n ≥ 3f + 1* is tight: without authentication it matches the Lamport–Shostak–Pease lower bound, and it arises from a simple quorum-intersection argument that recurs in every protocol we study.

### 2.2 Quorum Intersection: The One Argument You Need

Let *n = 3f + 1* and define a **quorum** as any set of *2f + 1* replicas. For any two quorums *Q₁, Q₂*:

$$\lvert Q_1 \cap Q_2 \rvert \;\geq\; (2f+1) + (2f+1) - (3f+1) \;=\; f + 1$$

Since at most *f* replicas are Byzantine, every pair of quorums intersects in **at least one correct replica**. This correct-replica intersection is the skeleton of every safety proof in PBFT, HotStuff, and DAG-BFT alike: a value "locks" on a quorum, and any later quorum that could finalize a conflicting value must contain a correct replica that remembers the lock.

### 2.3 State-Machine Replication

All three generations implement *Byzantine state-machine replication* (BFT-SMR): clients submit operations, and all correct replicas execute an identical total order. As Schneider's classic tutorial establishes, SMR reduces to *atomic broadcast* [10]. PBFT and HotStuff implement the broadcast directly through leader-proposed blocks; DAG protocols split broadcast into *reliable broadcast of a DAG* followed by a deterministic *ordering rule* applied locally to the same causal history.

### 2.4 Authenticators and the O(n²) Barrier

PBFT's original cost came from all-to-all multicast of signed messages: each phase costs *O(n²)* MAC authenticators. Two cryptographic tools reshaped this cost curve:

- **Threshold signatures** (e.g., BLS): *2f + 1* partial signatures combine into a single *quorum certificate* (QC) of constant size — the enabling technology of HotStuff's linearity [3].
- **Erasure coding and reliable broadcast**: DAG protocols disseminate transaction batches once through the DAG, amortizing dissemination over many consensus decisions [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 PBFT: The Three-Phase Commit That Made BFT Practical

Castro and Liskov's protocol runs a *primary* (leader) per *view* *v*. For each client request, the normal case executes three phases [1]:

1. **PRE-PREPARE** — the primary assigns sequence number *n* and broadcasts `⟨PRE-PREPARE, v, n, d⟩` (where *d* is the request digest). Backups verify the signature, view, and that they have not accepted a different digest for *(v, n)*.
2. **PREPARE** — each backup multicasts `⟨PREPARE, v, n, d, i⟩` to all replicas. A replica that collects *2f* matching PREPAREs plus the PRE-PREPARE forms a **prepared certificate** and marks the request *prepared*.
3. **COMMIT** — each prepared replica multicasts `⟨COMMIT, v, n, d, i⟩`. Collecting *2f + 1* matching COMMITs *commits* the request; the replica executes it and replies to the client.

```
Client ──REQUEST──▶ Primary
Primary ──PRE-PREPARE(v,n,d)──▶ All backups        [phase 1, O(n) msgs]
Each i  ──PREPARE(v,n,d,i)──▶ All replicas         [phase 2, O(n²) msgs]
Each i  ──COMMIT(v,n,d,i)──▶ All replicas          [phase 3, O(n²) msgs]
Replica ──REPLY──▶ Client  (after f+1 matching replies, client accepts)
```

Safety rests on two invariants. *Prepared* requires a quorum of *2f + 1* witnesses; *committed* requires a (possibly different) quorum. Their intersection contains a correct replica, so no two correct replicas commit different digests at *(v, n)*. **View change** (triggered by *f + 1* VIEW-CHANGE messages after timeout) collects prepared certificates from *2f + 1* replicas into a NEW-VIEW message, preserving every possibly-committed request across leader rotations [1].

> **Theorem:** *(PBFT Safety)* If a correct replica commits request *r* with sequence number *n* in view *v*, no correct replica commits a different request *r′ ≠ r* with sequence number *n* in any view *v′ ≥ v* [1].

*Proof sketch.* Committing requires a commit-quorum *Q_c* of *2f + 1*. Any later view-change quorum *Q_v* intersects *Q_c* in a correct replica *c* that prepared *r*; *c*'s prepared certificate propagates into the NEW-VIEW message, and the new primary must propose *r* for *n*. ∎

**Complexity.** Normal case: *O(n²)* authenticators per request (the all-to-all phases), constant latency of ~4–5 message delays. View change: *O(n³)* in the naive formulation. Optimizations in the paper — MAC authenticators instead of signatures, tentative execution, read-only fast path — brought the BFT NFS prototype to within 3% of unreplicated NFS throughput [1].

### 4.2 HotStuff: Linearity through Chaining

HotStuff's insight is algebraic: PBFT's prepare and commit phases can be *pipelined* so that a single voting round serves double duty. In **chained HotStuff**, the protocol runs in *rounds* (instead of PBFT's views), each with a rotating leader, and each block carries a QC for its parent. A block becomes committed when it is the head of a **three-chain**: *B₁ ← B₂ ← B₃* where each extends the previous and each carries the QC of the previous — the three QCs correspond exactly to PBFT's prepare, pre-commit, and commit phases [3].

```
Round r    leader proposes B_r extending B_{r-1} with QC_{r-1}
Replicas   vote → leader forms QC_r (threshold sig, O(n) msgs)
Block B commits when B ← B' ← B'' ← B''' forms (3-chain rule)
```

Key properties established by Yin et al. [3]:

- **Linear communication**: each phase is leader-to-all broadcast plus all-to-leader votes aggregated by threshold signatures — *O(n)* authenticators per decision versus PBFT's *O(n²)*.
- **Responsiveness**: after GST, the protocol advances at network speed (the leader need not wait for a maximal Δ timeout), because votes arrive as fast as the network delivers them.
- **Rotating leaders**: every round has a new leader, so a faulty leader is replaced within one round rather than via an expensive view-change sub-protocol. Liveness reduces to a simple **Pacemaker** that synchronizes round entry after GST.

> **Theorem:** *(HotStuff Safety)* If blocks *B* and *B′* conflict (neither extends the other) and both are committed by correct replicas, contradiction: the commit rules require 3-chains whose first QCs intersect in a correct replica that voted only once per round [3].

HotStuff's three-chain commit rule directly generalizes PBFT's locking: a replica *locks* on the pre-commit QC (grandparent), and the safety argument is again quorum intersection — any conflicting 3-chain must pass through a correct replica holding the lock.

**Trade-off.** Linearity depends on *threshold signatures*: the leader must collect *2f + 1* partial signatures and combine them, adding a cryptographic pipeline stage that dominates CPU cost in some deployments. Variants (two-chain HotStuff, Fast-HotStuff) trade latency against this cost.

### 4.3 DAG-Based BFT: Separating Dissemination from Ordering

The DAG family — **DAG-Rider** [5], **Narwhal/Tusk** [6], **Bullshark** [7], **Mysticeti** [8] — observes that HotStuff's bottleneck is not voting but *data*: the leader must disseminate every transaction before ordering it. The fix is architectural: split the protocol into two layers.

**Layer 1 — DAG mempool (Narwhal).** Every validator reliably broadcasts one vertex per round; each vertex contains a batch of transactions and *causal references* (hashes) to at least *2f + 1* vertices of the previous round. Because references are validated before a vertex is added, every correct validator eventually holds the *same* DAG with the *same* causal histories — a consistent, high-throughput dissemination fabric with no leader on the data path [6].

**Layer 2 — Consensus on the DAG.** A deterministic rule selects *anchor* vertices (leaders elected per wave of rounds) and orders each anchor's causal history:

| Protocol | Waves / structure | Commit latency (rounds) | Key innovation |
|---|---|---|---|
| DAG-Rider [5] | 4 rounds/wave, randomized anchor | ~4 (expected) | First asynchronous DAG-BFT with optimal resilience |
| Tusk [6] | 3 rounds/wave | 3 | Simplest rule; built on Narwhal mempool |
| Bullshark [7] | 2 rounds/wave (partial sync) | 2 | Steady-state + fallback leaders; best partially-synchronous latency |
| Mysticeti [8] | Certified + uncertified DAG | ~3 message delays | Fast path: uncertified DAG commits in 3 delays; 500k+ TPS reported |

> **Theorem:** *(DAG-Rider Safety Sketch)* All correct validators commit anchors in the same order, and each anchor's causal history is identical across correct validators; hence the total order derived by deterministic traversal is identical [5].

The safety argument is structural rather than vote-based: because causal histories are *immutable once referenced* and quorum intersection guarantees overlap of validator sets between rounds, a committed anchor's history is fixed for all correct validators.

**Why this matters for throughput.** In Narwhal, every validator streams transaction batches continuously — the mempool is never idle waiting for a leader. Reported results: Narwhal/Tusk sustained ~130k TPS at sub-3s latency on 50 validators in the original evaluation [6]; Mysticeti's uncertified fast path reports exceeding 500k TPS with sub-second latency [8].

### 4.4 Pacemakers, View Changes, and Life after GST

All three generations need a mechanism to advance past faulty leaders once GST arrives:

- **PBFT**: explicit view-change with *O(n³)* NEW-VIEW assembly; exponential backoff on view timers guarantees eventual overlap of a correct primary with a synchronous window [1].
- **HotStuff**: the **Pacemaker** advances rounds on timeouts or on seeing a QC; rotating leaders mean the "view change" is the normal path, and Bracha-style synchronization keeps correct replicas within Δ of each other after GST [3].
- **DAG-BFT**: Bullshark's partially-synchronous path uses timeouts only for its *fallback* leaders; the steady-state path commits without any timeout, advancing on DAG progress alone — a qualitative shift from "timeout-driven liveness" to "data-driven liveness" [7].

```tla+
---- MODULE PbftViewChange ----
EXTENDS Naturals
CONSTANTS Replicas, F
Quorum == { Q \in SUBSET Replicas : Cardinality(Q) = 2*F + 1 }
(* Safety invariant: any two commit quorums share a correct replica *)
SafetyInvariant ==
    \A Q1, Q2 \in Quorum :
        Cardinality((Q1 \cap Q2) \ Faulty) >= 1
THEOREM SafetyInvariantHolds ==
    (* Follows from |Q1 ∩ Q2| >= f+1 and |Faulty| <= f *)
    TRUE
====
```

### 4.5 Complexity Comparison

| Protocol | Normal-case msgs | Latency (fault-free) | Resilience | Liveness after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [1] | O(n²) | 4–5 delays | n ≥ 3f+1 | GST + view change |
| HotStuff [3] | O(n) | 7 delays (3-chain) | n ≥ 3f+1 | GST + pacemaker |
| Tendermint [9] | O(n²) | 3 delays | n ≥ 3f+1 | GST + proposer rotation |
| DAG-Rider [5] | O(n²) (amortized) | ~4 waves | n ≥ 3f+1 (async) | N/A — fully asynchronous |
| Bullshark [7] | O(n²) (amortized) | 2 rounds steady-state | n ≥ 3f+1 | GST (partial-sync path) |
| Mysticeti [8] | O(n) per commit (amortized) | ~3 delays fast path | n ≥ 3f+1 | GST |

Latency is measured in one-way message delays under fault-free operation after GST. DAG protocols amortize dissemination cost across many committed transactions, so per-transaction authenticator cost trends toward *O(1)* — the decisive economic argument for the DAG architecture at scale.

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proof Structure

### 5.1 What the Papers Report

- **PBFT** [1]: a replicated NFS service on 4 replicas achieved throughput within 3% of unreplicated NFS; null-request latency under 1 ms on a LAN.
- **HotStuff** [3]: the PODC 2019 evaluation showed linear scaling of authenticator cost and throughput competitive with PBFT at small *n*, diverging favorably as *n* grows; the protocol underpins production systems (notably the Diem/Libra lineage and its successor Aptos), giving the design real deployment validation.
- **Narwhal/Tusk** [6]: ~130,000 TPS at <3 s latency with 50 geo-distributed validators, versus single-digit thousands for the HotStuff baseline in the same harness — the mempool separation, not the consensus rule, accounts for most of the gap.
- **Mysticeti** [8]: >500,000 TPS with ~0.5 s latency in the Sui deployment's evaluation, using an uncertified DAG fast path that commits in 3 message delays when validators are honest and the network is synchronous.

### 5.2 The Safety Proof Pattern, Once More

Every protocol in this thesis proves safety by the same two-step template:

1. **Locking.** A value *v* becomes *locked* at a quorum *Q*: *2f + 1* replicas attest to *v* (PBFT prepared/commit certificates; HotStuff QCs; DAG anchor commitment).
2. **Intersection.** Any quorum *Q′* that could finalize a conflicting *v′* intersects *Q* in a correct replica that remembers the lock and refuses to attest to *v′*.

```python
def quorum_intersection(n: int, f: int) -> int:
    """Minimum overlap of two (2f+1)-quorums in an n=3f+1 system."""
    assert n == 3 * f + 1
    quorum = 2 * f + 1
    overlap = 2 * quorum - n          # = f + 1
    assert overlap >= f + 1          # at least one correct replica
    return overlap

for f in (1, 2, 5, 33):
    print(f"f={f}: quorum overlap = {quorum_intersection(3*f+1, f)} "
          f"(>= {f+1} => >=1 correct)")
```

Liveness proofs differ by generation (view-change timers, pacemakers, DAG progress), but the safety core has not changed in twenty-five years — a sign that Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer's model captured the right abstraction [2].

## 6 Limitations

**PBFT's fragility.** The O(n³) view change is a denial-of-service amplifier: a faulty primary forces the entire system through the most expensive path. MAC-authenticator optimizations complicate key management, and the single primary is a bandwidth bottleneck as *n* grows.

**HotStuff's cryptographic tax.** Linearity is purchased with threshold signatures. The combine-and-verify pipeline adds CPU latency to the critical path, and threshold key setup (DKG) is itself a hard distributed problem. Two-chain variants reduce phases but weaken the lock structure, trading proof simplicity for latency.

**DAG-BFT's open wounds.** (i) *Optimistic responsiveness*: DAG-Rider and Tusk are not optimistically responsive — commit latency is a function of wave structure, not network speed [5,6]; Bullshark restores responsiveness only on its steady-state path [7]. (ii) *Validator-set changes*: the DAG's causal structure assumes a fixed committee; reconfiguration mid-DAG remains delicate. (iii) *Censorship*: a slow or Byzantine validator's transactions may wait for anchor inclusion; fairness mechanisms (e.g., Mysticeti's proposer slots) mitigate but do not eliminate the issue. (iv) *Storage*: every validator stores the full DAG — the throughput miracle has a disk bill.

**Model-level limits.** Partial synchrony assumes GST *exists*. Under sustained asynchrony, all three generations preserve safety but halt — FLP's shadow. Randomized asynchronous protocols make progress without GST but pay in expected latency and cryptographic complexity.

---

## 7 Conclusion

From PBFT's three-phase commit to HotStuff's chained pipeline to the DAG mempool revolution, the history of practical Byzantine agreement is the history of learning to spend the partially synchronous model's eventual-delay bound more efficiently. PBFT proved BFT could be practical [1]; HotStuff proved it could be linear and pipelined [3]; the DAG family proved that consensus need not sequence data at all, only order it after the fact [5,6,7,8]. The quorum-intersection argument — *n = 3f + 1*, quorums of *2f + 1*, one correct replica in every overlap — has survived every transition intact, which suggests it is the invariant the field will carry into whatever comes next. The model Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer formalized in 1988 [2] remains the ground on which all of it stands.
