# Autocatalytic Sets and the Origin of Life: RAF Theory, the RNA World, and Compositional Inheritance

## Abstract

How did chemistry become biology? This thesis unifies three pillars of modern origins-of-life theory — reflexively autocatalytic food-generated (RAF) sets, the RNA world, and compositional inheritance — into a single narrative of how molecular networks crossed the threshold from non-living chemistry to Darwinian evolution. We begin with Stuart Kauffman's original proposal of collectively autocatalytic sets and its rigorous reformulation as RAF theory by Hordijk and Steel, which supplies polynomial-time detection algorithms and sharp probabilistic guarantees for the spontaneous emergence of self-sustaining networks in the binary polymer model. We then survey the RNA world hypothesis, from self-splicing introns to in vitro-evolved polymerase ribozymes and Lincoln and Joyce's cross-replicating ribozymes. Third, we analyze the GARD model of Segré and Lancet, in which amphiphilic assemblies inherit composition rather than sequence. Synthesizing these frameworks, we argue that catalytic closure, template replication, and compositional heredity are complementary rather than rival accounts of life's origin, and we identify the open problems — evolvability, realism of catalysis assignments, and experimental validation — that define the field's frontier.

## 1 Introduction

The origin of life is the problem of explaining how a planet with only geochemistry came to host systems that grow, maintain themselves, replicate, and evolve. Two intuitions have organized a century of thought: *replication-first*, in which heredity came first, and *metabolism-first*, in which self-sustaining chemistry came first and heredity emerged later.

This thesis examines three formal frameworks that make these intuitions precise and testable:

1. **RAF theory** — Hordijk and Steel's mathematical formalization of autocatalytic sets, asking when a reaction system contains a subset that is both *reflexively autocatalytic* and *food-generated* [1][2].
2. **The RNA world hypothesis** — the proposal that RNA once served as both gene and catalyst, grounded in ribozyme discovery and in vitro evolution [5][6].
3. **Compositional inheritance** — the GARD model of Segré and Lancet, in which lipid-like assemblies transmit information through *composition* rather than sequence, offering heredity without nucleic acids [7][8].

These frameworks are often presented as competitors. We argue instead that they describe nested levels of a single story: RAF theory characterizes the *organization* making self-sustaining chemistry possible; the RNA world characterizes the *informational refinement* making heredity high-fidelity; compositional inheritance characterizes a *pre-RNA scaffolding* that hosted the first replicators [1].

> **Thesis statement:** Life emerged when a reflexively autocatalytic, food-generated chemical network — plausibly first realized in lipid-compositional assemblies and inorganic catalysts — acquired sequence-based templating in the form of RNA, converting low-fidelity compositional heredity into high-fidelity informational heredity capable of open-ended evolution.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Kauffman to RAF

The modern study of autocatalytic sets begins with Stuart Kauffman, who proposed that life originated not with a single self-replicating molecule but with a *collectively* autocatalytic set: molecules each of whose formation is catalyzed by another member of the collection, so that the set as a whole catalyzes its own production from simple precursors [1]. Kauffman's key insight was a phase-transition argument: as molecular diversity grows, the number of possible reactions grows faster (roughly quadratically), so beyond some critical diversity, catalytic closure becomes *overwhelmingly likely*.

Kauffman's formulation was largely qualitative. The decisive advance came with Hordijk and Steel, who gave autocatalytic sets a precise graph-theoretic formalization — RAF theory — with efficient algorithms and rigorous theorems where before there had been mostly simulation and intuition [1][2].

### 2.2 Formal preliminaries

We work with the standard formalism. A **chemical reaction system (CRS)** is a triple *(X, R, C)*: *X*, molecule types; *R*, reactions mapping reactant sets to product sets; *C*, a catalysis relation of *(molecule, reaction)* pairs. A subset *F* of *X* is the **food set**: molecule types freely available from the environment. A non-empty subset *R'* of *R* is then a **RAF set** — *reflexively autocatalytic and food-generated* — when it satisfies the following:

> **Definition (RAF):** A non-empty subset *R'* of reactions is *reflexively autocatalytic and food-generated* if (i) each reaction in *R'* is catalyzed by a molecule involved in *R'* (as reactant, product, or food), and (ii) all reactants in *R'* are constructible from the food set *F* using only reactions in *R'*.

The conjunction matters. Autocatalysis alone is cheap — a single reaction whose product catalyzes itself is trivially autocatalytic but explains nothing about the origin of *organization*. Together, the two conditions express a *catalytically closed, self-sustaining* subsystem: the network makes its own catalysts out of environmental raw materials.

### 2.3 The binary polymer model

The canonical test bed is Kauffman's **binary polymer model**: molecule types are bit strings up to maximum length *n*, built from a food set of short strings. Reactions are ligation and cleavage; catalysis is assigned randomly, each molecule catalyzing each reaction independently with probability *p* — a minimal model asking a purely structural question: *given random catalysis, how likely is a RAF, and how does that likelihood scale?*

The answer, proved by Hordijk, Steel, and collaborators, is striking: there is a sharp threshold, and when the catalysis probability *p* exceeds a critical value that *decreases* with increasing maximum polymer length *n*, RAF sets appear with probability approaching one [1][2]. Sufficiently complex random reaction systems *almost surely* contain self-sustaining autocatalytic cores — the rigorous counterpart of Kauffman's phase-transition intuition.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a theoretical synthesis. Its method is to reconstruct the three frameworks from their primary literature, compare their formal structures, and evaluate their empirical support. Three methodological traditions are involved, and understanding each is essential to judging what the frameworks can and cannot establish.

### 3.1 Mathematical analysis: the RAF algorithm

A central methodological contribution of RAF theory is algorithmic: Hordijk and Steel showed that "does this CRS contain a RAF?" does not require searching an exponential space of reaction subsets — a **polynomial-time algorithm** finds the *maximal* RAF (the union of all RAFs, itself a RAF) or correctly reports that none exists [1][2]. It is an iterated pruning procedure: start with all reactions; compute the food set's *closure* (all molecules constructible from *F*); remove reactions lacking a catalyst or reactants in the closure; repeat to fixed point. Applied to real metabolic networks with thousands of reactions [4], it makes RAF theory *falsifiable in silico*.

The following Python sketch illustrates the core pruning loop on a toy CRS:

```python
def closure(food, reactions):
    reachable = set(food)
    changed = True
    while changed:
        changed = False
        for reactants, products in reactions:
            if all(r in reachable for r in reactants):
                for p in products:
                    if p not in reachable:
                        reachable.add(p); changed = True
    return reachable

def max_raf(food, reactions, catalysis):
    active = list(range(len(reactions)))
    while True:
        reach = closure(food, [reactions[i] for i in active])
        keep = [i for i in active
                if catalysis[i] & reach
                and all(r in reach for r in reactions[i][0])]
        if len(keep) == len(active):
            return [reactions[i] for i in keep]
        active = keep
        if not active:
            return []
```

### 3.2 Stochastic simulation and in vitro evolution

The GARD model is studied through stochastic simulation: an assembly's **composition vector** evolves by kinetic equations in which a matrix *β* of catalytic rate enhancements modulates monomer recruitment, with fission at a maximal size *N_max* [7][8]. Gillespie-type Monte Carlo dynamics reveal whether heredity-like phenomena — homeostatic growth, composome attractors, response to selection — emerge.

The RNA world's methodology is experimental and Darwinian: *SELEX*-style selection on large random RNA pools (often around ten to the fifteenth molecules) — retain molecules performing a target reaction, amplify, mutate, repeat — has produced ribozymes for ligation, polymerization, and nucleotide synthesis [5][6]. Its strength is demonstrating *what RNA can do*; its limit, for origins purposes, is contrived experimenter-imposed selection.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The RAF formalism: closure, catalysis, and the percolation threshold

The deep content of RAF theory is three interlocking results: a polynomial-time *algorithm* for finding RAFs, *probabilistic theorems* about their emergence, and a *structural theory* of their decomposition.

**Probabilistic emergence.** In the binary polymer model with maximum length *n* and catalysis probability *p*, RAF probability undergoes a sharp, percolation-like transition: once *p* exceeds a critical value decreasing in *n*, RAFs appear with probability approaching one [1]. Required catalysis per molecule grows only *linearly* in *n* while reactions grow exponentially — modest promiscuous catalysis suffices.

> **Theorem (informal, after Mossel–Steel and Hordijk–Steel):** In the binary polymer model, if each molecule catalyzes each reaction with probability *p* above a threshold scaling mildly with maximum length *n*, the probability of containing a RAF tends to 1 as *n* grows; below a lower threshold it tends to 0. Like percolation in random graphs, catalytic closure becomes near-certain past a critical density.

![Percolation threshold plot for autocatalytic set formation](public/thesis/ths_1789368005000_02285-3.webp)

**Structural decomposition.** Hordijk and Steel's theory of *subRAFs* and *RAF decompositions* into independently viable cores [2] gives large RAFs a primitive form of variation — gain or loss of modules — on which selection could act [3].

**Catalysis realism.** Random catalysis is the binary polymer model's most criticized feature. Hordijk, Wills, and Steel extended RAF theory to *template-based* catalysis (the Wills–Henderson model) — a molecule catalyzing reactions involving its complement — showing emergence is robust, though thresholds shift [2].

### 4.2 RAFs in real biochemistry: from theory to metabolism

RAF theory has found its subject matter in nature twice, in results bearing directly on the origin of metabolism.

First, Sousa and colleagues identified a RAF embedded in the metabolic network of *Escherichia coli*: a large, food-generated, reflexively autocatalytic core in one of the best-mapped biochemical networks known [1] — RAF organization is not an artifact of toy models.

Second, Xavier, Hordijk, Kauffman, Steel, and Martin searched for RAFs in reconstructed ancient anaerobic autotroph metabolism — organisms resembling the last universal common ancestor's ecology, living on hydrogen and carbon dioxide [4]. With small-molecule catalysts, these networks generate RAFs producing acetyl-CoA, amino acids, and nucleobases. Without organic catalysts, no metabolic RAFs form: the inorganic catalysts are load-bearing.

This suggests a concrete hypothesis: the earliest autocatalytic sets were catalyzed not by organics but by **metals and minerals** — iron-sulfur clusters, metal ions, mineral surfaces — abundant at hydrothermal vents [1]. The Hadean ocean's food set plus mineral-catalyzed reactions may already have contained RAFs: a self-sustaining metabolic core *before* polymers, within which the RNA world later emerged.

| System | Food set | Catalysts | RAF found? | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Binary polymer model (random catalysis) | Monomers, dimers | Random assignment, probability *p* | Yes, above threshold | Proof of principle; percolation |
| Template-based (Wills–Henderson) model | Short oligomers | Complementarity-based | Yes, shifted threshold | Robustness to realistic catalysis [2] |
| *E. coli* metabolism | Environmental nutrients | Enzymes | Yes | RAF organization in modern cells [1] |
| Ancient autotroph metabolism | H2, CO2, small molecules | Inorganic (metals, minerals) | Yes | Prebiotic plausibility; yields amino acids and bases [4] |

### 4.3 The RNA world: sequence, catalysis, and the replicase problem

The RNA world hypothesis, articulated by Gilbert in 1986, proposes that before DNA and protein enzymes, RNA stored genetic information *and* catalyzed chemistry [5]. Its foundation: catalytic RNA — Cech's self-splicing introns, Altman's ribonuclease P — and the ribosome itself, a ribozyme at its catalytic heart.

The hypothesis demands an **RNA replicase**: a ribozyme copying RNA templates, including itself, with enough fidelity and processivity to sustain Darwinian evolution. Three decades of in vitro evolution have approached it stepwise:

- **Ligase ribozymes.** Paul and Joyce's 2002 ligase assembled itself from two fragments — minimal self-replication, not general polymerization [5].
- **Cross-replicating ribozymes.** Lincoln and Joyce (2009) engineered a *pair*, E and E′, each assembling the other from four substrates; supplied with substrates, the pair amplifies exponentially — Darwinian dynamics in a purely RNA system whose architecture is *collectively* autocatalytic [5].
- **Polymerase ribozymes.** The class I polymerase lineage, culminating in variant 24-3, catalyzes template-directed polymerization: ~24-nucleotide templates, tens of thousands of copies per day, even functional tRNA [6]. Yet it cannot copy *itself*, and longer templates are overrun by shorter parasites.

![RNA world self-replicating ribozyme cycle diagram](public/thesis/ths_1789368005000_02285-1.webp)

The RNA world thus faces a central paradox: RNA catalysis is extraordinary and in vitro evolution keeps improving replicases, yet a *general, self-sustaining* RNA replicase — copying sequences long enough to encode itself — remains out of reach [5][6]. Three obstacles recur: **(1) fidelity versus length** — fidelity must beat an error threshold tightening with genome length (Eigen's paradox); **(2) strand inhibition** — double-stranded products resist separation without proteins, choking amplification; **(3) prebiotic plausibility** — the whole edifice assumes activated nucleotides whose prebiotic synthesis is contested.

These obstacles motivate the search for *scaffolding*: simpler systems that could have hosted and concentrated the first replicators. That is where compositional inheritance enters.

### 4.4 Compositional inheritance: the GARD model and the lipid world

The GARD model of Segré, Ben-Eli, and Lancet proposes that the first evolvable entities were **compositional assemblies** of amphiphilic molecules [8]: an assembly's state is its *composition vector* (counts of each of *N_G* types), growth follows mutually catalytic kinetics encoded in a rate-enhancement matrix *β*, and at maximal size the assembly fissions, daughters inheriting parental composition.

The model's central discovery is the **composome**: a compositional attractor. From random assemblies, dynamics converge on quasi-stationary compositions reproducing themselves with high fidelity across fission generations — *compositional inheritance* without sequences [8]. Since different composomes grow at different rates, environmental pressure selects among them: Darwinian dynamics on *compositions* [7].

![Lipid vesicle reproduction stages showing compositional inheritance](public/thesis/ths_1789368005000_02285-2.webp)

GARD's relationship to the other frameworks is illuminating:

- **GARD assemblies are autocatalytic networks in the RAF sense**, but closure comes from *partitioning* (selective molecular attraction to the assembly) rather than covalent chemistry — a ubiquitous behavior of simple molecules [3].
- **GARD heredity is low-fidelity**: the composome repertoire is limited, and critics argue compositional systems lack *unlimited heredity* [7]. Rebuttals note the critiques under-sampled composome dynamics — the evolvability question stays open [7].
- **GARD scaffolds the RNA world**: lipid assemblies concentrate reactants, compartmentalize replicators against parasites, and fission — the infrastructure a fledgling replicase needs. The "lipid world" is thus the RNA world's *predecessor and host*, not its rival.

Information-theoretic measurements confirm compositional transmission is real but modest — what one expects of a scaffold: good enough to start evolution, not to finish it [7].

### 4.5 Synthesis: layered emergence

The three frameworks assemble into a single layered narrative:

1. **Geochemical RAFs.** Mineral- and metal-catalyzed networks among simple food molecules contained RAF cores — self-sustaining, catalytically closed, needing no organic catalysts [1][4]. Organization before information.
2. **Compositional assemblies.** Amphiphiles self-assembled into vesicles; mutually catalytic recruitment produced composomes with homeostatic growth and compositional inheritance [8] — heredity, but low-fidelity and repertoire-limited.
3. **Encapsulation.** Composomes concentrated reactants and co-localized replicators with products — conditions under which templated polymerization pays and parasites are controllable [6].
4. **The RNA takeover.** Oligonucleotides replicated by template-directed mechanisms — first Lincoln–Joyce-type ligase networks, then polymerase ribozymes [5][6]. Sequence heredity's larger repertoire won; the composome became the protocell.
5. **Refinement.** Fidelity improvements — eventually coded protein synthesis via the ribosome, itself a ribozyme relic — lifted the error threshold, permitting longer genomes [5].

On this view the replication-first versus metabolism-first dichotomy dissolves: *metabolism (as RAF organization) came first; replication (as templating) came later; but replication, once present, reorganized metabolism around itself.* Each layer solves the previous layer's problem, and evolvability emerges from the *succession* of mechanisms, each bootstrapping the next.

## 5 Empirical Results

Because this thesis is synthetic, its "results" are the literature's key findings, presented as quantitative benchmarks for origins scenarios.

### 5.1 RAF emergence: simulation benchmarks

In the binary polymer model, RAF-formation probability follows the predicted sigmoidal transition. Representative findings [1][2]:

- For *n* = 10 (1,022 molecule types, ~2 million reactions), RAFs appear with high probability once each molecule catalyzes a modest single-digit average number of reactions.
- Required catalysis grows *linearly* in *n* while reactions grow *exponentially*: the *fraction* any molecule must catalyze *shrinks* with system size.
- In template-based catalysis variants, RAF emergence is preserved with shifted thresholds [2].
- The *E. coli* metabolic RAF and ancient-autotroph RAFs (acetyl-CoA, amino acids, nucleobases from H2 and CO2 with inorganic catalysts) were found by the polynomial-time maxRAF algorithm at realistic scale [1][4].

### 5.2 RNA world: laboratory benchmarks

| System | Achievement | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Paul–Joyce ligase ribozyme (2002) | Self-assembly from two fragments | Not a general polymerase [5] |
| Lincoln–Joyce cross-replicators (2009) | Sustained exponential amplification of a ribozyme pair | Requires pre-formed substrates [5] |
| Class I polymerase 24-3 (2016) | Copies ~24-nt templates; ~40,000-fold amplification in 24 h | Cannot copy itself; strand inhibition; parasites [6] |
| In vitro selection generally | Ribozymes for ligation, polymerization, nucleotide synthesis | Experimenter-imposed selection; nucleotide supply unresolved [5] |

### 5.3 GARD: compositional heredity benchmarks

- Random assemblies converge to composome attractors stable over many fission generations [8].
- Compositional information transmission is measurable but low-fidelity; *compotypes* (composome quasispecies) form a limited repertoire [7].
- Under simulated selection, efficient composomes displace inefficient ones [8].
- Critiques show repertoire limits bounding open-endedness; rebuttals show the critiques under-sampled composome dynamics [7]. *How much* heredity, exactly, remains the active frontier.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating plainly what these frameworks do *not* establish.

**1. The catalysis assignment problem.** Random catalysis is a mathematical convenience, not a chemical hypothesis. Template-based extensions improve realism [2], but real prebiotic catalysis had specific, correlated, environment-dependent patterns no model captures. Until RAF analysis is applied to *experimentally measured* prebiotic networks, the percolation argument remains a plausibility proof, not a historical claim.

**2. Evolvability is not settled.** The sharpest critiques of autocatalytic sets and GARD target *open-ended* adaptive evolution rather than mere self-maintenance [3][7]. RAF theory characterizes organization; it does not supply variation, heredity, and selection with fidelity sufficient for indefinite innovation. Our synthesis *proposes* evolvability emerges across transitions — a hypothesis awaiting models where RAFs, composomes, and replicators co-evolve.

**3. Dynamics versus statics.** Classical RAF theory is *structural*: it asks whether a RAF exists, not whether dynamics converge to it or maintain it against dilution. Reaction-network extensions [3] identify motifs predicting self-maintenance and homeostasis, but a full dynamical RAF theory for open systems remains incomplete.

**4. The RNA world's missing replicase.** No laboratory ribozyme yet combines the processivity, fidelity, and strand-separation ability needed to sustain its own replication [5][6]. The Lincoln–Joyce cross-replicators demonstrate the *principle* of RNA Darwinian dynamics but not a self-contained RNA organism.

**5. GARD's heredity ceiling.** Compositional heredity is real but limited in fidelity and repertoire [7]. Whether composomes could cross to unlimited heredity alone — or strictly require the RNA takeover — is unknown, and no laboratory has demonstrated sustained composome evolution in actual vesicles.

## 7 Conclusion

Decomposed into layers, the origin of life looks less mysterious, each layer with its own formalism and evidence. **RAF theory** shows catalytic closure — life's organizational signature — is a generic, efficiently detectable, percolation-like property of complex reaction systems, realized in ancient autotroph metabolism with only inorganic catalysts [1][2][4]. **The RNA world** shows one molecule can be both gene and enzyme; lab evolution has pushed ribozymes toward true self-replication, though the complete replicase remains elusive [5][6]. **Compositional inheritance** shows heredity predates sequences: lipid assemblies transmit compositional information, supplying the compartments and crude Darwinian dynamics within which RNA replicators arose [7][8].

> **Closing thesis:** Autocatalytic sets are necessary but not sufficient for life [1]. What made them sufficient — in the only instance we know — was layering templated replication onto autocatalytic organization via compositional compartments. The frontier is their *integration*: dynamical RAF theory with realistic catalysis, experimental composome evolution, and replicator encapsulation in vesicles.

Life is not a molecule; it is an *organization* — of catalysts making catalysts, of compartments inheriting compositions, of sequences copying sequences. Kauffman's intuition survives four decades of formalization: what ignited on the early Earth was not a replicator but a *network*, and everything else was commentary.
