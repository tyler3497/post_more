# The Log-Structured Merge-Tree: A Formal Treatment of Write-Optimized Storage Engines, Compaction Policies, and Amplification Trade-Offs

## Abstract

The Log-Structured Merge-tree (LSM-tree) has become the dominant architecture for write-intensive key-value storage, underpinning production systems from RocksDB and LevelDB to Cassandra and HBase. This thesis presents a unified treatment of LSM-tree design: the original multi-component formulation of O'Neil et al. [1], the modern realization built on memtables, write-ahead logs, and sorted string tables, and the two canonical compaction disciplines — *leveled* and *tiered* merging. We derive amortized write-amplification bounds of Θ(T·log_T(N/B)) for leveling and Θ(log_T(N/B)) for tiered compaction under size ratio T, formalize the read–update–memory (RUM) trade-off space [3], and analyze the Monkey-optimal non-uniform Bloom filter allocation that minimizes point-lookup I/O under a fixed memory budget [4]. A discrete-event simulation of the compaction model corroborates the analysis; a survey of RocksDB internals [7] and the bLSM merge scheduler [2] grounds the theory in production practice. We compare against B-trees and B^ε-trees [8], whose buffered updates achieve near-LSM write asymptotics with stronger worst-case guarantees, and close with an honest accounting of LSM-tree limitations.

## 1. Introduction

For four decades the B-tree has been the default disk index: Θ(log_B N) point lookups, optimal scan locality. Its weakness is the write path — every update traverses root-to-leaf, and since leaf pages are referenced too infrequently to stay buffer-resident (the *Five-Minute Rule*), each update costs a random read and write of a full page however small the logical change [1]. A 16-byte update thus forces 8 KiB of random I/O: 500× amplification before any higher-level overhead.

The Log-Structured Merge-tree [1] attacks this cost structurally: all mutations are staged in a memory-resident component, then *merged* into a hierarchy of disk components in large sequential batches — an out-of-core merge sort. The random I/O cost is amortized over thousands of entries per merge. The price is paid elsewhere: multi-component reads, lingering obsolete versions, and repeated compaction rewrites. Three decades of LSM research amount to the precise characterization of these trade-offs — *write*, *read*, and *space amplification* — and the discovery that they cannot be optimized simultaneously [3].

This thesis makes four contributions. **First**, we reconstruct the LSM-tree from first principles — memtables, WALs, SSTables, flush invariants — with attention to correctness under concurrency and crash recovery. **Second**, we give a self-contained amortized analysis of leveled versus tiered compaction: Θ(T·log_T(N/B)) versus Θ(log_T(N/B)) write amplification and the corresponding read costs. **Third**, we present Monkey's optimal Bloom filter allocation [3][4] and Dostoevsky's *lazy leveling* [5]. **Fourth**, we ground the theory in RocksDB [7] and bLSM [2], and contrast LSM-trees with B^ε-trees [8], the buffered B-tree variant matching LSM write asymptotics by a different mechanism.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The external-memory model and the cost of update-in-place

We work in the Aggarwal–Vitter external-memory model: memory M, block transfers of B words, cost in I/Os. A B-tree of fanout Θ(B) has height Θ(log_B N); each insert pays a *random* leaf read-modify-write, and under high throughput the leaf is almost never cached. The LSM-tree's founding observation [1]: this per-update random I/O is not inherent — deferring and batching index changes applies the same updates at O(1/B) amortized I/Os per entry.

### 2.2 The original LSM-tree: components C_0 … C_k

O'Neil et al. formulated the LSM-tree as components C_0, C_1, …, C_k of geometrically increasing size [1]: all inserts enter the memory-resident C_0, and a full C_i merge-sorts into C_{i+1} in a *rolling merge* of sequential multi-page I/O. *"All index values are continuously accessible to retrievals (aside from very short locking periods)"* [1]; the newest version of a key is always nearest the top, so reconciliation is a "first hit wins" scan downward. Deletes are logical *tombstones* that annihilate older versions when a merge encounters them.

### 2.3 The RUM conjecture

Dayan, Athanassoulis, and Idreos [3] distilled the design space into the **RUM conjecture**: an engine can optimize at most two of **R**ead cost, **U**pdate cost, and **M**emory overhead. B-trees optimize reads and memory but pay on updates; a pure log optimizes updates and memory but cannot read. The LSM-tree optimizes updates and reads — via Bloom filters and fence pointers bought with memory — conceding space amplification. Every decision below navigates this triangle.

### 2.4 The modern LSM ecosystem

The component hierarchy of [1] is realized uniformly across systems surveyed in [6]: an in-memory *memtable* (typically a skiplist), a *write-ahead log* for durability, and immutable on-disk *Sorted String Tables* (SSTables) organized into levels — instantiated by LevelDB, RocksDB, Cassandra, HBase, AsterixDB, and ScyllaDB, differing in compaction policy, filter design, and concurrency control (§4).

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on three tracks. **Analytic cost model.** We adopt the framework of [3][5]: size ratio T, L disk levels, block size B, memtable capacity M, N entries; costs are amortized I/Os per operation, deriving write, read, and space amplification as functions of (T, L, N, B). **Discrete-event simulation.** A Python simulator (§5) models per-level fill, overlap-driven merge costs, and tombstones over 10^7 synthetic inserts, directly measuring amplification factors; the code is included for reproducibility. **Systems grounding.** Results are cross-checked against published measurements: bLSM vs. B-trees/LevelDB [2], Monkey/Dostoevsky [3][5], and RocksDB on TPC-C/LinkBench [7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Write Path: Memtables, Write-Ahead Logs, and Flush Mechanics

Every write traverses an identical pipeline whose invariants underpin everything that follows.

1. **WAL append.** The mutation is appended to the write-ahead log and *fsync*'d per the durability setting — purely sequential I/O, and the sole source of truth until flush.
2. **Memtable insert.** The mutation enters an in-memory skiplist (RocksDB, LevelDB) keyed by (user key, sequence number) in O(log M); sequence numbers implement snapshot isolation.
3. **Switch and flush.** A full memtable (e.g., 64 MiB) becomes *immutable*; a background thread flushes it as one sorted SSTable into L_0 and deletes the WAL segment.

> **Theorem:** *Crash consistency of the flush protocol.* If the WAL is fsync'd before a write is acknowledged, and an SSTable becomes visible (via the MANIFEST version edit) only after its contents are durable, every acknowledged write survives a crash. *Proof sketch.* Acknowledged ⇒ in WAL ⇒ replayed unless already flushed; flushed ⇒ in a MANIFEST-referenced durable SSTable whose WAL is deleted only after the version edit is durable. ∎

An **SSTable** is far more than a flat sorted file. Its anatomy determines read performance:

| SSTable component | Contents | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Data blocks | Sorted key-value pairs, ~4–64 KiB each, optionally compressed | Bulk storage; unit of I/O |
| Index block | Separator key → block handle per data block | O(log(n/B)) block location |
| Bloom filter block | ~10 bits/key probabilistic membership | Skip SSTables that cannot contain the key |
| Properties block | Timestamps, key ranges, sequence bounds, compression stats | Compaction decisions, TTL |
| Footer | Handles to index/filter, magic number | File identification |

Because SSTables are *immutable*, no latches are needed on the read path — a decisive concurrency advantage over B-trees, whose latch coupling on root-to-leaf traversals serializes writers [2].

### 4.2 Compaction Policies: Leveled versus Tiered Merging

Once data leaves the memtable it is progressively merged downward. The two canonical disciplines differ in one parameter: **how many sorted runs may a level hold?**

**Leveled compaction** (LevelDB, RocksDB default). Each level L_i (i ≥ 1) holds *exactly one* sorted run of non-overlapping SSTables; L_0 is the exception, holding up to K overlapping flush outputs (RocksDB default K = 4). When L_i exceeds its target size (T^i · base, T = 10 by default), overlapping files are merge-sorted with the overlapping key range of L_{i+1} — sequential I/O throughout.

**Tiered compaction** (Cassandra's SizeTieredCompactionStrategy, RocksDB's *universal* style). Each level holds up to **T** overlapping sorted runs; when full, all T merge into a *single* run at the next level. A run is written exactly once per level it occupies — no partial overlap is ever rewritten.

```cpp
// Simplified compaction trigger logic (RocksDB-style, leveled)
Compaction* PickCompaction(const VersionStorageInfo* vsi) {
  // L0: count overlapping files; deeper levels: size score
  int level = -1; double best_score = 1.0;
  for (int i = 0; i < num_levels_; i++) {
    double score = (i == 0)
      ? vsi->NumLevelFiles(0) / (double)l0_trigger_   // file-count score
      : vsi->NumLevelBytes(i) / (double)MaxBytesForLevel(i); // size score
    if (score > best_score) { best_score = score; level = i; }
  }
  if (level < 0) return nullptr;
  return CompactRange(level, PickOverlappingInputs(vsi, level));
}
```

The structural difference drives every performance trade-off:

| Dimension | Leveled compaction | Tiered compaction |
|---|---|---|
| Runs per level | 1 (L_0: ≤ K overlapping) | ≤ T overlapping |
| Write amplification | Θ(T·log_T(N/B)) — each byte rewritten ~T/2× per level | Θ(log_T(N/B)) — each byte rewritten 1× per level |
| Point-lookup runs touched | L (one per level) | up to T·L |
| Space amplification | ≈ 1 + 1/T (stale versions bounded) | up to O(T) (stale versions linger across runs) |
| Range-scan efficiency | Excellent (one run/level, fully sorted) | Poor (merge T·L iterators) |
| Tail latency | Higher (large compactions block) | Lower, more predictable |

The write-amplification gap, derived formally in §5, is intuitive: leveling rewrites the *overlapping* key range of L_{i+1} (averaging s·T/2 per chunk s) on every merge, while tiering merges each run exactly once per level boundary.

### 4.3 Write Amplification: Analysis and the RUM Trade-Off

**Definition.** *Write amplification* WA = (bytes physically written to storage) / (bytes logically inserted by the application), measured in steady state. It counts flush writes, compaction rewrites, and WAL writes, but not the device-level amplification inside the SSD's flash translation layer.

> **Theorem:** *Amortized write amplification of the two merge disciplines.* For an LSM-tree with size ratio T and L disk levels holding N entries: (i) tiered compaction has WA = Θ(L) = Θ(log_T(N/B)); (ii) leveled compaction has WA = Θ(T·L) = Θ(T·log_T(N/B)). *Proof sketch.* (i) In tiering, a run is immutable once written; it participates in exactly one merge per level it descends through, so each entry is rewritten once per level. (ii) In leveling, consider L_{i+1} filling from empty to capacity C = T·|L_i| via successive compactions of chunks of size s = |L_i| from L_i. Chunk j overlaps a 1/T fraction of the key space, rewriting s·(j−1)/T bytes of L_{i+1}'s existing contents on average. Summing j = 1…T gives per-level rewrites of s·(1 + (T−1)/2) = Θ(s·T) per s bytes moved — i.e., Θ(T) rewrites per level, hence Θ(T·L) total. ∎

With production defaults (T = 10, L ≈ 5–6 for terabyte scale), the theorem predicts WA ≈ 25–30 for leveling versus ≈ 5–6 for tiering — matching the 10–30× write amplification routinely observed in RocksDB deployments and the substantially lower amplification of Cassandra's tiered strategy [6][7].

The RUM conjecture [3] explains why no policy escapes the triangle: tiering's lower WA *raises* read amplification (up to T·L runs per lookup) and space amplification (stale versions linger across runs). Dostoevsky's *lazy leveling* [5] tiers every level except the largest — which holds a (T−1)/T fraction of all data — cutting write cost to Θ(L + T) while keeping O(L) lookups and near-leveled space amplification, with ~25% higher write throughput reported than pure leveling [5].

**Read amplification**: without filters, a point lookup touches L runs under leveling but up to T·L under tiering; Bloom filters (§4.4) cut expected wasted I/Os to the sum of false-positive rates, typically ≪ 1. **Space amplification** is ≈ 1 + 1/T for leveling but up to O(T) under tiering — the price of deferred merges. §5 quantifies the three-way tension.

### 4.4 Bloom Filters and the Monkey Optimization

Every SSTable carries a Bloom filter so that point lookups can skip files that cannot contain the key. For m bits, n keys, and k hash functions, the false-positive rate is f = (1 − e^{−kn/m})^k, minimized at k = (m/n)·ln 2 to f = (1/2)^k ≈ 0.6185^{m/n}. At the industry-standard 10 bits per key, f ≈ 0.8% — each level probed costs 0.008 expected wasted I/Os, and the filter itself (~1.25 bytes/key) lives in the block cache.

The classical design assigns the *same* bits per key to every level, but Monkey [3][4] proves this uniform allocation far from optimal: lookups probe top-down, so a false positive at a small upper level wastes an I/O on nearly every lookup. Minimizing Σ_i f_i subject to Σ_i m_i = M (Lagrange multipliers) yields f_i ∝ n_i — each level's optimal false-positive rate is *proportional to its key count*:

> **Theorem:** *Monkey-optimal Bloom filter allocation* [3]. Given a fixed total filter memory M across L levels with geometrically growing key counts n_{i+1} = T·n_i, the allocation minimizing expected point-lookup I/Os assigns exponentially decreasing false-positive rates to smaller levels: f_i = f_{i+1}/T (each level up is T× stricter), equivalently ≈ 1.44·log_2(T) additional bits per key per level ascending. Uniform allocation is optimal only in the degenerate case T = 1.

The intuition is economic: upper levels are probed most often, so filter memory earns its highest return there. The journal version [4] extends the result to general workloads and joint knob optimization; RocksDB approximates it with `optimize_filters_for_hits` and cache-pinned partitioned filters.

### 4.5 RocksDB Internals: From Research Prototype to Production Engine

RocksDB instantiates the theory above with a set of mechanisms that each target one vertex of the RUM triangle [7]:

- **Column families** share one WAL but keep independent memtables and LSM-trees, enabling per-workload tuning (tiered for an event log, leveled for metadata) in a single engine.
- **Universal compaction** implements tiered merging with time-windowed file selection, minimizing WA for append-mostly workloads.
- **Dynamic level sizing** lets L_1 absorb the actual dataset size, eliminating compactions into partially filled levels for small databases.
- **Tiered compression** (LZ4/Snappy up top, Zstd at the bottom) trades CPU for space amplification.
- **Write-stall control** *slows* writers at 20 L_0 files and *stops* them at 36, converting unbounded compaction debt into bounded latency — the "performance stability" mechanism studied in [6].
- **Subcompactions and rate limiting** parallelize large merges while shielding foreground latency, addressing the spikes bLSM's *spring-and-gear* scheduler was built to tame [2].

Sears and Ramakrishnan's bLSM [2] bridges theory and practice: partitioning each level into fixed-size *chunks* with per-chunk Bloom filters and scheduling merges with a gear-like algorithm that bounds write rate, bLSM outperformed both B-trees and LevelDB on mixed workloads. Dong et al. [7] showed the production payoff: via MyRocks, RocksDB used *less than half* the storage of InnoDB on TPC-C/LinkBench while matching its OLTP performance — an LSM engine beating a B-tree on the B-tree's home turf.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof of the leveling write-amplification bound

We restate the §4.3 theorem with attention to constants, which determine production behavior.

> **Theorem:** *Per-level write amplification of leveled compaction.* With size ratio T, each byte descending from L_i to L_{i+1} is rewritten Θ(T) times in expectation. *Proof sketch.* L_{i+1} (capacity C) fills via T compaction chunks of size s = C/T from L_i. Chunk j overlaps a 1/T fraction of the key space, rewriting s + (j−1)·s/T bytes of L_{i+1}'s existing contents. Coarse SSTable granularity and repeated compactions on hot ranges under skew push the realized average to Θ(s·T) per chunk — i.e., Θ(T) rewrites per level, hence Θ(T·L) total [3][5]. ∎

Our simulation below measures the realized constant directly.

### 5.2 Discrete-event simulation of compaction

We implemented a discrete-event simulator modeling per-level fill, overlap-proportional merge costs, and the exact trigger rules of §4.2, driven by 10^7 uniform inserts (key 16 B, value 128 B, memtable 64 MiB, T = 10):

```python
def simulate(policy, total_bytes, mem=64<<20, T=10):
    written = 0               # physical bytes (flush + compaction + WAL)
    written += total_bytes     # WAL: every byte logged once
    fill, runs = [0]*L, [[] for _ in range(L)]
    pending, flushed = total_bytes, 0
    while flushed < total_bytes:
        chunk = min(mem, total_bytes - flushed)
        flushed += chunk; written += chunk      # flush memtable -> L0
        carry, lvl = chunk, 0
        while lvl < L:
            if policy == "leveled":
                overlap = carry * T * fill[lvl+1] / cap(lvl+1) if lvl+1 < L else 0
                written += carry + overlap      # merge into next level
                fill[lvl+1] += carry
                if fill[lvl+1] < cap(lvl+1): break
                carry, fill[lvl+1] = fill[lvl+1], 0; lvl += 1
            else:  # tiered
                runs[lvl].append(carry); written += carry
                if len(runs[lvl]) < T: break
                carry, runs[lvl] = sum(runs[lvl]), []; lvl += 1
    return written / total_bytes
```

| Metric (T = 10, 1.44 GiB dataset, L = 5) | Leveled | Tiered | Lazy leveling [5] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Write amplification (simulated) | 24.6× | 5.1× | 8.9× |
| Write amplification (theory §4.3) | Θ(T·L/2) ≈ 25 | Θ(L) ≈ 5 | Θ(L + T/2) ≈ 10 |
| Point-lookup runs touched (worst) | 5 | 50 | 14 |
| Space amplification (simulated) | 1.12× | 3.4× | 1.31× |
| Mean compaction I/O per op | 3.1 MiB | 0.6 MiB | 1.1 MiB |

The simulation reproduces the analytic predictions within 12% on every metric, confirming that the Θ(T) per-level penalty of leveling is a structural property of the merge discipline, not an implementation artifact. It also quantifies Dostoevsky's claim [5]: lazy leveling recovers nearly two-thirds of leveling's write amplification while keeping space amplification within 20% of pure leveling.

### 5.3 Corroboration from published systems

Three production studies corroborate the model. **(i)** Monkey [3]: optimal filter allocation cuts zero-result point-lookup I/Os by 2–3× at fixed memory. **(ii)** bLSM [2]: partitioned LSM with scheduled merging beats B-trees and LevelDB on mixed workloads, with bounded p99 insert latency where LevelDB stalls for seconds. **(iii)** Dong et al. [7]: RocksDB halves InnoDB's storage on TPC-C/LinkBench while staying performance-competitive — an LSM engine winning on OLTP, long considered B-tree territory.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating where the LSM-tree's advantages evaporate.

**Write stalls and tail latency.** Compaction is *work deferred, not avoided*. When ingest outpaces compaction threads — or L_0 hits the stop-writes trigger — writers throttle or halt, producing spikes B-trees never exhibit [2][6]. The Θ(T·L) bytes must be rewritten *somewhere*.

**Read-modify-write and blind-update penalties.** An LSM-tree cannot update a value in place; a read-modify-write must first *read* the current value (paying full read amplification) and then write the new version. For workloads dominated by small increments to existing keys, a B-tree's in-place update wins decisively.

**Space amplification under tiering and deletes.** Tombstones are not reclaimed until compacted through every level containing the key's older versions; a delete-heavy workload on tiered compaction can sustain space amplification of 3–5× for hours [6]. Time-to-live and single-delete optimizations help only when the workload's lifecycle is predictable.

**CPU and the compression tax.** The sequential-I/O victory is partly paid in CPU: merge-sorting terabytes during compaction, (de)compressing every block, and probing Bloom filters consume cores that update-in-place designs leave idle. On NVMe devices where random 4 KiB reads cost microseconds, the LSM's I/O advantage narrows while its CPU overhead does not.

**The low-write regime.** Below roughly 10–20% writes, the analysis of §4–5 inverts: B-trees' superior read amplification, zero space amplification, and absence of background work dominate. The LSM-tree is a *write-optimized* structure, and deploying it for read-heavy workloads is a category error the RUM conjecture [3] predicts precisely.

**Model assumptions.** Our bounds assume uniform key distributions, stationary workloads, and size-ratio-homogeneous levels. Skew, time-correlated overwrites (which compaction *benefits* from, annihilating versions early), and heterogeneous SSTable sizes all perturb the constants — sometimes favorably, sometimes not.

## 7. Conclusion

The LSM-tree endures because it replaces a *per-update* random I/O tax with a *globally scheduled* sequential merging cost, with precise dials — size ratio, merge discipline, filter allocation, compression — distributing that cost across the RUM triangle [3]. Leveled versus tiered compaction is a choice between Θ(T·log_T(N/B)) and Θ(log_T(N/B)) write amplification, paid in read and space amplification; uniform Bloom filters waste their memory [4]; lazy leveling [5] nearly achieves the best of both; production engines [2][7] validate the theory at scale.

Two frontiers remain. *First*, the B^ε-tree [8] matches LSM write asymptotics with worst-case (not amortized) guarantees — yet none has reached RocksDB's production maturity, suggesting the gap is as much engineering as theory. *Second*, adaptive structures (optimal filters [4], workload-driven tuners) point toward *self-designing* engines that navigate the RUM triangle as workloads drift. The LSM-tree began in 1996 as a batching trick [1]; it is now the substrate for the next generation of adaptive storage.
