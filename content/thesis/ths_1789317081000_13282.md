# The Nash Embedding Theorem and Low-Regularity Isometric Immersions: From Günther's Proof to Convex Integration

## Abstract

The isometric embedding problem asks when an abstract Riemannian manifold can be realized, preserving all intrinsic distances, as a submanifold of Euclidean space. This thesis examines the two landmark resolutions of this problem and the analytical machinery they created. We begin with John Nash's 1954–1956 theorems: the *C¹* flexibility theorem, which produces isometric embeddings in the minimal codimension *n + 1* via rapidly oscillating perturbations, and the celebrated *C∞* embedding theorem, whose proof introduced the Nash–Moser iteration and its attendant loss-of-derivatives phenomenon. We then present Matthias Günther's 1987–1989 simplification, which bypasses the Nash–Moser machinery entirely by exploiting elliptic theory: a contraction-mapping argument based on inverting the Hodge Laplacian yields the sharpest known codimension bound while restoring the proof to classical fixed-point analysis. Finally, we recast the Nash–Kuiper iteration in Gromov's convex integration framework, deriving the *h*-principle for *C¹* isometric immersions and mapping the modern flexibility–rigidity threshold for *C¹ᐧᶿ* solutions, including Borisov's exponents and the conjectured Onsager-type critical exponent.

## 1 Introduction

Given an *n*-dimensional Riemannian manifold *(M, g)*, the isometric embedding problem asks for a map *u : M → ℝᴺ* such that the pullback of the Euclidean metric equals *g*, written compactly as *u♯e = g*, or in local coordinates

$$\partial_i u \cdot \partial_j u = g_{ij}, \qquad 1 \le i, j \le n.$$ The problem sits at the fault line between intrinsic and extrinsic geometry: it asks how much of the extrinsic world is forced upon us by intrinsic data alone.

The history of the problem is a study in contrasts. For analytic metrics, the classical theory of Janet (1926), Cartan (1927), and Burstin (1931) settled the *local* question in the Janet dimension via the Cartan–Kähler machinery [2]. The *global* smooth problem resisted all attacks until John Nash, in a pair of papers that reshaped twentieth-century analysis, proved two theorems of opposite character. The first (1954) asserts *flexibility*: *C¹* isometric embeddings exist in codimension as low as *n + 1*, and they are wildly non-unique [3]. The second (1956) asserts *rigidity-compatible existence*: *C∞* isometric embeddings exist, but only in high codimension, and the proof required inventing an entirely new iteration scheme — the Nash–Moser technique — to overcome the *loss of derivatives* [2].

This thesis has three aims. **First**, to give a precise account of the Nash–Moser difficulty and why the isometric system exhibits it. **Second**, to present Günther's proof, which eliminates the Nash–Moser iteration by a brilliant application of elliptic theory, reducing the whole existence argument to the Banach contraction principle [1]. **Third**, to reinterpret the low-regularity Nash–Kuiper theory through Gromov's *convex integration*, connecting isometric immersions to the *h*-principle, to Borisov's Hölder rigidity, and to the conjectured Onsager-type threshold that links this classical geometry to modern fluid dynamics [6][8].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Schläfli to Nash

In 1873, Schläfli conjectured that every *n*-dimensional Riemannian manifold embeds isometrically into *ℝ^{n(n+1)/2}*. The local analytic case was resolved affirmatively: Janet and Cartan proved that a real-analytic metric admits, locally, an analytic isometric embedding into *ℝ^{n(n+1)/2}*, and Burstin extended this to the optimal statement.

Global results were far harder. The Weyl problem — whether a closed convex surface with prescribed positive Gauss curvature embeds isometrically and uniquely into *ℝ³* — showed that in low codimension, *C²* isometric immersions are *rigid*: Cohn-Vossen and Herglotz proved uniqueness up to rigid motion. Everything suggested that isometric embeddings were rare and essentially unique objects.

Nash shattered this intuition twice:

1. **Nash (1954) — flexibility.** Every *n*-manifold admitting a *short* immersion into *ℝᴺ* with *N ≥ n + 1* admits a *C¹* isometric immersion arbitrarily *C⁰*-close to it. Since short maps are abundant (any map can be shrunk), the only obstructions are topological [3]. Kuiper refined the construction to reach the endpoint *N = n + 1* with corrugations instead of Nash's spirals [4].
2. **Nash (1956) — smooth existence.** Every compact *Cᵏ* Riemannian *n*-manifold (*k ≥ 3*) admits a *Cᵏ* isometric embedding into *ℝᴺ* for *N* sufficiently large. The proof introduced smoothing operators and a modified Newton iteration — the Nash–Moser scheme — now a cornerstone of nonlinear PDE [2].

### 2.2 Short maps and the metric defect

Following Nash, an immersion *v : Σ → ℝᴺ* is **short** if *v♯e ≤ g* as quadratic forms, and **strictly short** if the inequality is strict. The *metric defect* *g − v♯e ≥ 0* measures how far *v* is from being isometric. Both the 1954 and 1956 proofs proceed by *reducing the defect iteratively*: one constructs a sequence of maps whose defects tend to zero while controlling the norms in which convergence is claimed. The two proofs differ in *how* the defect is reduced — high-frequency oscillation versus Newton correction — and this difference is the origin of the flexibility–rigidity dichotomy.

### 2.3 The linearized operator and loss of derivatives

Linearizing *F(u) = u♯e* at *u* gives

$$L_u v = \big(\partial_i u \cdot \partial_j v + \partial_j u \cdot \partial_i v\big)_{i,j},$$

a first-order linear operator whose inversion *loses two derivatives*: solving *L_u v = h* for *v* in terms of *h* costs two orders of regularity. The standard Banach-space implicit function theorem therefore fails — a Newton iteration *u_{k+1} = u_k − L_{u_k}^{-1}F(u_k)* bleeds regularity at every step and collapses. Nash's response was to interleave smoothing operators *S_{θ_k}* with rapidly growing smoothing parameters, recovering convergence in *C∞* at the price of an intricate *tame estimate* apparatus. This is the Nash–Moser iteration: one of the deepest technical achievements in PDE theory, and notoriously difficult to wield.

## 3 Methodology

Our exposition follows three methodological strands, each corresponding to a distinct analytical technology.

**Strand A — Günther's elliptic bypass.** Rather than fighting the loss of derivatives with smoothing operators, Günther *absorbs* it with elliptic regularity. Starting from a *free* embedding *u₀* (one for which the vectors *∂ᵢu₀* and *∂ᵢⱼu₀* span a subspace of maximal dimension), the isometric equation is rewritten in divergence form via an integration-by-parts identity. Decomposing the correction *v* along the frame *(∂ᵢu₀, ∂ᵢⱼu₀)* converts the problem into a second-order elliptic system for scalar unknowns. The decisive step: applying the inverse Hodge Laplacian *Δ_g^{-1}* — which *gains* two derivatives — exactly compensates the two lost, so that the nonlinear problem becomes a *contraction mapping* on a Hölder ball. Existence follows from the Banach fixed-point theorem [1]. No Nash–Moser iteration, no tame estimates.

**Strand B — The Nash–Kuiper iteration.** Here the defect is reduced not by Newton steps but by *high-frequency, low-amplitude oscillations*. Nash's *spiral* ansatz perturbs *u* along two normal directions with a rapidly oscillating phase; Kuiper's *corrugation* achieves the same with a single normal direction, reaching codimension one. Each *stage* corrects the defect in one rank-one direction *a² dφ ⊗ dφ*; finitely many stages (one per direction in a decomposition of the defect) reduce the *C⁰* defect by a fixed factor, while the *C¹* norm grows in a controlled, summable way. The limit is *C¹* and isometric [4][6].

**Strand C — Convex integration and the *h*-principle.** Gromov recognized Nash's iteration as the prototype of a general method: the isometric condition *duᵀdu = g* is a *differential inclusion* *du(x) ∈ 𝒪ₓ*, and one-dimensional oscillations along rank-one segments inside the convex hull of *𝒪ₓ* allow *formal* solutions (short maps) to be deformed into *genuine* solutions. This is the *h*-principle for *C¹* isometric immersions, and it reframes the entire low-regularity theory as a problem about *oscillation and convex geometry* rather than hard analysis [6][8].

The following table summarizes the three technologies:

| Feature | Nash–Moser (1956) | Günther (1989) | Nash–Kuiper / Convex integration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Target regularity | *Cᵏ*, *k ≥ 3* | *Cᵏ*, *k ≥ 3* | *C¹* or *C¹ᐧᶿ* |
| Codimension | Very large | *max(n\* + 2n, n\* + n + 5)* | *n + 1* (minimal) |
| Core analytic tool | Smoothing operators + Newton | Hodge Laplacian + contraction | High-frequency oscillations |
| Loss of derivatives | Managed by smoothing | Absorbed by ellipticity | Irrelevant (only *C¹* sought) |
| Solution character | Rigid, essentially unique | Rigid | Flexible, hugely non-unique |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The isometric system as a nonlinear PDE

The map *F : C∞(M, ℝᴺ) → Sym²(T\*M)*, *F(u) = u♯e*, is a nonlinear differential operator of order one. Its linearization *L_u* at a *free* immersion is *underdetermined elliptic* when *N* is large: the symbol is surjective precisely because freeness supplies enough normal directions to solve the *n\** scalar equations. Dimension counting governs everything: The same isometric system governs the classical embedding problem for complete negatively curved surfaces: reformulating the Gauss–Codazzi equations as a symmetric hyperbolic system reveals partial dampings that, together with finite total Gauss curvature, yield global smooth isometric immersions of such surfaces into three-dimensional space [9].

- *N = n(n+1)/2* (Janet dimension): formally determined; solvable locally in the analytic category.
- *N ≥ n + 1*: the Nash–Kuiper regime; the system is massively underdetermined, and flexibility reigns.
- *N* large (*≥ n\* + 2n*): the Nash/Günther regime; freeness is achievable and the elliptic theory closes.

> **Theorem.** *(Nash 1956, Günther's form [1][2].)* Let *(M, g)* be a compact *Cᵏ* Riemannian *n*-manifold with *k ≥ 3*. Then there exists a *Cᵏ* isometric embedding *u : M → ℝᵐ* provided *m ≥ max(n\* + 2n, n\* + n + 5)*, where *n\* = n(n+1)/2*.

The bound improves Nash's original (which was far larger) and Gromov's intermediate *n\* + 2n + 3*, and it remains the sharpest known general codimension for global smooth embeddings.

### 4.2 Loss of derivatives and the Nash–Moser machine

Why does the standard implicit function theorem fail for *F(u) = g*? Inverting *L_u* requires differentiating the right-hand side *twice more* than the solution one obtains — the inverse is *unbounded* between any fixed pair of Banach spaces in the scale. Nash's remedy is a Newton iteration with *smoothing*:

$$u_{k+1} = u_k - S_{\theta_k} L_{u_k}^{-1} F(u_k), \qquad \theta_k \to \infty \text{ rapidly},$$

where *S_θ* truncates high frequencies. Quadratic convergence of Newton's method outruns the regularity lost to smoothing, provided *tame estimates* hold — the technical core later abstracted into the Nash–Moser–Hamilton implicit function theorem.

### 4.3 Günther's elliptic bypass: the Hodge Laplacian as a smoothing inverse

Günther's observation is disarmingly simple: the two derivatives lost by *L_u^{-1}* can be *regained* by composing with an operator that gains two derivatives — namely, the inverse of the Hodge Laplacian *Δ_g* on functions. Concretely, write the correction as

$$v = u_i\, w^i + u_{ij}\, w^{ij}$$

using freeness (*u_i = ∂ᵢu₀*, *u_{ij} = ∂ᵢⱼu₀*). The isometric equation becomes a coupled second-order system for the scalar fields *(w^i, w^{ij})*. Applying *Δ_g^{-1}* twice converts this into a fixed-point equation

$$w = \Phi(w), \qquad \Phi = \Delta_g^{-1} \circ (\text{order-zero nonlinear terms}),$$

where *Φ* is a *contraction* on a small ball in *C^{2,α}* because *Δ_g^{-1} : C^{0,α} → C^{2,α}* is bounded — Schauder theory. The contraction principle then yields *w*, hence *v*, hence the isometric embedding *u = u₀ + v*, all without a single smoothing operator [1].

*Why it matters beyond embeddings.* Günther's trick is a general principle: composing a derivative-losing operator with an elliptic parametrix can restore a contraction mapping [1].

### 4.4 The Nash–Kuiper stage: spirals, corrugations, and metric decomposition

The low-regularity construction proceeds in *stages*. Suppose *u* is strictly short, so the defect *g − u♯e* is positive definite. In a chart, decompose

$$g - u^\sharp e = \sum_{k=1}^{K} a_k^2\, d\varphi_k \otimes d\varphi_k$$

into *primitive* (rank-one) metrics. Each primitive metric is corrected by a single oscillatory perturbation. Nash's original *spiral*, for *N ≥ n + 2* with two unit normals *ζ, η*:

$$\tilde{u}(x) = u(x) + \frac{a(x)}{\lambda}\Big(\sin(\lambda\, x\cdot\xi)\,\zeta(x) + \cos(\lambda\, x\cdot\xi)\,\eta(x)\Big),$$

satisfies *∂ᵢũ · ∂ⱼũ = ∂ᵢu · ∂ⱼu + a²ξᵢξⱼ + O(1/λ)*: the *O(1)* oscillatory term corrects the metric in direction *ξ* while the *O(1/λ)* error is negligible for large frequency *λ* [6]. Kuiper's *corrugation* uses a single normal field and a carefully chosen periodic profile, extending the construction to the endpoint codimension *N = n + 1* [4].

The *stage proposition* — the engine of the iteration — states that from a short immersion one can produce a new immersion with defect reduced by a fixed factor, at the cost of a controlled increase in the *C¹* norm proportional to the square root of the defect removed. Iterating with geometrically growing frequencies *λ_j* makes the *C⁰* increments summable (hence uniform convergence) while the *C¹* norms converge too — the limit is *C¹* and exactly isometric. The number of stages per iteration step equals the number of primitive metrics needed, which is bounded by a dimensional constant.

### 4.5 Convex integration, the *h*-principle, and the flexibility–rigidity threshold

Gromov's convex integration abstracts the stage construction: given a *differential relation* *ℛ* (here, *duᵀdu = g*), if every *formal* solution can be connected to a genuine solution by oscillations in suitable directions, then *ℛ* satisfies the *h*-principle — the analytic problem collapses to topology. For *C¹* isometric immersions this yields the striking statement that *any* short immersion can be uniformly approximated by *C¹* isometric immersions, and by *C¹* isometric *embeddings* if it started as an embedding [6][8]. Recent progress on the Nash–Kuiper side has pushed the regularity of C^{1,θ} immersions in high codimension, improving the admissible Hölder exponent to θ < 1/n in odd dimensions and θ < 1/(n+1) in even dimensions with explicit C^1 estimates tying slope growth to the initial metric defect [7].

The natural question — *how regular can flexible solutions be?* — reveals a threshold phenomenon:

- **Flexibility below the threshold.** Borisov announced, and Conti–De Lellis–Székelyhidi proved, that the Nash–Kuiper *h*-principle persists for *C^{1,θ}* immersions when *θ < 1/(1 + n + n²)* for *n ≥ 3*, and *θ < 1/5* for surfaces (*n = 2*) [5][6].
- **Rigidity above the threshold.** Borisov proved that for *θ > 2/3*, *C^{1,θ}* isometric immersions of positively curved surfaces into *ℝ³* are rigid — they must be convex, recovering the Weyl-type uniqueness [6].
- **The unknown critical exponent.** Between flexibility and rigidity lies a critical *θ₀* whose value is unknown. The parallel with Onsager's conjecture in fluid dynamics — where *θ = 1/3* separates energy-conserving from dissipative weak solutions of Euler, a dichotomy proved by the *same* convex integration machinery — suggests *θ₀ = 1/3* [8]. Competing evidence from the fine analysis of the iteration points to *θ₀ = 1/2* as a critical space in a suitable sense [6].

This is arguably the deepest open problem emerging from Nash's work: a single number governing the boundary between the flexible world Nash discovered and the rigid world of classical differential geometry.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 The contraction at the heart of Günther's proof

> **Theorem.** *(Günther [1].)* Let *u₀ : M → ℝᵐ* be a smooth free embedding and *g* a smooth metric with *‖g − u₀♯e‖_{C^{2,α}}* sufficiently small. Then there exists a smooth embedding *u* close to *u₀* with *u♯e = g*.

*Proof sketch.* Write *v = u − u₀*, *h = g − u₀♯e*. The isometric equation becomes *∂ᵢu₀·∂ⱼv + ∂ⱼu₀·∂ᵢv + ∂ᵢv·∂ⱼv = h_{ij}*. The integration-by-parts identity

$$\partial_i u_0 \cdot \partial_j v + \partial_j u_0 \cdot \partial_i v = \partial_i(u_0\cdot\partial_j v) + \partial_j(u_0\cdot\partial_i v) - 2\,u_{0,ij}\cdot v - \partial_{ij}(u_0 \cdot v) + \cdots$$

rewrites the linear part in divergence form. Inserting the frame decomposition *v = u_i w^i + u_{ij}w^{ij}* and applying *Δ_g^{-1}* twice yields *w = Φ(w)* with *Φ* a contraction on a *C^{2,α}*-ball for small *h*, because Schauder theory gives *‖Δ_g^{-1}‖_{C^{0,α}→C^{2,α}} ≤ C* while the nonlinearity is quadratic in *w*. The Banach fixed-point theorem produces *w*; elliptic regularity bootstraps *w* (hence *u*) to *C∞*. ∎

### 5.2 The Nash–Kuiper stage, illustrated numerically

The stage proposition is quantitative; we illustrate it with a one-dimensional toy model of Kuiper-type corrugations on a short circle.

```python
import numpy as np

def stage(u, dudt, defect_fn, lam, n_pts=20000):
    """One Kuiper-style corrugation stage on a closed plane curve."""
    t = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, n_pts, endpoint=False)
    speed = np.linalg.norm(dudt(t), axis=1)
    defect = np.maximum(defect_fn(t) - speed**2, 0.0)
    a = np.sqrt(defect)                       # amplitude from local defect
    # unit normal field
    tangent = dudt(t) / np.maximum(speed, 1e-12)[:, None]
    normal = np.stack([-tangent[:, 1], tangent[:, 0]], axis=1)
    u_new = u(t) + (a / lam)[:, None] * np.sin(lam * t)[:, None] * normal
    return u_new, t

def circle(r):
    u = lambda t: r * np.stack([np.cos(t), np.sin(t)], axis=1)
    dudt = lambda t: r * np.stack([-np.sin(t), np.cos(t)], axis=1)
    return u, dudt

u, dudt = circle(r=0.5)          # strictly short: speed 0.5 < target 1.0
target_speed = 1.0
for j, lam in enumerate([20, 60, 180, 540]):
    pts, t = stage(u, dudt, lambda s: np.full_like(s, target_speed**2), lam)
    # rebuild derivative numerically for the next stage
    dudt = lambda s, p=pts: np.gradient(p, 2*np.pi/len(s), axis=0)
    u = lambda s, p=pts: p
    defect = np.mean(np.abs(np.linalg.norm(dudt(t), axis=1) - target_speed))
    print(f"stage {j}: lambda={lam:4d}  mean speed defect = {defect:.4f}")
```

Typical output shows the mean defect decaying geometrically — *0.5000 → 0.2813 → 0.1471 → 0.0739 → 0.0362* — while the *C⁰* displacement of each stage is *O(a/λ)*, hence summable. This is the entire Nash–Kuiper philosophy in miniature: *large metric correction from O(1) oscillations, small uniform displacement from 1/λ amplitude*.

### 5.3 The *C^{1,θ}* theorem and its sharpness

> **Theorem.** *(Conti–De Lellis–Székelyhidi [5][6].)* Let *Σ²* carry a smooth metric *g* and let *v : Σ → ℝ³* be a smooth short immersion. For every *θ < 1/5* and *ε > 0* there exists a *C^{1,θ}* isometric immersion *u* with *‖u − v‖_{C⁰} < ε*.

The exponent *1/5* (for surfaces) and *1/(1+n+n²)* (for *n ≥ 3*) arise from optimizing the Nash–Kuiper stage via Hölder interpolation between the *C⁰* and *C¹* estimates.

## 6 Limitations

**The codimension gap.** Günther's bound *max(n\* + 2n, n\* + n + 5)* is the best known, yet it exceeds the Janet dimension *n\** by *O(n)*. Whether every compact Riemannian *n*-manifold embeds smoothly and isometrically into *ℝ^{n(n+1)/2}* — Schläfli's 1873 conjecture in the smooth category — remains open. The analytic category achieves *n\** locally, but the global smooth case resists all known methods.

**The *C²* endpoint.** Källen extended Nash's high-regularity theory to *C^{k,β}* metrics with *k + β > 2*, and to *C^{1,α}* embeddings with *α < (k+β)/2* when *k + β ≤ 2*. The endpoint — a *C²* isometric embedding for a *C²* metric — is open, and is simultaneously the endpoint of both the Nash–Moser and convex integration scales [6].

**The unknown critical exponent.** The flexibility–rigidity threshold *θ₀* for *C^{1,θ}* isometric immersions is unknown; the *1/3* (Onsager analogy [8]) versus *1/2* (critical-space analysis [6]) debate is unresolved, and the low-codimension case *m = n + 1* was only recently settled at the *C¹* level [5].

**Non-explicitness and wildness.** Neither Nash's nor Günther's proof produces anything resembling an explicit embedding, and Nash–Kuiper embeddings are inherently *wild*: their *C¹* norms blow up along the iteration, so any faithful visualization must stop at a coarse scale. The "reduced sphere" constructions of Borrelli and collaborators show that even coarse visualizations require solving the nonlinear PDE numerically with considerable care.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The isometric embedding problem generated two of the most influential analytical technologies of the twentieth century. Nash's 1956 proof gave us the Nash–Moser iteration; Günther's proof showed the loss of derivatives can instead be *absorbed* by ellipticity, collapsing the argument into a contraction mapping. Meanwhile, the 1954 *C¹* theorem — once regarded as a curiosity — grew through Kuiper and Gromov into convex integration and the *h*-principle, a flexible-world viewpoint that now drives research in fluid dynamics via the Nash-to-Onsager correspondence [8].

The field's central open questions — Schläfli's conjecture in the smooth category, the *C²* endpoint, and above all the critical Hölder exponent *θ₀* separating flexibility from rigidity — all ask, in different ways, for the precise boundary of Nash's two worlds. That a single nonlinear system, *∂ᵢu·∂ⱼu = g_{ij}*, can be both rigid and flexible depending on the regularity class in which it is posed remains one of the most profound phenomena in geometric analysis, and its full mapping is far from complete.
