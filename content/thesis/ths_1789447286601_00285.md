# Large-Eddy Simulation and Subgrid-Scale Closure: Filtered Navier–Stokes Equations, Dynamic Eddy-Viscosity Models, and Wall-Layer Modeling

## Abstract

Large-eddy simulation (LES) occupies a middle ground between direct numerical simulation and Reynolds-averaged closure: it resolves the energy-bearing large eddies and models only the small subgrid scales. This thesis develops the theory of LES from first principles, beginning with the filtered incompressible Navier–Stokes equations and the decomposition of the subgrid-scale (SGS) stress tensor. We derive the classical Smagorinsky eddy-viscosity model, calibrate its constant through Kolmogorov inertial-range theory, and then show how the dynamic procedure of Germano and Lilly removes the need for an *a priori* coefficient by extracting local dissipation information from the smallest resolved scales. The WALE model is analyzed as an algebraic alternative that recovers correct near-wall asymptotic scaling without a dynamic procedure. Because the cost of wall-resolved LES grows prohibitively with Reynolds number, we treat wall-layer modeling — equilibrium laws, non-equilibrium formulations, and the log-layer mismatch — as an integral part of the closure problem. The thesis closes with empirical evidence from canonical flows, theoretical bounds on model performance, and open questions at the interface of physics-based and data-driven closure.

##1 Introduction

Turbulence is the last great unsolved problem of classical physics, but engineering cannot wait for its solution. The incompressible Navier–Stokes equations are believed to describe every turbulent flow of engineering relevance, yet their direct numerical simulation (DNS) requires resolving the full range of scales from the integral length to the Kolmogorov dissipation scale. The cost of DNS grows approximately as the cube of the Reynolds number [1], which places the simulation of an aircraft wing or a gas turbine combustor at realistic Reynolds numbers far beyond any conceivable hardware.

Two classical strategies reduce this cost. *Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes* (RANS) models time-average the equations and model the entire turbulent spectrum; they are cheap but discard the unsteady large-scale structures that drive mixing, noise, separation, and combustion. *Large-eddy simulation* takes the opposite compromise: it directly computes the large, energy-containing eddies and models only the small scales below a filter width, conventionally denoted Δ [2].

> **Theorem (Kolmogorov energy spectrum, 1941).** In the inertial subrange of homogeneous isotropic turbulence at high Reynolds number, the energy spectrum takes the universal form *E(κ) = Cₖ ε^(2/3) κ^(-5/3)*, where *κ* is the wavenumber, *ε* the dissipation rate, and *Cₖ ≈ 1.4* the Kolmogorov constant [3].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the turbulence theory and filtering formalism on which LES rests. Section 3 presents the methodology of SGS modeling, with the filtered equations as the starting point. Section 4 is the deep dive: five subsections on the filtered equations, the Smagorinsky model, the dynamic procedure, the WALE model, and wall modeling. Section 5 collects empirical results and theoretical proofs; Section 6 examines limitations; Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### 2.1 The scale problem in turbulence

A turbulent flow at integral scale *L* and root-mean-square velocity *u′* has a large-scale Reynolds number *Re = u′L/ν*. The Kolmogorov dissipation scale is *η = (ν³/ε)^(1/4)*, and dimensional analysis of the cascade gives the celebrated scale separation *L/η ∼ Re^(3/4)* [3]. DNS must resolve a volume proportional to *(L/η)³ ∼ Re^(9/4)*, and the time step must shrink as the small scales accelerate, leading to total cost scaling near *Re³* — a figure emphasized since the early days of computational fluid dynamics [1].

LES reduces the burden by resolving only scales larger than the filter width Δ. The number of degrees of freedom drops from *(L/η)³* to *(L/Δ)³*, and since Δ ≫ η at practical Reynolds numbers, the savings are enormous: five to six orders of magnitude for a boundary layer at *Re* of a million. The price paid is the *closure problem*: filtering the nonlinear convective term generates an unknown subgrid-scale stress that must be modeled.

### 2.2 Filtering as a mathematical operation

Filtering is a convolution with a kernel *G* of width Δ:

*ūᵢ(x,t) = ∫ G(x − x′, Δ) uᵢ(x′,t) dx′*

over the whole domain. Common filters include the box (top-hat) filter, the Gaussian filter, and the spectral cutoff filter. ### 2.3 Historical lineage

- **1963** — Smagorinsky proposes the first SGS model for atmospheric circulation: eddy viscosity proportional to the local strain rate times the square of the grid spacing [4].
- **1967** — Lilly analyzes the Smagorinsky model for isotropic turbulence and derives the theoretical constant *Cₛ ≈ 0.17* from the Kolmogorov spectrum [5].
- **1991** — Germano, Piomelli, Moin, and Cabot introduce the dynamic model: the coefficient is computed from the resolved field itself via the Germano identity [6].
- **1992** — Lilly refines the dynamic procedure with a least-squares contraction, making it robust in practice [7].
- **1999** — Nicoud and Ducros propose the WALE model, recovering correct near-wall scaling algebraically [8].
- **1993 onward** — Wall modeling develops from early proposals through equilibrium and non-equilibrium formulations used in modern solvers [9].

##3 Methodology

Our methodology is theoretical analysis combined with *a priori* and *a posteriori* validation:

1. **Derivation.** Starting from the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, we apply a spatial filter and isolate the SGS stress tensor. Each model is derived from explicit physical hypotheses (mixing-length analogy, scale similarity, local equilibrium).
2. **Calibration.** Model constants are fixed by matching to the Kolmogorov inertial-range spectrum or to canonical DNS databases, never by tuning to the target flow.
3. ***A priori* testing.** Model predictions for the SGS stress are compared against filtered DNS fields to measure *structural* accuracy — correlation coefficients between modeled and true stresses.
4. ***A posteriori* testing.** The model is run inside an actual LES and statistics (mean profiles, spectra, skin friction) are compared to experiment or DNS to measure *functional* accuracy.
5. **Asymptotic analysis.** Near-wall behavior is checked analytically: a correct SGS model must reproduce the cubic scaling of eddy viscosity with wall distance in the viscous sublayer [8].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The filtered Navier–Stokes equations

Filtering the incompressible momentum equation yields

*∂ūᵢ/∂t + ∂(ūᵢūⱼ)/∂xⱼ = −(1/ρ)∂p̄/∂xᵢ + ν ∂²ūᵢ/∂xⱼ∂xⱼ − ∂τᵢⱼ/∂xⱼ*

with the continuity constraint *∂ūᵢ/∂xᵢ = 0*. The new term is the SGS stress

*τᵢⱼ = (uᵢuⱼ)⁻ − ūᵢūⱼ*

which represents the effect of the unresolved scales on the resolved motion. The deviatoric part of *τᵢⱼ* must be modeled; the isotropic part *τₖₖ/3* is absorbed into a modified pressure [2].

The Germano decomposition further splits the SGS stress into three physically distinct contributions:

| Component | Definition | Physical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Leonard stress | *Lᵢⱼ = (ūᵢūⱼ)⁻ − ū̄ᵢū̄ⱼ* | Interaction of resolved scales among themselves |
| Cross stress | *Cᵢⱼ = (ūᵢu′ⱼ)⁻ + (u′ᵢūⱼ)⁻* | Interaction between resolved and subgrid scales |
| Reynolds SGS stress | *Rᵢⱼ = (u′ᵢu′ⱼ)⁻* | Interaction of subgrid scales among themselves |

Here *u′ᵢ = uᵢ − ūᵢ* is the subgrid velocity. Only the sum *τᵢⱼ = Lᵢⱼ + Cᵢⱼ + Rᵢⱼ* is Galilean invariant, and it is the sum that eddy-viscosity models target [6].

### 4.2 The Smagorinsky model and eddy viscosity

By analogy with Prandtl's mixing-length hypothesis, Smagorinsky proposed that the deviatoric SGS stress be aligned with the resolved strain-rate tensor:

*τᵢⱼ − (1/3)τₖₖδᵢⱼ = −2νₜS̄ᵢⱼ*

where *S̄ᵢⱼ = (1/2)(∂ūᵢ/∂xⱼ + ∂ūⱼ/∂xᵢ)* is the filtered strain rate and the eddy viscosity is

*νₜ = (CₛΔ)²|S̄|*, with *|S̄| = √(2S̄ᵢⱼS̄ᵢⱼ)*

The model is purely dissipative: the SGS dissipation *Π = −τᵢⱼS̄ᵢⱼ = 2νₜS̄ᵢⱼS̄ᵢⱼ ≥ 0* removes energy from the resolved scales at exactly the rate the cascade would [4][5].

> **Theorem (Lilly's calibration, 1967).** Assume a spectral cutoff filter with cutoff wavenumber *κc = π/Δ* lying in the inertial subrange, and assume the SGS dissipation balances the spectral energy flux *ε*. Then *Cₛ = (1/π)(2/(3Cₖ))^(3/4)*, which evaluates to *Cₛ ≈ 0.17* for *Cₖ ≈ 1.4* [5].

In practice, values between 0.1 and 0.2 are used; *Cₛ ≈ 0.1* is common in wall-bounded shear flows [10]. Near a solid wall, the model is far too dissipative unless corrected, because the strain rate does not vanish at the wall while true subgrid fluctuations do. The standard remedy is Van Driest damping, multiplying the length scale by *1 − exp(−y⁺/A⁺)* with *A⁺ = 26*, where *y⁺* is the wall-normal coordinate in viscous units [10].

Three structural defects of the Smagorinsky model deserve emphasis:

- **Over-dissipation in laminar and transitional regions.** The model produces eddy viscosity wherever the strain rate is nonzero, including laminar shear, suppressing transition. It cannot distinguish turbulence from organized strain.
- **No backscatter.** Real turbulence occasionally transfers energy from small to large scales; the Smagorinsky model only drains energy forward.
- **Wrong near-wall asymptotics.** Without damping, the eddy viscosity stays finite at the wall, whereas the correct behavior is cubic decay with wall distance [8].

### 4.3 The dynamic procedure and the Germano identity

The dynamic model of Germano et al. eliminates the *a priori* constant by using the resolved scales themselves as a test bed [6]. Apply a second, coarser *test filter* (denoted by a caret, width *αΔ* with *α ≈ 2*) to the filtered equations. The SGS stress at the test-filter level is *Tᵢⱼ = (uᵢuⱼ)̂ − ûᵢûⱼ*, and the two stress levels are related by the **Germano identity**:

*Lᵢⱼ = Tᵢⱼ − τ̂ᵢⱼ*

where *Lᵢⱼ = (ūᵢūⱼ)̂ − ûᵢûⱼ* is the resolved Leonard stress, computable entirely from the resolved field.

If both *τᵢⱼ* and *Tᵢⱼ* are modeled with the Smagorinsky form using the *same* coefficient *C*, the identity becomes an equation for *C*:

*Lᵢⱼ − (1/3)Lₖₖδᵢⱼ = −2C Mᵢⱼ*

with *Mᵢⱼ = α²Δ²|Ŝ|Ŝᵢⱼ − (Δ²|S̄|S̄ᵢⱼ)̂*. Lilly's least-squares contraction gives the celebrated result [7]:

*C = ⟨LᵢⱼMᵢⱼ⟩ / ⟨MₖₗMₖₗ⟩*

where *⟨·⟩* denotes averaging over homogeneous directions (or, in modern local formulations, Lagrangian averaging along pathlines). The consequences are profound:

1. *C* adapts to the local flow: it vanishes in laminar regions, permitting transition; it decreases near walls, recovering approximately correct asymptotics without damping functions [6].
2. Negative values of *C* are clipped (or allowed in controlled backscatter formulations), since sustained negative eddy viscosity is numerically unstable.
3. The only input is the filter ratio *α*; everything else is extracted from the simulation itself [7].

The dynamic procedure roughly doubles the cost of evaluating the SGS terms and requires care with averaging, but it transformed LES from a tuned technique into a predictive one [10].

### 4.4 The WALE model and structure-based closures

Nicoud and Ducros challenged the premise that the strain-rate magnitude is the right velocity-scale estimator [8]. DNS of isotropic turbulence had shown that turbulent activity concentrates in regions of both high strain *and* high rotation. The WALE (Wall-Adapting Local Eddy-viscosity) model builds its eddy viscosity from the square of the velocity-gradient tensor *ḡᵢⱼ = ∂ūᵢ/∂xⱼ*:

*Sᵢⱼᵈ = (1/2)(ḡᵢⱼ² + ḡⱼᵢ²) − (1/3)δᵢⱼḡₖₖ²*

with *ḡᵢⱼ² = ḡᵢₖḡₖⱼ*. The eddy viscosity is

*νₜ = (C𝓌Δ)² (SᵢⱼᵈSᵢⱼᵈ)^(3/2) / ((S̄ᵢⱼS̄ᵢⱼ)^(5/2) + (SᵢⱼᵈSᵢⱼᵈ)^(5/4))*

with *C𝓌 ≈ 0.325*. The key properties:

- **Correct wall scaling.** In the viscous sublayer, the invariant built from *Sᵢⱼᵈ* decays as the fourth power of wall distance while the strain invariant stays finite, so *νₜ* decays cubically — automatically, with no damping functions and no dynamic procedure [8].
- **Zero in pure shear.** For laminar shear, the invariant *SᵢⱼᵈSᵢⱼᵈ* vanishes, so the model is inactive in laminar regions and handles transition naturally.
- **Rotation sensitivity.** Because *Sᵢⱼᵈ* contains both strain and rotation invariants, the model responds to vortical structures that pure strain-rate models miss [11].

Related algebraic alternatives include the Vreman model and the σ-model, which share the goal of correct near-wall behavior without test filtering [12].

### 4.5 Wall modeling: the cost bottleneck

Wall-resolved LES must place the first grid point at *y⁺ ≈ 1* and resolve near-wall streaks; the number of points grows nearly as *Re^(13/7)* for a flat-plate boundary layer, barely better than DNS [9]. Wall-modeled LES (WMLES) instead resolves only the outer layer and models the inner layer, typically by one of two approaches:

- **Equilibrium wall models.** Assume the inner layer obeys the logarithmic law *u⁺ = (1/κ) ln y⁺ + B* with *κ ≈ 0.41*, and impose the resulting wall shear stress as a boundary condition on the outer LES [9].
- **Non-equilibrium wall models.** Solve simplified boundary-layer (TBLE) equations on an embedded near-wall mesh, capturing pressure-gradient and unsteady effects [9].

The persistent pathology of WMLES is the **log-layer mismatch**: an artificial shift in the logarithmic profile arising from inconsistent treatment of the modeled and resolved stresses at the matching height. Modern formulations reduce it through careful eddy-viscosity blending and stochastic forcing, but it remains an active research frontier [13].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Canonical validation: channel flow

Turbulent channel flow at friction Reynolds numbers from a few hundred to several thousand is the workhorse of LES validation. Key findings:

1. Dynamic Smagorinsky LES reproduces the mean velocity profile and Reynolds stresses of DNS, with the peak streamwise fluctuation near *y⁺ = 12* and streak spacing *λz⁺ ≈ 100*, matching experiment [6].
2. The dynamic coefficient self-adjusts to near zero at the wall, reproducing the effect of Van Driest damping without any empirical input [6][7].
3. WALE-based LES of periodic pipe flow captures transition and near-wall statistics with a static coefficient, confirming the algebraic wall-adaptation mechanism [8].

### 5.2 Spectra and the filter cutoff

In forced homogeneous isotropic turbulence, well-resolved LES with the Smagorinsky model reproduces the Kolmogorov *κ^(−5/3)* spectrum up to the cutoff wavenumber, with a pile-up or deficit near the cutoff that diagnoses the model's dissipation characteristics [5]. ### 5.3 A structural result: realizability

> **Theorem (Realizability of eddy-viscosity closures).** An eddy-viscosity model with *νₜ ≥ 0* guarantees non-negative SGS dissipation *Π ≥ 0*, and hence cannot produce unbounded growth of resolved kinetic energy through the model term alone.

> **Proof.** The modeled deviatoric stress is *τᵢⱼᵈ = −2νₜS̄ᵢⱼ*. The SGS energy transfer is *Π = −τᵢⱼS̄ᵢⱼ = −τᵢⱼᵈS̄ᵢⱼ = 2νₜS̄ᵢⱼS̄ᵢⱼ ≥ 0* whenever *νₜ ≥ 0*. Since the remaining terms in the resolved kinetic-energy equation are conservative or molecularly dissipative, the model term cannot inject energy. ∎

This explains the legendary numerical robustness of the Smagorinsky model — and simultaneously its structural limitation: true SGS stresses exhibit backscatter a substantial fraction of the time in *a priori* tests, which no purely dissipative model can represent [2].

### 5.4 Cost scaling

A Python estimate illustrates why wall modeling matters:

```python
import numpy as np

def grid_points(Re, mode="dns"):
    """Order-of-magnitude grid-point scaling with Reynolds number."""
    if mode == "dns":
        return Re ** (9/4)          # (L/eta)^3
    if mode == "wall_resolved_les":
        return Re ** (13/7)         # Chapman estimate, flat plate
    if mode == "wall_modeled_les":
        return Re ** 1.0            # outer-layer scaling
    raise ValueError(mode)

for Re in [1e4, 1e5, 1e6, 1e7]:
    print(f"Re={Re:.0e}: DNS={grid_points(Re,'dns'):.1e}  "
          f"WRLES={grid_points(Re,'wall_resolved_les'):.1e}  "
          f"WMLES={grid_points(Re,'wall_modeled_les'):.1e}")
```

At *Re = 10⁷*, DNS needs on the order of *10¹⁶* points while WMLES needs on the order of *10⁷* — the difference between impossible and routine [9].

##6 Limitations

1. **Commutation errors.** Filtering does not commute with differentiation on non-uniform grids or near boundaries; the resulting commutation terms are usually neglected but can dominate in complex geometries [2].
2. **The dynamic procedure's fragility.** Without averaging or clipping, the dynamic coefficient fluctuates wildly and can destabilize the simulation. Lagrangian dynamic models mitigate this but add complexity [7].
3. **Backscatter is unrepresented.** Pure eddy-viscosity models cannot transfer energy upscale; stochastic and deconvolution models address this at the cost of robustness.
4. **Transition and relaminarization.** While the dynamic and WALE models handle transition better than Smagorinsky, none predicts bypass transition from first principles.
5. **Wall modeling remains the pacing item.** Log-layer mismatch, non-equilibrium separation, and rough walls are incompletely solved; WMLES of a full aircraft at flight Reynolds number is still a grand-challenge computation [13].
6. **Implicit vs. explicit filtering.** Most practical LES never applies an explicit filter; the grid and numerics act as an implicit filter, entangling modeling error with discretization error in ways that are difficult to disentangle [12].

---

##7 Conclusion

Large-eddy simulation rests on a single physical bet: that the small scales of turbulence are sufficiently universal to be modeled by simple local closures, while the large scales — the ones that carry the engineering consequences — are computed directly. The Smagorinsky model made that bet quantitative; Lilly's calibration rooted it in Kolmogorov theory; the Germano–Lilly dynamic procedure made the coefficient an output rather than an input; and the WALE model showed that careful tensor invariants could recover near-wall physics algebraically. Wall modeling extends the same philosophy to the inner layer, trading resolution for modeling where the physics is quasi-universal.

The field's center of gravity is shifting toward closures that respect more of the true SGS physics — scale similarity, backscatter, anisotropy — while retaining the robustness that made eddy viscosity ubiquitous, and toward wall models that survive separation and strong pressure gradients. Whether the next leap comes from better mathematics or from data-driven discovery, the filtered Navier–Stokes equations will remain the arena in which every candidate closure must prove itself.
