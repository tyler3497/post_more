# Mechanistic Interpretability of Transformers: Circuits, Superposition, and Sparse Autoencoders

## Abstract

Mechanistic interpretability aims to reverse engineer trained neural networks into human-comprehensible algorithms, treating the model as compiled code to be decompiled into source. This thesis surveys the foundations and frontiers of mechanistic interpretability for transformers: the mathematical circuits framework that decomposes attention into virtual QK and OV circuits [1]; landmark circuit discoveries including induction heads [2] and the indirect-object-identification circuit in GPT-2 small [3]; the superposition hypothesis, which explains polysemanticity as networks packing more features than neurons into activation space [4]; and the sparse-autoencoder paradigm that extracts monosemantic feature dictionaries from trained models, scaling from one-layer models [5][6] toward frontier systems. We formalize probing versus causal methodologies—activation patching, attribution patching [7], and automated circuit discovery—and the criteria of faithfulness, completeness, and minimality by which circuit explanations are evaluated [3]. We conclude with the field's central limitations: the linearity assumptions underlying dictionary learning, the identifiability problem of multiple consistent explanations, and the scaling gap between toy circuits and the full behavioral envelope of large models.

## 1 Introduction

Language models are simultaneously the most capable and the most opaque artifacts in machine learning. We possess their complete parameters—billions of floating point numbers—and yet cannot answer, in any satisfying sense, *how* they work. Behavioral evaluation probes the input–output mapping; it treats the model as a black box and can never, by itself, tell us *why* a behavior arises or *whether it will generalize* [1].

**Mechanistic interpretability** (MI) pursues a different standard of understanding: reverse engineering the detailed computations performed by the network, analogous to decompiling a binary into human-readable source code. If successful, it would enable systematic auditing of capabilities, anticipation of failures, and targeted model editing—benefits that black-box evaluation cannot provide [1][5].

The field rests on two methodological traditions. The older, *circuits* program—originating in vision models and ported to transformers [1]—seeks end-to-end algorithmic explanations of specific behaviors, naming individual heads and neurons as components of a circuit. The newer, *features-first* program argues that the correct unit of analysis is not the neuron but the *feature*: a direction in activation space that the network represents in *superposition*, and which can be recovered by sparse dictionary learning [4][5].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the mathematical framework of transformer circuits and the superposition hypothesis. Section 3 describes the core methodologies: activation patching, attribution patching, and sparse autoencoders. Section 4 dives into landmark results—induction heads, the IOI circuit, toy models of superposition, and scaling sparse autoencoders to frontier models. Section 5 presents empirical results and theoretical proofs. Section 6 examines limitations, including interpretability illusions and the identifiability problem.

> **Theorem:** A reverse-engineered explanation is only as good as the causal evidence that grounds it; correlation between a component's activity and model behavior establishes *probes*, not *circuits*.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Behavioral versus Mechanistic Interpretability

Interpretability methods fall on a spectrum from *behavioral* to *mechanistic*:

| Paradigm | Question asked | Example method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Behavioral | What does the model do? | Benchmarks, red-teaming | Scales to any model | Cannot explain *why* |
| Probing | What information is present? | Linear probes | Localizes representations | Correlational only |
| Mechanistic | What computation occurs? | Activation patching [7] | Causal grounding | Expensive, partial |
| Features-first | What are the atoms of representation? | Sparse autoencoders [5][6] | Universal ontology | Approximate |

Linear probes decode information from activations but suffer a fundamental ambiguity: a probe may detect information the model never uses [1]. Mechanistic methods resolve this by *intervention*—changing an activation and observing the effect on behavior—which is why causal patching has become the field's gold standard [3][7].

### 2.2 The Mathematical Framework of Transformer Circuits

The residual stream of a transformer provides a shared communication channel: every attention head and MLP block *reads* from it and *writes* to it [1]. Elhage et al. showed that in attention-only models, each head decomposes into two *virtual circuits* [1]:

- **The QK circuit**, $W_E^\top W_Q W_K^\top W_E$, determines *where* the head attends—its attention pattern as a function of token embeddings.
- **The OV circuit**, $W_U W_O W_V W_E$, determines *what* information is moved from attended tokens into the output logits.

Three forms of *composition* between heads structure multi-layer computation: Q-composition (one head's output controls another's queries), K-composition (controls keys), and V-composition (controls values) [1]. A **circuit** is then a subgraph of these components sufficient to implement an algorithm.

> **Theorem:** In an attention-only transformer, the attention pattern of head $h$ at position $i$ attending to position $j$ is governed (up to the softmax and layer-norm factors) by the bilinear form $x_i^\top W_Q W_K^\top x_j$, so head behavior is fully determined by the QK and OV circuits and the residual-stream vectors they act on.

### 2.3 Polysemanticity and the Superposition Hypothesis

Individual neurons in trained networks are typically *polysemantic*: a single neuron responds to mixtures of seemingly unrelated inputs—for instance, cat faces and car fronts in vision models, or academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests, and Korean text in a small language model [5]. Polysemanticity blocks the neuron-as-unit analysis.

The **superposition hypothesis** [4] offers an explanation: networks represent *more features than they have neurons* by encoding features as directions in activation space rather than as individual neurons. When features are *sparse* (rarely active together), a model can embed $m$ features in $n < m$ dimensions because compressed-sensing-like geometry lets a downstream readout disambiguate them. Polysemanticity is then not noise but an *efficient compression scheme*: the interference terms are small whenever the represented features are sparse [4].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Probing versus Causal Methods

Methods are distinguished by what they license us to conclude:

1. **Probing** — train a classifier $f(\text{activations}) \to \text{property}$. Establishes that information is *decodable*, not that it is *used*.
2. **Activation patching** — replace activations at some component with those from a counterfactual input; measure the change in a behavioral metric. Establishes *causal* dependence [3][7].
3. **Attribution patching** — a linearized approximation to activation patching using gradient-times-activation products, orders of magnitude cheaper, enabling automated circuit discovery at scale [7].
4. **Automated circuit discovery (ACDC)** — greedy edge-removal over the computational graph, keeping only edges whose removal degrades the task metric [7].

The relationship is one of increasing causal rigor at increasing computational cost. Attribution patching [7] emerged precisely because exact patching of every edge in a large model is infeasible, and ACDC-style methods require efficient edge scoring.

### 3.2 Sparse Autoencoders as Dictionary Learning

A sparse autoencoder (SAE) learns an *overcomplete* dictionary of features from model activations. Given activations $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, an SAE learns encoder $f$ and decoder with dictionary directions $d_1, \dots, d_m$ ($m \gg n$) such that

```python
def sae_forward(x, W_enc, b_enc, W_dec, b_dec):
    f = relu(x @ W_enc + b_enc)          # sparse feature activations, f in R^m
    x_hat = f @ W_dec + b_dec            # reconstruction, x_hat in R^n
    loss = ((x - x_hat) ** 2).sum() + lmbda * f.abs().sum()
    return f, x_hat, loss
```

The reconstruction term forces fidelity to the original activations; the $L_1$ penalty forces *sparsity* so that each activation is explained by a small set of features. Under the superposition hypothesis, the true features are a sparse linear combination of neuron directions, and the SAE recovers the overcomplete basis the model itself is using [4][5].

### 3.3 Evaluating Explanations: Faithfulness, Completeness, Minimality

Wang et al. proposed three quantitative criteria for circuit explanations [3]:

- **Faithfulness**: the circuit alone (with all other components ablated) performs the task as well as the full model.
- **Completeness**: the circuit contains *all* nodes used by the model for the task—knocking out the circuit should destroy the behavior.
- **Minimality**: every node in the circuit matters—removing any single node should measurably degrade performance.

These criteria operationalize the difference between a *story* and an *explanation*. Most published circuits satisfy faithfulness but struggle with completeness: models are rife with *backup* and *redundant* mechanisms that only appear under ablation [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Induction Heads and the Origin of In-Context Learning

**Induction heads** implement the pattern-completion algorithm $[A][B]\dots[A] \to [B]$: attend to the token following the previous occurrence of the current token, and copy it forward [2]. Mechanistically, an induction head is composed of a *previous-token head* (which shifts information one position forward) followed by the induction head proper, which performs prefix matching via K-composition [1][2].

Olsson et al. [2] presented six lines of evidence that induction heads are the mechanistic source of most *in-context learning*—the decreasing loss at increasing token indices that underlies few-shot prompting:

1. Induction heads form at precisely the training step where in-context learning ability undergoes a phase change, visible as a *bump* in the loss curve.
2. Perturbing the architecture so the bump moves shifts induction-head formation with it.
3. Ablating induction heads in small attention-only models destroys in-context learning while leaving other capabilities intact.
4. The phase change replicates across model scales and data distributions.

> **Theorem:** In-context learning is not an emergent mystery but, to first order, the compositional output of a specific, localizable circuit motif—the induction head—whose developmental trajectory can be tracked through training.

### 4.2 The Indirect Object Identification Circuit

Wang et al. [3] performed the largest end-to-end reverse engineering of a natural behavior in a language model: the **indirect object identification (IOI)** task, e.g. *"When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to ___"*, which GPT-2 small completes with *Mary*. Their explanation spans 26 attention heads in 7 functional classes [3]:

| Head class | Role in the algorithm |
|---|---|
| Duplicate token heads | Detect the repeated name (the subject S) |
| S-inhibition heads | Suppress attention to the duplicated subject |
| Name mover heads | Copy the remaining name (the indirect object) to the output |
| Previous token heads | Route positional information forward |
| Induction heads | Support name-tracking via prefix matching |
| Backup name movers | Redundant copying paths revealed by ablation |
| Negative name movers | Suppress incorrect completions |

The circuit was validated with activation patching and knockout experiments, and scored on the faithfulness–completeness–minimality criteria [3]. The IOI work remains the canonical demonstration that *natural* behaviors of *trained* models admit algorithmic decomposition—and the canonical warning that completeness is hard: backup heads mean the "true" circuit is larger than the minimal one [3].

### 4.3 Toy Models of Superposition

Elhage et al. [4] constructed minimal models in which superposition can be *fully understood*. The setup: an autoencoder $f(x) = \mathrm{ReLU}(W^\top W x + b)$ with $m$ hidden features and $n < m$ neurons, trained to reconstruct sparse feature vectors $x \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Key findings:

- **Phase change**: as feature sparsity increases, the model transitions sharply from representing features *orthogonally* (one feature per neuron) to representing them in *superposition* (overlapping directions).
- **Geometry**: the learned feature directions arrange themselves like vertices of uniform polytopes—e.g., in 2D, the optimal arrangement of 3 features is an equilateral triangle—minimizing interference under the sparsity prior [4].
- **Nonlinearity is load-bearing**: the linear model cannot exhibit superposition; the ReLU's ability to suppress small interference terms is what makes the compressed representation decodable.
- **Adversarial connection**: superposition creates *interference* between features, which the authors link to the existence of adversarial examples—small perturbations along an interfering direction corrupt a feature's readout [4].

```python
# Core toy model: m features, n neurons (n < m), sparse inputs
m, n = 20, 5
W = torch.randn(n, m) / math.sqrt(n)          # embedding: n-dim per feature
x = sparse_features(batch, m, sparsity=0.9)  # most features inactive
h = torch.relu(x @ W.T @ W + b)              # bottleneck through n neurons
x_hat = h @ W.T                              # decode back to feature space
```

### 4.4 Sparse Autoencoders: From Toy Models to Frontier Models

Bricken et al. [5] operationalized dictionary learning on a real (one-layer) transformer: training SAEs on the MLP activations of a 512-neuron model over 8 billion tokens, with expansion factors from $1\times$ to $256\times$ (up to 131,072 learned features). The resulting features were strikingly *monosemantic*—separately representing DNA sequences, legal language, HTTP requests, Hebrew text, and nutrition statements—where the underlying neurons had been hopeless mixtures [5]. Crucially, the same features reappeared across independently trained models, evidence they reflect structure in the *data distribution* rather than training accidents [5].

Cunningham et al. [6] independently demonstrated that SAE features enable *precise model editing*: ablating the features responsible for pronoun prediction removed that capability while disrupting overall behavior less than neuron-level ablations, and SAE features pinpointed the causally responsible components of the IOI circuit [3] at finer resolution than prior decompositions [6]. Subsequent scaling work extended the paradigm to production-scale models—most notably, extracting millions of features from Claude 3 Sonnet—confirming that the dictionary-learning approach survives the jump from toy to frontier scale [5].

### 4.5 The Limits of Linear Features

The SAE program rests on the **linear representation hypothesis**: that features correspond to *directions* in activation space and combine *linearly*. This is an idealization. Known challenges include:

- **Feature splitting and absorption**: as dictionary size grows, a single coherent concept may split across multiple latents, or a broad latent may absorb narrower ones, making the "feature inventory" scale-dependent rather than canonical.
- **Nonlinear features**: some computations (e.g., in MLPs with SiLU/GELU nonlinearities) may not decompose into linear directions at all; the superposition story is cleanest for the residual stream.
- **The identifiability problem**: many different dictionaries can reconstruct the same activations with equal fidelity—sparsity alone does not uniquely pick out "the" features the model uses.

These concerns motivate the critiques of Section 6: SAEs give us *a* decomposition, and a useful one, but the claim that it is *the* decomposition requires stronger causal validation than reconstruction loss provides.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Developmental Evidence for Induction Heads

The induction-head phase change is among the most replicated developmental findings in MI. Across model sizes, the training step at which prefix-matching scores of heads jump coincides with the loss-curve bump marking the onset of in-context learning [2]. The causal variant of the experiment—shifting the bump by architectural intervention and watching induction-head formation track it—rules out mere correlation [2].

### 5.2 Quantitative Validation of the IOI Circuit

The IOI circuit's 26 heads were evaluated against the three criteria [3]. The circuit alone recovered the majority of the full model's logit difference between the correct and incorrect name (faithfulness); ablating the circuit while leaving the rest of the model intact destroyed IOI performance (completeness, modulo backup heads); and individual head knockouts showed graded, interpretable degradations (minimality, with redundancy concentrated in the backup name movers) [3].

### 5.3 The Superposition Phase Transition

In the toy models, plotting the reconstruction loss and the learned geometry $W^\top W$ as a function of feature sparsity reveals a sharp transition: below a critical sparsity, features are represented orthogonally; above it, they collapse into superposition with polytope-like geometry [4]. This is a genuine *phase change* in the learned solution, not a gradual interpolation—suggesting that superposition is a discrete regime of network computation with its own geometry.

### 5.4 Scaling Laws of Dictionary Learning

Empirically, SAE quality follows predictable scaling: reconstruction fidelity improves with dictionary size (expansion factor) and training tokens, while the fraction of interpretable features rises [5]. At frontier scale, millions of extracted features include fine-grained concepts (specific error messages, code idioms, biomedical entities) alongside the broad features found in small models—evidence that the feature ontology of large models is richly hierarchical [5].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Correlation is not causation, even with patches.** Activation patching establishes causal *dependence* on a component, but patching with out-of-distribution activations can produce misleading effects; the choice of baseline (zero ablation, mean ablation, counterfactual patch) changes conclusions, and *interpretability illusions*—where a method reports structure that is an artifact of the method—are well documented.
2. **Completeness remains elusive.** Backup and redundant mechanisms mean minimal circuits understate the model's true machinery; Wang et al. themselves found their IOI circuit incomplete under stringent tests [3].
3. **The linear representation hypothesis is unproven.** If features are not linear directions, dictionary learning recovers a useful fiction rather than the model's ontology (Section 4.5).
4. **Evaluation of explanations is circular.** Automated interpretability scoring (using one model to explain another's features) inherits the evaluator's own biases; there is no ground-truth feature inventory for frontier models against which to calibrate.
5. **Scaling gap.** The most complete circuit (IOI) covers 26 heads in a 144-head model on a narrow task [3]. Extrapolating from task-specific circuits to a full account of a frontier model's behavior remains speculative by orders of magnitude.
6. **Superposition may be fundamental, not incidental.** If networks *intrinsically* compute in superposition [4], then any fixed feature inventory is an approximation, and perfect monosemantic decomposition may be unattainable in principle.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Mechanistic interpretability has progressed from a research program to a discipline with genuine results: the circuits framework gave us the language of QK/OV decomposition and composition [1]; induction heads gave us a mechanistic account of in-context learning [2]; the IOI circuit proved that natural behaviors in trained models admit algorithmic reverse engineering with quantitative validation [3]; the superposition hypothesis explained *why* neurons are polysemantic [4]; and sparse autoencoders turned that explanation into an engineering discipline that now scales to frontier models [5][6]. The methodological frontier—attribution patching and automated discovery [7]—is steadily lowering the cost of causal evidence.

Yet the field's hardest problems are conceptual rather than technical: the identifiability of feature inventories, the evaluation of explanations without ground truth, and the gap between task-specific circuits and whole-model understanding. Whether mechanistic interpretability can mature from illuminating case studies into a *science of model internals*—with the predictive and interventional power that science implies—remains the open question on which the safety case for advanced AI may depend.
