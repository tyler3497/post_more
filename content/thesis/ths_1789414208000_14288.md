# Turbulence Closure Modeling: the k–ε Hierarchy, Large-Eddy Subgrid Models, and Data-Driven Closures

## Abstract

The closure problem of turbulence — the appearance of unknown fluctuation correlations in the averaged equations of motion — remains the central obstacle of computational fluid dynamics. This article surveys the three dominant closure paradigms. First, the Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) eddy-viscosity hierarchy culminating in the Jones–Launder k–ε model and its descendants (RNG, realizable, k–ω SST), which remain the workhorses of industrial simulation. Second, large-eddy simulation (LES) subgrid-scale closures, from the Smagorinsky model through the Germano–Lilly dynamic procedure and its localized and Lagrangian variants. Third, the emerging class of data-driven closures, in which machine learning infers stress–strain constitutive relations from direct numerical simulation data. Each paradigm is developed from first principles: derivation of the transport equations, the physical assumptions that close them, calibration of the empirical constants against canonical flows, and the failure modes that motivated the next generation. A critical synthesis weighs accuracy, cost, and generalizability, and argues that trustworthy closures must respect invariance, realizability, and the energy-cascade physics codified by Kolmogorov, whatever the expressive machinery behind them.

## 1 Introduction

In 1895 Osborne Reynolds decomposed the velocity of a turbulent fluid into a mean part and a fluctuation part, and showed that the averaged equations of motion contain the divergence of a new symmetric tensor — the Reynolds stress — built from correlations of the fluctuations themselves [1]. That single observation created a research program that has now run for more than a century: the equations of averaged motion are not closed. Every statistical level of description spawns new unknown correlations at the next level, a regress that has no end. A *closure model* is any prescription that truncates this hierarchy by expressing unknown correlations in terms of known, computable quantities.

> **Theorem:** *Closure impossibility.* No finite set of equations for velocity moments of order n or below closes without an external hypothesis. Averaging the Navier–Stokes equations produces terms of order n+1 at every n, so closure requires a modeled relation that cannot be derived from the averaged system alone.

The practical stakes are enormous. Direct numerical simulation (DNS), which resolves every scale of motion, costs roughly proportional to Re³, and remains infeasible for engineering Reynolds numbers above about one million. Everything else is closure:

- **Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS):** model all turbulent scales; cheapest, most widely used.
- **Large-eddy simulation (LES):** resolve the large eddies, model only the subgrid scales; expensive but increasingly routine.
- **Detached-eddy and hybrid methods:** RANS near walls, LES elsewhere.
- **Machine-learned closures:** data-driven constitutive laws embedded in any of the above.

This article traces the intellectual line from Reynolds's 1895 decomposition through the k–ε industry standard to modern learned closures, with the aim of explaining *why* each model works where it works, and precisely where it breaks.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Reynolds-averaged equations

Begin with the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations for velocity uᵢ and pressure p:

```
∂uᵢ/∂t + uⱼ ∂uᵢ/∂xⱼ = −(1/ρ) ∂p/∂xᵢ + ν ∂²uᵢ/∂xⱼ∂xⱼ
```

Reynolds's decomposition writes uᵢ = ūᵢ + uᵢ′, where the overbar denotes an ensemble (or, in practice, a long-time) average and the fluctuation satisfies overline{uᵢ′} = 0 [1]. Averaging the momentum equation yields the Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) equations:

```
∂ūᵢ/∂t + ūⱼ ∂ūᵢ/∂xⱼ = −(1/ρ) ∂p̄/∂xᵢ + ν ∂²ūᵢ/∂xⱼ∂xⱼ − ∂/∂xⱼ (overline{uᵢ′uⱼ′})
```

Six new unknowns appear — the components of the symmetric Reynolds-stress tensor τᵢⱼ = overline{uᵢ′uⱼ′} — and four equations describe ten unknowns. The transport equation for τᵢⱼ can be derived exactly, but it contains triple correlations, pressure–strain correlations, and a dissipation tensor; the hierarchy is open, and it stays open.

### 2.2 The cascade and the meaning of dissipation

The closure enterprise needs a theory of *what* turbulence does with energy. Kolmogorov's 1941 phenomenology supplies it [2]. Energy injected at large scales cascades through an inertial subrange, where the statistics depend only on the dissipation rate ε, to viscous scales where it is destroyed. Dimensional reasoning gives the celebrated spectrum

```
E(κ) = C_K ε^(2/3) κ^(−5/3)
```

and the Kolmogorov microscales η = (ν³/ε)^{1/4}. Two consequences shape every closure:

1. The small scales are approximately universal and isotropic — this is what makes LES subgrid modeling plausible at all.
2. In statistically stationary turbulence, production and dissipation balance — this is the *local equilibrium* hypothesis that underlies the Smagorinsky model and the k–ε constants alike.

### 2.3 The Boussinesq hypothesis

Boussinesq (1877) proposed treating the Reynolds stress by analogy with viscous stress:

```
−overline{uᵢ′uⱼ′} = ν_t (∂ūᵢ/∂xⱼ + ∂ūⱼ/∂xᵢ) − (2/3) k δᵢⱼ
```

where k = (1/2) overline{uᵢ′uᵢ′} is the turbulent kinetic energy and ν_t is an *eddy viscosity*. The hypothesis forces the anisotropic part of the Reynolds stress to be instantaneously aligned with the mean strain rate S̄ᵢⱼ. This is a strong — often wrong — assumption, and the entire k–ε hierarchy is the story of what can be achieved inside it, and what cannot.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Deriving the k–ε system

The standard k–ε model, in the form that dominated engineering practice after Jones and Launder [4], introduces modeled transport equations for two scalar turbulence scales: the turbulent kinetic energy k and its dissipation rate ε. The exact k-equation is known; production, dissipation, and diffusion are identified and modeled by gradient-diffusion arguments. The ε-equation has no exact derivation of comparable rigor — it is constructed by dimensional and asymptotic reasoning, a fact the field has never fully forgiven.

The modeled system, for incompressible flow:

```
∂k/∂t + ūⱼ ∂k/∂xⱼ = P_k − ε + ∂/∂xⱼ [ (ν + ν_t/σ_k) ∂k/∂xⱼ ]

∂ε/∂t + ūⱼ ∂ε/∂xⱼ = C_ε1 (ε/k) P_k − C_ε2 ε²/k + ∂/∂xⱼ [ (ν + ν_t/σ_ε) ∂ε/∂xⱼ ]

ν_t = C_μ k²/ε
```

with production P_k = −overline{uᵢ′uⱼ′} ∂ūᵢ/∂xⱼ. Dimensional consistency is immediate: k²/ε has units of viscosity, and ε/k is an inverse time scale, so the ε-equation's source terms carry the right dimensions. The standard constants,

| Constant | Value | Fixed by |
|----------|-------|----------|
| C_μ | 0.09 | log-law slope + local equilibrium |
| C_ε1 | 1.44 | decay of grid turbulence |
| C_ε2 | 1.92 | decay exponent of isotropic turbulence |
| σ_k | 1.0 | turbulent Prandtl number for k |
| σ_ε | 1.3 | turbulent Prandtl number for ε |

are not fitted to general flows. Each is anchored to a canonical asymptotic state: C_ε2 to the decay exponent of homogeneous isotropic turbulence, the combination C_μ, σ_ε, C_ε1, C_ε2 to the von Kármán constant of the logarithmic layer [4]. A model calibrated this way inherits formal consistency across those limits — and inherits fragility everywhere else.

### 3.2 LES filtering and subgrid stress

Large-eddy simulation applies a spatial filter G of width Δ to the Navier–Stokes equations. The filtered equations read:

```
∂ūᵢ/∂t + ∂(ūᵢ ūⱼ)/∂xⱼ = −(1/ρ) ∂p̄/∂xᵢ + ν ∂²ūᵢ/∂xⱼ∂xⱼ − ∂τᵢⱼ/∂xⱼ
```

with the subgrid-scale (SGS) stress τᵢⱼ = overline{uᵢ uⱼ} − ūᵢ ūⱼ. Closure requires a constitutive law for τᵢⱼ in terms of the resolved field ū.

Smagorinsky's 1963 proposal, originally introduced as a lateral diffusion for atmospheric circulation models [3], assumes the subgrid scales are in local equilibrium — production of subgrid energy equals its dissipation — and that dissipation follows the Kolmogorov cascade [2]. The result is the algebraic model:

```
τᵢⱼ − (1/3) τ_kk δᵢⱼ = −2 ν_sgs S̄ᵢⱼ ,   ν_sgs = (C_s Δ)² |S̄|
```

where |S̄| = (2 S̄ᵢⱼ S̄ᵢⱼ)^{1/2}. One constant, C_s ≈ 0.1–0.2, tunes everything. Lilly's refinement of the Smagorinsky formulation for three-dimensional isotropic turbulence [5] is the version most LES codes implement. It is elegant, cheap, and wrong in transitional flow, near walls, and in any flow where a single universal constant cannot hold.

### 3.3 The dynamic procedure

Germano and collaborators observed that the resolved field itself contains scale information: filtering at a second, coarser test-filter scale exposes the stresses between the two resolved levels [6]. Define the test-filtered Leonard stress Lᵢⱼ = \widehat{ūᵢ ūⱼ} − ûᵢ ûⱼ. If the Smagorinsky form holds at both filter levels, the Germano identity Lᵢⱼ = Tᵢⱼ − τ̂ᵢⱼ (with Tᵢⱼ the subtest stress) yields an overdetermined system for C_s². Lilly's least-squares contraction [7] gives the celebrated dynamic coefficient:

```
C_s² = ⟨Lᵢⱼ Mᵢⱼ⟩ / ⟨2 M_kₗ M_kₗ⟩ ,   Mᵢⱼ = Δ̂² |Ŝ| Ŝᵢⱼ − Δ² \widehat{|S̄| S̄ᵢⱼ}
```

where ⟨·⟩ denotes averaging over homogeneous directions and the hat denotes test filtering. The coefficient is now a function of space and time, vanishes in laminar flow and at solid walls, and needs no tuning. The procedure's power — extracting the constant from the flow rather than imposing it — is why the dynamic Smagorinsky model became the default against which every later SGS model is compared [8].

### 3.4 Machine-learned closures

Data-driven closures reverse the modeling direction: instead of postulating a constitutive law and calibrating constants, they *learn* the law from high-fidelity data (DNS, experiments) subject to physical constraints. The canonical example is the tensor-basis neural network (TBNN), which expands the normalized Reynolds-stress anisotropy aᵢⱼ in the ten-term integrity basis of the mean strain and rotation tensors:

```
aᵢⱼ = Σ_n g^(n)(λ) T^(n)ᵢⱼ
```

where the basis tensors T^(n) are known functions of S̄ and Ω̄, the scalar invariants λ are known inputs, and the coefficients g^(n) are represented by a neural network trained on DNS data. Because the expansion is a complete polynomial basis for the anisotropy tensor, the learned model is exactly Galilean invariant — a property hand-built into the architecture rather than hoped for in the data [9]. Related programs include field-inversion and machine learning (FIML), sparse symbolic regression (SINDy) for discovering closed-form corrections, and direct learning of the subgrid stress from filtered DNS. The survey by Brunton, Noack, and Koumoutsakos frames these efforts within the broader convergence of machine learning and fluid mechanics [8], while Duraisamy, Iaccarino, and Xiao emphasize the central tension: learned closures interpolate DNS data brilliantly and extrapolate to new flows dangerously [9].

A minimal illustration of the dynamic-coefficient computation:

```python
import numpy as np

def dynamic_smagorinsky_coefficient(L, M, eps=1e-12):
    """Lilly least-squares contraction of the Germano identity.
    L : Leonard stress tensor field, shape (..., 3, 3)
    M : Germano model tensor field, shape (..., 3, 3)
    Returns spatially local C_s^2; caller applies averaging/clipping.
    """
    numerator = np.sum(L * M, axis=(-2, -1))
    denominator = 2.0 * np.sum(M * M, axis=(-2, -1))
    cs2 = numerator / (denominator + eps)
    return np.clip(cs2, 0.0, 0.1)  # realizability guard, ad hoc but standard
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The k–ε transport system and the eddy-viscosity hypothesis

The k–ε model's durability is best understood not as empirical luck but as asymptotic design. The k-equation's production, dissipation, and gradient-diffusion terms are each justified in a distinct canonical flow: homogeneous shear for production, decaying isotropic turbulence for C_ε2, the logarithmic layer for the remaining constants. When applied to flows resembling those limits — attached boundary layers with mild pressure gradients, free shear layers at high Reynolds number, confined flows far from separation — the model predicts skin friction and mean profiles within engineering tolerance.

The price of the Boussinesq hypothesis is structural. Because the modeled anisotropy is forced to align with S̄ᵢⱼ, the k–ε family cannot produce:

- **Stress anisotropy in complex strain:** streamline curvature, system rotation, and three-dimensional boundary layers all generate anisotropy the model cannot represent.
- **History effects:** the algebraic ν_t = C_μ k²/ε responds instantaneously to local strain; sudden changes in strain rate are mispredicted because real turbulence retains memory.
- **Non-equilibrium production–dissipation imbalance:** in separating and reattaching flows, P_k/ε departs from unity by factors of several, while the ε-equation's construction assumes order-unity imbalance.

> **Theorem:** *The round-jet anomaly.* The standard k–ε model with universal constants cannot simultaneously predict the spreading rates of the plane mixing layer and the axisymmetric jet. The discrepancy — about 40 percent in spreading rate — is structural: the modeled ε-production term scales identically in both flows, while the true dissipation production differs. Variants (RNG, realizable) exist precisely because the standard form fails this elementary test.

### 4.2 Near-wall treatment and the model zoo

The standard high-Reynolds-number k–ε model is invalid at walls, where viscous effects dominate and the turbulent Reynolds number k²/(νε) collapses. Two strategies compete:

1. **Wall functions** bridge the viscous sublayer with the log law, placing the first grid point at y⁺ ≈ 30–100. Cheap, robust, and wrong whenever the boundary layer separates, because the log law itself fails there.
2. **Low-Reynolds-number formulations** (Jones–Launder damping functions, Launder–Sharma) integrate to the wall with damping of C_μ and extra source terms. Accurate near-wall behavior at the cost of stiff grids and y⁺ ≈ 1 resolution.

The model's offspring each repair one defect while inheriting the rest:

- **RNG k–ε** derives the constants from renormalization-group analysis rather than calibration, improving strained flows modestly.
- **Realizable k–ε** enforces positivity of normal stresses and the Schwarz inequality on shear stresses, fixing the round-jet anomaly at its source.
- **k–ω (Wilcox)** replaces ε with the specific dissipation ω = ε/k, dramatically improving near-wall and adverse-pressure-gradient behavior, but with pathological free-stream sensitivity.
- **SST (Menter)** blends k–ω near walls with k–ε in the free stream, plus a shear-stress transport limiter. It is arguably the best general-purpose RANS model in current use — and still a linear eddy-viscosity model, blind to anisotropy by construction.

### 4.3 Subgrid-scale closures: from Smagorinsky to the dynamic model and beyond

The LES closure problem is kinder than the RANS one: the subgrid scales carry a small fraction of the energy and are closer to universal, so crude models suffice — when the grid is fine enough. The Smagorinsky constant's non-universality (0.1 in shear flows, up to 0.2 in isotropic turbulence, effectively zero in transition) was the field's embarrassment until the dynamic procedure turned the embarrassment into data [6].

The dynamic model's limitations spawned a literature of its own:

- **Negative and wildly fluctuating coefficients** required averaging over homogeneous directions or ad hoc clipping, which fails in complex geometries with no homogeneous direction.
- **Dynamic localization** (Ghosal et al.) solved the coefficient from an integral equation, removing the need for homogeneous directions.
- **The Lagrangian dynamic model** (Meneveau et al.) averages along fluid pathlines, restoring locality without homogeneous directions — at significant computational cost.
- **Scale-similarity and mixed models** (Bardina, Liu–Meneveau–Katz) add a structural term τᵢⱼ^sim = overline{ūᵢ ūⱼ} − overline{ū}ᵢ overline{ū}ⱼ that correlates far better with the true subgrid stress in a-priori tests, though it under-dissipates and must be mixed with an eddy-viscosity term.

The deeper point: LES inherits the closure problem rather than escaping it. The filter width Δ is a modeling choice disguised as a numerical parameter, and wall-modeled LES — required whenever the Reynolds number makes wall-resolved LES unaffordable — reintroduces RANS-like closure assumptions exactly where they are least trustworthy.

### 4.4 Data-driven and machine-learned closures

The learned-closure program rests on a simple observation: DNS databases now contain more information about the true Reynolds-stress constitutive relation than any human-derived model can encode. The TBNN architecture is the cleanest demonstration [9]. By expanding the anisotropy in the integrity basis and learning only scalar functions of invariants, it guarantees:

1. **Galilean invariance** by construction, not by data augmentation.
2. **Realizability** can be enforced through the output parameterization.
3. **Interpretability** at the level of which basis tensors activate in which flow regions.

Yet the field's central warning, articulated forcefully by Duraisamy, Iaccarino, and Xiao, is that learned models are *interpolators with physics-shaped priors* [9]. A TBNN trained on channel flow and a backward-facing step predicts a periodic hill passably and a wing at stall badly. The failure is not a bug in the network — it is the oldest lesson in the closure literature restated: no finite training set spans the space of turbulent flows, and extrapolation in turbulence is punished more severely than in almost any other domain of applied machine learning.

Sparse regression approaches (SINDy) offer a different bargain: discover compact, closed-form corrections to existing models rather than black-box replacements. A sparse correction to the k–ε model that activates only where the baseline is known to fail preserves the baseline's calibrated asymptotics while repairing its known defects — a surgical strategy that the pure deep-learning program cannot easily match.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The models above are not judged by elegance but by numbers. The canonical validation ladder:

1. **Decay of homogeneous isotropic turbulence.** The standard k–ε model reproduces the power-law decay k ∝ t^{−n} with n ≈ 1.1–1.3, consistent with C_ε2 = 1.92. This is a calibration target, not a prediction — but failure here would disqualify the model outright.
2. **Fully developed channel flow (DNS comparison).** At friction Reynolds numbers up to Re_τ ≈ 2000, low-Re k–ε variants reproduce the mean velocity profile and the log-law constants within a few percent; the Reynolds shear stress is captured, while the normal-stress anisotropy is not — a direct consequence of the Boussinesq alignment.
3. **Backward-facing step (separation and reattachment).** The standard model overpredicts the reattachment length by 20–30 percent; RNG and realizable variants reduce the error substantially. This flow is the graveyard of linear eddy-viscosity models and the benchmark every new closure must survive.
4. **Decaying and forced isotropic turbulence (LES).** Dynamic Smagorinsky reproduces the Kolmogorov spectrum through the inertial subrange with no tuned constant, whereas the static model requires C_s adjusted per flow — the experiment that established the dynamic procedure's superiority [6].
5. **Transitional boundary layers.** The static Smagorinsky model predicts spurious subgrid viscosity in the laminar region and delays or destroys transition; the dynamic coefficient vanishes correctly, and transitional channel-flow LES matches DNS [6].
6. **A-priori tests of learned closures.** TBNN trained on channel and step flows reduces the Reynolds-stress prediction error on held-out flows by an order of magnitude relative to linear eddy viscosity in the training envelope, with degradation — sometimes severe — outside it [9].

> **Theorem:** *A-priori versus a-posteriori.* High correlation between a modeled and true subgrid stress in a-priori tests (computed from filtered DNS) does not imply stable or accurate LES when the model is run a-posteriori. Scale-similarity models correlate above 0.8 a-priori yet under-dissipate catastrophically in live simulation. Validation requires both.

## 6 Limitations

No survey of closures is honest without cataloguing what remains out of reach:

- **The Boussinesq ceiling.** Every linear eddy-viscosity model, from 1972 k–ε to SST, cannot represent stress anisotropy. Reynolds-stress transport models escape the ceiling but introduce their own closure terms (pressure–strain, dissipation anisotropy) that are modeled no more rigorously than what they replaced.
- **Non-equilibrium and history.** The algebraic eddy viscosity responds instantaneously to strain; rapid distortion, impulsive separation, and rotating flows expose the missing memory.
- **Wall modeling.** Both RANS wall functions and wall-modeled LES rest on the law of the wall, which fails precisely in the separated and three-dimensional boundary layers where accuracy matters most.
- **The dynamic model's fragility.** Negative coefficients, dependence on homogeneous directions, and ad hoc clipping mean the "parameter-free" model carries hidden numerical parameters.
- **Learned closures and extrapolation.** Data-driven models inherit the oldest failure in the field: they perform inside their training envelope and degrade outside it, and turbulence offers no cheap way to certify the envelope's boundary [9].
- **Cost.** Wall-resolved LES at flight Reynolds numbers remains orders of magnitude beyond reach; DNS of a full aircraft will not arrive this century. Every practical simulation therefore contains a closure, and every closure contains an assumption.

## 7 Conclusion

The history of turbulence closure is the history of a single equation — Reynolds's averaged momentum balance — and the century of ingenuity spent deciding what to write in place of the unknown. The k–ε model succeeded because its constants were anchored to asymptotic truths rather than fitted to convenience, and it fails wherever those asymptotics do not apply. Large-eddy simulation narrowed the modeling burden to the small scales that Kolmogorov made nearly universal, and the Germano–Lilly dynamic procedure turned the model's worst parameter into a measurement. Machine learning now offers to learn the constitutive relation directly, and its early successes are real — but they are bounded by the same extrapolation barrier that defeated every earlier generation of empiricism.

The through-line is a design discipline: closures earn trust when they respect invariance, realizability, and cascade physics, and they lose it when they fit data without understanding. The most promising path forward is not any single paradigm but their combination — sparse, interpretable corrections to asymptotically consistent baselines, trained on DNS, validated a-posteriori, and honest about their envelope. Turbulence will not be solved; it will be managed, one well-understood approximation at a time.
