# Nominal Adapton: Demand-Driven Self-Adjusting Computation via the Demanded Computation Graph, Nominal Matching, and From-Scratch Consistency

## Abstract

Incremental computation — *self-adjusting computation* — studies how a program's output can be updated efficiently after a small change to its input. This thesis develops a systematic account of **Adapton**, the demand-driven incremental framework of Hammer, Khoo, Hicks, and Foster [1], and its nominal successor **Nominal Adapton** [2]. We analyze *change propagation* over a *demanded computation graph* (DCG), contrast it with full recomputation, and explain how *demand-driven memoization* lets inner computations be reused across contexts while outer observers pull updates only when needed. We then examine *first-class names* and *nominal matching*, which identify computations to reuse across runs and let graph nodes be overwritten safely. The centerpiece is the *from-scratch consistency* theorem: incremental evaluation provably yields the same answer as re-running from scratch, so misuse of names can degrade performance but never correctness. We survey empirical benchmarks, the miniAdapton implementation [3], and typed successors such as Fungi [6], closing with open limitations.

## 1 Introduction

Consider a spreadsheet with ten thousand cells, a compiler incrementally rebuilding after one file changes, or an IDE that re-highlights syntax after every keystroke. In each case, a small change to the input of a computation invalidates only a *fraction* of the output; recomputing everything is wasteful, sometimes asymptotically so. **Incremental computation (IC)**, pioneered in the language-based tradition by Acar, Blelloch, and Harper's *self-adjusting computation* [5], is the disciplined study of re-executing programs efficiently under input change.

Classical self-adjusting computation works as follows. A program runs once, recording a *trace* — or, more modernly, a *dynamic dependency graph* (DDG) — of the dependencies between its computations and the mutable *modifiables* it reads. When an input is mutated, a *change propagation* algorithm walks the graph, *dirties* the computations transitively dependent on the changed input, and re-executes them in dependency order, reusing everything else [5]. For certain algorithms and change classes this yields order-of-magnitude, even asymptotic, speedups over from-scratch re-evaluation.

Adapton [1], introduced by Matthew Hammer, Yit Phang Khoo, Michael Hicks, and Jeffrey S. Foster at PLDI 2014, observed two sharp limitations of the prior state of the art. First, classical change propagation is *eager and demand-oblivious*: when an input changes, *all* derived values are recomputed, even those no observer currently cares about. In an interactive spreadsheet, editing a cell on a hidden sheet should not force recomputation of a visible formula until the user returns to that sheet. Second, programs are incrementalized *as a unit*: results cannot easily be reused outside their original context — for example, when two computations' inputs are merely *reordered* (the classic "swap" problem in list processing).

Adapton's answer is a **demand-driven semantics** formalized in a core calculus, λCDDIC, and embodied in a **demanded computation graph (DCG)** that tracks changes *hierarchically*. An explicit separation is drawn between *inner* incremental computations and *outer observers*: recomputation happens only as demanded by observers, while inner computations are liberally shared and reused. Nominal Adapton [2] then adds **first-class names**: programmers explicitly identify computations and data to be reused across runs, and *nominal matching* lets names steer memoization precisely, even for patterns like `map`, `fold`, and `unfold` over mutable structures.

This thesis presents the design, theory, and practice of this lineage: from dependency graphs and dirtying discipline, through demand-driven memoization and nominal matching, to the *from-scratch consistency* correctness theorem and its empirical validation.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Self-adjusting computation

The language-based approach to incremental computation originates with Acar's thesis work and the Adaptive Functional Programming (AFL) framework [5]. The central abstraction is the *modifiable*: a mutable cell whose reads and writes are tracked. A run of an adaptive program builds a **dynamic dependency graph** whose nodes are computations (reads, memoized function calls) and whose edges record read-dependencies on modifiables. Mutation of a modifiable triggers **change propagation**: a two-phase algorithm that (i) *invalidates* (dirties) every computation transitively depending on the changed cell, and (ii) *re-executes* dirty computations in an order respecting dependencies, patching their results into the graph.

Crucially, change propagation runs in time proportional to the number of *affected* computations plus the cost of re-executing them — often dramatically sublinear in the total program size. Memoization with *trace matching* (or, in later work, memo tables keyed on arguments) allows computations whose inputs are unchanged to be reused wholesale [5].

### 2.2 Structural vs. nominal matching

Classical memoization matches computations *structurally*: a memoized call `f(x)` is reused when an identical call with equal arguments is found in the trace. This works poorly under *reordering* — e.g., swapping two list elements produces structurally different calls even though most work is identical — and under mutation of composite inputs, where the identity of a subcomputation is lost. **Nominal matching** instead keys memoization on programmer-supplied *names*: two computations match when their names coincide, regardless of structural accidents [2]. Names are *first-class* values in Nominal Adapton: they can be computed, stored in data structures, and passed to combinators like `fork`, which relates the names of an input structure to those of the output.

### 2.3 The demanded computation graph

Adapton replaces the classical trace with the **demanded computation graph (DCG)** [1]. Nodes are *memoized refs* (mutable cells, `aref`) and *memoized computations* (thunks, `athunk`); edges are their dependencies. Unlike the earlier trace semantics, which idealized the graph as an ever-growing tree of traces, the DCG is a *changing graph* — nodes' bodies and values can be overwritten, and demand is tracked so that only *demanded* portions are kept consistent. This is the implementation-level structure that Nominal Adapton's metatheory models directly [2].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is that of programming-languages research: **design a core calculus, implement it faithfully, and prove a correspondence theorem** relating the incremental semantics to a simple non-incremental reference semantics.

1. **Calculus design.** We study λCDDIC [1] (Adapton) and λNomA [2] (Nominal Adapton): small functional calculi with explicit constructs for mutable refs (`ref`/`get`/`set`), memoized thunks (`thunk`/`force`), and, in λNomA, first-class names with allocation and nominal matching.
2. **Reference semantics.** Each calculus carries a *basic* (from-scratch) big-step semantics with no caching: every `force` re-executes its body. This is the ground truth.
3. **Incremental semantics.** A second semantics threads the DCG through evaluation: `force` consults the memo table and reuses clean results; `set` dirties dependents; a change-propagation phase restores consistency *on demand*.
4. **Metatheory.** We prove **from-scratch consistency**: for well-formed stores, incremental evaluation yields the same value (up to the recorded trace) as the basic semantics [1][2]. The proof proceeds by a simulation argument relating incremental traces to basic derivations, with a *checking* judgement that validates cached traces against the current store.
5. **Implementation and measurement.** The calculi are realized as OCaml libraries (`aref`/`athunk`) [1], a minimal Scheme implementation (miniAdapton/microAdapton) [3], and a Rust prototype for the typed successor Fungi [6]. Benchmarks measure incremental update time versus from-scratch recomputation across spreadsheets, list combinators, trees, and tries.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Change propagation in the demanded computation graph

Change propagation is the engine of all self-adjusting systems. In Adapton's DCG formulation it has two logically distinct phases.

**Phase 1 — Dirtying.** Writing to a memoized ref marks the ref *dirty* and propagates dirtiness along reverse dependency edges: every thunk that (transitively) read the ref becomes dirty. Dirtiness is *monotone*: a dirty node stays dirty until it is re-evaluated, and clean nodes are never spuriously dirtied.

**Phase 2 — Reevaluation in dependency order.** Dirty thunks are re-executed *topologically*: a thunk is re-forced only after all thunks it depends on are clean. When a re-executed thunk reads a ref whose value changed, it may itself produce a new value, dirtying *its* dependents in turn — a cascading, fixpoint-like process that terminates because each reevaluation strictly reduces the dirty set under a well-founded measure on the graph.

The crucial contrast with naive recomputation is *selectivity*: propagation touches only the affected subgraph. If a program of size *n* has an update affecting *k ≪ n* computations, change propagation costs *O(k)* plus reevaluation — versus *Ω(n)* for recomputation.

| Aspect | From-scratch recomputation | Change propagation |
|---|---|---|
| Work per update | Θ(total program) | Θ(affected subgraph) |
| Reuse across runs | None | Memoized thunks and refs |
| Demand sensitivity | N/A (eager total) | Only demanded nodes refreshed |
| Correctness argument | Trivial (fresh run) | From-scratch consistency theorem |
| Memory overhead | None beyond program | DCG: one node per thunk/ref |

### 4.2 Demand-driven memoization

Classical IC is *eager*: any input change triggers immediate, total re-propagation. Adapton's radical move is to make propagation **demand-driven** [1]. The DCG distinguishes *inner* computations from *outer observers*. An observer *demands* a value by forcing a thunk; only then does the system ensure that the demanded subgraph is consistent, re-executing dirty thunks along the demand path and memoizing the results.

This yields three interaction patterns that eager systems cannot express efficiently:

- **Laziness.** Do not recompute obscured sheets: if an observer never looks at a derived value, its dirty subgraph is never refreshed. The work is simply *skipped*, not deferred.
- **Switching.** When demand itself changes — e.g., a formula switches from `f(A)` to `g(B)` — the old dependency edges are pruned and the new ones demanded, without touching unrelated parts of the graph.
- **Sharing.** Multiple observers can demand overlapping subgraphs; inner computations are computed once and shared, because the DCG is a graph, not a tree.

Memoization in Adapton is *adaptive* [5]: memo tables remember the dependence graphs of memoized calls, so on a hit the stored graph is spliced into the current DCG and change-propagated against the new arguments — reusing work even when the call site differs from the original context.

```ocaml
(* Adapton OCaml API, simplified from [1] *)
type 'a aref    (* memoized mutable reference *)
type 'a athunk  (* memoized suspended computation *)

val ref   : 'a -> 'a aref
val get   : 'a aref -> 'a
val set   : 'a aref -> 'a -> unit        (* dirties dependents *)
val thunk : (unit -> 'a) -> 'a athunk
val force : 'a athunk -> 'a              (* demand: reuse or re-execute *)
val memo  : ('a -> 'b) -> 'a -> 'b       (* adaptive memoization *)
```

### 4.3 Nominal matching and first-class names

Structural memo matching breaks down when programs *reorder* or *rebuild* data: a `map` over a list whose spine was reconstructed will miss every memo hit, even though each element's computation is unchanged. Nominal Adapton's answer is to make **names first-class** [2]. Every ref and thunk carries a name; memo lookup matches on names rather than on structure. The programmer — or a library of naming combinators — controls reuse explicitly:

- Names identify *which* computations correspond across runs. A list cell named `α₀` in run one matches the cell named `α₀` in run two, even if surrounding structure changed.
- The `fork` combinator relates the names of an input structure to the names of the output structure, so `map`, `fold`, and `unfold` can propagate names through the computation and achieve reuse element-by-element.

> **Theorem (From-scratch consistency, informal).** *Incremental evaluation under Nominal Adapton produces the same answer as re-evaluating the program from scratch. In particular, misuse of names — ambiguous or colliding allocations — can degrade performance but cannot change the meaning of a program.* [2]

This is the deep payoff of the nominal design: names are a *performance* annotation, not a *correctness* obligation. A name is *precise* when it identifies at most one value or subcomputation in an evaluation derivation, and *ambiguous* otherwise; the type system of Typed Adapton statically enforces precision [7], but even without it, ambiguity is safe.

### 4.4 Dirtying, invalidation, and reevaluation order

Getting the dirtying discipline right is subtle, and it is where implementations diverge from naive descriptions:

1. **Write barrier.** `set r v` writes `v` into ref `r` and enqueues `r`'s direct dependents.
2. **Transitive dirtying.** Dependents are marked dirty and their dependents enqueued, in a single graph traversal. Nodes already dirty are not re-enqueued (idempotence).
3. **Demand-gated reevaluation.** A dirty thunk is re-executed only when forced by an observer (or by another thunk on a demand path). Re-execution *cleans* the thunk and may write new values into its outgoing refs, which re-dirties downstream nodes — hence reevaluation and dirtying interleave.
4. **Order invariant.** A thunk is never re-executed while any of its dependencies is still dirty; the implementation enforces this by checking dependency cleanliness on force, recursively demanding dirty dependencies first.

A classic pitfall is *dirty-then-clean oscillation*: a thunk re-executes, reads a ref whose value changed and then changed back, and must still be marked clean to avoid infinite loops. Adapton handles this by comparing old and new values at ref writes — if the value is *unchanged*, dependents are *not* re-dirtied — a form of *equality-based short-circuiting* that prunes propagation dramatically in practice.

### 4.5 The metatheory: from-scratch consistency

The formal development distills the implementation to λNomA and proves the fundamental correctness property [2]. The proof architecture, inherited from λCDDIC [1], is worth studying:

- **Basic semantics.** `S ⊢ e ⇓ S'; v`: ordinary big-step evaluation over a store, with no caching. This is the specification.
- **Incremental semantics.** `K; S₁ ⊢ e ⇓ S₂; T`: evaluation that may reuse the *knowledge* `K` (the DCG: prior traces, memo tables) and produces a trace `T` recording input–output dependencies.
- **Checking judgement.** `S ⊢ T √`: validates that a cached trace is consistent with the current store — every recorded `force` produced the same terminal expression as last time (`CHECK-FORCE`), and every recorded `get` reads the store's current value (`CHECK-GET`).
- **Soundness.** If the incremental semantics derives a result from well-formed knowledge, the basic semantics derives the corresponding from-scratch result, and vice versa. Hence *extensional consistency*: initial and final conditions of the two runs coincide.

> **Theorem (Soundness / From-scratch consistency).** *Suppose Γ ⊢ K wf and Γ ⊢ S₁ wf. Then K; S₁ ⊢ e ⇓ S₂; T if and only if S₁ ⊢ e ⇓ S₂; trm(T), for a suitable erasure trm of traces to terminal expressions. Every incremental reduction has a corresponding basic reduction, and vice versa.* [1]

The Nominal Adapton version strengthens this to a DCG that is a *changing graph* — nodes' bodies and values may be overwritten via nominal matching — making the metatheory model the implementation faithfully rather than idealizing the graph as an eternal tree of traces [2].

## 5 Empirical Results

Adapton was evaluated as an OCaml library on a range of benchmarks [1], and Nominal Adapton was compared against both from-scratch evaluation and original Adapton [2]. The headline findings:

| Benchmark family | Change class | Observed behavior (per [1][2]) |
|---|---|---|
| Spreadsheet (100s of cells) | Single-cell edit | Only the dependent chain re-executes; obscured sheets untouched under demand semantics |
| `map` / `filter` over lists | Element update, insertion | Nominal names recover per-element reuse; structural matching degrades to near from-scratch |
| `fold` / tree reductions | Leaf mutation | Logarithmic-ish affected subgraph; large speedups over full re-fold |
| Quicksort / quickhull | Small input perturbation | Reuse of unaffected partitions via nominal matching |
| Probabilistic trees / tries | Key insert/delete | Efficient incremental tries built with name combinators [2] |
| Demand switching | Formula/observer change | Lazy semantics skips all non-demanded recomputation |

Qualitatively, Adapton reports *reliable* speedups across the suite and *dramatic* speedups — one to three orders of magnitude — where changes are small relative to the computation, outperforming prior state-of-the-art IC systems on the same workloads [1]. Nominal Adapton shows *large speedups over both from-scratch computation and Adapton itself* on name-sensitive patterns such as incremental `map` and trie maintenance [2]. The pedagogical miniAdapton reproduces the core behaviors in a few hundred lines of Scheme, confirming the design's modest complexity budget [3].

## 6 Limitations

No honest account omits the costs.

- **Programmer burden of naming.** Nominal Adapton's performance hinges on *good* names. Choosing precise, stable names for data and subcomputations is a genuine programming skill; poor naming silently degrades to from-scratch performance (though never to wrong answers, by the consistency theorem). Typed Adapton [7] and Fungi [6] attack this with refinement types that statically enforce name precision, but the annotations are nontrivial.
- **Memory overhead.** The DCG retains one node per thunk and ref plus dependency edges — a constant-factor (sometimes large) space tax over the base program, and a potential space leak if observers hold references to stale subgraphs. Real deployments need graph pruning or weak references.
- **Mutation discipline.** Arbitrary untracked mutation breaks the model: every input that can change must flow through a tracked `ref`. Interfacing with effectful libraries (I/O, FFI) requires careful wrapping, and the write barrier imposes per-mutation cost even when nothing is dirty.
- **Granularity mismatch.** Change propagation is only as good as the dependency granularity. Coarse thunks re-execute too much; overly fine thunks drown in graph-maintenance overhead. Tuning granularity remains more art than science.
- **Concurrency.** The classical DCG assumes sequential evaluation with a global dirtying discipline. Parallel change propagation and lock-free DCG maintenance are largely open problems.
- **Formalization gap.** The metatheory models a core calculus; production libraries (OCaml Adapton, the Rust Fungi prototype) add features — exceptions, polymorphism, foreign calls — outside the proven fragment.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Adapton and its nominal successor recast incremental computation around a single insight: *make demand explicit, and make identity explicit*. The **demanded computation graph** turns change propagation from an eager, total, demand-oblivious process into a lazy, selective one in which observers pull consistency only where they look. **First-class names with nominal matching** turn memoization from a structural accident into a programmer-controlled reuse discipline, recovering asymptotic speedups for reordering-heavy patterns that defeat classical systems. And the **from-scratch consistency theorem** turns all of this machinery from a bag of tricks into a trustworthy semantics: incremental evaluation *means* the same thing as re-running from scratch, so performance engineering with names can never corrupt results.

The lineage continues: miniAdapton [3] makes the ideas teachable in Scheme; Typed Adapton [7] and Fungi [6] make names statically precise via refinement types; and the Rust ecosystem is beginning to carry these ideas into production incremental systems. The open frontier — automatic granularity selection, concurrent propagation, and verified compilation of the full library surface — remains rich.
