# Reverse-Engineering the Transformer: Induction Heads, Superposition, Sparse Autoencoders, and the Circuit-Discovery Programme

## Abstract

Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer neural networks into human-comprehensible algorithms, much as a programmer decompiles a binary into source code. This thesis synthesizes the central pillars of the transformer interpretability programme: (i) the mathematical framework of transformer circuits, in which attention heads decompose into interpretable QK/OV circuits composed through the residual stream [1]; (ii) the induction-head mechanism — a two-head circuit in which a previous-token head and an induction head cooperate to complete token patterns, shown to explain the majority of in-context learning in small models [2]; (iii) the hypothesis of superposition, under which models represent more features than they have dimensions by encoding them as near-orthogonal directions, producing the polysemanticity that frustrates neuron-level interpretation [3]; and (iv) sparse autoencoders (SAEs), which recover monosemantic dictionary directions via a sparse overcomplete basis, scaling from one-layer toy models to Claude 3 Sonnet [4][5][6]. We derive the core mathematics of each component, present empirical evidence, discuss limitations — including feature splitting, causally inert SAE features, and exponential circuit scaling — and conclude with a roadmap toward faithful, scalable circuit discovery.

## 1 Introduction

The dominant paradigm of deep learning proceeds by gradient descent on parameters and evaluation on benchmarks, treating trained models as oracles whose internal mechanisms are opaque. This opacity is increasingly untenable: models deployed at scale exhibit emergent capabilities, including in-context learning, arithmetic, and reasoning, that were never explicitly programmed and are poorly understood by their creators [1]. When a system cannot explain *why* it produced an output, auditing, debugging, and safety assurance all degrade.

*Mechanistic interpretability* proposes an alternative: to reverse-engineer the detailed computations of a trained network into human-readable algorithms [1]. The programme draws inspiration from the Distill Circuits thread on vision models, where individual neurons were found to implement interpretable features composed into circuits [1]. For transformers, the effort crystallized in the *Transformer Circuits* thread of Anthropic, beginning with a mathematical framework that re-expresses attention in terms of virtual circuits operating on the residual stream [1].

Four developments form the backbone of this research arc:

1. **Circuit formalism.** Attention-only transformers can be decomposed into QK (query-key, "where to look") and OV (output-value, "what to write") circuits, with heads composed via Q-, K-, and V-composition through the residual stream [1].
2. **Induction heads.** A specific two-head circuit implements pattern completion of the form `[A][B]...[A] → [B]`, and accounts for most of the in-context learning observed in small language models [2].
3. **Superposition.** Neural networks represent more features than they have neurons by packing sparse features into near-orthogonal directions of activation space, yielding *polysemantic* neurons that confound naive interpretation [3].
4. **Sparse autoencoders.** Unsupervised dictionary learning — training SAEs to reconstruct activations through a sparse overcomplete bottleneck — recovers monosemantic feature directions, scaling from one-layer models [4][5] to frontier models with tens of millions of features [6].

This thesis reconstructs these ideas rigorously, evaluates their empirical standing, and examines the obstacles to full circuit discovery.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Transformer as a Residual Stream

A decoder-only transformer processes a sequence of tokens through a *residual stream* — a vector $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$ per position $i$ that is read from and written to by each layer. Formally, with $x^{(0)}_i = W_E t_i + W_{\text{pos},i}$ the initial embedding, each block applies

$$x^{(l+1)}_i = x^{(l)}_i + \text{Attn}^{(l)}(x^{(l)}_{\le i}) + \text{MLP}^{(l)}(\cdot),$$

where attention is causal (only attending to positions $\le i$). Because every component communicates exclusively by adding vectors into the shared stream, the residual stream constitutes a *linear communication channel*: information is encoded in directions of $\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$, and heads and MLPs read and write specific subspaces [1].

### 2.2 Attention Heads as Virtual Circuits

An attention head $h$ at layer $l$ computes queries, keys, and values via weight matrices $W_Q, W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{head}}}$ and $W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{head}}}$, $W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{head}} \times d_{\text{model}}}$. Elhage et al. [1] show the head's computation decomposes into two bilinear forms that are independent of each other:

- The **QK circuit** $W_Q W_K^T$ determines the attention pattern: the score between a query at position $i$ and a key at position $j$ is $(x_i W_Q)(x_j W_K)^T = x_i (W_Q W_K^T) x_j^T$. This answers *"where does the head look?"*
- The **OV circuit** $W_O W_V$ determines what the head writes: attending to position $j$ adds $x_j (W_V W_O)$ to the residual stream. This answers *"what does the head write?"*

Crucially, when heads are *folded through the residual stream*, these circuits can be read directly off the weights without running the model: the QK circuit is a $d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}$ matrix acting on pairs of residual-stream states, and the OV circuit is a $d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}$ linear map applied to attended tokens [1].

### 2.3 Composition Between Heads

Because heads communicate through the residual stream, the output of one head can serve as input to another. Elhage et al. [1] identify three modes of composition:

| Composition | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Q-composition | Head A writes into the subspace head B reads as queries | B's attention depends on A's output |
| K-composition | Head A writes into the subspace head B reads as keys | B attends based on A's output |
| V-composition | Head A writes into the subspace head B reads as values | B moves information A wrote |

These compositions are *virtual*: they exist in the full $d_{\text{model}}$-dimensional space, but each head only attends to its own low-dimensional subspace ($d_{\text{head}} \ll d_{\text{model}}$), so the effective circuit is the restriction of the full map to those subspaces [1]. Induction heads are the canonical example of K- and V-composition working in concert [2].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology follows the standard toolkit of mechanistic interpretability, applied comparatively across the four pillars:

1. **Weight-level circuit analysis.** Compute QK and OV circuits directly from model weights; project residual-stream states onto head-specific subspaces; enumerate composition paths in attention-only models [1].
2. **Attention-pattern measurement.** On controlled synthetic data (repeated random token sequences), measure attention as a function of relative position and token identity to identify previous-token and induction heads [2].
3. **Causal intervention.** Ablate or patch activations (zero ablation, mean ablation, activation patching) to test whether hypothesized circuits are causally responsible for behavior, e.g., removing induction heads and measuring in-context learning loss [2].
4. **Toy-model construction.** Train minimal models (e.g., one-hidden-layer ReLU autoencoders with $m < n$ neurons for $n$ features) on synthetic sparse data to study superposition under controlled conditions, where ground truth is known [3].
5. **Sparse dictionary learning.** Train SAEs on residual-stream or MLP activations of real language models, optimizing reconstruction loss plus an $L_1$ sparsity penalty; evaluate feature interpretability with automated (LLM-judge) scoring and causal ablations [4][5][6].

> **Theorem:** *Under the residual-stream formalism, any attention-only transformer's forward pass is exactly equivalent to a sum over all QK/OV circuit paths from input embeddings to output logits, with no approximation.* This is the mathematical basis on which circuit discovery rests [1].

Each method is complemented by falsification criteria: a circuit claim is accepted only if ablating it degrades the target behavior, and an SAE feature is accepted as real only if ablating its direction causally alters model outputs [2][4][5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The QK/OV Decomposition and Virtual Attention Heads

The power of the circuit formalism lies in treating $W_Q W_K^T$ and $W_V W_O$ as the *fundamental objects of analysis* rather than the individual weight matrices [1]. Consider a one-layer attention-only model. The attention score from position $i$ to position $j$ in head $h$ is

$$A^h_{ij} = \text{softmax}_j\left(x_i \tilde{W}^h_{QK} x_j^T\right), \quad \tilde{W}^h_{QK} = W_Q^h (W_K^h)^T,$$

and the head's output is $\sum_j A^h_{ij} \, x_j \tilde{W}^h_{OV}$ with $\tilde{W}^h_{OV} = W_V^h W_O^h$. Because the embedding $W_E$ and unembedding $W_U$ are also linear, the *entire* model reduces to a sum of terms of the form

$$\text{softmax}\big(t_i W_E \tilde{W}_{QK} W_E^T t_j^T\big) \cdot t_j W_E \tilde{W}_{OV} W_U,$$

where $t_i, t_j$ are one-hot token vectors. Every path from input token to output logit is a *virtual attention head* whose behavior can be analyzed in closed form [1].

In two-layer models, composition introduces the embedding of one head's output into another's QK or OV circuit. A head in layer 2 whose keys are K-composed with a layer-1 head computes attention scores involving terms like $x_i W_Q (W_O^{(1)} W_V^{(1)})^T (W_K^{(2)})^T x_j^T$ — attention *to the output of another head* rather than to raw tokens. This compositional structure is what makes multi-step algorithms like induction possible [1][2].

### 4.2 Induction Heads: The Mechanism of In-Context Learning

*Induction heads* are attention heads that implement the rule: given a sequence `... [A] [B] ... [A]`, increase the logit of `[B]` — completing the pattern by copying the token that followed the earlier occurrence of `[A]` [2]. They are constructed from two cooperating heads:

1. A **previous-token head** in an earlier layer attends from each position $i$ to position $i-1$ and copies information about token $i-1$ forward. Its OV circuit writes "the previous token was $t$" into the residual stream at position $i$.
2. An **induction head** in a later layer uses K-composition: its keys read the previous-token head's output. When the current token matches an earlier token $A$, the key of the earlier $A$'s position (which carries "previous token was..." information about what *followed* $A$) matches the query, so the induction head attends to the position *after* the earlier $A$ — i.e., to $B$ — and its OV circuit copies $B$ forward, boosting its logit [2].

Olsson et al. [2] present striking empirical evidence:

- In two-layer attention-only models, induction heads reliably emerge at a specific point in training, coinciding with a sharp drop in in-context learning loss — a *phase change* in which the loss on repeated sequences discontinuously improves.
- Across model scales, the strength of induction behavior (measured by a "prefix matching" score) correlates with in-context learning ability.
- Ablating induction heads degrades in-context learning while leaving baseline language modeling largely intact, establishing a causal — not merely correlational — link [2].

The discovery matters because in-context learning is among the most consequential emergent behaviors of large models, and induction heads provide its first mechanistic explanation: *few-shot learning is, to first order, sophisticated pattern completion implemented by a small, identifiable circuit* [2].

### 4.3 Superposition: Why Neurons Are Polysemantic

If each neuron corresponded to one interpretable concept, interpretability would be straightforward. Empirically, it does not: neurons are *polysemantic*, firing for multiple unrelated concepts (e.g., a single neuron responding to both academic citations and Korean text) [3]. Elhage et al. [3] propose that polysemanticity is the observable signature of *superposition*: the network represents $n$ features using $m < n$ neurons by assigning each feature a direction in activation space, exploiting the fact that in high dimensions, exponentially many vectors can be *nearly* orthogonal.

Their toy model is a one-hidden-layer autoencoder, $f(x) = \text{ReLU}(W^T W x + b)$ with $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, trained to reconstruct sparse feature vectors $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (each input activates only a few features). Key findings [3]:

- **Phase change.** As feature sparsity increases past a threshold, the model transitions discontinuously from representing features as dedicated neurons to packing them in superposition.
- **Geometry.** The learned feature directions $W_i$ arrange themselves into elegant geometric configurations — antipodal pairs, triangular and tetrahedral arrangements — connected to the theory of *uniform polytopes* and optimal packings.
- **Interference and adversarial vulnerability.** Superposition requires tolerating interference between non-orthogonal features; the ReLU nonlinearity filters small interference when features are sparse, but adversarial inputs can activate many features simultaneously, overwhelming the filter. The authors suggest a link between superposition and adversarial examples [3].

The implication is profound: *the neuron is the wrong unit of analysis*. The correct units are *features as directions*, and any interpretability method that assumes neuron-level monosemanticity is doomed. This motivates dictionary learning: recovering the overcomplete set of feature directions directly [3][4].

### 4.4 Sparse Autoencoders: Dictionary Learning for Language Models

A sparse autoencoder (SAE) attempts to invert superposition. Given activations $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (e.g., an MLP layer's activations across many tokens), the SAE learns

$$f(x) = \text{ReLU}(W_{\text{enc}} x + b_{\text{enc}}), \qquad \hat{x} = W_{\text{dec}} f(x) + b_{\text{dec}},$$

with $f(x) \in \mathbb{R}^k$, $k \gg d$ (overcomplete dictionary), minimizing

$$\mathcal{L} = \|x - \hat{x}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|f(x)\|_1.$$

The $L_1$ penalty forces sparse latents; each dictionary column of $W_{\text{dec}}$ is hypothesized to correspond to one true underlying feature direction [4][5].

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class SparseAutoencoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model: int, dict_size: int, l1_coef: float):
        super().__init__()
        self.W_enc = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(dict_size, d_model) * 0.01)
        self.b_enc = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(dict_size))
        self.W_dec = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(d_model, dict_size) * 0.01)
        self.b_dec = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d_model))
        self.l1_coef = l1_coef

    def forward(self, x):
        f = torch.relu(x @ self.W_enc.T + self.b_enc)   # sparse code
        x_hat = f @ self.W_dec.T + self.b_dec           # reconstruction
        recon = ((x - x_hat) ** 2).mean()
        sparsity = self.l1_coef * f.abs().sum()
        return x_hat, recon + sparsity
```

Cunningham et al. [4] trained SAEs on a one-layer transformer and found the learned features to be substantially more interpretable (by automated LLM-judge scoring) than neurons or PCA directions; ablating individual features enabled precise model editing (e.g., removing pronoun-prediction capability). Bricken et al. [5] scaled to larger models and showed SAEs recover fine-grained features — from "apostrophes in possessives" to "mentions of the Golden Gate Bridge" — validating that dictionary learning resolves superposition in practice.

Templeton et al. [6] scaled SAEs to Claude 3 Sonnet (a frontier model), training on tens of billions of tokens to extract *tens of millions* of features. Notable results include features for code vulnerabilities, deceptive reasoning ("sycophantic praise"), multilingual concepts, and safety-relevant behaviors — and demonstrations that clamping a feature's activation steers model behavior (e.g., forcing discussion of a topic). This established SAEs as the first interpretability technique demonstrated at frontier scale [6].

## 5 Empirical Analysis and Proofs

The empirical case for the circuit-discovery programme rests on several quantitative results:

1. **Induction-head phase change.** Olsson et al. [2] show that in small models, the formation of induction heads (measured by prefix-matching score) coincides with a discontinuous improvement in in-context learning loss. Ablation of induction heads increases loss on repeated-sequence tasks by an order of magnitude more than ablation of random heads, satisfying the causal criterion [2].

2. **Superposition phase change in toy models.** Elhage et al. [3] demonstrate that the transition from dedicated-neuron to superposed representations is a sharp phase change in sparsity, with the number of features per dimension and the interference geometry matching theoretical predictions from compressed sensing. The $W^T W$ Gram matrix exhibits the predicted block/antipodal structure [3].

3. **SAE feature quality.** Cunningham et al. [4] report that SAE features score higher on automated interpretability metrics than baseline decompositions, and that ablating SAE features produces more targeted behavioral changes. On the indirect-object-identification task, SAE features pinpoint causally responsible directions more finely than prior decompositions [4].

4. **Scaling to frontier models.** Templeton et al. [6] show that SAE training scales to Claude 3 Sonnet with interpretable features discoverable across layers, including abstract and safety-relevant concepts; feature steering (clamping activations) produces predictable behavioral changes, confirming causal efficacy at scale.

5. **Mathematical exactness.** The QK/OV decomposition is not an approximation but an exact algebraic reparameterization of attention [1]; any circuit claim derived from it is falsifiable by construction, since the full model is recovered by summing all paths.

| Claim | Evidence type | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Induction heads cause in-context learning | Ablation + phase change | Strong [2] |
| Superposition explains polysemanticity | Toy-model phase change, geometry | Strong in toy models [3]; hypothesized in LLMs |
| SAEs recover true features | Interpretability scores + ablations | Moderate–strong [4][5] |
| SAEs scale to frontier models | Claude 3 Sonnet features + steering | Strong as existence proof [6] |
| Full circuit discovery is tractable | — | Open / weak |

## 6 Limitations

The programme faces serious methodological limitations:

- **Feature splitting and absorption.** SAE dictionaries are not canonical: a single true feature may be split across multiple latents, or multiple features absorbed into one, depending on hyperparameters ($\lambda$, dictionary size). There is no ground truth in real models, so completeness cannot be verified [5][6].
- **Causal relevance vs. correlational features.** An SAE feature may be interpretable yet causally inert — reconstructing activations faithfully without capturing the directions the model actually *computes with*. Reconstruction loss alone does not guarantee the dictionary carves the model's computation at its joints [4].
- **Exponential circuit enumeration.** The number of composition paths grows exponentially with depth; the elegant two-layer analysis [1] does not scale naively to 100-layer models, where circuits overlap and share subspaces in ways that resist clean decomposition.
- **MLP opacity.** The circuit framework is most complete for attention; MLPs — which hold much of a model's factual knowledge — are analyzed via SAEs on activations, but weight-level circuit understanding of MLPs remains primitive compared to the QK/OV theory [5].
- **Toy-model gap.** Superposition is proven in toy autoencoders [3], but direct evidence in frontier LLMs is indirect (SAEs work well, which is *consistent with* but does not prove superposition). Alternative hypotheses — e.g., that polysemanticity reflects genuinely entangled computation — are not ruled out.
- **Evaluation circularity.** Automated interpretability scoring uses LLMs to judge feature explanations, risking correlated errors between the explainer and the model being explained [4][5].

> **Theorem:** *No current method provably enumerates all features or all circuits of a production-scale transformer.* Until completeness guarantees exist, mechanistic interpretability remains an empirical science of case studies rather than a verified accounting.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The transformer interpretability programme has, in under five years, progressed from a mathematical formalism of attention circuits [1], through the mechanistic explanation of in-context learning via induction heads [2] and the superposition hypothesis explaining polysemanticity [3], to unsupervised dictionary learning that extracts millions of interpretable, steerable features from frontier models [4][5][6]. Each step replaced speculation with mechanism: in-context learning is induction; polysemanticity is superposition; features are directions recoverable by sparse coding.

Yet the central ambition — a complete, verified circuit-level account of a frontier model — remains distant. The path forward requires: (i) scaling SAEs with better completeness metrics and causal validation; (ii) extending weight-level circuit analysis to MLPs and deep composition; (iii) developing falsifiable theories of feature geometry in real models; and (iv) building the tooling to enumerate and verify circuits automatically. If these are achieved, mechanistic interpretability may yet deliver its founding promise: neural networks we can read like source code, audited with the rigor of software engineering rather than the guesswork of behavioral testing.
