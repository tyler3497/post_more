This thesis presents a unified comparative analysis of three modern general-purpose memory allocators — jemalloc, mimalloc, and snmalloc — together with the Transparent Huge Page (THP) substrate that increasingly determines their real-world footprint. We reconstruct each allocator's core mechanism: jemalloc's independent arenas with size-class runs, per-thread caches, and a decay-driven extent lifecycle; mimalloc's extreme free-list sharding with one free list per 64 KiB page and uncontended cross-thread frees via atomic thread-free lists; and snmalloc's lock-free message-passing scheme that returns remotely freed objects to the owning allocator in batches. Fragmentation is decomposed into internal, external, and blowup components with explicit bounds, including a bin-packing theorem for huge-page granularity. We analyze how sub-release, khugepaged collapse, and MADV_COLLAPSE interact with each design, and map each allocator to the workload class where it dominates.

## 1 Introduction

Dynamic memory allocation is among the oldest and most consequential problems in systems software. Every `malloc` and `free` negotiates between *throughput*, *scalability* across growing thread counts, and *footprint* — and because allocation sits on the critical path of nearly every workload, single-digit improvements compound across entire fleets [4].

Classical solutions — `dlmalloc`, glibc's `ptmalloc2` — were designed for modest core counts. Multicore ubiquity forced a re-examination of the central tension of concurrent allocation: **fast, thread-local fast paths inevitably hide free memory from the global view, and that hidden memory is fragmentation.** Three designs now define the modern landscape, each taking a distinct position:

- **jemalloc** [1][2], by Jason Evans, partitions the heap into independent *arenas* that absorb contention, with per-thread caches smoothing the hot path and a sophisticated *extent* lifecycle (decay, purge, retention) managing the return of memory to the operating system.
- **mimalloc** [3][4], by Daan Leijen, Benjamin Zorn, and Leonardo de Moura at Microsoft Research, pushes locality to an extreme with *free-list sharding*: instead of one free list per size class, there is a free list **per 64 KiB page**, keeping allocations pinned inside a single page until it fills.
- **snmalloc** [5], by a team spanning Microsoft Research Cambridge and Imperial College London, attacks the *producer/consumer* pathology — threads that free memory allocated by other threads — with a lock-free **message-passing** scheme that returns deallocated objects to the originating allocator in batches.

Orthogonal to all three is the virtual-memory substrate. **Transparent Huge Pages (THP)** [8] collapse 4 KiB pages into 2 MiB pages behind the application's back — but allocators that sub-release with `MADV_DONTNEED` *splinter* huge pages, and khugepaged's collapse heuristics fight allocator-level fragmentation control [9][12]. Modern tcmalloc derivatives bin-pack allocations into huge-page-aligned spans to keep entire huge pages releasable [7], interacting subtly with every other fragmentation mechanism.

This thesis unifies these threads: the three allocator architectures, the fragmentation theory evaluating them, and the huge-page integration layer determining their real-world footprint.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Fragmentation: Internal, External, and Blowup

Fragmentation is the gap between the memory an application *logically* needs and the memory its allocator *physically* holds. Three distinct phenomena compose it:

1. **Internal fragmentation** arises when an allocator rounds a request up to the nearest size class. If size classes grow geometrically with ratio *r*, worst-case internal fragmentation is bounded by *1 − 1/r*. With jemalloc's fine-grained classes (*r* ≈ 1.25 for many ranges), this stays under ~20%; coarser class ladders pay more.
2. **External fragmentation** arises when free memory is scattered in chunks too small or awkwardly placed to satisfy future requests, even though the total free volume is sufficient.
3. **Blowup fragmentation**, identified as a first-class concept in the allocator literature [11], measures the pathological case: threads cache freed objects locally and never return them to a globally usable pool, so total heap size balloons far beyond live data. Producer/consumer workloads are the canonical trigger.

> **Theorem:** *For any segregated-fits allocator with size-class growth ratio r and per-thread caches of unbounded size, there exist allocation traces with blowup fragmentation arbitrarily large.* The bound follows from constructing a producer thread that allocates N objects while M consumer threads free them into thread-local lists never reclaimed; heap occupancy stays Ω(M·N) while live data is O(N). This motivates every reclamation mechanism discussed in §4.

Berger, Zorn, and McKinley's compositional framework [10] showed that allocator design is best understood as a composition of *policies* (which free block to reuse) and *mechanisms* (how blocks are split, coalesced, and returned to the OS) — a lens we apply throughout.

### 2.2 The Hardware Substrate: TLBs, NUMA, and Huge Pages

On x86-64, a 2 MiB huge page maps 512× the memory of a single 4 KiB TLB entry, cutting TLB misses and page-table walk depth for large heaps [8]. Linux's THP machinery operates in two modes:

- `always`: the kernel aggressively backs anonymous mappings with huge pages.
- `madvise`: only regions explicitly marked `MADV_HUGEPAGE` are candidates; the default on most distributions.

The tension with allocators is immediate. khugepaged *collapses* 4 KiB pages into 2 MiB pages by scanning and migrating — but it cannot collapse a region containing even a single live 4 KiB page into a *free* huge page, and allocators that partially release spans with `MADV_DONTNEED` punch holes that defeat collapse for the entire 2 MiB region [9]. The result: an allocator can do everything right at the size-class level and still present a fragmented physical footprint because its *sub-release granularity* fights the kernel's *collapse granularity*.

### 2.3 Thread Caching and Its Discontents

Almost every modern allocator employs some form of thread caching: freed objects are kept in thread-local lists rather than returned to a shared pool [5]. When allocation and deallocation are roughly symmetric per thread, this is nearly free — allocation becomes a pointer bump with no atomics. But it is precisely this caching that (a) hides memory from other threads, (b) defeats coalescing, and (c) creates the blowup scenarios of §2.1. The three allocators studied here differ primarily in *how they bound, reclaim, and recycle* these thread-local holdings.

## 3 Methodology

Published benchmark numbers across allocator papers are notoriously sensitive to workload, compiler, kernel version, and THP settings [3][5]; our analysis is therefore comparative and mechanism-driven:

1. **Architectural reconstruction** — recovering each allocator's fast path, slow path, and reclamation path from its primary publication and current implementation documentation [1][2][3][4][5].
2. **Fragmentation accounting** — decomposing each design's memory footprint into internal, external, and blowup components with explicit bounds where the literature provides them.
3. **Mechanism interaction analysis** — examining how each allocator's OS-interface layer (mmap/MADV policies, extent decay, page purging) interacts with THP collapse and `MADV_COLLAPSE` [9].
4. **Workload-class mapping** — identifying, for each design, the workload class where it dominates (balanced multithreaded, producer/consumer, latency-critical language runtimes) and where it regresses.

Quantitative claims are attributed to their sources: mimalloc's redis measurements (7% over tcmalloc, 14% over jemalloc [3]), snmalloc's producer/consumer benchmarks [5], Evans' jemalloc characterization [1][2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 jemalloc: Arenas, Extents, and the Decay Lifecycle

jemalloc's organizing principle is **partitioning**. The heap is divided into *arenas* — fully independent allocation domains, each maintaining its own chunks/extents, from which it carves page runs for small and large objects. Application threads are assigned arenas round-robin on first allocation (via TLS or a thread-ID hash where TLS is unavailable), and — critically — **freed memory is always returned to the arena it came from, regardless of which thread performs the free** [1]. This single rule bounds blowup: a consumer thread cannot accumulate another arena's memory in its own cache; instead it pushes the object back across the arena boundary (taking the arena's lock, or in modern jemalloc, using per-size-class bin locks).

Within an arena, jemalloc segregates by size class with three categories [1]:

| Category | Size range (64-bit default) | Backing |
|---|---|---|
| Small | 8 B – ~3.5 KiB | Slabs carved from runs; metadata shared per run |
| Large | 4 KiB – ~4 MiB | Dedicated extents per allocation |
| Huge | > 4 MiB | Dedicated chunks; metadata in a global red-black tree |

Virtual memory is managed in chunk-aligned units (historically 4 MiB, later generalized to the *extent* abstraction), which permits **constant-time metadata lookup for interior pointers** via address masking — a decisive fast-path win — and logarithmic lookup for huge objects via the red-black tree [1].

The per-thread cache (*tcache*) sits in front of the arenas: each thread holds recently freed objects per size class, refilling and flushing in batches so the common case never touches an arena lock. The *extent lifecycle* then governs unneeded memory: extents transition through **dirty → muzzy → retained** states, with time-based *decay* policies deciding when dirty extents are purged and retained extents unmapped — a `decay_ms` knob trading *RSS responsiveness* against *reuse warmth*.

```c
/* Simplified jemalloc tcache fast path (conceptual) */
void *tcache_alloc(tcache_t *tc, size_t size) {
    szind_t ind = sz_size2index(size);      /* size class lookup */
    cache_bin_t *bin = &tc->bins[ind];
    if (likely(bin->ncached > 0)) {
        return cache_bin_alloc(bin);       /* no locks, no atomics */
    }
    return arena_alloc_slow(tc->arena, ind); /* refill from arena */
}
```

jemalloc's fragmentation story is thus: *internal* fragmentation bounded by fine size classes; *blowup* bounded by the always-return-to-origin-arena rule plus tcache flush thresholds; *external* fragmentation managed by extent coalescing and decay. Its weakness is lock-adjacent: under extreme thread counts, arena count must be tuned, and cross-thread frees still serialize on arena bin locks.

### 4.2 mimalloc: Free-List Sharding and Thread-Local Heaps

mimalloc, developed at Microsoft Research to back the reference-counted Lean and Koka language runtimes [3], inverts jemalloc's partitioning philosophy: instead of *few large arenas shared by many threads*, it gives **every thread its own heap** and shards free lists to an extreme — **one free list per 64 KiB mimalloc page**, not per size class.

The key data structure insight: each page maintains *three* sharded free lists [3][4]:

1. The **free list** — blocks available for the owning thread's immediate allocation.
2. The **local_free list** — blocks freed by the owning thread, not yet merged.
3. The **thread_free list** — blocks freed by *other* threads, pushed via a single atomic compare-and-swap.

Because there are thousands of such per-page lists, the probability that two threads concurrently free into the *same* page's `thread_free` list is low, so cross-thread frees are **uncontended atomic pushes in the common case** — no locks, and no arena-wide serialization point [3]. Allocation stays pinned inside one page until the page fills, which is the *locality* payoff: sequential allocations land in adjacent cache lines of the same page regardless of what is freed elsewhere in the heap.

mimalloc additionally exploits **temporal cadence**: after a fixed number of fast-path allocations, the allocator deliberately falls into the slower generic path to perform maintenance — merging `local_free`/`thread_free` lists, handling deferred frees, and checking for memory pressure. This is the mechanism that supports the deferred reference-count decrements Lean and Koka need: the allocator cooperates with the runtime by resuming deferred work exactly when memory pressure makes it profitable [3].

The page/segment geometry is:

| Unit | Size | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Page | 64 KiB | Sharded free lists; single size class per page |
| Segment | 32 MiB | Container of pages; unit of OS interaction |
| Large objects | > ~1 MiB | Direct segment allocation |

Fragmentation-wise, sharding is a double-edged sword. *Locality* is superb and *blowup* is contained because pages are reclaimed per-page when fully free. But per-page sharding can strand *partially filled* pages: a page holding one live 16-byte object cannot be released, and with thousands of pages the "one live object per page" pathology is the sharded analogue of external fragmentation. mimalloc counters this with eager page reset and segment-level decommit heuristics [3].

### 4.3 snmalloc: Message Passing for Producer/Consumer Workloads

snmalloc starts from an empirical observation: in producer/consumer workloads — one thread allocates, another frees — *every* conventional thread cache fails. Either the consumer's cache grows without bound (blowup), or cross-thread frees serialize on shared structures (scalability collapse) [5]. snmalloc's answer is to treat deallocation as **message passing**.

Each allocator (typically one per thread) owns 64 KiB *slabs* described by a novel **bump-pointer free-list** requiring only **64 bits of metadata per slab** [5]. When thread B frees an object owned by thread A's allocator, B does not touch A's data structures at all: it appends the freed object to a **message queue** destined for A. Messages are batched, so **thousands of remote deallocations are retired with a single atomic operation** [5]. Thread A drains its message queue lazily — when it next allocates, or when it polls — and recycles the objects into its own free lists.

The protocol has exactly two branches on the malloc fast path on Linux/Clang [5]:

```c
/* snmalloc fast paths (conceptual) */
void *sn_malloc(size_t size) {
    /* branch 1: size class fits cached slab? */
    /* branch 2: bump pointer within slab?   */
    ... /* else: slow path — new slab, drain messages */
}

void sn_free(void *p) {
    Slab *s = slab_of(p);              /* constant-time via alignment */
    if (s->owner == my_alloc())
        local_free_list_push(s, p);    /* no atomics, no locks */
    else
        message_queue_push(s->owner, p); /* batched, single atomic */
}
```

Because remote frees never contend on the owner's structures, snmalloc scales nearly linearly on producer/consumer benchmarks where jemalloc and tcmalloc degrade sharply [5]. The cost is *reclamation latency*: memory freed remotely is invisible to the owner until it drains its queue, so a thread that allocates once and then idles can strand memory — the design explicitly notes the need for care when "the owning thread never allocates again or is blocked" [5]. Modern snmalloc has evolved its metadata layout substantially since the original paper, but the message-passing return path remains the architectural core [5].

### 4.4 Huge Pages, THP, and Fragmentation Control at the OS Boundary

All three allocators must eventually talk to the kernel, and here THP changes the game. Three interactions create fragmentation at the OS boundary:

1. **Splintering by sub-release.** When an allocator returns part of a 2 MiB region with `MADV_DONTNEED`, the kernel *zaps the PMD* — the entire huge page is split. If the allocator later re-touches that memory, khugepaged may eventually re-collapse it, but collapse requires scanning and migration, and any single live 4 KiB page pins the region [9].

2. **Collapse timing vs. decay timing.** jemalloc-style decay wants to purge idle extents *promptly* to control RSS; khugepaged wants long-lived, densely populated regions to collapse. An allocator that purges aggressively produces precisely the holey regions khugepaged cannot collapse — the two controllers fight.

3. **Bin-packing into huge pages.** The modern tcmalloc approach (*Temeraire*) explicitly allocates in huge-page-sized spans and **bin-packs** small objects into already-partially-used huge pages, maximizing the probability that *entire* huge pages become free and releasable [7][12]. This is fragmentation control moved up one level of the memory hierarchy: the unit of release is no longer the 4 KiB page but the 2 MiB huge page.

`MADV_COLLAPSE` (Linux 6.x) gives allocators a synchronous collapse primitive: an allocator managing hugepage-sized chunks that previously sub-released with `MADV_DONTNEED` can now explicitly re-collapse hot memory, regaining dTLB performance on demand, with CPU charged to the requesting process rather than to khugepaged's unpredictable schedule [9]. This turns huge-page management from a kernel heuristic into an **allocator-directed policy** — the natural endpoint of the trend this thesis traces: control migrating from global heuristics into the allocator's own hands.

> **Theorem:** *Huge-page-aware bin-packing lower-bounds releasable memory.* If an allocator packs allocations into huge-page-aligned spans using a first-fit-decreasing discipline over live objects, the number of partially occupied huge pages is at most ⌈*L*/*H*⌉ + *k*, where *L* is live bytes, *H* the huge-page size, and *k* the number of size classes — i.e., fragmentation at huge-page granularity is bounded by class count, not by allocation count. The proof mirrors the classic bin-packing argument applied at 2 MiB granularity [7][12].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 What the Primary Sources Report

- **mimalloc** shows 7% improvement over tcmalloc and 14% over jemalloc on redis, and consistent wins across sequential and concurrent benchmarks — attributed directly to sharding (locality) and the uncontended `thread_free` path [3].
- **snmalloc** outperforms existing allocators specifically on producer/consumer benchmarks, where message batching converts O(n) remote-free synchronization into O(1) amortized atomic operations per batch [5].
- **jemalloc**'s published characterization centers on scalability: near-linear scaling of allocation throughput with arena count on multicore FreeBSD workloads, with fragmentation held flat by the arena-return invariant [1][2].

### 5.2 A Unified Fragmentation Comparison

| Allocator | Internal frag. bound | Blowup control | Cross-thread free cost | Huge-page story |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| jemalloc | Fine classes (~≤20%) | Return-to-origin-arena + tcache flush | Arena/bin lock | Extent decay; THP-agnostic by default |
| mimalloc | Fine classes | Per-page reclaim; eager reset | Uncontended CAS into `thread_free` | Segment decommit; sharding vs. density tension |
| snmalloc | Fine classes | Message-queue drain; owner recycles | Batched: 1 atomic per 1000s of frees | Slab-aligned; message latency vs. release |
| tcmalloc (Temeraire) | Fine classes | Page-heap spans | Central page-heap lock (spans) | **Bin-packing into 2 MiB spans** [7] |

### 5.3 Sketch of the Sharding Locality Argument

*Claim.* Under free-list sharding with one list per page, the expected number of distinct pages touched by *n* sequential allocations is minimized relative to any global-free-list discipline.

*Proof sketch.* With a global per-class free list, allocations draw from a list scattered across all pages holding that class. With per-page sharding, allocation consumes the *current* page's list until exhaustion; hence *n* allocations touch at most ⌈*n*/*c*⌉ + 1 pages (*c* = objects per page), a tight bound. This is the formal content of Leijen et al.'s locality claim [3]: sharding converts *global* reuse order into *page-local* reuse order, which is what prefetchers and TLBs reward.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Benchmark fragility.** Each paper chooses flattering workloads — redis for mimalloc [3], producer/consumer microbenchmarks for snmalloc [5]. Kernel version, THP mode, and compiler can shift results by more than the reported deltas.
2. **The idle-owner problem.** Both mimalloc's `thread_free` lists and snmalloc's message queues strand memory when the owning thread stops allocating [4][5]. Mitigations exist (background reclamation, polling) but add complexity and wakeups.
3. **Huge-page bin-packing vs. latency.** Packing for huge-page releasability [7] concentrates allocation into fewer pages, which can *increase* contention on those pages' metadata and hurt tail latency — a tradeoff the Temeraire design acknowledges.
4. **Security surface.** Fine-grained allocator metadata adjacent to heap objects is a long-standing exploitation target; mimalloc's *secure mode* (guard pages, randomized allocation) trades throughput for hardening [3], and snmalloc documents an evolving security model [5]. None of the performance claims in §5 hold under hardened configurations.
5. **NUMA blindness.** The designs analyzed are largely NUMA-oblivious at the allocation layer; arena-per-NUMA-node or segment-per-node policies exist as extensions but complicate every invariant above.
6. **This thesis is analytic, not experimental.** The fragmentation bounds and comparisons are derived from published mechanisms and proofs; independent reproduction on current kernels (6.x, with `MADV_COLLAPSE` [9]) remains future work.

## 7 Conclusion

Three allocators, three answers to one question — *where should a freed object go?* jemalloc answers "back to the arena that owns it, through a thread cache, on a decay schedule" [1][2]; mimalloc answers "into the per-page shard it came from, with cross-thread frees parked in an uncontended atomic list" [3]; snmalloc answers "into a message for the owning allocator, delivered in batches without locks" [5]. Each bounds blowup fragmentation by a different invariant, and each pays a different price: lock adjacency, page-stranding, or reclamation latency.

Above them all, the OS is renegotiating the contract. THP rewards allocators that think in 2 MiB units, punishes careless sub-release, and — with `MADV_COLLAPSE` — hands collapse decisions back to the allocator [8][9]. The next generation of allocators will be *designed around* huge pages, with 2 MiB-granularity bin-packing [7][12] as fundamental as size classes.

The deepest lesson is Berger et al.'s [10], a quarter-century old and still undefeated: allocators are compositions of policies and mechanisms, and progress comes from choosing the composition whose invariants match the workload. Sharding matches locality-hungry runtimes. Message passing matches producer/consumer pipelines. Arenas match general-purpose multithreaded servers. And huge-page awareness matches everything with a large, long-lived heap. The art — as ever — is knowing which composition you are running.
