# Canonical Graphs for Boolean Worlds: Reduced Ordered Binary Decision Diagrams and Symbolic Model Checking

## Abstract

The verification of finite-state reactive systems confronts the state-space explosion: a circuit with *n* latches has 2^*n* reachable configurations, far beyond explicit enumeration for any realistic *n*. This thesis develops the theoretical core of the most successful response to that crisis — *symbolic model checking* — in which sets of states and transition relations are encoded not as extensional enumerations but as compact canonical data structures: *reduced ordered binary decision diagrams* (ROBDDs). We present Bryant's canonicity theorem and its proof sketch, the *Apply* and *Reduce* algorithms (including the ternary ITE operator) with tight complexity bounds, static and dynamic variable-ordering heuristics culminating in Rudell's sifting, the relational-product machinery for symbolic pre- and post-image computation, and the fixpoint formulation of CTL model checking over Kripke structures. We situate these ideas inside the historical SMV and NuSMV toolchains and the breakthrough experiment of Burch, Clarke, McMillan, Dill, and Hwang (1992), who verified systems with over 10^20 states. We close with the fundamental limitations — exponential lower bounds for integer multiplication, the NP-completeness of optimal variable ordering, and intermediate BDD blowup — that delimit what symbolic methods can achieve and motivate the partitioned, SAT-based, and abstraction-refinement countermeasures in use today.

## 1 Introduction

Explicit-state model checking represents a system's reachable states one by one. The approach is conceptually simple but dies by the same exponential that makes finite-state verification interesting: for *n* Boolean state variables, the state space *S* = {0,1}^*n* doubles with every added latch. Bryant's 1986 paper [1] introduced a representation that breaks this impasse in practice by exploiting *structure* rather than *size*. An ordered binary decision diagram (OBDD) is a rooted directed acyclic graph that evaluates a Boolean function by branching on variables in a fixed total order. Two reduction rules — merging isomorphic subgraphs and eliminating nodes whose two children coincide — produce the *reduced* ordered BDD, and Bryant's central result is that this representation is *canonical*: for a fixed variable order, two functions are equal if and only if their ROBDDs are graph-isomorphic [1].

Canonicity transforms the Boolean equivalence problem into a pointer comparison. Compositional operations — AND, OR, XOR, substitution, existential quantification — reduce to a handful of memoized graph algorithms whose running time is bounded by products of argument graph sizes. Because equality testing is constant time, symbolic fixpoint computations become feasible: the set of states satisfying a CTL formula is computed as a least or greatest fixpoint of monotone predicate transformers, each step manipulating BDDs rather than state enumerations [2][7].

This thesis reconstructs the full stack. Section 2 develops the combinatorial foundations: Shannon expansion, OBDD semantics, the reduction operators, and the canonicity theorem. Section 3 presents the manipulation algorithms and their complexity, variable-ordering heuristics, and the relational-product kernel of symbolic reachability. Section 4 gives deep dives into canonicity's proof mechanics, the Apply/Reduce pseudocode and its operation cache, dynamic reordering via sifting, the partitioned-transition-relation machinery, and the SMV/NuSMV realization of CTL checking. Section 5 collects the key complexity results in tabular form and reviews the empirical evidence. Section 6 discusses the provable limitations, and Section 7 concludes with the state of countermeasures.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Boolean functions and Shannon expansion

A Boolean function *f* : {0,1}^*n* → {0,1} admits, for every variable *x*, the *Shannon decomposition*

> *f* = *x̄* · *f*|_{*x*=0} + *x* · *f*|_{*x*=1}

where *f*|_{*x*=*b*} is the cofactor of *f* with *x* fixed to *b*. Recursive decomposition yields the *binary decision tree*: a complete binary tree of depth *n* with 2^*n* leaves labeled by function values. The tree is canonical but always exponential — it is exactly the truth table in tree form. The insight of Bryant was that enormous sharing is available: many cofactors coincide, and entire levels can often be skipped.

### 2.2 Ordered BDDs and the reduction rules

Fix a total variable ordering *x*₁ < *x*₂ < … < *xₙ*. An *ordered* BDD requires that along every root-to-leaf path, variables appear in strictly increasing order. Two reduction rules convert an ordered BDD into reduced form [1]:

- **R1 (merge):** If two distinct nonterminal nodes have the same variable label and identical low and high children, merge them.
- **R2 (eliminate):** If a node has identical low and high children, delete it, redirecting its incoming edges to its single child.

Iterated to a fixpoint, these rules yield the *ROBDD*. Implementations assign each node a unique table entry keyed by the triple *(variable, low-child, high-child)* — the *unique table* — so that R1 is enforced by construction through *hash-consing*: a node is created only if its triple is absent, and identical triples return the same pointer [5].

> **Theorem (Bryant's canonicity, 1986).** For a fixed variable ordering, the reduced OBDD representing a Boolean function is unique: any two ROBDDs for the same function are isomorphic, and distinct functions have non-isomorphic ROBDDs.

The theorem follows by induction on the number of variables: the root of an ROBDD for *f* must be labeled by the smallest variable *xᵢ* on which *f* depends (if *f* is constant, the ROBDD is a terminal); its low and high children are, by the induction hypothesis, the unique ROBDDs of the two cofactors. ### 2.3 Complexity landscape of ROBDD size

ROBDD size is radically order-sensitive. A *multiplier* circuit has ROBDDs of size exponential in the operand width *for every variable ordering* — Bryant's celebrated 1991 lower bound for integer multiplication [9]. Conversely, symmetric functions, adders, and parity have linear-size ROBDDs under natural orders. The optimal-ordering problem is NP-complete even for fixed orderings of restricted function classes [10]. This tension — canonicity per order versus hardness of choosing the order — drives the entire algorithmic agenda of Section 4.3.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Apply: binary operations via ITE

All binary Boolean operations are implemented through a single *universal* operator: `ITE(F, G, H) = F·G + F̄·H` (if-then-else). AND is `ITE(F, G, 0)`, OR is `ITE(F, 1, G)`, XOR is `ITE(F, Ḡ, G)`, and so on. The *Apply* algorithm computes `ITE` on three ROBDDs recursively using Shannon expansion, memoizing visited triples *(u, v, w)* in a *computed table* (operation cache) so that no triple is expanded twice [1][5]:

```python
def ite(F, G, H):
    # terminals
    if F == ONE:  return G
    if F == ZERO: return H
    if G == H:    return G
    if G == ONE and H == ZERO: return F
    # memoize on the triple
    key = (id(F), id(G), id(H))
    if key in cache: return cache[key]
    x = min(var(F), var(G), var(H))          # topmost variable
    lo = ite(cofactor(F, x, 0), cofactor(G, x, 0), cofactor(H, x, 0))
    hi = ite(cofactor(F, x, 1), cofactor(G, x, 1), cofactor(H, x, 1))
    R = make_node(x, lo, hi)                  # unique table enforces R1/R2
    cache[key] = R
    return R
```

Because the recursion visits at most |F|·|G|·|H| triples, `ITE` runs in time *O*(|F|·|G|·|H|), and each binary operation in time *O*(|F|·|G|) — proportional to the *product* of argument sizes, not to the state space they denote. This is the engine of symbolic manipulation: functions over 10^20 states are combined in milliseconds when their graphs stay small.

### 3.2 Reduce: bottom-up canonicalization

When BDDs are built by other means (e.g., from a decision tree or a formula), *Reduce* canonicalizes in a single bottom-up pass: process nodes in reverse topological order, redirect each node's children to their canonical forms, then look up the resulting triple in the unique table — creating a fresh node only on a miss [1]. Reduce runs in linear time *O*(|G|) and is the correctness backbone of every package: the unique table guarantees that the result of Apply is *already* reduced, so Apply never calls Reduce explicitly [5].

### 3.3 Existential quantification and the relational product

Model checking needs *projection*: given a relation *R*(x⃗, x⃗′) and a set *S*(x⃗), the *image* (forward reachable states in one step) is

> **Img**(*S*) = ∃x⃗. [*S*(x⃗) ∧ *R*(x⃗, x⃗′)]

The naive recipe — compute the conjunction (a BDD of size up to |S|·|R|), then quantify each variable (existential quantification of *xᵢ* is `OR(cofactor(f,xᵢ,0), cofactor(f,xᵢ,1))`, each step potentially squaring size) — builds an intermediate conjunction that can be exponentially larger than the final result. The *relational product* fuses conjunction and quantification into one recursive descent: quantify a variable *as soon as* it no longer appears in any unprocessed conjunct, so intermediate BDDs stay small [2][7]. This early-quantification scheduling, together with *partitioned transition relations* (Section 4.4), is what makes symbolic traversal scale.

### 3.4 Variable ordering heuristics

Because size is order-dependent and optimal ordering is intractable, practice relies on heuristics:

- **Static heuristics:** topology-driven orders (e.g., interleave present-state and next-state variables, keep strongly coupled variables adjacent) derived from the circuit or program structure before any BDD is built [1].
- **Dynamic reordering:** periodically permute the global order *during* computation — most famously Rudell's *sifting* (Section 4.3) — accepting a temporary slowdown to collapse accumulated bloat [6].

The interleaving heuristic for model checking deserves emphasis: ordering …,*xᵢ*, *xᵢ*′,… (each state variable adjacent to its primed copy) keeps the transition relation's cofactors local and is almost universal in SMV-family tools [2][12].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The canonicity theorem: proof mechanics

Bryant's proof proceeds by strong induction on *n*, the number of variables [1]. Base case (*n* = 0): only two functions exist, represented by the terminals **0** and **1**. Inductive step: let *G* be an ROBDD for *f* over *x*₁ < … < *xₙ₊₁*. If *f* is independent of *x*₁, no node can be labeled *x*₁ (R2 would have removed it), so *G* is an ROBDD over *n* variables and uniqueness follows by hypothesis. Otherwise the root is labeled *x*₁, and — crucially — R1 and R2 together force the low and high subgraphs to be *exactly* the ROBDDs of the cofactors *f*|₀ and *f*|₁, which are unique by the inductive hypothesis. No other ROBDD for *f* can differ: any would have the same root label and the same (unique) cofactor subgraphs.

Three corollaries make canonicity the fulcrum of the field: (i) *equivalence checking* is a pointer comparison — satisfiability and tautology reduce to comparing against the terminals; (ii) *memoization* is sound, because structurally identical subproblems are guaranteed to be semantically identical; (iii) fixpoint iteration can detect *convergence* by pointer equality, which is how symbolic reachability knows it has stabilized [2].

![Trace of the Apply (ITE) algorithm: two input BDDs, recursion over variable levels, computed-table memoization, and the canonical result](/thesis/ths_1789097465477_23282-1.webp)

The computed table is not merely an optimization; it is what bounds the recursion. Without memoization, repeated subproblems along shared subgraphs cause exponential blowup; with it, the recursion graph has at most one node per triple, giving the *O*(|F|·|G|·|H|) bound [1]. Practical packages (Brace–Rudell–Bryant [5], CUDD [8]) add two engineering pillars:

- **Complement edges:** a single bit on each edge negates the pointed-to subgraph, halving node counts for many functions and making negation *O*(1). Canonicity is preserved by normalizing (e.g., complement only the low edge).
- **Garbage collection with reference counts:** unreachable nodes are reclaimed, and the computed table is flushed on collection to avoid pinning dead subgraphs [5].

Quantification deserves a precise statement. `Exists(x, f) = Restrict(f, x, 0) OR Restrict(f, x, 1)` costs *O*(|f|²) in the worst case per variable, and *n* successive quantifications can in principle square size repeatedly — yet in practice early quantification inside the relational product keeps the operands small, and BDD-based quantification remains the workhorse of symbolic image computation [7].

### 4.3 Variable ordering: sifting and dynamic reordering

Rudell's 1993 algorithm [6] treats reordering as a sequence of *adjacent swaps*.

```python
def sift(variable v):
    best_pos, best_size = pos(v), size(G)
    # move v to the top
    while pos(v) > 0:
        swap_adjacent(v, predecessor(v))
        if size(G) < best_size: best_pos, best_size = pos(v), size(G)
    # move v to the bottom
    while pos(v) < n - 1:
        swap_adjacent(v, successor(v))
        if size(G) < best_size: best_pos, best_size = pos(v), size(G)
    move v back to best_pos
def dynamic_reorder():
    for v in variables_sorted_by_subgraph_size_descending():
        sift(v)   # symmetric sifting variant
```

*Symmetric sifting* interleaves the upward and downward passes so that the best position found so far is never more than one pass away [6]. The total cost is *O*(*n*² · swaps), each swap cheap, and — decisively — reordering runs *inside* the package with garbage collection, so external pointers held by the application remain valid [5][6]. Empirical studies show sifting routinely recovering an order of magnitude in node count during long symbolic traversals, at the cost of periodic pauses.

### 4.4 Symbolic reachability and partitioned transition relations

Symbolic model checking encodes a Kripke structure *M* = (*S*, *S*₀, *R*, *L*) with BDDs: *S*₀(x⃗) is the characteristic function of initial states, *R*(x⃗, x⃗′) the transition relation, and each CTL subformula a BDD over x⃗ [2].

> Reach₀ = S₀;  Reach_{k+1} = Reach_k ∪ Img(Reach_k);  stop when Reach_{k+1} = Reach_k,

with equality tested by pointer identity thanks to canonicity.

The transition relation is the scaling bottleneck: for a system with many components, the monolithic *R*(x⃗, x⃗′) explodes.

### 4.5 SMV, NuSMV, and CTL checking complexity

Clarke and Emerson's CTL [3] gives the specification language; McMillan's SMV [7] gave the first symbolic checker.

| Operator | Fixpoint characterization |
|---|---|
| **EX** φ | Img⁻¹(⟦φ⟧) (pre-image, one relational product) |
| **EG** φ | νZ. ⟦φ⟧ ∧ EX Z (greatest fixpoint) |
| **E**[φ **U** ψ] | μZ. ⟦ψ⟧ ∨ (⟦φ⟧ ∧ EX Z) (least fixpoint) |
| **AX** φ | ¬EX ¬φ; **AG** φ = ¬E[true U ¬φ]; **AF**, **AU** dual |

where ⟦·⟧ maps formulas to BDDs.

| CTL model checking (symbolic) | poly in BDD sizes per fixpoint step | [2][7] |

Empirically, the watershed is Burch et al. [2]: verifying the Futurebus+ cache-coherence protocol and pipelined designs with state spaces exceeding 10^20 — roughly a *billion* times beyond explicit enumeration — in minutes on 1990 hardware. Clarke, Grumberg, Hiraishi, Jha, Long, McMillan, and Ness subsequently found previously undetected errors in the IEEE Futurebus+ standard itself, the first formal verification of an IEEE standard [14]. The SMV lineage scaled further: NuSMV handles industrial hardware blocks, and the partitioned-relation technique of [13] pushed symbolic traversal into designs with hundreds of latches. A persistent empirical pattern is that BDD size tracks *coupling structure* — control-dominated logic stays compact, while datapaths (multipliers, wide comparators) resist every ordering.

## 6 Limitations

**Provable exponentials.** Bryant's multiplier bound [9] is unconditional: no variable ordering, no heuristic, no dynamic reordering can compress integer multiplication below exponential ROBDD size. Arithmetic datapaths are therefore outside BDD model checking's reach in principle — a limitation later addressed by *BMDs, *PHDDs, and ultimately SAT/SMT-based word-level methods.

**Ordering hardness.** Optimal ordering is NP-complete [10]; sifting is hill-climbing with no guarantees and can stall at poor local minima. Worse, the order that minimizes the transition relation need not minimize the reachable set or the property BDDs, and the "best" order drifts as the fixpoint evolves — dynamic reordering chases a moving target.

**Intermediate blowup.** The final ROBDD of a fixpoint iterate may be small while the conjunctions and quantifications *inside* one relational product transiently explode — the classic *intermediate BDD blowup*. Partitioning [13] and conjunctive decomposition mitigate but do not eliminate it; a single bad image step can exhaust memory even when the answer is compact.

**Expressiveness ceiling.** ROBDDs encode Boolean functions over finite domains. Unbounded integers, reals, arrays, and heap structures need extensions (ADDs/MTBDDs [8], or abstraction), and liveness properties beyond CTL's reach push toward LTL and fairness machinery with its own fixpoint costs [12].

These limits are not defects of implementation but theorems about the representation — they are precisely what justifies the modern portfolio: BDDs for control logic, bounded model checking and IC3/PDR for deep bugs, and counterexample-guided abstraction refinement to bridge the gap.

## 7 Conclusion

Bryant's ROBDD turned Boolean functions from objects of exponential description into objects of *manipulable* description, whenever structure permits. The canonicity theorem made equality trivial; the Apply/Reduce algorithms made composition polynomial in the representation; sifting made the representation adaptive; and the relational product plus CTL fixpoints turned those ingredients into the first verification technology to cross the 10^20-state barrier [1][2][6]. The limitations are as instructive as the successes: exponential lower bounds for multiplication, NP-completeness of optimal ordering, and intermediate blowup are theorems that carve out exactly where symbolic methods dominate and where successors — SAT-based bounded model checking, interpolation, IC3 — take over. Four decades on, every symbolic model checker still bottoms out in a unique table and a computed table, and every one of them is, at heart, an implementation of the six pages of algorithms Bryant published in 1986 [1][5].
