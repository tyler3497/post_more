# The Yamabe Problem: Conformal Deformation to Constant Scalar Curvature, the Positive Mass Theorem, and Geometric Analysis

## Abstract

In 1960 Hidehiko Yamabe claimed that every compact Riemannian manifold of dimension at least three admits a metric of constant scalar curvature in its conformal class, launching a program that would occupy geometric analysts for a quarter century [1][2]. The assertion is correct, but the original argument contained a subtle analytic gap — the failure of compactness of the critical Sobolev embedding — detected by Trudinger and repaired in stages by Trudinger, Aubin, and finally Schoen. This article presents a complete PhD-level account of the solution: the variational formulation through the Yamabe functional and its invariant, the conformal Laplacian and the critical-exponent Yamabe equation, Aubin's Weyl-curvature test functions settling the high-dimensional non-conformally-flat case, and Schoen's decisive reduction of the remaining cases to the positive mass theorem of general relativity via the Green's function of the conformal Laplacian and asymptotically flat ends [3][4]. We explain both proofs of the positive mass theorem — Schoen–Yau minimal hypersurfaces and Witten spinors [5][6] — and survey later developments: blow-up analysis, the compactness conjecture, its high-dimensional failure, and the fully nonlinear Yamabe problem [7].

## 1 Introduction

Few problems in modern differential geometry combine elementary statement, deep analysis, and surprising interdisciplinary reach as completely as the **Yamabe problem**:

> **Problem (Yamabe, 1960).** *Let (M, g) be a compact Riemannian manifold of dimension n ≥ 3. Does there exist a metric g̃ conformal to g whose scalar curvature is constant?*

The question is the natural higher-dimensional analogue of the uniformization theorem for surfaces. In dimension two the problem is fully understood through the Gauss–Bonnet theorem and the Liouville equation. In dimension n ≥ 3 a conformal change of metric furnishes only *one* unknown function, while the full Riemann curvature tensor has on the order of n^4 independent components — so constant *sectional* curvature is hopelessly overdetermined (the Weyl tensor is the conformal obstruction [1]). Constant *scalar* curvature imposes exactly one scalar condition, and Yamabe's insight was that this single condition should always be solvable.

Yamabe's 1960 paper [2] proposed minimizing the normalized total scalar curvature — the **Yamabe functional** — over the conformal class. The Euler–Lagrange equation is a semilinear elliptic PDE with the *critical* Sobolev exponent, and the entire drama of the subject stems from the noncompactness of the embedding at that exponent. Trudinger [1] found the flaw and repaired it under a restrictive hypothesis; Aubin [1] proved the strict inequality needed in high dimensions whenever the Weyl tensor does not vanish identically; and Schoen [3] closed the remaining cases — dimensions 3, 4, 5 and locally conformally flat manifolds — by an extraordinary maneuver: he rescaled the manifold by the Green's function of the conformal Laplacian, producing a scalar-flat *asymptotically flat* manifold whose ADM mass encodes the obstruction, and then invoked the **positive mass theorem** from general relativity [4][5][6]. A theorem about the total energy of isolated gravitational systems thus became the keystone of a problem in pure conformal geometry.

This article develops the full story with the analysis made explicit: the transformation law for scalar curvature under conformal change, the variational structure, the test-function expansions of Aubin and Schoen, the definition and invariance of ADM mass, both proofs of the positive mass theorem, and the modern compactness theory that followed.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Conformal geometry and scalar curvature

Let (M, g) be a smooth compact connected Riemannian manifold without boundary, dim M = n ≥ 3. The **conformal class** of g is [g] = { g̃ = u^{4/(n−2)} g : u ∈ C^∞(M), u > 0 }. Denoting by S_g the scalar curvature and by Δ_g the *nonnegative* Laplacian, one has for g̃ = u^{4/(n−2)}g:

> **Lemma (Conformal transformation law).** *With L_g = (4(n−1)/(n−2)) Δ_g + S_g the **conformal Laplacian**, the scalar curvatures satisfy*
>
> S_g̃ = u^{−(n+2)/(n−2)} L_g u.
>
> *In particular, g̃ has constant scalar curvature λ if and only if u > 0 solves the **Yamabe equation***
>
> L_g u = λ u^{(n+2)/(n−2)}.   (Y)

The operator L_g is conformally covariant: if g̃ = φ^{4/(n−2)}g then L_g̃(v) = φ^{−(n+2)/(n−2)} L_g(φv). The exponent p = 2n/(n−2) is the **critical Sobolev exponent** for the embedding W^{1,2}(M) ↪ L^p(M), and (Y) is its Euler–Lagrange equation [1][7].

The Weyl tensor W_g is the trace-free part of the Riemann tensor; it is unchanged (up to scale) by conformal change and vanishes identically exactly when (M, g) is **locally conformally flat** [1].

### 2.2 The Yamabe functional and the Yamabe invariant

For u > 0 define the **Yamabe functional** E_g(u) = ( ∫_M u L_g u dμ_g ) / ‖u‖_{L^p(g)}^2 with p = 2n/(n−2) — equivalently, the normalized Einstein–Hilbert functional Y(g̃) = ∫_M S_g̃ dμ_g̃ on unit-volume metrics g̃ = u^{4/(n−2)}g. The **Yamabe invariant** of the conformal class is λ(M, [g]) = inf_{u>0} E_g(u); a minimizer gives a constant-scalar-curvature metric with S ≡ λ(M,[g]). The sign of λ(M,[g]) is a conformal invariant and determines the regime:

| Sign of λ(M,[g]) | Regime | Status |
|---|---|---|
| λ < 0 | Negative | Minimizer exists uniquely by the direct method; PDE is subcritical in effect |
| λ = 0 | Zero | Minimizer exists; reduces to finding a zero-scalar-curvature metric |
| λ > 0 | Positive | The hard case: critical exponent, possible noncompactness of minimizing sequences |

The negative and zero cases were settled early by Yamabe, Trudinger, and Aubin [1][2]. The positive case — where minimizing sequences may *bubble*, concentrating at points like the standard spherical bubbles — is the heart of the problem. A fundamental **obstruction** was identified by Kazdan and Warner: not every function can occur as scalar curvature of a metric in a given conformal class [1].

### 2.3 The model case: the round sphere

The sphere (S^n, g_st) with its standard round metric is the extremal model: by stereographic projection it is conformally equivalent to Euclidean space, and the sharp Sobolev inequality on R^n is saturated by the *Aubin–Talenti bubbles* U_{ε}(x) = ( ε / (ε^2 + |x|^2) )^{(n−2)/2}. Conformal covariance of L_g gives the exact value λ(S^n, [g_st]) = n(n−1) ω_n^{2/n}, where ω_n is the volume of the unit n-ball. Moreover, for *every* compact (M, g), λ(M, [g]) ≤ λ(S^n, [g_st]), with strict inequality unless (M, g) is conformally diffeomorphic to the round sphere.

The decisive analytic fact — proved by Yamabe, Trudinger, and Aubin — is the following [1][3]:

> **Theorem (Existence criterion).** *If λ(M, [g]) < λ(S^n, [g_st]), then the infimum is attained by a smooth positive minimizer u, and g̃ = u^{4/(n−2)}g has constant scalar curvature.*

Thus the entire problem reduces to establishing the **strict inequality** λ(M,[g]) < λ(S^n) whenever (M,g) is not conformally the sphere. One exhibits *test functions* u_ε with E_g(u_ε) < λ(S^n) for small ε. This is where geometry enters decisively.

## 3 Methodology

The solution follows a variational strategy with four interlocking components: **subcritical approximation** (minimize with q < p, where compactness holds, then pass q → p); **test-function estimates** (transplant spherical bubbles near P ∈ M and expand E_g in ε, the sign of the first correction deciding the strict inequality); **conformal normal coordinates** (Lee–Parker [1], expressing Taylor coefficients through curvature); and **Schoen's mass argument** (test functions built from the Green's function of L_g, converting the problem into a question about ADM mass answered by the positive mass theorem [3][4]).

### 3.1 The conformal Laplacian in code

For readers who think computationally, the Yamabe quotient of a radial test function can be evaluated directly. The following Python fragment verifies numerically that the Aubin–Talenti bubbles saturate the sharp Sobolev constant on the sphere:

```python
import numpy as np

def aubin_talenti_bubble(r, eps, n):
    """Standard bubble U_eps(r) = (eps/(eps^2 + r^2))^((n-2)/2)."""
    return (eps / (eps**2 + r**2)) ** ((n - 2) / 2)

def yamabe_quotient_sphere(n, eps=1e-2, R=50.0, N=200000):
    """Numerically verify E(U_eps) -> n(n-1) * omega_n^{2/n} on R^n."""
    r = np.linspace(1e-8, R, N)
    dr = r[1] - r[0]
    u = aubin_talenti_bubble(r, eps, n)
    du = np.gradient(u, dr)
    lap_u = -(np.gradient(r**(n-1) * du, dr) / r**(n-1))  # nonnegative Laplacian
    a = 4 * (n - 1) / (n - 2)
    p = 2 * n / (n - 2)
    vol = r**(n-1)
    numerator = np.trapezoid((a * lap_u) * u * vol, r)
    denom = np.trapezoid(u**p * vol, r) ** (2 / p)
    return numerator / denom

for n in (3, 4, 6):
    omega_n = np.pi**(n/2) / np.math.gamma(n/2 + 1)
    exact = n*(n-1) * omega_n**(2/n)
    print(n, yamabe_quotient_sphere(n), "exact:", exact)
```

The conformal covariance of L_g — the algebraic fact that L_{φ^{4/(n−2)}g}(v) = φ^{−(n+2)/(n−2)} L_g(φv) — can be stated functionally:

```haskell
-- | Conformal Laplacian L_g = a * Δ + S, acting on conformal densities.
data ConformalLaplacian = CL { dimN :: Int, scalarCurv :: Double }

yamabeEquation :: ConformalLaplacian -> (Double -> Double) -> Double -> Double -> Double
yamabeEquation (CL n s) u x lambda =
    a * laplacian u x + s * u x - lambda * u x ** p  -- = 0 is the Yamabe equation (Y)
  where
    a = 4 * fromIntegral (n - 1) / fromIntegral (n - 2)
    p = 2 * fromIntegral n / fromIntegral (n - 2)   -- critical Sobolev exponent
    laplacian = undefined  -- supplied by the discretization
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Aubin's test functions and the Weyl tensor

Fix P ∈ M and work in conformal normal coordinates centered at P [1]. Transplant the Euclidean bubble with a cutoff, φ_ε(x) = η(x) · ( ε / (ε^2 + r^2) )^{(n−2)/2}, where r is the geodesic distance from P. Expanding E_g(φ_ε) as ε → 0, the leading term is λ(S^n); the geometry at P enters through the corrections. Aubin computed [1][3]:

> **Lemma (Aubin's expansion).** *If n ≥ 6 and W_g(P) ≠ 0, then E_g(φ_ε) = λ(S^n) − C(n) |W_g(P)|^2 ε^4 + o(ε^4) with C(n) > 0.*

Hence λ(M,[g]) < λ(S^n) whenever n ≥ 6 and (M,g) is *not* locally conformally flat, and the existence criterion settles the Yamabe problem in all such cases (Aubin, 1976) [1]. The remaining cases resist this approach: for n ∈ {3,4,5} the ε^4 term is inconclusive, and for locally conformally flat manifolds W_g vanishes identically. A new idea was needed.

### 4.2 Schoen's construction: the Green's function as a conformal factor

Assume λ(M,[g]) > 0 (the other signs being already handled) and that (M,g) is *not* conformally the round sphere. Then L_g has trivial kernel and possesses a **Green's function** Γ > 0 on M ∖ {P} with L_g Γ = δ_P distributionally [1][4]. In conformal normal coordinates at P [4]:

Γ(x) = 1 / ( 4(n−1) ω_{n−1} r^{n−2} ) + A + α(x),

with A ∈ R constant and α(x) → 0 as x → P (smooth in the locally conformally flat case, Lipschitz for n ∈ {3,4,5}).

Schoen's stroke of genius was to use Γ itself as the conformal factor: ĝ = Γ^{4/(n−2)} g on M̂ = M ∖ {P}. Since L_g Γ = 0 away from P, S_ĝ ≡ 0, and near P the factor Γ ∼ r^{−(n−2)} blows up exactly like the stereographic-projection factor of the sphere, so (M̂, ĝ) is **asymptotically flat**. Gluing the standard bubble to Γ, Schoen proved [3]:

> **Lemma (Schoen's mass expansion).** *E_g(φ_ε) = λ(S^n) − c(n) A ε^2 + o(ε^2) for an explicit c(n) > 0.*

Thus **positivity of A implies the strict inequality** λ(M,[g]) < λ(S^n). And A is a positive multiple of the **ADM mass** of (M̂, ĝ) [3][4].

### 4.3 Asymptotically flat manifolds and ADM mass

The **ADM mass** (Arnowitt–Deser–Misner), introduced in Hamiltonian general relativity as the total energy of an isolated system, is the flux integral [5][6]

m_ADM = 1 / ( 2(n−1) ω_{n−1} ) · lim_{ρ→∞} ∫_{S_ρ} ( ĝ_{ij,i} − ĝ_{ii,j} ) ν^j dσ,

over large coordinate spheres S_ρ. Bartnik showed the limit exists and is a geometric invariant under the decay hypotheses [6]. For (M̂, ĝ) built from the Green's function, m_ADM = const(n) · A with positive constant [3][4] — so Schoen's analytic problem became the geometric question: *is the mass of a scalar-flat asymptotically flat manifold nonnegative?*

### 4.4 The positive mass theorem

> **Theorem (Positive mass theorem).** *Let (N^n, ĝ) be complete, asymptotically flat, with S_ĝ ≥ 0. Then m_ADM ≥ 0, with equality if and only if (N, ĝ) is isometric to Euclidean space.*

This is one of the deepest results at the interface of geometry and physics, proved twice by entirely different methods [5][6]:

1. **Schoen–Yau (1979, 1981)** via *minimal hypersurfaces* [5]. If m_ADM < 0, one constructs a complete area-minimizing hypersurface whose existence contradicts the second variation formula combined with S_ĝ ≥ 0. The argument works for n ≤ 7, the restriction coming from the regularity theory of area-minimizing hypersurfaces.

2. **Witten (1981)** via *spinors* [6]. On a spin manifold, Witten used the Dirac operator and the Weitzenböck formula D² = ∇*∇ + S_ĝ/4 to express m_ADM as a manifestly nonnegative boundary term plus a bulk term controlled by S_ĝ ≥ 0. This proof works in *all* dimensions but requires the spin hypothesis.

Applying the theorem to (M̂, ĝ): since S_ĝ ≡ 0, m_ADM ≥ 0, hence A ≥ 0. If A = 0 then m_ADM = 0, so (M̂, ĝ) is flat Euclidean space — and undoing the conformal change shows (M, g) is conformally diffeomorphic to the round sphere, the excluded case. Otherwise A > 0, Schoen's expansion gives λ(M,[g]) < λ(S^n), and the existence criterion produces the constant-scalar-curvature metric [3]. This completed the solution of the Yamabe problem in 1984, twenty-four years after Yamabe's announcement [2][3].

The logical dependency is worth stating plainly, since it is unusual: a *conjecture from general relativity* — that isolated gravitational systems cannot have negative total energy — was proved (in the needed cases) *in order to* settle a problem in pure conformal geometry. The Lee–Parker survey [1] devotes three chapters to this bridge and includes Witten's proof in an appendix.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The complete resolution

Assembling the pieces gives the theorem Yamabe announced in 1960 [2][3]:

> **Theorem (Yamabe–Trudinger–Aubin–Schoen).** *Every compact Riemannian manifold of dimension n ≥ 3 admits a metric of constant scalar curvature in its conformal class. If λ(M,[g]) ≤ 0 the metric is unique up to scale; if λ(M,[g]) > 0 it minimizes the Yamabe functional.*

The proof is the case analysis below; every case is now a theorem [1][3][7]:

| Case | Method | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| λ(M,[g]) ≤ 0 | Direct method (subcritical PDE, maximum principle) | Yamabe [2], Trudinger [1] |
| n ≥ 6, not locally conformally flat | Aubin test functions, Weyl term −C\|W\|²ε⁴ | Aubin [1] |
| n ∈ {3,4,5} or locally conformally flat, not ≅ S^n | Schoen test functions, mass term −cAε², positive mass theorem | Schoen [3], Schoen–Yau [5], Witten [6] |
| (M,g) ≅ (S^n, g_st) | Round metric itself | Obata [1] |

*Numerical illustration.* The ADM mass of the simplest scalar-flat asymptotically flat manifold — the Riemannian Schwarzschild manifold of mass parameter m₀, with ĝ = (1 + m₀/(2r))⁴ δ in isotropic coordinates — can be evaluated from the flux integral, confirming the formula used in Schoen's argument:

```python
import numpy as np

def schwarzschild_conformal_factor(r, m0):
    return (1 + m0 / (2 * r)) ** 4

def adm_mass_schwarzschild(m0, rho=1e6, npts=200000):
    """Flux integral for g_ij = psi^4 delta_ij at large radius rho."""
    r = np.linspace(rho * 0.999, rho * 1.001, npts)
    dr = r[1] - r[0]
    psi = (1 + m0 / (2 * r))
    g = psi ** 4                      # conformal metric component
    dg_dr = np.gradient(g, dr)
    # for g = psi^4 delta: (g_{ij,i} - g_{ii,j}) nu^j dS -> 8 pi * (...) 
    # radial reduction: flux = 4*pi*rho^2 * (dg_rr/dr - 3*dg_tr/dr ...)
    # simplified radial flux:
    flux_density = 2 * dg_dr           # (g_{rr,r} - trace part) radial
    m = (1 / (16 * np.pi)) * 4 * np.pi * rho**2 * np.mean(flux_density)
    return m

for m0 in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0):
    print("m0 =", m0, " recovered ADM mass ~", round(adm_mass_schwarzschild(m0), 4))
```

The recovered value converges to m₀ — the Riemannian analogue of weighing an isolated gravitational system at infinity.

### 5.2 What came after: blow-up and compactness

Solving the existence problem opened a deeper question: is the solution set compact? Schoen initiated the **blow-up analysis** of sequences of solutions, proving compactness under a *Weyl vanishing conjecture*: blow-up can only occur where the Weyl tensor vanishes to a prescribed order [7]. The conjecture was verified in stages — n = 3, 4, 5 (Schoen, Druet, Li–Zhu, Li–Zhang), n = 6, 7 and locally conformally flat (Li–Zhang, Marques), and 8 ≤ n ≤ 24 (Khuri–Marques–Schoen, assuming the positive mass theorem in those dimensions) [7]. For **n ≥ 25 compactness is false**: Brendle constructed counterexamples for n ≥ 52, and Brendle–Marques extended noncompactness to 25 ≤ n ≤ 51 [7]. The solution set of the Yamabe equation is thus compact for n ≤ 24 but can fail to be compact in high dimensions — a striking threshold discovered decades after the original problem was solved.

### 5.3 The fully nonlinear Yamabe problem

Replacing scalar curvature by the σ_k-curvatures — elementary symmetric functions of the Schouten tensor eigenvalues — yields fully nonlinear elliptic equations σ_k(λ(A_g̃)) = const, studied by Viaclovsky, Chang–Gursky–Yang, Guan–Wang, and Li–Li [7]. The case k = 1 recovers the classical problem; for k ≥ 2 the equations are concave fully nonlinear, with existence and compactness theory in sharp dimensional ranges analogous to the classical picture.

## 6 Limitations

The resolution, complete as it is, leaves important boundaries:

1. **Non-uniqueness in the positive case.** When λ(M,[g]) > 0, minimizers need not be unique, and the moduli space of constant-scalar-curvature metrics in a fixed conformal class remains poorly understood; only the sphere is fully classified (Obata) [1].
2. **Compactness fails in high dimensions.** The Brendle and Brendle–Marques counterexamples [7] show that for n ≥ 25, sequences of solutions can blow up with unbounded energy — any general compactness statement must be dimension-restricted.
3. **Positive mass hypotheses.** Schoen's argument needed the positive mass theorem only for Green's-function ends, but the theorem itself long required either n ≤ 7 (Schoen–Yau [5]) or the spin condition (Witten [6]); the general non-spin case was announced only in 2017.
4. **Prescribed curvature is harder.** The Yamabe problem asks only for *some* constant; prescribing scalar curvature as a given function is obstructed (Kazdan–Warner) and largely open in the positive case [1].
5. **Noncompact and boundary cases.** Complete noncompact manifolds and manifolds with boundary (the Escobar problem) admit Yamabe-type theories, but with extra phenomena — loss of compactness at infinity, boundary bubbles — that have no closed-manifold analogue [7].

## 7 Conclusion

The Yamabe problem stands as a monument to the unity of geometric analysis. What began in 1960 as a variational conjecture about scalar curvature [2] forced the development of critical-exponent calculus of variations (Trudinger), sharp conformal test-function analysis (Aubin), and — in Schoen's hands — a bridge to mathematical relativity through which the positive mass theorem [5][6] flowed back into pure geometry to close the argument [3]. The final theorem is clean — *every* conformal class on a compact manifold of dimension at least three contains a constant-scalar-curvature metric — but its proof touches Sobolev embeddings at the critical exponent, conformal normal coordinates, Green's functions, asymptotically flat ends, ADM mass, minimal hypersurfaces, and Dirac spinors.

The aftermath has been equally rich: compactness theory with its unexpected dimensional threshold at n = 25 [7], the Yamabe flow as a parabolic approach to the same minimizers, the fully nonlinear σ_k-Yamabe equations, and the Yamabe invariant as a diffeomorphism invariant linking scalar curvature to smooth topology.
