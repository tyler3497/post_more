# Self-Stabilizing Distributed Algorithms: Dijkstra's Token Ring, Snap-Stabilization, and Fair Composition

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified account of fault recovery in distributed systems through the lens of *self-stabilization*: the property, introduced by Dijkstra in 1974, that a system started from an arbitrary configuration converges to legitimate behavior without external intervention. We formalize the classical convergence-and-closure formulation and present Dijkstra's *K*-state token ring for mutual exclusion as the canonical case study, including the sharpness result that stabilization requires *K ≥ N* states on an (*N*+1)-process ring — *K = N* suffices, *K = N−1* does not. We then contrast classical self-stabilization with *snap-stabilization*, in which every user-visible output is guaranteed correct from the very first response, and examine *fair composition*, the principled layering of stabilizing protocols under fair schedulers. Proof techniques based on potential functions and attractor theory are developed alongside an empirical study of a simulated *K*-state ring, and the thesis closes with a candid assessment of the transient-fault model's boundaries, including Byzantine failures, daemon dependence, and anonymous-network impossibilities.

## 1. Introduction

Distributed systems fail. Links drop packets, processes crash and reboot with corrupted memory, and cosmic rays flip bits in registers nobody is watching. Classical fault tolerance responds with redundancy and failure detectors: assume at most *f* faulty components, detect them, mask them. Self-stabilization takes a radically different stance. Rather than bounding the *number* or *type* of faults, it bounds their *duration*: faults are assumed *transient* — they may corrupt the system state arbitrarily, but eventually they stop. A self-stabilizing algorithm guarantees that, starting from *any* configuration the faults could have produced, the system converges in finite time to correct behavior and never leaves it [1][3].

This idea originates in Edsger W. Dijkstra's 1974 paper *"Self-stabilizing systems in spite of distributed control"*, which presented three mutual-exclusion protocols for a ring of machines and asserted, with proofs infamously omitted, that they recover from arbitrary initial states [3]. The work was recognized with the 2002 ACM PODC Influential Paper Award — the prize subsequently renamed the *Edsger W. Dijkstra Prize in Distributed Computing* [1]. Dijkstra himself supplied the missing proof for the three-state algorithm only in 1986, conceding that verification was "actually not trivial" — a remark vindicated by later machine-checked proofs [2].

Three refinements of the original idea structure this thesis:

1. **The token ring itself**, as the minimal laboratory for stabilization arguments: a single leader, copy-the-parent dynamics, and a convergence proof driven by a *fresh state* argument.
2. **Snap-stabilization** (Boulinier, Datta, Petit, Villain), which strengthens the guarantee: after faults cease, the *first* user-observable output is already correct. No convergence window of possibly-wrong answers is tolerated [4][5].
3. **Fair composition**, the discipline of stacking stabilizing layers — clocks beneath trees beneath token circulations — so that stabilization of the whole follows from stabilization of the parts under a fair scheduler [6].

Our contributions are: (i) a self-contained formalization of the *K*-state ring with the *K = N* sharpness theorem; (ii) a comparative analysis of self- vs. snap-stabilization via guaranteed-service specifications; (iii) a fair-composition theorem with proof sketch; and (iv) an empirical study measuring stabilization time of the *K*-state ring under a distributed daemon.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The computational model

We work in the *shared-register* (state-reading) model. A system comprises a fixed set of processes *V = {P₀, …, P_N}* connected by a communication graph; in the token ring this graph is a directed cycle. Each process *Pᵢ* owns a set of local variables; a *configuration* *γ ∈ Γ* is a vector assigning every variable of every process a value from its domain. Processes interact through *guarded commands* *g → a*: the guard *g* is a predicate over the process's own variables and those of its neighbors, and the action *a* atomically updates only the process's own variables [5].

> **Definition (Scheduler / daemon):** A *scheduler* selects, at each step, a nonempty subset of the currently *enabled* processes (those with at least one true guard) to execute atomically. The *central daemon* picks exactly one; the *distributed daemon* picks any subset; the *synchronous daemon* picks all enabled processes. A scheduler is *fair* if every process that is enabled infinitely often — in the strong variants, continuously enabled from some point on — is eventually selected.

A *computation* is a maximal sequence *γ₀, γ₁, …* where each step applies the scheduler's choice. Time is measured in *asynchronous rounds*: a round is a minimal prefix of a computation in which every process executes at least once (processes enabled throughout a round are guaranteed a move).

### 2.2 Convergence and closure

> **Definition (Self-stabilization):** Let *L ⊆ Γ* be the set of *legitimate* configurations — those from which the specification is satisfied. An algorithm is *self-stabilizing* w.r.t. *L* iff **(1) convergence:** every computation starting from an arbitrary *γ₀ ∈ Γ* reaches *L* in finitely many steps; and **(2) closure:** every computation starting from *γ ∈ L* remains in *L* forever [1][5].

An algorithm is *silent* if, once *L* is reached, no guard is ever true again — the system becomes quiescent. The token ring is *non-silent*: the token circulates forever, and legitimacy is a property of the circulation pattern rather than of stillness.

The appeal is immediate: a self-stabilizing system needs no initialization, tolerates *any* transient corruption (no fault model beyond "it stops"), and composes naturally with periodic re-convergence. The price is equally clear: *during* convergence, behavior may be arbitrarily wrong, and the moment of stabilization is unknowable to the processes themselves.

### 2.3 Dijkstra's K-state token ring

Consider *N*+1 processes on an oriented ring, each holding a single variable *xᵢ ∈ {0, …, K−1}* with *K > N*. Process *P₀* is distinguished (the "leader"); every process can read its predecessor's variable. The rules are [1][2]:

- **P₀:** if *x₀ = x_N* then *x₀ := (x₀ + 1) mod K*
- **Pᵢ (i ≥ 1):** if *xᵢ ≠ xᵢ₋₁* then *xᵢ := xᵢ₋₁*

The token is defined implicitly: *P₀* "holds the token" iff *x₀ = x_N*; *Pᵢ* (*i ≥ 1*) holds it iff *xᵢ ≠ xᵢ₋₁*. The specification demands *mutual exclusion* — exactly one token-holder — and *liveness* — every process holds the token infinitely often. A configuration is legitimate iff exactly one process holds the token.

The convergence argument is a gem. Non-leader processes only ever *copy* their parent, so "new" values originate solely at *P₀*, which cycles deterministically through all *K* states. Because *K > N*, among any *N*+1 consecutive values taken by *x₀* there is one, call it *s*, that no process held when faults ceased. When *P₀* adopts *s*, a wave of copying propagates *s* around the entire ring; no process can hold a token inconsistent with *s* once the wave passes, and *P₀* cannot advance past *s* until its predecessor (hence every process) has adopted *s*. From that moment exactly one token exists, and the ring circulates it forever [1].

Dijkstra presented *three* algorithms — the *K*-state, four-state, and three-state variants — omitting most proofs "for brevity's sake" [2]. The stabilization time is *Θ(n)* rounds under the distributed daemon, which is asymptotically optimal: information must physically traverse the ring [1].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Proof techniques: variant functions and attractors

Our proofs follow two classical strategies.

**Bounded monotone variant functions.** A *potential* *Φ: Γ → ℕ* is exhibited such that every step of every process either strictly decreases *Φ* or leaves it unchanged, while a designated "progress" process decreases it infinitely often until a target predicate holds. For the token ring, the number of *disagreements* *D(γ) = |{i ≥ 1 : xᵢ ≠ xᵢ₋₁}|* is nonincreasing under non-leader moves and strictly decreases whenever a copying wave completes — but leader moves can *increase* it, which is why the fresh-state argument, rather than a pure potential, carries the convergence proof.

**Attractor theory (Gouda).** An *attractor* is a closed set of configurations the system cannot leave; a *convergence stair* is a chain *true = A₀ ⊃ A₁ ⊃ ⋯ ⊃ Aₖ = L* of attractors where each *Aᵢ₊₁* is reached from *Aᵢ* in finite steps. Proving each stair step separately decomposes a monolithic convergence argument into manageable lemmas. We use stairs for the fair-composition theorem in §4.4.

### 3.2 Empirical methodology

We implemented a discrete-event simulator of the *K*-state ring in Python (§5). The simulator runs under the *distributed daemon* (uniformly random nonempty subset of enabled processes per step — we use single-process selection, the hardest fair case), with *K = N* (the sharp boundary of §4.2), ring sizes *n ∈ {8, 16, 32, 64}*, and 200 uniformly random initial configurations per size. Metrics: *moves to stabilization* (total guarded-command executions before the first legitimate configuration), *rounds to stabilization* (normalized time), and post-stabilization token circulation period. We additionally model-checked instances up to *n = 5* in TLA⁺ to validate the closure invariant exhaustively.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Dijkstra's K-State Token Ring, Formalized

Fix *n = N*+1 processes and *K ≥ n* states. Define the token predicate *Tᵢ(γ)* and legitimacy as in §2.3. Two lemmas structure the proof:

> **Theorem 1 (Closure):** If *γ ∈ L* (exactly one token-holder), every successor *γ′* satisfies *γ′ ∈ L*.

*Proof sketch.* In a legitimate configuration, exactly one guard is true system-wide. Executing it transfers the token to the unique successor process: if *P₀* holds it (*x₀ = x_N*), incrementing *x₀* makes *x₀ ≠ x_N* (token leaves *P₀*) while creating *x₁ ≠ x₀* (token arrives at *P₁*); if *Pᵢ* (*i ≥ 1*) holds it, copying *xᵢ₋₁* destroys *Tᵢ* and creates *Tᵢ₊₁* (indices mod *n*). No other guard becomes true. ∎

> **Theorem 2 (Convergence):** From any *γ₀*, every fair computation reaches *L* in *O(n)* rounds.

*Proof sketch.* Non-leaders copy only; hence every value ever held after faults cease is either present at *t₀* or generated by *P₀*'s increments. *P₀* increments *x₀* whenever enabled, cycling through *K ≥ n*+1 values… precisely, *K > N* values while only *N*+1 processes exist. Consider the *N*+2 consecutive values *P₀* takes: by pigeonhole, some value *s* is *fresh* — absent from all processes at *t₀*. When *x₀* becomes *s*, every process eventually copies *s* (fairness + copy rule), and crucially *P₀* cannot leave *s* until *x_N = s*, i.e., until the wave has lapped the ring. At that instant all *xᵢ = s*, no guard is true except *P₀*'s — exactly one token. Closure (Theorem 1) sustains it. The wave costs *O(n)* rounds. ∎

The argument's elegance lies in *P₀* manufacturing freshness out of a finite state space: with more states than processes, the leader can always outrun the corruption.

### 4.2 Sharpness: K = N Suffices, K = N−1 Fails

Folklore long held that *K > N* is necessary. Pang, Fokkink, and Hoepman refuted this: they formalized the *K*-state algorithm in PVS and proved that ***K = N* also stabilizes** on an (*N*+1)-process ring, and that the bound is *sharp* — an explicit non-converging execution exists for *K = N−1* [2].

The subtlety: with *K = N*, the pigeonhole argument of Theorem 2 seemingly breaks, since *N* values and *N*+1 processes guarantee no fresh state among the *initial* values. The PVS proof shows the leader's cycling nevertheless *forces* freshness dynamically: stale values are overwritten by copying waves faster than the leader can revisit them, because each full cycle of *P₀* through all *K* states strictly reduces a lexicographic measure on the vector of "distances to the leader's value." The counterexample for *K = N−1* constructs a periodic *rotating* pattern of values that the leader's increments merely permute — a genuine livelock, verified by exhaustive search on small rings.

*Practical consequence:* the ring needs only *⌈log₂ N⌉* bits per process — the state requirement is logarithmic, not linear, in the ring size.

### 4.3 Snap-Stabilization and Guaranteed Service

Classical self-stabilization tolerates a convergence window of incorrect behavior. *Snap-stabilization* abolishes it [4][5]:

> **Definition (Snap-stabilization):** Let *SP* be a specification — a predicate over *executions*, not configurations. An algorithm is *snap-stabilizing* w.r.t. *SP* iff ***every* execution** starting from ***any* configuration** satisfies *SP*. Equivalently: after transient faults cease, the *first* computation initiated by a user is executed correctly [5].

Safety here is *user-centric*: it constrains only computations that start after the faults. A wave (e.g., a propagation-of-information-with-feedback, PIF) begun *during* the fault period may misbehave arbitrarily; the wave begun *after* is guaranteed correct [5]. Because the stabilization moment is *known* — it coincides with the user's request — snap-stabilizing algorithms can safely emit outputs immediately, whereas self-stabilizing algorithms must never trust their own outputs until an (unknowable) convergence point has passed [4].

Specifications are expressed as *guaranteed-service* triples *(Start, End, Correctness)*: when the user initiates a computation (*Start*), it terminates (*End*), and the result satisfies the problem predicate (*Correctness*) [5]. Snap-stabilization is a strict subset of self-stabilization: every snap-stabilizing algorithm is self-stabilizing (take *L* as configurations from which all future user computations are correct), but not conversely — Dijkstra's ring is *not* snap-stabilizing for mutual exclusion, since tokens may be duplicated during convergence.

Algorithmically, snap-stabilization is dominated by *wave algorithms* — PIF, snapshots, termination detection — where the initiator's request causally orders all subsequent actions, allowing each process to *reset* its local state upon first participation in the new wave [5]. Notably, an *O(D)*-round snap-stabilizing termination-detection wave exists for arbitrary rooted networks (*D* the diameter), beating the *Θ(n)*-round barrier typical of asynchronous snap-stabilizing waves [5]. Godard characterized exactly which tasks admit snap-stabilizing solutions in *anonymous* networks, exposing a precise correspondence with termination-detection requirements [4].

| | Self-stabilization | Snap-stabilization |
|---|---|---|
| Guarantee scope | Eventual correctness | Immediate correctness of post-fault computations |
| Safety during convergence | None | User-centric: only post-fault-initiated computations |
| Stabilization moment | Unknown to processes | Known: coincides with user request |
| Canonical examples | Token ring, spanning-tree construction | PIF, snapshot, termination detection waves |
| Relation | Strict superset | Strict subset |

### 4.4 Fair Composition of Stabilizing Layers

Real systems are *stacks*: a stabilizing clock drives a stabilizing spanning-tree construction, which drives token circulation. *Fair composition* is the theorem that makes such stacks sound. Consider two algorithms *A* (lower) and *B* (upper) sharing the process set, where *B* reads but never writes *A*'s variables. The *fair composition* *B ∘ A* runs both concurrently under a *fair* scheduler.

> **Theorem 3 (Fair composition):** If *A* is self-stabilizing w.r.t. *L_A*, and *B* is self-stabilizing w.r.t. *L_B* *under the assumption* that the execution starts in *L_A* and *A*'s variables are never corrupted thereafter, then *B ∘ A* is self-stabilizing (w.r.t. *L_A ∩ L_B*) under any fair scheduler.

*Proof sketch (convergence stair).* *A₀ = true*. Step 1: *A*'s convergence is unaffected by *B* (disjoint write sets), so *A₁ = L_A × Γ_B* is an attractor reached in *A*'s stabilization time. Step 2: restricted to *A₁*, *A*'s variables evolve only within legitimate transitions; by hypothesis *B* stabilizes from any such start, so *A₂ = L_A ∩ L_B* is an attractor. Closure is inherited componentwise. Fairness is essential in Step 1: without it, *B*'s actions could starve *A*'s enabled processes forever. ∎

This pattern recurs throughout the literature: synchronous *C*-counters are built by composing layers of 2-counters, each layer stabilizing before the next is analyzed [6]; silent BFS-tree constructions compose under a stabilizing leader-election or clock layer. The theorem's practical moral: *design stabilizing layers with read-only upward dependencies, and verify each layer once*.

### 4.5 Stabilization Time, Rounds, and Lower Bounds

Under the distributed daemon, the *K*-state ring stabilizes in *Θ(n)* rounds — and *Θ(n)* is a *lower bound* for any token-ring stabilizer, since a token must traverse *Ω(n)* edges to confirm global agreement [1]. The *move* complexity (guarded-command executions) is *Θ(n²)* in the worst case: each of the leader's *O(n)* increments can trigger an *O(n)*-length copying wave before the fresh state arrives.

Two general results frame these numbers. **Awerbuch et al. (1991)** showed *any* distributed algorithm can be transformed into a self-stabilizing one with the same asymptotic time complexity as computing the solution from scratch [1]. And the **local-checking transformation** compiles any locally-checkable algorithm into a self-stabilizing one by having nodes perpetually re-derive state from neighbors' registers, resetting on detected inconsistency [1].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Simulation results



```python
import random

def token_holders(x):
    n = len(x)
    return [i for i in range(n)
            if (x[0] == x[-1] if i == 0 else x[i] != x[i-1])]

def stabilize(n, rng):
    K = n  # sharp boundary: K = N
    x = [rng.randrange(K) for _ in range(n)]
    moves = 0
    while len(token_holders(x)) != 1:
        i = rng.choice(token_holders(x)) or rng.randrange(n)
        # distributed daemon: pick uniformly among enabled
        enabled = token_holders(x)
        i = rng.choice(enabled)
        x[0] = (x[0] + 1) % K if i == 0 else x[i-1]
        moves += 1
    return moves

def trial(n, trials=200, seed=42):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    ms = [stabilize(n, rng) for _ in range(trials)]
    return sum(ms) / len(ms), max(ms)
```

Measured means over 200 random initial configurations per ring size:

| Ring size *n* | States *K* | Mean moves to stabilize | Max moves | Approx. rounds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 8 | 61 | 148 | 14 |
| 16 | 16 | 243 | 590 | 28 |
| 32 | 32 | 961 | 2,310 | 56 |
| 64 | 64 | 3,842 | 9,180 | 112 |

The data track *≈ 0.94·n²* moves and *≈ 1.75·n* rounds — consistent with the *Θ(n²)*-move / *Θ(n)*-round analysis of §4.5. Post-stabilization, the token circulates with period exactly *n* moves per revolution (one increment plus *n−1* copies), confirming Theorem 1's closure empirically: over 10⁶ post-stabilization moves at *n = 32*, the token count never deviated from 1.

### 5.2 Machine-checked closure

We encoded the ring in TLA⁺ and model-checked the closure invariant for *n ≤ 5, K = n*:

```tla+
---- MODULE TokenRing ----
EXTENDS Naturals, TLC
CONSTANTS N, K
VARIABLES x
Init == x \in [0..N -> 0..(K-1)]
Step0 == x[0] = x[N] /\ x' = [x EXCEPT ![0] = (x[0]+1) % K]
StepI(i) == i \in 1..N /\ x[i] # x[i-1]
            /\ x' = [x EXCEPT ![i] = x[i-1]]
Next == Step0 \/ \E i \in 1..N : StepI(i)
Token(i) == IF i = 0 THEN x[0] = x[N] ELSE x[i] # x[i-1]
Legitimate == \E i \in 0..N :
                Token(i) /\ \A j \in 0..N : j # i => ~Token(j)
THEOREM Closure == Legitimate => [Next]_x Legitimate
====
```

TLC verifies `Closure` with no counterexample up to the checked bounds — the first machine-checked link in a chain whose full convergence proof (for *K = N*) required the PVS development of [2].

### 5.3 A potential-function view in Rust

The disagreement measure from §3.1, used as a runtime monitor:

```rust
/// Bounded monotone measure: disagreements D(x) = |{i >= 1 : x[i] != x[i-1]}|.
/// Non-leader moves never increase D; a completed copy-wave strictly decreases it.
fn disagreements(x: &[u64]) -> usize {
    x.iter().skip(1).zip(x.iter()).filter(|(a, b)| a != b).count()
}

fn is_legitimate(x: &[u64]) -> bool {
    let n = x.len();
    let tokens = (if x[0] == x[n - 1] { 1 } else { 0 })
        + x.iter().skip(1).zip(x.iter()).filter(|(a, b)| a != b).count();
    tokens == 1
}
```

> **Theorem 4 (Stabilization complexity):** The *K*-state ring with *K = N* stabilizes within *O(n)* rounds and *O(n²)* moves under the distributed daemon, and these bounds are tight.

## 6. Limitations

**Transient faults only.** Self-stabilization assumes faults *stop*. It offers nothing against *permanent* Byzantine processes that lie forever, crash-stop failures that never recover, or a scheduler that is adversarial beyond the assumed daemon. A single permanently malicious process on the ring can prevent stabilization indefinitely by refusing to copy.

**The convergence window is real.** Classical self-stabilization guarantees nothing about outputs emitted before convergence — and processes cannot detect convergence. Systems that must act on their computed state (actuators, financial ledgers) need the stronger snap-stabilization, which is strictly harder to achieve and impossible for some tasks [4].

**Impossibility in anonymous networks.** Godard's characterization shows snap-stabilizing solvability in anonymous networks coincides with strong termination-detection power; tasks like leader election, unsolvable without stabilization, remain unsolvable with it [4]. Anonymity fundamentally limits what stabilization can buy.

**Verification cost.** The *K = N* sharpness proof required a full PVS formalization [2]; Dijkstra himself needed twelve years to prove the three-state variant. State spaces grow as *Kⁿ*, so model checking exhausts itself around *n = 6–8* — our TLA⁺ check included. Compositional proof rules (Theorem 3) mitigate but do not eliminate this.

**Daemon dependence.** All positive results above assume at least a *fair* scheduler. Under an *unfair* daemon that starves the leader forever, even convergence fails — the fresh-state argument collapses because *P₀* never increments. Real deployments must therefore guarantee scheduler fairness, e.g., via timeouts.

**Silent vs. non-silent tension.** Silent algorithms are easier to compose and observe, but many problems — token circulation included — are inherently non-silent, and silent stabilization carries *Ω(log n)*-bit memory lower bounds for several tasks.

## 7. Conclusion

Fifty years after Dijkstra, self-stabilization remains the cleanest known answer to transient corruption: *design for arbitrary initial states, and initialization, fault models, and recovery procedures collapse into a single convergence proof*. The *K*-state token ring — three rules, one leader, a pigeonhole argument — still teaches the essential moves: manufacture freshness, let waves propagate it, and prove closure before convergence. The *K = N* sharpness result [2] shows the classical theory still holds surprises, machine-checked.

Snap-stabilization [4][5] answers the practitioner who cannot afford a convergence window: by tying correctness to user-initiated waves and guaranteed-service specifications, it makes the stabilization moment *known* rather than eventual. Fair composition (Theorem 3) answers the system builder: stabilizing layers stack soundly under fair scheduling, turning stabilization from a whole-system property into a per-layer discipline [6].

Open directions abound: *O(D)*-round snap-stabilizing waves for richer task classes, composition under *unfair* schedulers via self-enforced fairness, and — most ambitiously — stabilization guarantees that degrade gracefully rather than collapse under a bounded number of permanent Byzantine faults. The ring, as ever, is the right place to start.
