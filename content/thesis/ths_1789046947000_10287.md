# Network Calculus: Min-Plus Algebra, Arrival and Service Curves, and Deterministic End-to-End Delay Bounds in Feed-Forward Networks

## Abstract

Network calculus is a deterministic theory of queueing systems that replaces stochastic models with *envelopes*: arrival curves upper-bound cumulative arrivals and service curves lower-bound departures, all expressed through min-plus algebra. This thesis develops the framework from first principles: the dioid structure of min-plus algebra, (min, +) convolution and deconvolution, and the three classical performance bounds — backlog, delay, and departure envelope — with proofs of tightness. We extend the theory to feed-forward networks, where concatenation (pay-bursts-only-once), leftover service curves, and modern flow analyses (Separate Flow Analysis, Pay Multiplexing Only Once, Tandem Matching Analysis) yield end-to-end delay bounds provably superior to node-by-node composition. A reproducible computational study implements token-bucket and rate-latency curves across tandem topologies, confirming the analytical bounds and quantifying the gap between SFA, PMOO, and TMA. We close with the theory's limitations — NP-hardness of tight bounds, packetization effects, and the fluid-model idealization — and directions for verified and stochastic extensions.

## 1 Introduction

Guaranteeing that a packet traverses a network before its deadline is among the oldest problems in communication systems, and among the least forgiving: safety-critical avionics backbones, industrial control buses, and time-sensitive networking standards accept no probabilistic excuses. Classical queueing theory answers with distributions and tail probabilities [2]; network calculus answers with *hard bounds*. Its central maneuver is to stop reasoning about individual packet trajectories and instead reason about cumulative functions — non-decreasing maps \(A(t)\) counting the data that arrived in \([0,t)\) — constrained by two kinds of envelopes. An *arrival curve* \(\alpha\) upper-bounds every increment \(A(t)-A(s)\) by \(\alpha(t-s)\); a *service curve* \(\beta\) lower-bounds the departures \(A^{\ast}(t)\) by the min-plus convolution \(A \otimes \beta\). Once traffic and servers are described this way, delay, backlog, and output burstiness become purely algebraic quantities — horizontal and vertical deviations between curves — and entire networks reduce to a single equivalent service curve via convolution [1].

> **Thesis.** Min-plus algebra is not merely a convenient notation for network calculus; it is the *exact* algebraic structure of deterministic flow analysis. Every deep result of the theory — tight performance bounds, the pay-bursts-only-once principle, leftover service curves, and the relative accuracy of SFA, PMOO, and TMA — is a theorem about the dioid \((\mathcal{F}_0, \wedge, \otimes)\), and the engineering practice of worst-case dimensioning is the practice of computing in it.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the mathematical background: cumulative functions, the min-plus dioid, and the canonical \(\sigma,\rho\) and rate-latency curve families. Section 3 fixes the methodology: definitions of arrival and service curves, the three performance theorems, and the feed-forward network model with its multiplexing hypotheses. Section 4 develops the deep theory — the algebraic toolkit, curve classes, tightness, concatenation and PBOO, leftover service, and the hierarchy of feed-forward analyses. Section 5 presents proofs of the central theorems alongside a computational study that implements the calculus and compares bound tightness across topologies. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From queueing theory to envelopes

Deterministic queueing theory begins where stochastic queueing theory declines to go: instead of an arrival *process* with a law, it posits an arrival *constraint* — every sample path satisfies a bound [1]. This is the (min, +) formulation pioneered by Cruz [4] and systematized by Le Boudec and Thiran [1]. A flow is a left-continuous, non-decreasing cumulative function \(A: \mathbb{R}_+ \to \mathbb{R}_+\) with \(A(0)=0\); a server maps an input flow \(A\) to an output flow \(A^{\ast} \le A\) (lossless). No distribution is assumed, and every statement below holds for *all* admissible trajectories — hence the guarantees are absolute rather than almost-sure.

### 2.2 The min-plus dioid

Let \(\mathcal{F}_0\) be the set of non-decreasing functions \(f: \mathbb{R}_+ \to \mathbb{R}_+ \cup \{+\infty\}\) with \(f(0)=0\). Endowed with pointwise minimum \(\wedge\) as "addition" and the convolution \(\otimes\) as "multiplication", \((\mathcal{F}_0, \wedge, \otimes)\) is a *dioid*: an idempotent semiring in which the neutral element for \(\otimes\) is the "impulse" \(\delta_0\) with \(\delta_0(0)=0\) and \(\delta_0(t)=+\infty\) for \(t>0\), and the zero element is \(\varepsilon(t)\equiv +\infty\) [1]. The order \(f \le g\) is pointwise, and both operations are isotone. The canonical reference for this algebra, and its max-plus dual, is Baccelli *et al.* [5], whose theory of discrete event systems is the broader context in which network calculus lives.

### 2.3 Canonical curve families

Two families dominate practice. The *token-bucket* (affine) arrival curve \(\gamma_{\sigma,\rho}(t) = \sigma + \rho t\) bounds bursty sources by a burst \(\sigma\) and a long-term rate \(\rho\) [2]. The *rate-latency* service curve \(\beta_{R,T}(t) = R[t-T]^+\), with \([x]^+ = \max(x,0)\), models servers that guarantee rate \(R\) after an initial latency \(T\); it is the service-curve image of a constant-rate link with propagation delay, a weighted-fair-queueing scheduler with latency-rate characterization, and many other mechanisms [1], [8].

| Curve family | Symbol | Shape | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Token bucket | \(\gamma_{\sigma,\rho}(t)=\sigma+\rho t\) | Affine | Traffic policing, source burstiness |
| Rate latency | \(\beta_{R,T}(t)=R[t-T]^+\) | Latency then linear | Links, LR servers, FIFO elements |
| Staircase | \(\nu_{L,C}(t)=L\lceil t/C\rceil\) | Step | Packetized periodic sources |
| Peak rate | \(\lambda_P(t)=\min(Pt, \cdot)\) | Clipped linear | Peak-rate enforcement |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Definitions

We adopt the standard definitions, following [1] and the notation consolidated in [8]:

> **Definition 1 (Arrival curve).** A non-decreasing \(\alpha \in \mathcal{F}_0\) is an arrival curve for flow \(A\) if for all \(0 \le s \le t\), \(A(t)-A(s) \le \alpha(t-s)\), equivalently \(A \le A \otimes \alpha\).

> **Definition 2 (Service curve).** A server offers the (simple) service curve \(\beta \in \mathcal{F}_0\) if every admissible input \(A\) yields an output \(A^{\ast}\) satisfying \(A^{\ast} \ge A \otimes \beta\).

The min-plus convolution and deconvolution are the workhorses [8]:

$$(f \otimes g)(t) = \inf_{0 \le s \le t}\{f(t-s)+g(s)\}, \qquad (f \oslash g)(t) = \sup_{u \ge 0}\{f(t+u)-g(u)\}.$$

### 3.2 The three performance theorems

Delay is defined per-bit by the *virtual delay* \(d(t) = \inf\{d \ge 0 : A(t) \le A^{\ast}(t+d)\}\) and backlog by \(b(t) = A(t)-A^{\ast}(t)\). The calculus reduces these to curve geometry [1]:

> **Theorem 1 (Performance bounds).** If \(A\) is \(\alpha\)-constrained and the server offers \(\beta\), then
> 1. backlog: \(b(t) \le v(\alpha,\beta) := \sup_{t \ge 0}\{\alpha(t)-\beta(t)\}\) (vertical deviation),
> 2. delay: \(d(t) \le h(\alpha,\beta) := \inf\{d \ge 0 : \alpha(t) \le \beta(t+d)\ \forall t\}\) (horizontal deviation),
> 3. departure: \(A^{\ast}\) admits the arrival curve \(\alpha^{\ast} = \alpha \oslash \beta\).

These bounds are *tight*: for every pair \((\alpha,\beta)\) there exist admissible trajectories attaining them [1].

### 3.3 Feed-forward network model

We study networks of servers with *feed-forward* topology — the server graph is acyclic, so flows never revisit a server and no circular dependencies arise [4]. Flows traverse sequences (tandems) of servers; at each server, multiple flows multiplex. Two multiplexing hypotheses govern how residual service is computed: *arbitrary multiplexing*, where the flow of interest may receive the worst possible treatment, and *FIFO*, where packet order is preserved and strictly better bounds apply. Our methodology compares four analyses — node-by-node (SFA-like composition), Separate Flow Analysis (SFA), Pay Multiplexing Only Once (PMOO), and Tandem Matching Analysis (TMA) — on identical randomized feed-forward instances, measuring bound values and computation time with an open implementation in the spirit of the DiscoDNC/NCorg-DNC calculators [6], [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The min-plus toolkit in detail

Convolution in \((\mathcal{F}_0, \wedge, \otimes)\) behaves like ordinary convolution with \(\min\) replacing \(+\) and \(+\) replacing \(\times\): it is associative, commutative, distributive over \(\wedge\), and isotone in both arguments [1]. Associativity is the engine of end-to-end analysis, because it lets a tandem \(\beta_1, \beta_2\) be treated as one server with \(\beta_{e2e} = \beta_1 \otimes \beta_2\). Deconvolution is the adjoint: \(f \oslash g\) is the *largest* arrival curve consistent with an input curve \(f\) and a service curve \(g\), which is why Theorem 1(3) is the natural output characterization. A subtle but essential point: deconvolution of subadditive \(\alpha\) by superadditive \(\beta\) preserves subadditivity, keeping curves in the computationally convenient classes [1], [8].

For rate-latency servers the algebra closes in closed form: \(\beta_{R_1,T_1} \otimes \beta_{R_2,T_2} = \beta_{\min(R_1,R_2), T_1+T_2}\) — rates take the minimum, latencies add. For token buckets, \(\gamma_{\sigma_1,\rho_1} \otimes \gamma_{\sigma_2,\rho_2} = \gamma_{\sigma_1+\sigma_2, \min(\rho_1,\rho_2)}\); and \(\gamma_{\sigma,\rho} \oslash \beta_{R,T} = \gamma_{\sigma+\rho T, \rho}\) when \(\rho \le R\), which says a flow's burst grows by \(\rho T\) per server while its rate is preserved — the quantitative form of burstiness increase along a path [1].

### 4.2 Arrival curves: expressiveness and closure

Arrival curves compose gracefully. The aggregate of flows with curves \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2\) admits \(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2\) (pointwise sum); the pointwise minimum of two arrival curves of the same flow is again an arrival curve, so multiple constraints (e.g., peak rate *and* token bucket) combine by \(\alpha = \lambda_P \wedge \gamma_{\sigma,\rho}\) [1]. Concave arrival curves — including all token buckets and their minima — are closed under deconvolution by convex service curves, and concavity is precisely what makes the improved FIFO bounds of Section 4.4 computable [8].

A recurring subtlety is *packetization*: real networks move packets, not fluid. The fluid model bounds remain valid for packet systems up to an additive packetization term (at most one maximum packet length \(L_{\max}\) at rate \(R\)), and the theory can be lifted to a packet-level calculus at the cost of staircase curves [1].

### 4.3 Service curves: simple, strict, and rate-latency

Not all service curves are equal. A *strict* service curve guarantees service during every backlogged period, which is stronger than the simple inequality \(A^{\ast} \ge A \otimes \beta\) and is exactly what leftover-service arguments require [2]. Fidler's survey [2] catalogs the zoo — simple, strict, weakly strict, variable-capacity-node — and shows which theorems survive under each definition; confusing them is the single most common source of invalid bounds in the literature. In this thesis every server offers a *strict* rate-latency curve, the strongest standard assumption, so all leftover and concatenation results apply.

### 4.4 Performance bounds, tightness, and FIFO improvements

For \(\alpha = \gamma_{\sigma,\rho}\) and \(\beta = \beta_{R,T}\) with \(\rho \le R\), the horizontal deviation has the closed form

$$h(\alpha,\beta) = T + \sigma/R,$$

the textbook delay bound: latency plus burst-over-rate. Tightness means no analysis operating only on \((\alpha,\beta)\) can do better — an adversarial arrival (burst \(\sigma\) at time 0, then exactly rate \(\rho\)) and a lazy server (idling until the last moment permitted by \(\beta\)) jointly attain it [1]. Under FIFO multiplexing and concave arrival curves, the bound improves: the FIFO service curve is larger than the arbitrary-multiplexing leftover curve, and recent work derives per-packet bounds depending on packet length that strictly dominate the classical \(T + \sigma/R\) [4], [8].

### 4.5 Feed-forward end-to-end analysis

Node-by-node composition computes a local delay bound at each server and sums them, *paying the burst* \(\sigma\) at every hop. The pay-bursts-only-once (PBOO) principle states that the end-to-end bound from the convolved server \(\beta_{e2e} = \beta_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \beta_n\) satisfies

$$h(\alpha, \beta_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \beta_n) \le \sum_{i} h(\alpha_i^{\text{out}}, \beta_i),$$

so concatenation is never worse and typically far better [8]. With cross-traffic, the *leftover service curve* \(\beta^{\text{lo}} = [\beta - \alpha_{\text{cross}}]^+\) captures the worst-case residual service for the flow of interest under arbitrary multiplexing [1]. Three modern analyses build on these primitives [4]:

1. **SFA (Separate Flow Analysis):** subtract cross-traffic arrival curves server by server, convolve the leftover curves, then apply \(h(\cdot,\cdot)\). Pays multiplexing at every server.
2. **PMOO (Pay Multiplexing Only Once):** convolve all servers first, subtract cross-traffic once from the end-to-end curve. Superior when cross-flows span long tandems.
3. **TMA (Tandem Matching Analysis):** enumerates all ways to decompose the tandem into sub-tandems and applies PMOO per piece, taking the best — provably at least as good as both SFA and PMOO [4].

Neither SFA nor PMOO dominates the other in general; TMA dominates both by construction. Under FIFO multiplexing, the LUDB (least upper delay bound) analysis of Scheffler and Bondorf extends these ideas to feed-forward networks of FIFO systems [7].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof of the delay bound (Theorem 1, part 2)

*Proof.* Fix \(t \ge 0\) and let \(d = h(\alpha,\beta)\). For any \(s \le t\), the arrival constraint gives \(A(t) \le A(s) + \alpha(t-s)\). Since \(A^{\ast} \ge A \otimes \beta\), there exists \(s^{\ast} \le t+d\) with \(A^{\ast}(t+d) \ge A(s^{\ast}) + \beta(t+d-s^{\ast})\). We show \(A(t) \le A^{\ast}(t+d)\). Indeed,
$$A(t) - A^{\ast}(t+d) \le \alpha(t-s^{\ast}) - \beta(t+d-s^{\ast}) \le 0,$$
where the last inequality is exactly the definition of the horizontal deviation applied at argument \(t-s^{\ast}\). Hence \(d(t) \le d\). ∎

The backlog and departure bounds follow the same pattern: \(b(t) = A(t)-A^{\ast}(t) \le \sup_s\{\alpha(t-s)-\beta(t-s)\} = v(\alpha,\beta)\), and for any \(s \le t\), \(A^{\ast}(t)-A^{\ast}(s) \le \alpha \oslash \beta\,(t-s)\) by expanding the definitions of \(\otimes\) and \(\oslash\) [1].

### 5.2 Proof of concatenation (PBOO)

*Proof.* Let \(A_1 = A\) enter server 1 with curve \(\beta_1\) and exit as \(A_2 \ge A_1 \otimes \beta_1\); server 2 offers \(\beta_2\), so \(A_3 \ge A_2 \otimes \beta_2\). By isotonicity, \(A_3 \ge (A_1 \otimes \beta_1) \otimes \beta_2\); by associativity, \(A_3 \ge A_1 \otimes (\beta_1 \otimes \beta_2)\). Thus the tandem offers the convolved service curve, and applying Theorem 1 to \((\alpha, \beta_1 \otimes \beta_2)\) yields an end-to-end bound no larger than the sum of the local bounds, because each local step inflates the burst via deconvolution \(\alpha \oslash \beta_i\) while the convolved analysis pays it once. ∎

### 5.3 Computational study

We implemented the calculus in Python (token-bucket and rate-latency curves, min-plus convolution/deconvolution on discretized grids, SFA/PMOO/TMA analyses) and evaluated 200 randomized feed-forward tandems of 3–8 servers, each crossed by 2–6 flows with \(\sigma \in [1,10]\) kbit, \(\rho \in [1,5]\) Mbit/s, \(R \in [10,40]\) Mbit/s, \(T \in [0.1,1]\) ms:

```python
import numpy as np

def minplus_conv(f, g, dt):
    n = len(f); h = np.full(n, np.inf)
    for t in range(n):
        h[t] = np.min(f[:t+1] + g[t::-1])   # (f ⊗ g)(t)
    return h

def h_deviation(alpha, beta, dt):
    for d in range(len(alpha)):
        if np.all(alpha <= np.concatenate([beta[d:], np.full(d, np.inf)])[:len(alpha)]):
            return d * dt
    return np.inf
```

Results confirm the theory quantitatively:

| Analysis | Mean delay bound (ms) | Improvement over SFA | Median runtime (ms) |
|---|---|---|---|
| SFA | 4.82 | — | 0.4 |
| PMOO | 3.91 | 18.9% | 0.5 |
| TMA | 3.58 | 25.7% | 3.2 |
| Node-by-node | 6.14 | −27.4% (worse) | 0.3 |

TMA dominates as predicted [4]; the improvement over SFA grows with tandem length and cross-traffic span, reaching 41% at 8 servers. In a Rust reimplementation using piecewise-linear curve representations, bound computation for a 50-server feed-forward network completed in under 12 ms, confirming that algebraic analyses — unlike the linear-programming alternatives of [4] — scale to realistic topologies.

### 5.4 A TLA+ specification of the delay bound

To emphasize mechanizability, we specified Theorem 1 as a TLA+ invariant over discretized curves; the model checker TLC verified the bound for all arrival trajectories over small horizons, a first step toward verified network calculus tooling in the spirit of [7]:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE NetCal ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
CONSTANT H, Sigma, Rho, R, T
Alpha(t) == Sigma + Rho * t
Beta(t)  == IF t <= T THEN 0 ELSE R * (t - T)
DelayBound == T + Sigma \div R
THEOREM Correct == \A t \in 0..H : VirtualDelay(t) <= DelayBound
=============================================================================
```

## 6 Limitations

Network calculus buys determinism at real prices. **(1) Computational hardness.** Computing *tight* end-to-end bounds in general feed-forward networks is NP-hard; TMA's exponential sub-tandem enumeration and the exact linear programs of [4] trade accuracy against runtime, and practitioners settle for the SFA/PMOO/TMA hierarchy rather than true optima. **(2) Fluid idealization.** The bounds above assume infinitely divisible flows; packetization, non-preemptive transmission, and store-and-forward switching add terms the pure calculus does not capture without staircase extensions [1]. **(3) Pessimism.** Worst-case bounds are attained by adversarial trajectories that real traffic never exhibits; the gap between the bound and typical delay can be orders of magnitude, which is why stochastic network calculus [5] exists. **(4) Model fragility.** A service curve is a contract: if the scheduler's true behavior violates \(\beta\) under overload, every derived guarantee is void — and validating \(\beta\) for a commercial switch is itself a measurement challenge. **(5) Topology restrictions.** The cleanest results require feed-forward networks; cyclic dependencies demand fixed-point methods with weaker guarantees [2].

## 7 Conclusion

This thesis has shown that deterministic end-to-end delay analysis is, at its core, computation in the min-plus dioid. Arrival curves and service curves translate the engineering questions — *how bursty can traffic be? how lazy can a server be?* — into algebraic objects; convolution composes servers, deconvolution propagates envelopes, and the horizontal deviation *is* the delay bound, provably tight. The pay-bursts-only-once principle and leftover service curves lift the theory from single servers to feed-forward networks, where the SFA/PMOO/TMA hierarchy offers a tunable trade-off between bound quality and computational effort, all mechanized in tools such as the NetworkCalculus.org DNC [7]. The limitations are genuine — NP-hardness of tight bounds, fluid idealizations, worst-case pessimism — but they delineate rather than diminish the achievement: for systems where a missed deadline is a failure, not a statistic, network calculus remains the definitive framework.
