# Differentiable Programming as a Language Design Problem: Adjoint Semantics, Checkpointing, and Higher-Order Automatic Differentiation

## Abstract

We argue that differentiable programming is fundamentally a *language design* problem: the correctness, performance, and composability of automatic differentiation (AD) are determined less by numerical analysis than by the semantics of the host language in which programs are written. We develop this thesis through three linked investigations: (1) adjoint semantics, giving reverse-mode AD a precise mathematical meaning as the propagation of *cotangent vectors* through a program's dataflow; (2) checkpointing, the language-level mechanism trading memory for recomputation in reverse-mode sweeps, with binomial schedules achieving provably optimal time–memory tradeoffs; and (3) higher-order AD, where nested differentiation exposes the subtle *perturbation confusion* bug that has afflicted fielded implementations for decades. Along the way we contrast Wengert-list (tracing, operator-overloading) designs with source-transformation designs that emit adjoint code, and we show how modern systems such as JAX treat differentiation as one composable program transformation among several. The synthesis is a set of design principles for differentiable languages grounded in verified literature.

---

## 1. Introduction

A decade of deep learning has normalized the idea that a program can be *differentiated*: given code computing a scalar loss from millions of parameters, a single library call returns the gradient. Yet the conceptual machinery behind this — automatic differentiation — predates machine learning by half a century, and its integration into programming languages remains surprisingly unsettled. Is differentiation a library feature, implemented by operator overloading and runtime tracing? A compiler transformation, applied to an intermediate representation? Or a primitive of the language itself, with a denotational semantics on equal footing with evaluation?

The dominant industrial answer has drifted toward the third option. Systems like **JAX** expose differentiation as one of several *composable transformations* — `grad`, `jit`, `vmap` — applied to ordinary numerical programs and lowered through XLA to accelerators [7]. Swift for TensorFlow experimented with making the derivative operator a first-class, compiler-integrated construct with an explicit adjoint model [8]. These systems embody a hypothesis: *differentiable programming is a language design problem*, and getting the language right determines whether AD is correct, fast, and composable.

This thesis defends that hypothesis through three pressure points where language design bites hardest:

1. **Adjoint semantics.** Reverse mode propagates *adjoints* backward through a computation graph; giving this a rigorous semantics requires choosing an abstraction level and a mathematical model of programs, from Wengert lists to diffeological spaces.
2. **Checkpointing.** Reverse mode's memory grows with program length; checkpointing trades recomputation for storage, with binomial schedules achieving provably optimal tradeoffs [6].
3. **Higher-order AD.** Differentiating programs that themselves differentiate — Hessians, meta-learning — breaks naive implementations through *perturbation confusion*, where distinct formal epsilons become indistinguishable despite tagging [1][2].

We proceed from foundations to deep dives, then to empirical and proof-theoretic evidence, limitations, and conclusions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The two modes of automatic differentiation

Automatic differentiation is *not* numerical differentiation (finite differences) and *not* symbolic differentiation (expression swell). It evaluates exact derivatives (up to floating-point rounding) by systematically applying the chain rule to the elementary operations of a program [4].

Decompose a program into inputs $v_{-n}\dots v_0$, intermediates $v_1 \dots v_\ell$, and outputs $v_{\ell+1} \dots v_{\ell+m}$, each defined by an elementary operation $\varphi_i$ of its predecessors. Two accumulation strategies exist:

- **Forward mode** propagates *tangents* $\dot{v}_i = \partial v_i / \partial x_j$ alongside primals. One sweep computes one column of the Jacobian; cost scales with the number of *inputs* $n$.
- **Reverse mode** propagates *adjoints* $\bar{v}_i = \partial y_k / \partial v_i$ backward from outputs. One sweep computes one row of the Jacobian; cost scales with the number of *outputs* $m$, independent of $n$.

> **Theorem (Cheap gradient principle):** For $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, the arithmetic complexity of evaluating $\nabla f$ by reverse-mode AD is bounded by a small constant multiple (classically $\leq 5\times$, Baur–Strassen) of the cost of evaluating $f$ itself, *independent of $n$* [4].

This is why reverse mode — backpropagation — dominates machine learning: one reverse sweep yields the full gradient of a scalar loss over millions of parameters.

### 2.2 Adjoint vectors and the backward recurrence

Reverse mode's mathematical core is the *adjoint recurrence*. With partials $d_{ij} = \partial v_i / \partial v_j$ for $j \prec i$ (predecessors), adjoints satisfy

$$\bar{u}_j = \sum_{i \succ j} \bar{u}_i \, d_{ij}, \qquad \bar{u}_{\ell+k} = e_k,$$

computed backward from the terminal seeds. Incrementally, each elementary operation contributes $\bar{v}_i \mathrel{+}= \bar{u}_i \nabla \varphi_i(v_i)$ to its arguments' adjoints. The recurrence is linear in the adjoints even when the primal program is nonlinear — differentiation is, at its core, a *linear* program transformation.

### 2.3 Where language design enters

Every AD implementation must answer three language questions: **how programs are represented** for differentiation (runtime traces, source syntax, or compiler IR); **what is stored versus recomputed** (the checkpointing policy); and **what nesting means** (a semantics for higher-order differentiation that keeps distinct perturbations distinct). The remainder of this work treats each as a design problem with verifiable answers.

---

## 3. Methodology

Our method is *comparative language analysis grounded in the verified literature*: we **formalize** each design decision as a semantics question, **contrast** operator-overloading tracing with source-transformation adjoint code on correctness, performance, and expressiveness, **verify** claims against primary sources [1]–[8], and **quantify** tradeoffs with complexity analysis — since language-design claims without cost models are merely aesthetic. The analysis is deliberately cross-paradigm: dynamic tracing systems (PyTorch, Autograd), static transformation systems (Tapenade, Enzyme, Swift for TensorFlow), and trace-and-compile hybrids (JAX).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Adjoint semantics: what reverse mode *means*

The adjoint recurrence is usually presented operationally — "run the tape backward." A *semantics* asks instead: what mathematical function does the differentiated program denote, and is it the derivative of what the original denotes? At first order the answer is reassuring: interpreting programs as smooth maps between Cartesian spaces, the syntactic forward-mode macro $\vec{\mathcal{D}}$ is provably correct by induction on syntax [3], and reverse mode denotes the *adjoint* (transpose) of the Jacobian via cotangent vectors.

The subtlety — and the language-design problem — is *higher types*. Cartesian spaces do not support function spaces: one cannot inject $\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^p$ for all $d$ (a Borsuk–Ulam argument), so a semantics that interprets $\mathbf{real} \to \mathbf{real}$ as a Euclidean space collapses [3]. Yet differentiable programming *needs* higher-order functions: neural network layers are naturally expressed as higher-order combinators (`map`, `fold`, layer composition). The resolution is to move from Cartesian spaces to **diffeological spaces**, which admit well-behaved function spaces in which every definable function is smooth [3]. Under this semantics, the CHAD (Combinatory Homomorphic Automatic Differentiation) transformation is proved *compositionally* correct for programs of arbitrary higher-order type. The moral: *a language whose type system cannot denote function spaces cannot give reverse mode a compositional semantics* — which is why operator-overloading AD in dynamically typed languages harbors soundness bugs at higher order.

### 4.2 Wengert lists versus adjoint code

The two classical implementation strategies differ in *when* the adjoint program comes into existence.

**Wengert lists (tracing).** Operator overloading intercepts each elementary operation at runtime, appending a record — the Wengert list, or *tape* — capturing inputs, outputs, and the local partials. The reverse sweep then walks this tape backward. It works on unmodified host-language code and handles data-dependent control flow naturally (the trace *is* the executed path). Costs are structural: *memory* (every operation allocates tape entries), *dispatch overhead* (often an order-of-magnitude slowdown versus the primal [4]), and *opacity* (the differentiated program exists only as data, invisible to downstream compilers).

**Adjoint code (source transformation).** Systems like Tapenade, and the Swift for TensorFlow design [8], generate the adjoint program *statically*: the compiler transforms the function body into code that computes both primals and adjoints, or into a separate adjoint function. It has zero runtime tracing overhead, full optimizer visibility (inlining, fusion, vectorization), and a compile-time artifact amenable to semantic reasoning. Costs: the transformation must handle the full language — closures, mutation, control flow — with *activity analysis* for data-dependent branches.

| Dimension | Wengert list (tracing) | Adjoint code (source transform) |
|---|---|---|
| When differentiation happens | Runtime, per execution | Compile time, per program |
| Memory overhead | Tape proportional to op count | Primal checkpoints only |
| Control flow | Free (trace is the path) | Needs static analysis |
| Optimizer visibility | None (opaque tape) | Full (ordinary code) |
| Higher-order correctness | Fragile (see §4.4) | Provable with right semantics [3] |
| Exemplars | PyTorch, Autograd | Tapenade, Enzyme, Swift for TF |

A concrete forward-mode illustration in Python shows the tracing idea at its simplest — dual numbers carried through overloaded arithmetic:

```python
class Dual:
    """Forward-mode dual number: value + tangent."""
    def __init__(self, x, dx):
        self.x, self.dx = x, dx
    def __add__(self, other):
        o = other if isinstance(other, Dual) else Dual(other, 0.0)
        return Dual(self.x + o.x, self.dx + o.dx)
    def __mul__(self, other):
        o = other if isinstance(other, Dual) else Dual(other, 0.0)
        # product rule: d(x*y) = dx*y + x*dy
        return Dual(self.x * o.x, self.dx * o.x + self.x * o.dx)

def grad(f, x):
    return f(Dual(x, 1.0)).dx   # seed tangent = 1

grad(lambda x: x * x + 2 * x, 3.0)  # -> 8.0
```

The reverse-mode analogue replaces tangents with adjoints and requires the tape; the *language question* is whether that tape is a runtime data structure or a compile-time program. JAX's answer is a hybrid: trace once into a first-order IR (jaxpr), then transform the IR — obtaining static adjoint code from dynamic tracing [7].

Higher-order nesting already strains the naive approach, as the Haskell `ad` package demonstrates. Its solution to the confusion problems of §4.4 uses *rank-2 polymorphism* to make perturbation tags unforgeable at the type level:

```haskell
-- Simplified sketch of the ad package's nested differentiation
diff :: (forall s. AD s Double -> AD s Double) -> Double -> Double
diff f x = tangent (f (auto x))
-- The 'forall s' prevents the perturbation tag 's' from escaping
-- its differentiation scope: nested 'diff' calls get distinct tags
-- by parametricity, ruling out perturbation confusion *by typing*.
```

This is language design as bug prevention: the type system keeps distinct epsilons distinct.

### 4.3 Checkpointing: the memory–recompute frontier

Reverse mode's dirty secret is memory. The adjoint sweep needs primal intermediate values — either stored during the forward sweep or recomputed on demand. Storing everything costs $O(\ell)$ memory for $\ell$ steps; recomputing everything costs $O(\ell^2)$ time. **Checkpointing** navigates between these extremes: save the program state at selected *checkpoints* during the forward sweep, then recompute forward segments between checkpoints as the reverse sweep needs them.

For programs with a natural sequential structure — time-stepping simulations, loop nests, deep network layers — Griewank and Walther proved a striking optimality result: with a fixed number $c$ of checkpoints, the schedule minimizing total recomputation is the **binomial checkpointing** schedule, implemented in the `revolve` algorithm [6]. The key combinatorial fact: $c$ checkpoints and $r$ recomputations (revolutions) suffice to reverse $\binom{c+r}{c}$ time steps. Inverting this, reversing $\ell$ steps with $c$ checkpoints needs only $r \approx \log$-scale recomputation growth — exponentially better than the naive extremes.

A small instance ($c = 2$ checkpoints, $\ell = 6$ steps):

| Phase | Action | Checkpoints held |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Run $F_0 \dots F_5$; store states at steps 0, 3 | $\{s_0, s_3\}$ |
| 2 | Restore $s_3$; run $F_3 \dots F_5$; reverse $\bar{F}_5 \bar{F}_4 \bar{F}_3$ | $\{s_0\}$ |
| 3 | Restore $s_0$; run $F_0 \dots F_2$; reverse $\bar{F}_2 \bar{F}_1 \bar{F}_0$ | $\{\}$ |

Total: 6 forward steps stored once, 3 steps recomputed — versus 6 stored with no checkpointing. Asymptotically, binomial schedules achieve $O(\log \ell)$ recomputation blowup with $O(\log \ell)$ checkpoints, and Griewank–Walther show no schedule with $c$ checkpoints can do better [6].

Checkpointing can be **manual** (programmer-inserted `checkpoint` markers, as in JAX's `jax.checkpoint` / `remat` [7]), **automatic** (the compiler selects a binomial schedule given a memory budget, as planned in the Swift for TensorFlow AD design [8]), or **two-level** (checkpoints spill to disk, with provably optimal multi-stage schedules extending revolve). The design lesson: checkpointing is a *semantic policy* about which values a differentiated program retains, and languages should expose it deliberately rather than letting it emerge from tape allocation.

### 4.4 Higher-order AD and perturbation confusion

The deepest language-design failure in AD's history is **perturbation confusion**. Forward mode attaches a formal infinitesimal $\varepsilon$ (with $\varepsilon^2 = 0$) to each number and propagates it. When differentiation is *nested* — the derivative of a function that itself computes a derivative — two distinct $\varepsilon$'s coexist, and they must be kept distinct. Implementations tag each perturbation with a unique identifier per application of the derivative operator. Siskind and Pearlmutter exhibited the bug: *tagging is not enough* [1].

The essence: a fresh tag is created when taking the derivative *of a function at a point*, but with higher-order functions, "taking a derivative" and "applying at a point" need not coincide. Consider a higher-order $f$ whose derivative $f'(x)$ is itself a function computing a derivative — arising naturally with curried functions. The inner and outer differentiation scopes can end up sharing a tag, and perturbations from distinct $\varepsilon$'s are confused, silently producing wrong derivatives [1]. Manzyuk et al. later showed the confusion persists *despite* the tagging machinery in fielded implementations, exhibiting concrete failing cases and analyzing two candidate fixes — eta-expansion to delay tag creation, and tag-substitution wrappers — both of which threaten forward mode's complexity guarantees [2].

Solutions are all language-design solutions:

- **Rank-2 polymorphism** (Haskell `ad`): tags become phantom type parameters that cannot escape their scope; parametricity *proves* confusion impossible [1][8].
- **Compiler-managed tags** (Swift for TensorFlow): lacking rank-2 polymorphism, sound nesting required the *compiler* to emit diagnostics and reject malformed cases — language and AD co-design made unavoidable [8].
- **Tracer-owned tags** (JAX): unique tags are threaded through trace levels, so nested `grad(grad(f))` composes correctly because the *tracer*, not user code, owns tag allocation [7].

> **Theorem (informal, after [1][2]):** No purely dynamic tagging discipline that allocates tags at derivative-operator application time can be both correct for arbitrary higher-order programs and complexity-preserving; soundness requires either static scoping of tags (types) or delayed tag creation tied to actual differentiation points.

As the pitfalls taxonomy [5] emphasizes, AD differentiates *the program as implemented at a chosen abstraction level*. Higher-order AD makes that level load-bearing: whether nested differentiation is even meaningful depends on the language's semantics for closures, tags, and effects.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Complexity landscape

The following table summarizes the asymptotic costs that any language design must respect. Here $n$ = inputs, $m$ = outputs, $\ell$ = operations, $c$ = checkpoints.

| Method | Time (ops) | Memory | Gradient of $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Forward mode | $O(n \cdot \ell)$ | $O(1)$ extra | $n$ sweeps |
| Reverse mode (full tape) | $O(\ell)$ | $O(\ell)$ | 1 sweep |
| Reverse + $c$ checkpoints (binomial) | $O(\ell \cdot t(c,\ell))$ | $O(c)$ | 1 sweep, $t$ = recomputation factor |
| Finite differences | $O(n \cdot \ell)$ | $O(1)$ | $n{+}1$ evals, inexact |

The recomputation factor $t(c, \ell)$ grows only logarithmically: $\binom{c+r}{c} \geq \ell$ determines $r$, the extra forward revolutions. For $\ell = 10^6$ and $c = 100$, $r \approx 6$ — negligible time for a $10^4\times$ memory reduction [6].

### 5.2 Proof sketch: optimality of binomial checkpointing

Griewank and Walther's optimality argument [6] is a dynamic program on the *revolve* recurrence. Let $T(c, r)$ be the maximum steps reversible with $c$ checkpoints and $r$ recomputation passes. The optimal strategy for the last segment either keeps a checkpoint through it or not, giving $T(c,r) = T(c, r-1) + T(c-1, r)$ — Pascal's recurrence, so $T(c,r) = \binom{c+r}{c}$. Every schedule is bounded by this recurrence; the binomial schedule attains the bound by construction. Notably, the proof is *about programs*: the differentiated program's memory policy is a combinatorial object with a provable optimum, the kind of guarantee only a language-level abstraction delivers uniformly.

### 5.3 Empirical signature of perturbation confusion

Perturbation confusion is detectable by differential testing: compare nested forward-mode AD against a trusted reference (forward-over-reverse or symbolic differentiation) on higher-order programs. Siskind and Pearlmutter's counterexample — nestings where tag allocation and application diverge — silently produces wrong values in systems that allocate tags eagerly [1]; Manzyuk et al. confirmed the failure in fielded implementations and showed both proposed repairs threaten the $O(\ell)$ complexity guarantee [2]. The lesson: differential testing of nested `grad` is a necessary conformance suite for any differentiable language.

### 5.4 Compositionality in practice: JAX

JAX provides the existence proof that the language-design view scales. Differentiation (`grad`), compilation (`jit`), vectorization (`vmap`), and parallelism (`pmap`) are *composable program transformations* on a shared IR, so `jit(grad(vmap(f)))` is an ordinary expression with predictable semantics [7]. Checkpointing appears as `jax.checkpoint` (rematerialization), a transformation the programmer applies deliberately — the language exposes the memory–compute frontier rather than hiding it. XLA then lowers the transformed program to accelerators. The design succeeds because differentiation was treated as a language transformation with a cost model, not as a numerical afterthought.

## 6. Limitations

**Nonsmoothness and the abstraction gap.** AD differentiates the implemented program $F$, not the intended function $f$; at discontinuities, kinks, chaos, lookup tables, and iterative solvers, the derivative may be formally correct for $F$ yet useless for $f$ [5]. No language design fixes this — but languages can surface it with warnings at nondifferentiable primitives.

**Control flow and effects.** Source-transformation AD must statically reason about branches, loops, and mutation; tracing AD handles them dynamically but pays the tape cost and forfeits optimization. Neither dominates: the choice is a genuine tradeoff, and hybrid designs (trace-to-IR, then transform) inherit complexity from both sides [7][8].

**Checkpointing assumptions.** Binomial optimality assumes uniform step costs and free checkpoint I/O; real programs have heterogeneous operators and non-negligible store/load costs, where multi-stage (memory/disk) schedules are needed and the clean binomial result weakens [6].

**Higher-order fragility.** Despite known solutions, nested differentiation remains the least-tested corner of most AD systems. Rank-2 polymorphism is unavailable in mainstream type systems; compiler-managed tags are still research-grade [8]; dynamic tag disciplines require extreme care [2]. Until conformance suites for nested `grad` are standard, higher-order AD should be treated as *experimental* in any new differentiable language.

**Semantic foundations are still settling.** Diffeological semantics [3] gives a beautiful model, but connecting it to the operational reality of accelerators, floating point, and parallelism is ongoing work. The gap between "provably correct AD" and "fast AD on a TPU" remains wide.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Differentiable programming rewards being treated as a language design problem. The three investigations converge on a single pattern: *every hard AD problem is a scoping problem*. Adjoint semantics asks what scope a cotangent vector lives in (solved by choosing models with function spaces, e.g., diffeologies [3]). Checkpointing asks what scope a primal value is retained in (solved optimally by binomial schedules [6], exposed as a language-level policy [7][8]). Higher-order AD asks what scope a perturbation tag belongs to (solved by type-level or compiler-level tag discipline [1][2][8]).

The design principles that emerge are:

1. **Differentiate programs, not executions.** Static adjoint code (or trace-to-IR) beats opaque tapes for optimization and reasoning [7][8].
2. **Give the transform a denotational semantics.** Without a meaning for differentiation — especially at higher types — soundness bugs like perturbation confusion are inevitable [1][2][3].
3. **Expose the memory–compute frontier.** Checkpointing with provable schedules should be a first-class, budgeted policy [6].
4. **Test nesting as conformance.** Differentially testing nested differentiation is mandatory; history shows this is where implementations silently fail [1][2].

Languages that treat `grad` with the same semantic seriousness as `lambda` will define the next decade of scientific computing. Those treating differentiation as a library trick will keep rediscovering, one silent wrong gradient at a time, that it was a language problem all along.
