# Optimal Transport: Kantorovich Duality, the Wasserstein Geometry of Probability Measures, and Entropic Regularization in Machine Learning

## Abstract

Optimal transport (OT) studies how to move probability mass from one distribution to another at minimal cost, and has grown from a classical problem in analysis into one of the most influential mathematical frameworks in modern machine learning. This thesis develops the theory from Monge's 1781 formulation through Kantorovich's relaxed linear program and its duality theorem, showing how the resulting *Wasserstein distances* endow the space of probability measures with a Riemannian-like geometry — geodesics given by displacement interpolation and a formal Otto calculus recasting diffusion equations as gradient flows. We then examine the computational revolution of entropic regularization: the Sinkhorn algorithm reduces cubic-cost linear programming to near-quadratic matrix scaling with linear convergence, making optimal transport differentiable and GPU-friendly. Finally, we survey the machine-learning applications that followed — Wasserstein GANs, Sinkhorn divergences for generative modeling, domain adaptation, gradient-flow samplers, and trajectory inference — and close with the limitations and open frontiers of the field.

## 1 Introduction

In 1781, Gaspard Monge asked how to move a pile of sand into a hole of equal volume at minimal cost [3]. The problem resisted rigorous treatment until Leonid Kantorovich relaxed *transport maps* into *transport plans* — joint distributions with the two given measures as marginals. His 1942 formulation is a linear program over probability measures, and its dual — *Kantorovich duality* — shows the transport cost equals the maximum profit of potentials constrained by the ground cost.

This thesis develops three pillars:

1. **Kantorovich duality**, converting constrained minimization over couplings into unconstrained maximization over potentials; for ground cost *c(x, y) = d(x, y)* this yields the *Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula*, identifying the Wasserstein-1 distance as a supremum over 1-Lipschitz functions.
2. **Wasserstein geometry**: the quadratic Wasserstein distance *W₂* makes the space *P₂(ℝᵈ)* a complete geodesic metric space with geodesics given by *displacement interpolation*; Otto's formal Riemannian structure turns the heat, Fokker–Planck, and porous-medium equations into *gradient flows*, rigorously developed by Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré [5].
3. **Entropic regularization**, introduced to machine learning by Cuturi [2] and systematized by Peyré and Cuturi [1]: a Kullback–Leibler penalty makes the Kantorovich problem strictly convex, reducing *O(n³)* linear programming to *O(n²)* Sinkhorn matrix scaling with linear convergence — and making the transport loss differentiable.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Monge problem

Let *X = Y = ℝᵈ* and *μ, ν ∈ P(ℝᵈ)*. Monge sought a measurable map *T : X → Y* pushing *μ* forward to *ν* (*T♯μ = ν*) minimizing total cost:

> **Definition (Monge):** *M(μ, ν) = inf { ∫ c(x, T(x)) dμ(x) : T♯μ = ν }*,

with ground cost typically *c(x, y) = ‖x − y‖ᵖ*, *p ≥ 1*. The Monge problem is ill-posed: if *μ = δ₀* (a Dirac mass) and *ν* is diffuse, no pushforward map exists, since a map cannot split mass. Even when optimizers exist, the problem is non-convex and non-symmetric [3].

### 2.2 The Kantorovich relaxation

Kantorovich's decisive move was to allow *mass splitting*. A *transport plan* (coupling) is *π ∈ P(X × Y)* with marginals *μ* and *ν*:

> **Definition (Kantorovich):** *K(μ, ν) = inf { ∫ c(x, y) dπ(x, y) : π ∈ Π(μ, ν) }*.

This is an infinite-dimensional linear program with three decisive advantages over Monge's formulation:

- **Existence:** for lower semicontinuous *c* bounded below, a minimizer always exists by weak compactness of *Π(μ, ν)* [3][7].
- **Convexity:** *K(μ, ν)* is jointly convex in *(μ, ν)*.
- **Metric property:** for *c(x, y) = d(x, y)ᵖ*, *W_p(μ, ν) = K(μ, ν)^{1/p}* is a metric — the *Wasserstein distance* — on *P_p(X)*, measures with finite *p*-th moment [3].

Every transport map *T* induces a plan *π_T = (Id, T)♯μ*, so *K(μ, ν) ≤ M(μ, ν)*, with equality when *μ* is atomless.

### 2.3 The c-transform and c-concavity

For *φ : X → ℝ ∪ {−∞}*, the **c-transform** is *φᶜ(y) = inf_x { c(x, y) − φ(x) }*; *ψ* is *c-concave* if *ψ = φᶜ* for some *φ*. For quadratic cost, c-concavity is equivalent to *‖x‖²/2 − φ* being convex — a bridge to convex analysis. The c-transform is order-reversing and involutive on c-concave functions, mirroring the Legendre–Fenchel bidual [7].

| Formulation | Decision variable | Convexity | Existence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monge | Transport map *T* | Non-convex | Not guaranteed |
| Kantorovich (primal) | Coupling *π* | Linear program | Always (l.s.c. cost) |
| Kantorovich (dual) | Potentials *(φ, ψ)* | Concave maximization | Under regularity |
| Entropic (Sinkhorn) | Gibbs kernel *K* | Strictly convex | Always, unique |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Kantorovich duality theorem

The analytical heart of the subject [3][7]:

> **Theorem (Kantorovich duality):** *Let X, Y be Polish and c lower semicontinuous, bounded below. Then*
> *inf_{π ∈ Π(μ, ν)} ∫ c dπ = sup { ∫ φ dμ + ∫ ψ dν : φ(x) + ψ(y) ≤ c(x, y) }.*

The proof uses Fenchel–Rockafellar duality; the constraint may be restricted to *ψ = φᶜ*, reducing the dual to a single c-concave potential. **Complementary slackness** characterizes optimality: *π* and *(φ, φᶜ)* are jointly optimal iff *φ(x) + φᶜ(y) = c(x, y)* for *π*-a.e. *(x, y)* — the optimal plan is supported on the *c-subdifferential* of *φ* [7].

### 3.2 The Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula

For metric cost *c(x, y) = d(x, y)*, the constraint forces *ψ ≤ −φ* and the dual collapses to one 1-Lipschitz function:

> **Theorem (Kantorovich–Rubinstein):** *W₁(μ, ν) = sup { ∫ f d(μ − ν) : f is 1-Lipschitz }.*

This powers Wasserstein GANs: the generator minimizes *W₁(μ_data, μ_G)* while the critic maximizes the dual over 1-Lipschitz functions — an integral probability metric far better behaved than the Jensen–Shannon divergence [4].

### 3.3 Brenier's theorem

For quadratic cost and absolutely continuous *μ*, the Kantorovich problem admits a *Monge* solution with striking structure [3]:

> **Theorem (Brenier):** *Let μ ≪ Lebesgue with finite second moment, c(x, y) = ½‖x − y‖². Then there is a unique (μ-a.e.) optimal map T = ∇u for a convex u, and π = (Id, ∇u)♯μ.*

The optimal map is the gradient of a convex potential — the higher-dimensional monotone rearrangement. The potential solves the **Monge–Ampère equation** *det(D²u(x)) = f(x)/g(∇u(x))* for densities *f, g*, a fully nonlinear PDE whose regularity theory (Caffarelli) is among the deepest in analysis. Brenier's polar factorization further decomposes any map into a convex gradient composed with a measure-preserving rearrangement.

```haskell
-- Brenier map as gradient of a convex potential (conceptual sketch)
-- Monge-Ampere residual: det(D^2 u) - f / (g . grad u)
mongeAmpereResidual :: (Vec -> Vec) -> (Vec -> Double) -> (Vec -> Double) -> Vec -> Double
mongeAmpereResidual gradU f g x = det (hessian u x) - f x / g (gradU x)
  where u = convexPotential gradU  -- integrate the monotone gradient field
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Riemannian geometry of *P₂*: Otto calculus

With *W₂*, the space *P₂(ℝᵈ)* is a complete, separable, *geodesic* metric space. Geodesics are **displacement interpolations** (McCann): if *T = ∇u* is the Brenier map from *μ₀* to *μ₁*, then *μ_t = ((1 − t)Id + tT)♯μ₀* is the constant-speed geodesic, generalizing Euclidean straight lines to distributions [5].

Otto's insight: *P₂* is formally an infinite-dimensional Riemannian manifold with tangent space *T_ρP₂ = { −∇·(ρ∇s) }* and metric *⟨s₁, s₂⟩_ρ = ∫ ⟨∇s₁, ∇s₂⟩ dρ*. The gradient flow of *F(ρ)* then satisfies *∂_t ρ = ∇·(ρ∇δF/δρ)*, and classical PDEs emerge as Wasserstein gradient flows:

- the **heat equation** *∂_t ρ = Δρ* is the *W₂*-gradient flow of Boltzmann entropy *H(ρ) = ∫ ρ log ρ*;
- the **Fokker–Planck equation** is the flow of the free energy *F(ρ) = H(ρ) + ∫ V dρ*;
- the **porous medium equation** is the flow of Rényi entropy.

This is made rigorous by the JKO minimizing-movement scheme, *ρ_{k+1} = argmin_ρ { F(ρ) + W₂²(ρ, ρ_k)/(2τ) }* — implicit Euler discretization in *W₂* geometry [5].

### 4.2 Entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn algorithm

The Kantorovich LP costs *O(n³ log n)* for discrete measures of size *n* — prohibitive beyond a few thousand points [1]. Cuturi's breakthrough [2] adds an entropic penalty:

> **Definition (Entropic OT):** *K_ε(μ, ν) = min_{π ∈ Π(μ, ν)} { ⟨C, π⟩ + ε·KL(π ‖ μ ⊗ ν) }*.

The regularized problem is *strictly convex* with a *unique* Gibbs-form optimizer *π*ᵢⱼ = uᵢKᵢⱼvⱼ*, where *K = exp(−C/ε)*. Enforcing marginals reduces to **matrix scaling**, solved by Sinkhorn–Knopp iterations:

```python
import numpy as np

def sinkhorn(a, b, C, eps, max_iter=1000, tol=1e-9):
    """Entropic OT via Sinkhorn iterations. O(n^2)/iter, linear convergence."""
    K = np.exp(-C / eps)        # Gibbs kernel
    u = np.ones_like(a); v = np.ones_like(b)
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        u_prev = u.copy()
        u = a / (K @ v)         # row scaling
        v = b / (K.T @ u)       # column scaling
        if np.linalg.norm(u - u_prev) < tol:
            break
    P = np.diag(u) @ K @ np.diag(v)
    return P, float(np.sum(P * C))
```

Each iteration costs *O(n²)*, convergence is *linear* (contraction in Hilbert's projective metric), iterations are GPU-parallelizable, and log-domain stabilization via log-sum-exp avoids underflow at small *ε* [1].

### 4.3 Debiased Sinkhorn divergences and statistical behavior

Entropic regularization introduces **entropy bias**: *K_ε(μ, μ) ≠ 0*. Genevay, Peyré, and Cuturi [6] proposed the **Sinkhorn divergence**

*S_ε(μ, ν) = K_ε(μ, ν) − ½K_ε(μ, μ) − ½K_ε(ν, ν)*,

which is positive definite, vanishes exactly when *μ = ν*, metrizes weak convergence as *ε → 0*, and — critically — has sample complexity *O(n^{−1/2})* independent of dimension for fixed *ε*, versus the *O(n^{−1/d})* curse of unregularized *W_p* [6][1]. It is differentiable in the input measures via the dual potentials, enabling backpropagation.

| Quantity | Complexity (size *n*) | Sample complexity | Differentiable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact *W_p* (LP) | *O(n³ log n)* | *O(n^{−1/d})* | No |
| Entropic *K_ε* (Sinkhorn) | *O(n²)* per iteration | *O(n^{−1/2})* (fixed ε) | Yes |
| Sinkhorn divergence *S_ε* | *O(n²)*, debiased | *O(n^{−1/2})* | Yes |
| Sliced *W_p* | *O(L·n log n)* | *O(n^{−1/2})* (1-D rates) | Yes |

### 4.4 Barycenters, unbalanced OT, and Gromov–Wasserstein

The **Wasserstein barycenter** *argmin_μ Σᵢ λᵢW₂²(μ, νᵢ)* generalizes the Euclidean mean to distributions, computed by iterative Bregman projections, with uses in Bayesian inference and texture mixing [1]. **Unbalanced OT** relaxes marginal constraints with *KL* penalties to handle unequal mass — essential for single-cell trajectory inference where cells divide and die. **Gromov–Wasserstein** matches *pairwise* distance structures across incomparable spaces, aligning heterogeneous modalities such as single-cell RNA and ATAC embeddings [1]. JKO-inspired proximal schemes also underlie Wasserstein gradient-flow samplers and the variational view of score-based diffusion models.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Convergence of Sinkhorn

Sinkhorn iteration contracts in Hilbert's projective metric: *d_H(u^{(k+1)}, u*) ≤ λ·d_H(u^{(k)}, u*)* with *λ < 1* depending on the Gibbs kernel's projective diameter *Δ*, which grows as *ε → 0* — the fundamental *accuracy-vs-speed* tradeoff [1]:

1. **Large ε:** convergence in tens of iterations, but heavy blur toward the independent coupling.
2. **Small ε:** near-exact transport, but thousands of iterations with log-domain stabilization mandatory.
3. **ε-annealing** (large → small) recovers near-exact solutions with practical iteration counts [1].

Empirically, on *n = 10⁴* points in *ℝ⁶⁴*, GPU Sinkhorn with *ε = 0.01* reaches *10⁻⁶* marginal error in under 200 iterations (seconds), where exact network-simplex LP is infeasible — a gap of several orders of magnitude [1][2].

### 5.2 Proof sketch: complementary slackness

Let *π** be primal-optimal and *(φ*, ψ*)* dual-optimal with *φ* + ψ* ≤ c*. Then

*∫ c dπ* = ∫φ* dμ + ∫ψ* dν + ∫(c − φ* − ψ*) dπ**.*

Strong duality forces the last integral to vanish; the integrand being nonnegative, *c(x,y) = φ*(x) + ψ*(y)* for *π**-a.e. *(x,y)*. The optimal plan concentrates where the dual constraint binds — on the graph of the c-subdifferential *∂ᶜφ**. For quadratic cost this gives *y = ∇φ(x)* a.e.: Brenier's map, closing the loop between duality and Monge structure [3][7].

### 5.3 Wasserstein GANs and Sinkhorn generative models

Arjovsky, Chintala, and Bottou [4] replaced the GAN's Jensen–Shannon objective — discontinuous in generator parameters when supports are disjoint — with *W₁*, continuous under mild conditions, training the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual with a Lipschitz-constrained critic. This reduced mode collapse and gave a *meaningful loss curve* correlated with sample quality. Genevay et al. [6] then showed minimizing the Sinkhorn divergence *S_ε* yields a valid generative objective with dimension-free sample complexity and smooth gradients, via auto-differentiation through the Sinkhorn loop. Together these papers turned OT from an analytical tool into a *differentiable loss layer* in deep learning.

### 5.4 Quantitative rates

The statistical theory is sharp: for measures on a bounded domain in *ℝᵈ*, *E|W_p(μ̂_n, μ)| = O(n^{−1/d})* for *d > 2p* (Dudley; Weed–Bach), minimax-optimal without further assumptions — the curse of dimensionality is *intrinsic* to Wasserstein estimation [1]. With fixed-*ε* entropic smoothing, the rate improves to parametric *O(n^{−1/2})* [6].

## 6 Limitations

- **Curse of dimensionality in estimation.** The *O(n^{−1/d})* rate for *W_p* is minimax-optimal; entropic smoothing restores *O(n^{−1/2})* but changes the estimand — one pays in *bias* what one gains in *variance* [1][6].
- **Entropy bias and blurring.** Regularized plans are diffuse, shrinking geodesic interpolations toward the mean. Debiasing [6] corrects the value at *μ = ν* but does not fully restore sharp plans at fixed *ε*.
- **The ε → 0 limit is computationally hostile.** Contraction degrades, underflow demands log-domain arithmetic, and iteration counts explode precisely when the approximation is most faithful [1].
- **Geometric pathologies.** *W₂* geodesics between empirical measures show mass-splitting artifacts; neural critics approximate the *W₁* dual only inexactly, and Lipschitz enforcement (spectral normalization, gradient penalty) can distort the estimated distance [4].
- **Gromov–Wasserstein is non-convex.** Its quadratic-assignment structure makes global optimization intractable; entropic GW inherits local-optima issues [1].
- **Gauge freedom of potentials.** Kantorovich potentials are defined only up to additive constants, complicating their use as features.

> **Open problem:** *Is there a polynomial-time algorithm achieving (1 + δ)-approximation of the exact Wasserstein distance with dimension-free sample complexity and no entropic bias? Current evidence suggests a fundamental tension between statistical efficiency, computational tractability, and geometric fidelity.*

## 7 Conclusion

Optimal transport has traveled from Monge's sand piles to the loss functions of generative models without losing its mathematical soul. Kantorovich duality remains the field's engine: one theorem converting geometric coupling problems into potential-theoretic ones, specializing to the Kantorovich–Rubinstein formula behind Wasserstein GANs [3][4]. The Wasserstein space's Riemannian geometry — geodesics as displacement interpolations, PDEs as gradient flows via JKO — unifies dynamics on distributions [5]. Entropic regularization, through Sinkhorn, resolved the tension between the theory's elegance and its computational cost, delivering differentiable GPU-friendly transport at the price of a tunable blur [1][2][6].

The frontier lies in removing that price: sharper understanding of the *ε → 0* limit, dimension-free *unregularized* estimation under realistic structure, scalable Gromov–Wasserstein, and deeper integration of OT geometry with the stochastic calculus of diffusion models. The question Monge posed in 1781 — how to move mass most efficiently — will keep generating first-order mathematics, because distributions, and how they differ, are what modern data science is made of.
