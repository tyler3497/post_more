# Hadley Circulation Dynamics: Tropical Expansion, Subtropical Drying, and the Eddy-Driven Jet

## Abstract

The Hadley circulation is the dominant meridional overturning of Earth's tropical atmosphere, redistributing energy, moisture, and momentum between the equator and the subtropics. This thesis synthesizes its classical axisymmetric theory, its observed poleward expansion over recent decades, and its coupling to the midlatitude eddy-driven jet. Starting from the Held–Hou (1980) nearly inviscid axisymmetric theory [1], we trace how modern understanding shifted toward eddy-mediated termination of the Hadley cell, where baroclinic eddy momentum fluxes set the cell edge [2]. We review observational evidence for tropical widening — roughly 0.5° latitude per decade since 1979 across streamfunction, tropopause, and radiation metrics [3][6] — and its attribution to greenhouse warming, ozone depletion, and internal variability [4][7]. We derive the thermodynamic constraints linking subtropical drying to hydrological scaling [5], quantify the poleward shift of the eddy-driven jet, and close with formal propositions, numerical experiments, and open questions about regional expression of the expansion.

##1 Introduction

Few features of the general circulation are as consequential for human civilization as the Hadley cell. Its ascending branch over the deep tropics sustains the world's most intense precipitation; its descending branch defines the subtropical deserts — the Sahara, the Kalahari, the Atacama, the Australian outback. Roughly forty percent of humanity lives within its meridional span, and the latitude of its descending edge is a first-order control on Mediterranean, southwestern Australian, and southwestern United States hydroclimate [3].

Two intertwined facts motivate this synthesis. First, multiple independent metrics agree that the tropical belt has widened since the late 1970s, at rates of order 0.5° latitude per decade in each hemisphere [3][6]. Second, the classical axisymmetric theory of the Hadley circulation [1], while conceptually foundational, cannot explain this expansion on its own: on Earth, the Hadley cell is not terminated by axisymmetric energetic closure but by interactions with midlatitude baroclinic eddies [2]. The width of the tropics is therefore a coupled problem — thermodynamic and dynamic at once.

This thesis pursues three questions:

1. What determines the meridional extent and strength of the Hadley circulation, and where does axisymmetric theory succeed or fail?
2. What is the evidence for — and the mechanism of — tropical expansion and subtropical drying under greenhouse warming?
3. How does the poleward migration of the eddy-driven jet couple to Hadley cell widening, and what does this imply for midlatitude storm tracks?

The answers matter because subtropical drying is among the most robust regional projections of climate change, and the credibility of that projection rests on the dynamical chain from tropical overturning through eddy momentum fluxes to surface hydroclimate [5][7].

##2 Background

### 2.1 The classical picture and the angular-momentum problem

In George Hadley's 1735 conception, differential solar heating drives a single thermally direct cell in each hemisphere: warm air rises at the equator, moves poleward aloft, descends at high latitude, and returns equatorward at the surface. This picture conserves angular momentum in a way reality does not permit. Air parcels moving poleward aloft, absent friction, conserve absolute angular momentum per unit mass $M = (\Omega a \cos\phi + u)\, a \cos\phi$. A parcel starting at the equator with $u = 0$ and reaching latitude $\phi$ acquires the *angular-momentum-conserving wind*

$$u_M(\phi) = \Omega a \,\frac{\cos^2\phi_0 - \cos^2\phi}{\cos\phi},$$

which for Earth's rotation rate exceeds 100 m s⁻¹ by midlatitudes — far stronger than the observed subtropical jets of ~30–40 m s⁻¹. The classical theory thus predicts a Hadley cell that is far too strong and too wide, and it cannot produce the midlatitude Ferrel cell at all, since the Ferrel circulation is thermally *indirect* and driven entirely by eddy momentum flux convergence [2].

> **Theorem:** *Angular-momentum constraint.* In a steady, axisymmetric, inviscid atmosphere, the zonal wind in the poleward branch of the Hadley cell cannot differ from $u_M(\phi)$ defined by conservation of the angular momentum imported from the ascending latitude. Any deviation requires momentum transport by eddies or friction.

### 2.2 The Held–Hou axisymmetric theory

Building on Schneider (1977), Held and Hou (1980) analyzed steady, axisymmetric circulations in a nearly inviscid, stably stratified, differentially heated Boussinesq fluid on a sphere [1]. The key assumptions are: (i) the poleward flow in the upper branch is nearly angular-momentum conserving; (ii) the circulation is *energetically closed* — diabatic warming in the ascending branch balances diabatic cooling in the descending branch; (iii) temperatures within the circulation join continuously to radiative-equilibrium temperatures at the cell edge.

With Newtonian relaxation toward $T_E \propto h\cos^2\phi$, the theory predicts the Hadley cell edge latitude

$$\phi_H \sim \left(\frac{g H \Delta_h}{\Omega^2 a^2 T_0}\right)^{1/2} = R_t^{1/2},$$

where $R_t$ is the *thermal Rossby number*, $\Delta_h$ the fractional equator-to-pole radiative-equilibrium temperature contrast, and $H$ the tropopause height [1]. The streamfunction extremum scales as $|\Psi_M| \sim \rho_0 (a^2 H_t/\tau)(g H_t \Delta_h/\Omega^2 a^2)(\Delta_h/\Delta_v)^{3/2}$, with $\tau$ the radiative relaxation time and $\Delta_v$ the gross vertical stability. For Earth-like parameters ($R_t \sim 0.5$), this yields $\phi_H \sim 30°$ — close to observations — along with the correct order of the poleward heat flux.

Yet the assumptions are violated on Earth: angular momentum is *not* conserved in the real upper branch, and the tropical atmosphere is not energetically closed [1]. The Held–Hou model is best understood as a *conceptual* limit — the circulation that would exist if eddies were switched off.

### 2.3 The eddy-mediated view

The modern consensus, articulated by Schneider (2006) [2], holds that the Hadley cell's poleward extent on Earth is set by *baroclinic eddy dynamics* in the subtropics:

1. In the deep tropics, eddy activity is weak and the circulation is close to angular-momentum conserving.
2. Moving poleward, the meridional temperature gradient steepens; the flow becomes baroclinically unstable (Eady growth rate $\sigma \sim 0.31 f |\partial_z \mathbf{u}| / N$).
3. Where eddies grow, they flux momentum *out of* the subtropics and *into* midlatitudes, decelerating the subtropical jet and terminating the Hadley cell's poleward reach.
4. The subtropical jet — thermally driven, near the tropopause break — sits where eddy momentum flux divergence begins; the *eddy-driven jet* sits farther poleward at the storm-track latitude, maintained by eddy momentum flux convergence [2].

The Hadley cell edge is thus the latitude of maximum eddy momentum flux divergence, and any climate change that shifts the baroclinic zone poleward shifts the cell edge with it [2].

| Regime | Cell edge set by | Typical $\phi_H$ | Angular momentum |
|---|---|---|---|
| Axisymmetric limit (Held–Hou) | Energetic closure, $R_t^{1/2}$ | ~30° | Conserved aloft |
| Earth (eddy-mediated) | Baroclinic instability onset | ~30° | Eddy-flux divergence |
| Slow rotation ($R_t \gg 1$) | Global Hadley circulation | ~90° | Near-conserved |
| Fast rotation ($R_t \ll 1$) | Narrow cell + strong Ferrel | ≪ 30° | Strongly eddy-mixed |

##3 Methodology

This thesis is a *synthesis and scaling analysis*. Our methodology combines four strands:

1. **Analytical scaling.** We re-derive the Held–Hou scaling [1] and the eddy-termination criterion [2], comparing their parametric sensitivities to warming (static stability, tropopause height, meridional temperature gradient).
2. **Observational meta-analysis.** We compile published tropical-width trends from five metric families: (a) the zero-crossing latitude of the mean meridional mass streamfunction $\Psi$ at 500 hPa; (b) tropopause-height statistics [6]; (c) outgoing longwave radiation thresholds; (d) total-column ozone regime boundaries; and (e) the subtropical jet latitude [3].
3. **Hydrological scaling.** We apply the Held–Soden (2006) energetic and thermodynamic constraints [5] to derive the subtropical drying response, separating the *dynamic* (circulation shift) from the *thermodynamic* (moisture increase) components of precipitation change.
4. **Numerical illustration.** We implement the Held–Hou axisymmetric model in Python and a scaling diagnostic in Haskell, demonstrating the $\phi_H \propto R_t^{1/2}$ law and its breakdown when eddy momentum flux is imposed. A TLA+ sketch formalizes the eddy-termination logic.

> **Theorem:** *Eddy termination.* In a statistically steady, eddying atmosphere, the Hadley cell edge $\phi_H$ is the latitude at which the eddy momentum flux divergence changes sign from divergence (subtropics) to convergence (midlatitudes). Poleward shifts of the baroclinic zone shift $\phi_H$ poleward, independent of the axisymmetric closure latitude [2].

```python
import numpy as np

# Held-Hou (1980) scaling demonstration: Hadley cell edge vs thermal Rossby number
def hadley_edge(Rt):
    """Cell edge latitude (radians) from Held-Hou scaling phi_H ~ Rt**0.5."""
    return np.sqrt(Rt)

def angular_momentum_wind(phi, phi0=0.0, Omega=7.292e-5, a=6.371e6):
    """u_M: angular-momentum-conserving wind at latitude phi (radians)."""
    return Omega * a * (np.cos(phi0)**2 - np.cos(phi)**2) / np.cos(phi)

Rt_earth = 0.5
phi_H = hadley_edge(Rt_earth)
print(f"phi_H = {np.degrees(phi_H):.1f} deg")
phis = np.radians(np.linspace(0, 60, 61))
uM = angular_momentum_wind(phis)
print(f"u_M at 30N = {uM[30]:.0f} m/s  (observed subtropical jet ~35 m/s)")
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Re-deriving Held–Hou and its climate-change sensitivities

The Held–Hou derivation proceeds in three steps [1]: (i) inside the cell, the zonal wind aloft equals $u_M(\phi)$; (ii) thermal-wind balance connects $u_M$ to a meridional temperature profile that is *colder than radiative equilibrium* in the tropics and *warmer* in the subtropics; (iii) energetic closure requires the area-integrated heating to vanish, with $\bar{\theta}$ continuous with $T_E$ at $\phi_H$. The result is $\phi_H \propto R_t^{1/2}$, with parametric sensitivities

$$\frac{\delta\phi_H}{\phi_H} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\delta H}{H} + \frac{\delta \Delta_h}{\Delta_h} - 2\frac{\delta\Omega}{\Omega} - \frac{\delta T_0}{T_0}\right).$$

Under greenhouse warming, the tropopause rises ($\delta H > 0$) and subtropical static stability increases, while the equator-to-pole contrast $\Delta_h$ *decreases* through polar amplification. The first two push toward widening; the third toward narrowing. In the Held–Hou framework the net effect is ambiguous — which is why the observed widening demands the eddy view [2].

### 4.2 Static stability, the tropopause, and the subtropical jet

Lu, Vecchi, and Reichler (2007) found that the Hadley cell expands poleward by roughly 1–2° latitude per century under strong forcing in coupled GCMs, attributing the expansion primarily to *increased subtropical static stability* [3]. As the moist-adiabatic lapse rate decreases in a warmer climate, subtropical static stability rises, suppressing baroclinic growth on the equatorward flank of the storm track and allowing the angular-momentum-conserving regime to extend farther poleward before eddies take over.

Complementing this, the *tropopause-height* mechanism of Seidel and Randel (2007) [6] shows the frequency of high-tropopause soundings — a proxy for tropical air — shifting poleward, consistent with a rising tropical tropopause pushing the subtropical jet (anchored at the tropopause break) poleward. The subtropical jet is *thermally driven*; the *eddy-driven jet* is maintained by eddy momentum flux convergence at storm-track latitudes [2]. Both shift poleward under warming, but for different reasons.

### 4.3 The eddy-driven jet and storm-track shift

The eddy-driven jet is the surface-intensified westerly maximum of midlatitudes, sustained against friction by convergence of meridional eddy momentum fluxes. Under warming, models robustly project a poleward shift of the Southern Hemisphere eddy-driven jet (amplified by stratospheric ozone depletion) and a poleward-and-upward shift of Northern Hemisphere storm tracks, with regional nuance over the North Atlantic and North Pacific [4].

The coupling to the Hadley cell is two-way:

- **Hadley → eddies:** A wider Hadley cell pushes the subtropical dry zone and the latitude of maximum baroclinicity poleward, shifting the storm track [2].
- **Eddies → Hadley:** A poleward-shifted eddy-driven jet reduces eddy momentum flux divergence in the subtropics, permitting the Hadley cell to extend further before encountering eddy drag [2].

This positive feedback is part of why tropical expansion is a *robust* feature across models despite uncertainty in any single mechanism [7].

> **Theorem:** *Jet–cell coupling.* Let $\phi_J$ be the eddy-driven jet latitude and $\phi_H$ the Hadley cell edge. In steady state, $\phi_H$ lies equatorward of $\phi_J$ by the width of the eddy momentum flux divergence zone; a poleward shift $\delta\phi_J > 0$ implies $\delta\phi_H > 0$ of comparable magnitude, mediated by the subtropical static-stability response [2].

### 4.4 Subtropical drying: thermodynamics meets dynamics

The hydrological consequence of widening is captured by decomposing precipitation change into thermodynamic and dynamic components [5]:

$$\delta P \;\approx\; \underbrace{\alpha\,\delta T\, P}_{\text{thermodynamic}} \;+\; \underbrace{\delta P_{\text{dyn}}}_{\text{dynamic}}, \qquad \alpha \approx 7\%\,\mathrm{K}^{-1},$$

where the thermodynamic term follows Clausius–Clapeyron scaling of specific humidity at roughly constant relative humidity, and the dynamic term captures circulation shifts. In the subtropics the two *oppose*: warming increases moisture (wettening tendency) while poleward expansion of the descending branch suppresses convection (drying tendency). Held and Soden (2006) showed that the *pattern* of $P - E$ intensifies — "wet get wetter, dry get drier" — because moisture convergence scales with moisture content itself, amplifying existing $P - E$ contrasts at ~7% K⁻¹ even as global-mean precipitation rises only 2–3% K⁻¹ under energetic constraints [5].

| Region | Thermodynamic term | Dynamic term (widening) | Net projected change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deep tropics (ITCZ) | Strong wettening | Weak | Wetter |
| Subtropical dry zones | Wettening | Strong drying | Drier (robust) |
| Poleward flank of dry zones | Wettening | Wettening | Wetter |
| High latitudes | Wettening | Wettening | Wetter |

Subtropical drying is among the most *robust* regional climate-change signals precisely because the moisture scaling [5] and the eddy-mediated widening mechanism [2] point the same way: the Mediterranean, southwestern Australia, southern Africa, and the southwestern United States all sit at the poleward margin of the expanding descending branch [7].

### 4.5 Attribution: greenhouse gases, ozone, aerosols, and internal variability

Seidel, Fu, Randel, and Reichler (2008) synthesized the early evidence: tropopause, streamfunction, OLR, and ozone metrics all indicated widening of order 0.5° per decade since 1979 [3]. Single-forcing experiments attribute the post-1958 widening largely to anthropogenic forcing — greenhouse gases plus stratospheric ozone depletion — while black carbon, tropospheric ozone, anthropogenic aerosols, and the Pacific Decadal Oscillation modulate the width, especially in the Northern Hemisphere [3].

Staten, Lu, Grise, Davis, and Birner (2018) injected necessary caution: observed trends depend strongly on the metric and reanalysis, with streamfunction-based trends exceeding tropopause-based ones, and internal variability (ENSO, PDO) can account for a substantial fraction of the observed trend [4]. The assessment consensus is *medium confidence* that human activities have contributed to the observed poleward expansion, with greenhouse gases, ozone depletion, and aerosols all implicated, while the *projected* 21st-century expansion is a robust model response [7].

##5 Empirical Evidence and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Observed trends across metrics

The five metric families and their approximate satellite-era trends (1979–2005/2009):

1. **Streamfunction zero-crossing** ($\Psi_{500} = 0$): ~0.4–0.6° decade⁻¹ per hemisphere poleward [3].
2. **Tropopause height** (SR07 metric): ~0.5° decade⁻¹, larger in the Southern Hemisphere [6].
3. **OLR threshold** (e.g., 250 W m⁻² contour): comparable poleward migration of the subtropical minimum [3].
4. **Total ozone regime boundary**: poleward shift of the tropical–midlatitude boundary [3].
5. **Subtropical jet latitude**: poleward shift of 0.2–0.6° decade⁻¹ [3].

Birner (2010) tested the sensitivity of the tropopause metric and confirmed the widening signal is not an artifact of the tropopause definition, though its magnitude depends on the chosen threshold [6].

### 5.2 Formal statement of the widening mechanism

> **Proposition 1 (Static stability).** Greenhouse warming increases subtropical static stability $\Delta_v$ via the moist-adiabatic lapse-rate response [5]. *Proof sketch:* at fixed relative humidity, warming follows a moist adiabat; the lapse rate decreases, so $N^2 = (g/\theta)\partial_z\theta$ increases in the subtropics. ∎

> **Proposition 2 (Baroclinic suppression).** Increased $\Delta_v$ reduces the Eady growth rate $\sigma \propto f|U_z|/N$ on the equatorward flank of the storm track, displacing the latitude of first baroclinic instability poleward [2]. *Proof sketch:* linear quasi-geostrophic theory; the instability criterion shifts with the stability-modified critical shear. ∎

> **Proposition 3 (Cell extension).** Where eddies are suppressed, the nearly angular-momentum-conserving Hadley regime extends farther poleward; the cell edge $\phi_H$ follows the instability latitude [1][2]. *Proof sketch:* Held–Hou closure applied locally up to the eddy-dominated latitude. ∎

> **Proposition 4 (Hydrological consequence).** The poleward-shifted descending branch moves the $P - E < 0$ zone poleward; moisture scaling amplifies the drying [5]. *Proof sketch:* $\delta(P-E) \approx \alpha\,\delta T\,(P-E) + \delta(P-E)_{\rm dyn}$, with both terms negative in the subtropics. ∎

### 5.3 Numerical confirmation

Idealized GCM experiments confirm each link: dry dynamical cores with imposed warming-like static-stability increases produce poleward-shifted eddy-driven jets and wider Hadley cells even without moisture [2]; full-physics models add the Held–Soden moisture amplification [5]; and single-forcing ensembles separate the greenhouse-gas, ozone, and aerosol contributions [3][4]. The *sign* of the response — widening, poleward jet shift, subtropical drying — is robust across the CMIP archive; the *magnitude* varies with metric, forcing mix, and internal variability [7].

##6 Limitations

1. **Metric dependence.** Tropical-width trends vary by a factor of ~2 across metrics, and reanalysis-based streamfunction trends may be inflated by observing-system changes [4].
2. **Internal variability.** ENSO and the PDO modulate tropical width on interannual-to-decadal scales; a substantial fraction of the satellite-era trend may be unforced [4].
3. **Regional structure.** Zonal-mean widening masks strong regional variation: expansion is largest over the eastern Pacific and Atlantic and weakest over parts of the Indo-Pacific warm pool, tied to regional SST patterns and stationary waves [4].
4. **Held–Hou limitations.** The axisymmetric theory neglects eddies, monsoonal asymmetries, and the seasonal ITCZ migration; it cannot produce the Ferrel cell and misrepresents momentum balance [1][2].
5. **Eddy-parameterization uncertainty.** The eddy-termination argument relies on quasi-geostrophic baroclinic theory; moist baroclinic instability, diabatic effects in storm tracks, and stratosphere–troposphere coupling add layers not captured in dry scaling [2][4].
6. **Hydrological scaling limits.** "Wet get wetter, dry get drier" holds for $P - E$ over oceans; over land, soil-moisture feedbacks, vegetation responses, and regional relative-humidity changes modify the picture [5].

##7 Conclusion

The Hadley circulation sits at the intersection of the oldest and newest ideas in atmospheric dynamics. The Held–Hou axisymmetric theory [1] gave us the thermal Rossby number, energetic closure, and the insight that rotation and stratification jointly set the scale of tropical overturning. The eddy-mediated view [2] corrected its central fiction and relocated the cell edge to the latitude where baroclinic eddies begin fluxing momentum poleward. Observations [3][6] show the cell edge migrating poleward at roughly half a degree per decade; theory attributes this to increased subtropical static stability and a poleward-shifted baroclinic zone; and hydrological scaling [5] converts that dynamical shift into the robust projection of subtropical drying.

Three frontiers remain. First, *attribution*: separating forced from unforced components of the observed trend, and the relative roles of greenhouse gases, ozone recovery, and aerosols in coming decades [7]. Second, *regionalization*: from zonal-mean widening to the longitudinal structure determining drought risk in the Mediterranean, the Sahel margin, and southwestern North America. Third, *coupling*: the two-way feedback between the Hadley cell and the eddy-driven jet, whose joint poleward migration reshapes storm tracks and the meridional structure of climate change itself.
