# The Geometry of Adversarial Examples: ℓp Threat Models, Gradient-Based Attacks, Certified Robustness via Randomized Smoothing, and the Accuracy–Robustness Tradeoff

## Abstract

## 1 Introduction

In 2013, Szegedy et al. reported a phenomenon that remains unsettling more than a decade later: state-of-the-art image classifiers, trained to near-perfect accuracy on standard benchmarks, could be made to misclassify inputs with overwhelming confidence by adding perturbations so small as to be invisible to a human observer [1]. An image of a school bus, correctly labeled, becomes an ostrich after the addition of noise whose pixel-wise magnitude never exceeds a few intensity levels out of 255. The perturbation is not random noise — white noise of comparable magnitude does not fool the network — but a *worst-case* displacement crafted against the model's own gradients [2].

The phenomenon raises a question that is at once empirical, geometric, and philosophical. Empirically: which perturbations fool which models, and which defenses genuinely close the vulnerability rather than merely hiding it? Geometrically: what is the structure of the set of adversarial examples surrounding each data point, and how do norm constraints — ℓ∞, ℓ2, ℓ0 — carve up the neighborhood of an input? Philosophically: does the existence of adversarial examples reveal a bug in how we train models, or a *feature* of the statistical regularities on which generalization itself depends [9]?

- **The threat model is a ball.** A norm-ball constraint B(x, ε) = {x′ : ‖x′ − x‖p ≤ ε} defines the adversary's playground. The choice of p determines both the geometry of allowed perturbations and the optimization algorithm used to find them.
- **Gradient-based attacks are constraint optimization.** FGSM, PGD, C&W, and DeepFool all solve — exactly or approximately — the same program: maximize the loss (or induce misclassification) subject to the perturbation remaining inside the norm ball.
- **Certification is geometric too.** Randomized smoothing converts the pointwise guarantee "no adversarial example exists within radius r" into a statement about the *measure* of decision regions under a Gaussian neighborhood [7].
- **The accuracy–robustness tradeoff is geometric.** Robust classification demands that the classifier's margin be wide in the directions the adversary can exploit; standard training may learn narrow, brittle margins that nonetheless achieve high clean accuracy [8][9].

We proceed systematically. Section 2 formalizes threat models and their geometries. Section 3 derives the attack repertoire. Section 4 dives into defensive landscapes, certified robustness, and the tradeoff question. Section 5 sketches empirical results and theoretical guarantees, Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Threat Models and Norm Geometry

An adversarial threat model specifies three things: the adversary's *goal* (untargeted misclassification vs. targeted misclassification to a chosen class), the adversary's *knowledge* (white-box access to model weights and gradients vs. black-box query-only access), and the adversary's *budget* — the set of permissible perturbations. We focus on budget, which is nearly always formalized as an ℓp ball.

For x ∈ [0,1]^n (e.g., n = 3072 pixels for CIFAR-10) and ε > 0, the adversary may return any

$$x' \in \mathcal{B}_p(x, \varepsilon) = \{x' : \|x' - x\|_p \le \varepsilon\},$$

with the ℓp norms defined as usual: ‖δ‖∞ = maxᵢ |δᵢ|, ‖δ‖₂ = (Σᵢ δᵢ²)^{1/2}, ‖δ‖₀ = |{i : δᵢ ≠ 0}|. The geometry of the ball dictates the attack surface:

| Threat model | Perturbation geometry | Typical ε (ImageNet) | Semantic interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| ℓ∞ | Axis-aligned hypercube | 4/255 or 8/255 | Every pixel may change slightly |
| ℓ2 | Euclidean ball | 0.5–3.0 | Total energy budget; large but smooth changes allowed |
| ℓ0 | Sparse coordinate set | 1–10 pixels | A few pixels may change arbitrarily |
| ℓ1 | Cross-polytope | rare in practice | Sparse + small total change |

> **Theorem:** (Norm equivalence on ℝⁿ). For any p, q ≥ 1, the ℓp and ℓq norms are equivalent: n^{−|1/p−1/q|}‖δ‖q ≤ ‖δ‖p ≤ n^{|1/p−1/q|}‖δ‖q. *Proof sketch:* by Hölder's inequality. 

The canonical benchmark threat model, established by Madry et al. [3], is the white-box ℓ∞ setting with ε = 8/255 on CIFAR-10 and ε = 4/255 on ImageNet: an adversary with full gradient access, constrained to change each pixel by at most ε, attempting untargeted misclassification.

### 2.2 The Linear Hypothesis

Early explanations of adversarial examples invoked extreme nonlinearity and overfitting [1]. Goodfellow, Shlens, and Szegedy countered with the *linear hypothesis*: modern networks are piecewise-linear compositions (ReLU, convolution, maxout) that behave approximately linearly over large regions of input space, and in high dimensions, many small linear displacements *accumulate* into a large change in the output [2]. Consider a linear model with weight vector w:

$$f(x + \eta) = w^\top x + w^\top \eta, \quad \eta = \varepsilon\,\mathrm{sign}(w).$$

Then wᵀη = ε‖w‖₁, which grows with the dimension n even when ε is tiny, because the ℓ1 norm of w concentrates. The same argument applies to the locally linear regions of a deep ReLU network. Three implications follow: (i) adversarial examples should *transfer* across architectures trained on the same data, since all models learn roughly the same linear separators — confirmed empirically [2]; (ii) adversarial training with cheap linear perturbations should confer some robustness — confirmed on MNIST [2]; and (iii) adversarial directions should be *dense*: for any input, a large fraction of random directions, if scaled appropriately, flip the label. Later work refined this picture: adversarial examples are not merely a linear artifact but occupy *manifolds* of large measure in input space, and the linear hypothesis alone cannot explain transferability of *targeted* attacks [10].

### 2.3 Robust Optimization Framework

Madry et al. recast adversarial defense as a saddle-point (min–max) problem [3]:

$$\min_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\max_{\delta \in \mathcal{B}_p(0,\varepsilon)} \mathcal{L}(f_\theta(x+\delta), y)\right].$$

The inner maximization finds the worst-case perturbation for each training point (this is exactly the attack problem), and the outer minimization trains the parameters against those worst cases. This formulation is conceptually satisfying: it reduces the entire field to (a) solving the inner max faithfully, and (b) minimizing the robust loss. PGD adversarial training — training on perturbations generated by iterative projected gradient descent — remains the strongest *empirical* defense against ℓ∞ white-box attacks [3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Fast Gradient Sign Method

FGSM [2] is the one-step linearization of the inner maximization under the ℓ∞ norm. Linearizing the loss around x:

$$\mathcal{L}(x+\delta) \approx \mathcal{L}(x) + \delta^\top \nabla_x \mathcal{L}(x,y),$$

the maximizer of the linear approximation over the ℓ∞ ball ‖δ‖∞ ≤ ε is attained at a vertex of the hypercube:

$$x' = x + \varepsilon \cdot \mathrm{sign}\big(\nabla_x \mathcal{L}(x, y)\big).$$

The derivation is instructive: the dual norm of ℓ∞ is ℓ1, so the linear program max_{‖δ‖∞≤ε} δᵀg has value ε‖g‖₁, achieved by aligning every coordinate's sign with the gradient. FGSM is cheap (one backward pass) and, for ε = 0.007–0.03 on MNIST-scale inputs, reliably fools naturally trained networks [2]. Its weaknesses: it is a *first-order, single-step* approximation, so it systematically underestimates the true inner maximum — a fact later exploited by "catastrophic overfitting" diagnostics, where FGSM-trained models collapse against multi-step attacks [11]. ```python
def fgsm(x, y, model, loss_fn, eps):
    """One-step FGSM under the l_inf threat model."""
    x = x.clone().detach().requires_grad_(True)
    loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)
    model.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    return torch.clamp(x + eps * x.grad.sign(), 0.0, 1.0)
```

### 3.2 Projected Gradient Descent

PGD [3] is the natural multi-step, first-order solver for the constrained maximization, and — on both convex and (empirically) non-convex losses — the strongest generic attack within its computational budget. With random initialization x₀ = x + U(−ε, ε) and step size α (typically α = ε/4 for T = 10–50 steps):

$$x_{t+1} = \Pi_{\mathcal{B}_\infty(x,\varepsilon)\,\cap\,[0,1]^n}\!\left(x_t + \alpha \cdot \mathrm{sign}\big(\nabla_x \mathcal{L}(x_t, y)\big)\right),$$

where Π projects back onto the feasible set (coordinate-wise clipping to [x − ε, x + ε] ∩ [0,1]). Restarts from several random initializations mitigate the non-convexity of the loss landscape. PGD's empirical primacy makes it the *de facto* evaluation standard: a defense that has not been tested against PGD with restarts has not been tested [4][6]. ### 3.3 The Carlini–Wagner Optimization Attack

Carlini and Wagner attacked the problem from the opposite direction: rather than fixing ε and maximizing loss, they *minimized distortion subject to misclassification* [4], the formulation closest to Szegedy et al.'s original L-BFGS attack [1] but engineered far more carefully. For a target class t and confidence parameter κ:

$$\min_{\delta}\; \|\delta\|_2 + c\cdot f(x+\delta), \quad x+\delta \in [0,1]^n,$$

$$f(x') = \max\big(\max_{i\ne t} Z(x')_i - Z(x')_t,\; -\kappa\big),$$

where Z are pre-softmax logits and c is tuned by binary search per example. Three design choices make C&W devastating: (i) the *change of variables* x′ = ½(tanh(w)+1) removes the box constraint, permitting unconstrained optimization with Adam; (ii) operating on *logits* rather than softmax outputs sidesteps the vanishing-gradient pathology that shattered defensive distillation [12]; (iii) the confidence parameter κ drives the attack to produce *high-confidence* misclassifications. Instantiations exist for ℓ2, ℓ∞, and ℓ0. The ℓ0 variant (one-pixel-style attacks) greedily selects pixels with the largest gradient magnitudes. C&W remained the strongest ℓ2 attack for years and set the methodological bar: defenses must be evaluated against *adaptive, well-optimized* attacks [4].

### 3.4 DeepFool and Boundary Geometry

DeepFool [5] makes the boundary geometry explicit: for a linearized classifier, the minimal perturbation flipping the prediction is the *orthogonal projection of x onto the decision boundary* — distance ‖w‖⁻¹(wᵀx + b) along the normal direction. DeepFool iterates: linearize around the current point, project to the linearized boundary, repeat. The resulting perturbations are *minimal* rather than ε-bounded, producing a distribution of distances-to-boundary across a dataset — a direct empirical probe of the classifier's margin geometry. DeepFool's average ℓ2 perturbations on ImageNet are typically an order of magnitude smaller than those of fixed-ε attacks, revealing how thin most models' margins are.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Curse of Dimensionality in Adversarial Geometry

Why do adversarial examples exist at all? A geometric argument in the style of Gilmer et al. [10]: in high dimension, the volume of an ε-shell around any manifold concentrates in an annulus, and the *surface-area-to-volume* ratio of typical decision regions grows with dimension. Consider the idealized case of a spherical decision boundary of radius R in ℝⁿ. The fraction of a random direction that changes classification within ℓ2 distance ε is governed by the cap area, which for n ≫ 1 is substantial even for ε/R ≪ 1 — the Gaussian isoperimetric inequality tells us that in high dimension, most of the Gaussian mass lies *near* any half-space boundary [10]. The conclusion: vulnerability is generic for any classifier whose boundary lies close to the data manifold, and *distance from the boundary*, not nonlinearity or overfitting, is the operative quantity. Empirically, standard-trained CIFAR-10 models have median ℓ2 boundary distance below 0.1, whereas adversarially trained models push it an order of magnitude higher [3].

### 4.2 Obfuscated Gradients: When Defenses Hide Instead of Fix

Athalye, Carlini, and Wagner [6] diagnosed a pervasive methodological failure: many proposed defenses did not remove adversarial examples but *obfuscated the gradients* that attacks use to find them, creating a false sense of security. They identified three mechanisms:

1. **Shattered gradients** — nonexistent or incorrect gradients (e.g., thermometer encoding, input discretization); defeated by the Backward Pass Differentiable Approximation (BPDA), replacing the non-differentiable layer with a smooth surrogate on the backward pass.
2. **Stochastic gradients** — randomization at test time (e.g., stochastic activation pruning); defeated by Expectation over Transformation (EoT), averaging gradients over the randomness.
3. **Vanishing/exploding gradients** — very deep unrolled defenses (e.g., PixelDefend's iterative purification); defeated by reparameterization or end-to-end attack through a differentiable approximation.

> **Theorem:** (Athalye et al., informal). If a defense relies on making the loss landscape uninformative to first-order optimization while the underlying decision boundary is unchanged, then an adaptive attack that restores usable gradient information (BPDA, EoT, or reparameterization) recovers the undefended attack success rate. *Corollary:* black-box transfer attacks and gradient-free attacks (e.g., SPSA) serve as diagnostic tests — a defense that falls to transfer attacks was never robust, only obscure [6].

The lesson reshaped evaluation standards: Carlini et al.'s checklist [13] requires testing against adaptive attacks, verifying that increasing attack iterations and ε monotonically increases success, and confirming that gradient-free and transfer attacks do not outperform white-box ones.

### 4.3 Randomized Smoothing: A Tight Certificate

Randomized smoothing [7] converts *any* base classifier f into a *smoothed* classifier

$$g(x) = \arg\max_{c}\; \mathbb{P}_{\delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)}\big[f(x+\delta) = c\big],$$

the majority vote of f under isotropic Gaussian noise. The central result is tight:

> **Theorem:** (Cohen, Rosenfeld, Kolter 2019). Suppose g classifies x as cA with probability at least p̲A under the noise distribution, and every other class c ≠ cA with probability at most p̄B. Then g(x + δ) = cA for all ‖δ‖₂ < R, where
>
> $$R = \frac{\sigma}{2}\big(\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p}_A) - \Phi^{-1}(\overline{p}_B)\big),$$
>
> Φ⁻¹ being the inverse standard Gaussian CDF. When p̲A > ½ this simplifies to R = σ·Φ⁻¹(p̲A).

*Proof idea:* the worst-case base classifier consistent with the observed probabilities is a half-space (by the Neyman–Pearson lemma); the Gaussian measure of the half-space on the other side of a translated boundary gives exactly the radius above, and no tighter radius is possible [7].

The certificate is *probabilistic* — p̲A and p̄B are estimated by Monte Carlo sampling with Clopper–Pearson confidence intervals — but it is a genuine guarantee: with probability 1 − α, no ℓ2 adversary within radius R can change the prediction. Cohen et al. achieved 49% certified top-1 accuracy on ImageNet at ℓ2 radius 0.5 (= 127/255), the first certified defense demonstrated at ImageNet scale [7]. 

```python
def certify_radius(n_top, n_total, sigma, alpha=1e-3):
    """Clopper-Pearson certified radius for a smoothed classifier."""
    from scipy.stats import beta, norm
    pA = beta.ppf(alpha, n_top, n_total - n_top + 1)  # lower bound
    return sigma * norm.ppf(pA) if pA > 0.5 else 0.0
```

### 4.4 The Accuracy–Robustness Tradeoff

Perhaps the deepest question in the field: must robustness cost accuracy? Tsipras et al. proved that the answer is *yes* in simple settings [8]: there exist distributions where any classifier with nontrivial standard accuracy has adversarial risk approaching 1, and — conversely — the Bayes-optimal robust classifier must sacrifice standard accuracy. The mechanism: features that are *weakly predictive* (useful for standard accuracy on average) but *easily manipulable* (brittle under the adversary's budget) are exactly what the robust classifier must ignore.

Ilyas et al. sharpened this into the "features, not bugs" thesis [9]: adversarial examples are not artifacts of bad training but consequences of the *non-robust features* — highly predictive, human-incomprehensible patterns — that standard training legitimately exploits. The empirical tradeoff is stark: 

Two caveats temper the pessimism. First, the tradeoff is *distribution-dependent*: on distributions where robust features suffice (MNIST), robust and standard accuracy nearly coincide [3]. Second, extra unlabeled data provably and empirically shrinks the gap — robust self-training recovers several points of clean accuracy [15]. Whether the tradeoff is fundamental or an artifact of finite data and imperfect optimization remains the field's central open question.

### 4.5 Transferability and the Shared Geometry of Models

Adversarial examples crafted on one model frequently fool independently trained models — even with different architectures [2][10]. Geometry explains the phenomenon: if two models learn approximately the same decision boundary (because they are trained on the same data to fit the same Bayes classifier), their *vulnerability directions* — the normals of the boundary near each data point — are highly correlated. Transferability is strongest for untargeted attacks and for models sharing training data; it degrades for targeted attacks and under architectural diversity, where boundary normals decorrelate. Black-box attacks exploit this systematically: train a local substitute, attack it white-box, transfer [16].

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

We consolidate representative results from the literature into two views: what attacks achieve, and what defenses guarantee.

### 5.1 Attack Strength (white-box, standard-trained models)

| Attack | Norm | CIFAR-10 success @ canonical ε | ImageNet success | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FGSM [2] | ℓ∞, 8/255 | ~75% | ~60% @ 4/255 | 1 gradient eval |
| PGD-20 [3] | ℓ∞, 8/255 | ~100% | ~95% @ 4/255 | 20 evals + restarts |
| C&W [4] | ℓ2 | ~100% (median dist. ≈ 0.5) | ~100% | ~10⁴ evals (Adam) |
| DeepFool [5] | ℓ2 minimal | ~100% (median dist. ≈ 0.03) | ~100% (median ≈ 0.2) | iterative projection |
| ℓ0 C&W (JSMA-style) [4] | ℓ0 | ~100% @ ~15 px | ~100% | greedy pixel selection |

### 5.2 Defense Landscape: Empirical vs. Certified

| Defense | Type | CIFAR-10 ℓ∞ (8/255) robust acc. | Certified radius | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adversarial training (PGD) [3] | Empirical | ~45–55% (modern variants) | None | Strongest empirical; no guarantee |
| TRADES [17] | Empirical | ~53–57% | None | Explicit robustness/accuracy tradeoff loss |
| Randomized smoothing (σ=0.25) [7] | Certified | — (ℓ2: ~60% @ r=0.5) | R = σΦ⁻¹(pA) | Only ImageNet-scale certified method |
| Interval bound propagation [18] | Certified | ~50% @ small ε | Deterministic | Tight on small nets; scales poorly |
| Obfuscated-gradient defenses [6] | Broken | ~0% under adaptive attack | None | Broken by BPDA/EoT |

> **Theorem:** (Robust generalization gap). Schmidt et al. showed that achieving adversarially robust generalization requires substantially more samples than standard generalization — on simple Gaussian-mixture models, the sample complexity of robust learning exceeds that of standard learning by a factor that grows with dimension. This *statistical* view complements the optimization view: even with perfect algorithms, robustness is data-hungry [19].

The theoretical picture is now fairly complete for the ℓ2/smoothing case (tight certificates exist) but remains open for ℓ∞ at scale: no certified defense achieves nontrivial ImageNet ℓ∞ guarantees, and empirical defenses plateau around 60–70% robust accuracy on CIFAR-10 even with massive compute and extra data [15].
