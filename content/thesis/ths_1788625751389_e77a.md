# Distributed Time: Hybrid Logical Clocks, TrueTime, and Serializability Without Centralized Timestamping

## Abstract

Ordering events across machines without a trusted global clock is the central problem of distributed timestamping. Pure logical clocks capture causality but drift arbitrarily far from physical time, while physical clocks permit snapshot reads yet collapse under synchronization uncertainty. This thesis unifies the two traditions. We develop the Hybrid Logical Clock (HLC) of Kulkarni et al. — a constant-size timestamp that preserves happened-before exactly like a Lamport clock while remaining close to wall-clock time — with a complete monotonicity proof and the five-case receive rule. We then analyze Google's TrueTime interval API and Spanner's commit-wait protocol, showing how bounded uncertainty intervals of width 2ε replace point timestamps to achieve external consistency (linearizability) for multishard transactions at global scale. Finally, we contrast Spanner's hardware-anchored approach with CockroachDB's HLC-based design, which trades commit-wait latency for uncertainty-restart optimism, and catalog failure modes when clocks misbehave: rogue time masters, NTP drift amplification, and the causal-reverse anomaly.

---

## 1 Introduction

Every distributed database must answer one question first: *in what order did things happen?* Concurrency control, snapshot isolation, serializability, and last-writer-wins registers all reduce to placing events on a shared timeline — trivial on one machine, a research program across networks.

Machines disagree about time *unobservably*: between datacenters no reference frame makes "now" simultaneously true for both. Practice oscillates between **logical clocks**, which order by causality alone, and **physical clocks**, which approximate a shared wall clock with uncertainty. Flagships: Lamport's 1978 logical clock [3] and Google's Spanner [2], which achieved external consistency across hundreds of datacenters not by perfecting synchronization but by *reifying uncertainty* in the time API.

This thesis argues that the most productive modern designs are hybrids that refuse the dichotomy. We make three contributions:

1. **A self-contained exposition of Hybrid Logical Clocks (HLC)** [1], including the send/local and receive update rules and a rigorous proof of per-process monotonicity — the property that makes HLC a drop-in timestamp for MVCC version ordering.
2. **A precise analysis of TrueTime and the commit-wait rule**, deriving why waiting out the uncertainty interval (approximately 2ε, averaging ~10 ms) converts interval time into externally consistent commit timestamps.
3. **A comparative study of Spanner versus CockroachDB**, showing how the same theoretical substrate (causality + bounded physical time) yields opposite engineering trades: hardware-anchored commit waits versus software-optimistic uncertainty restarts, and the exact failure modes each admits.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Logical time: Lamport and vector clocks

In 1978, Leslie Lamport observed that distributed systems rarely need *true* time — they need *consistent* time [3]. He defined the happened-before relation, denoted →, as the smallest relation satisfying:

- If *a* and *b* are events on the same process and *a* precedes *b* locally, then *a* → *b*.
- If *a* is the send of a message and *b* its receipt, then *a* → *b*.
- Transitivity: if *a* → *b* and *b* → *c*, then *a* → *c*.

A **logical clock** is a function *C* assigning integers to events such that the *clock condition* holds: *a* → *b* implies *C(a) < C(b)*. Lamport's construction is disarmingly simple:

```python
# Lamport clock update rules (process j)
def local_event():
    C[j] += 1
    return C[j]

def send(m):
    C[j] += 1
    m.ts = C[j]

def receive(m):
    C[j] = max(C[j], m.ts) + 1
```

The clock condition is one-directional: *C(a) < C(b)* does *not* imply *a* → *b*. Concurrent events receive arbitrary orders, repaired by tie-breaking with process identifiers to obtain a total order.

> **Theorem (Clock condition):** Under Lamport's rules, if *a* → *b* then *C(a) < C(b)*.

> **Lemma:** The converse fails. Two events with *C(a) < C(b)* may be causally unrelated.

**Vector clocks**, introduced independently by Fidge (1988) and Mattern (1989) [4], repair the converse at the cost of *O(n)* state, where *n* is the number of processes. Each process *j* maintains a vector *V_j* with one entry per process, counting how many events of each process it has observed. The characterization becomes exact:

> **Theorem (Vector clock characterization):** *a* → *b* iff *V(a) < V(b)* componentwise [4] — exact, but at *O(n)* size per timestamp.

### 2.2 Physical time and its discontents

Physical clocks — quartz oscillators disciplined by NTP, PTP, GPS — offer what logical clocks cannot: timestamps meaningful to humans, comparable across administrative domains, and usable for *snapshot reads* ("give me the database as of 14:30:00 UTC"). But they cannot be perfectly synchronized. NTP over the public Internet typically maintains agreement only within *tens of milliseconds*; under ideal LAN conditions, around one millisecond [1]. Two consequences follow:

1. **Overlapping uncertainty intervals.** Two clocks reading 10:00:00.000 ± 50 ms and 10:00:00.020 ± 50 ms cannot order two nearby events.
2. **Non-monotonicity.** NTP can step clocks *backward* — seconds during leap-second smearing, hours after misconfiguration.

Spanner's radical move [2] was to treat uncertainty as a first-class quantity and design the commit protocol *around* it rather than *despite* it.

| Clock | Size | Causality | Physical meaning | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lamport | O(1) | Clock condition (→ ⇒ <) | None | Arbitrary drift from wall clock |
| Vector | O(n) | Exact (↔) | None | Unbounded message overhead |
| NTP/PTP physical | O(1) | None | Approximate wall time | Backward steps, unbounded skew |
| **HLC** | O(1) | Clock condition (→ ⇒ <) | Close to wall time | Logical counter growth under skew |
| **TrueTime** | O(1) | Via commit wait | Interval [earliest, latest] | Commit-wait latency 2ε |

## 3 Methodology

We adopt the standard asynchronous message-passing model: a fixed set of processes communicate over reliable FIFO channels with unbounded but finite delay. Each process *j* has access to a physical clock *pt.j* that is *synchronized within a known bound* — the crucial and only physical assumption. Formally, there exists a constant ε such that for any two processes *j, k* and any real time *t*, *|pt.j(t) − pt.k(t)| ≤ ε*. We do not assume clocks never jump; we assume the synchronization *service* (NTP, PTP, or TrueTime's time masters) maintains the bound with overwhelming probability, and we analyze exactly what breaks when it does not (Section 6).

Our methodology is threefold: we present the HLC algorithm [1] in full operational detail including the five receive cases; we reconstruct TrueTime's interval semantics and the commit-wait rule [2] with a self-contained external-consistency proof; and we contrast Spanner's hardware-rooted intervals with CockroachDB's software HLC [6] to extract the general design space of *uncertainty management*: **wait it out** versus **restart on violation**.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Hybrid Logical Clock algorithm

The insight of Kulkarni et al. [1] is that a Lamport counter and a physical reading can be *fused* into a single constant-size timestamp ⟨*l*, *c*⟩: *l* tracks the maximum physical time seen (interpretable as wall-clock time, enabling snapshot reads) and *c* disambiguates events within the same physical tick. Ordered lexicographically and updated on every message, the pair satisfies the clock condition.

Each process *j* maintains HLC state ⟨*l.j*, *c.j*⟩. Let *pt.j* denote the current physical reading.

**Local event or send** (atomically):

```python
def hlc_local_or_send(j):
    l_old = l[j]
    l[j] = max(l[j], pt(j))     # never fall behind physical time
    if l[j] == l_old:
        c[j] += 1               # same physical tick: disambiguate
    else:
        c[j] = 0                # physical time advanced: reset
    return (l[j], c[j])
```

**Receive** of message *m* carrying ⟨*l.m*, *c.m*⟩ (atomically):

```python
def hlc_receive(j, m):
    l_old = l[j]
    l[j] = max(l[j], m.l, pt(j))
    if l[j] == l_old == m.l:      # all three agree
        c[j] = max(c[j], m.c) + 1
    elif l[j] == l_old:           # local physical time leads
        c[j] += 1
    elif l[j] == m.l:             # remote timestamp leads
        c[j] = m.c + 1
    else:                         # physical clock overtook everyone
        c[j] = 0
    return (l[j], c[j])
```

Timestamps compare lexicographically: ⟨*l₁*, *c₁*⟩ < ⟨*l₂*, *c₂*⟩ iff *l₁ < l₂* or (*l₁ = l₂* and *c₁ < c₂*). The five receive cases are exhaustive: *l.j* after the max-update must equal at least one of {*l_old*, *m.l*, *pt.j*}, and each equality pattern determines the counter so that the new timestamp strictly exceeds both the previous local timestamp and the received one. This case analysis is the heart of the algorithm, and it is what guarantees the following:

> **Theorem (HLC monotonicity):** At any process *j*, successive HLC timestamps are strictly increasing in lexicographic order, regardless of physical clock behavior — including backward steps.

*Proof sketch.* For a local event, either *l.j* increases (and the pair grows lexicographically regardless of *c.j*) or *l.j* is unchanged and *c.j* increments. For a receive, *l.j := max(l_old, m.l, pt.j) ≥ l_old*; if strictly greater, the pair grows; if equal to *l_old*, then *c.j* is set to *max(c_old, m.c) + 1* or *c_old + 1*, both exceeding the old counter. ∎

> **Theorem (HLC clock condition):** If *a* → *b* then *HLC(a) < HLC(b)* lexicographically [1].

*Proof sketch.* Same-process succession follows from monotonicity; for a send *a* and receipt *b*, the receive rule yields *l.b ≥ l.a* with *c.b ≥ c.a + 1* on ties, so *HLC(b) > HLC(a)*; transitivity extends to arbitrary → chains. ∎

Crucially, *l.j* can never lag the physical clock (*l.j := max(l.j, pt.j)* on every event) and cannot run ahead of true time by more than ε plus in-flight skew — a physical clock that is *causally safe*.

### 4.2 TrueTime: reifying uncertainty

Spanner's TrueTime [2] takes the opposite philosophical stance from HLC. Rather than fusing logical and physical time into a point, it exposes the synchronization uncertainty *directly* in the API. `TT.now()` does not return a number — it returns an interval:

```
TT.now() = [earliest, latest],   with  latest − earliest = 2ε
```

The semantic guarantee is absolute: for the event *e_now* that invoked the call, with *t_abs(e_now)* the true absolute time of the invocation,

> **Axiom (TrueTime correctness):** `tt.earliest ≤ t_abs(e_now) ≤ tt.latest`.

Derived predicates: `TT.after(t)` iff `TT.now().earliest > t` (*t* definitely passed); `TT.before(t)` iff `TT.now().latest < t` (*t* definitely not arrived).

**Implementation.** Each datacenter hosts *time masters* with GPS receivers and atomic clocks ("Armageddon masters," which keep time even if GPS fails [2]); every machine runs a *timeslave daemon* polling nearby masters. The advertised ε combines reference uncertainty with worst-case local drift (200 μs/s), growing as a sawtooth between polls: measured ε ranges 1–7 ms, averaging ~4 ms, so `TT.now()` intervals span 2ε ≈ 8–10 ms [2][5].

Rogue masters are handled by *Marzullo's algorithm*: the daemon keeps the interval agreed upon by a quorum and evicts dissenters. Over a year of production data, external consistency was never observed to be violated — and bad CPUs were measured to be about **six times more likely** than bad clocks [2].

### 4.3 Spanner's commit-wait rule and external consistency

Spanner assigns every read-write transaction a commit timestamp *s_i* and guarantees **external consistency** (equivalently, linearizability [2]): if transaction *T₁* commits before *T₂* starts — in absolute, wall-clock time — then *s(T₁) < s(T₂)*. The mechanism has two halves: timestamp *assignment* and the *commit-wait* rule.

After Paxos prepare, the coordinator picks `s_i = TT.now().latest` and applies the **commit-wait rule**: it waits until `TT.after(s_i)` (i.e., `TT.now().earliest > s_i`) before revealing the commit. Only then do participants apply the write at *s_i* and release locks.

> **Theorem (External consistency via commit wait):** If *T₁* commits (becomes visible) before *T₂* starts, then *s(T₁) < s(T₂)*.

*Proof.* Let *t₁^abs* be when *T₁*'s commit becomes visible (after commit wait) and *t₂^abs* when *T₂*'s coordinator calls `TT.now()`, with *t₁^abs < t₂^abs*. Commit wait gives *t₁^abs > s(T₁)* (visibility only after `TT.now().earliest > s(T₁)`, and *t_abs ≥ earliest*); assignment gives *s(T₂) = TT.now().latest ≥ t₂^abs*. Chaining: *s(T₁) < t₁^abs < t₂^abs ≤ s(T₂)*. ∎

The *wait* converts the uncertainty interval into a guarantee: by the time *s_i* is observable, it is provably in the past everywhere. The price is latency: on average **2ε ≈ 10 ms** on every read-write commit [2][5]; read-only transactions proceed lock-free at a snapshot timestamp.

---

```
T1 coordinator                    absolute time ──────────────►
  prepare done
  s1 = TT.now().latest ──► [e1..........s1]   (interval width 2ε)
  commit WAIT ............ until TT.now().earliest > s1
  commit visible at t1 ──► ●  (t1 > s1 guaranteed)
                                        T2 starts at t2 > t1
                                        s2 = TT.now().latest ≥ t2
                                        ──► [e2..........s2]
  ⇒ s1 < t1 < t2 ≤ s2  ∴  s1 < s2   (external consistency)
```

### 4.4 CockroachDB: HLC without the hardware

CockroachDB [6] shows Spanner's *protocol shape* survives without its *hardware*. Timestamps are HLC pairs exchanged on every RPC; MVCC versions, commit timestamps, and read snapshots are all constant-size, monotonic, physically interpretable HLC values.

The divergence is uncertainty handling. Spanner *waits out* uncertainty before making a commit visible; CockroachDB *proceeds optimistically* and **restarts** on detection: each transaction carries an uncertainty interval, and a read at *ts* that encounters a write stamped inside its uncertainty window — a write that *might* have preceded the read in real time — restarts at a higher timestamp [6].

Consequences are precise. **Default mode** gives single-key linearizability but not strict serializability, due to the **causal-reverse anomaly**: *T₁* commits, a client observes it and causally triggers *T₂* on a lagging gateway, yet *T₂* commits with an *earlier* HLC timestamp. **Linearizable mode** (`COCKROACH_EXPERIMENTAL_LINEARIZABLE`) adds a **commit wait** of the full configured `max_offset` after commit — Spanner's rule, ensuring the commit timestamp is below every other node's current HLC time [6]. Rogue clocks are handled by a `RemoteClockMonitor`: a node exceeding the max offset by ~80% versus a majority **self-terminates**, converting Byzantine clock behavior into a crash fault [6].

| Dimension | Spanner / TrueTime | CockroachDB / HLC |
|---|---|---|
| Timestamp | Interval [earliest, latest] | HLC pair ⟨wall, logical⟩ |
| Uncertainty source | GPS + atomic clocks, ε ≈ 1–7 ms | NTP-style sync, configurable max_offset |
| Uncertainty handling | Commit wait (~2ε ≈ 10 ms) | Optimistic reads + uncertainty restarts |
| Default consistency | External consistency | Single-key linearizability |
| Strict serializability | Always (commit wait) | Opt-in (`..._LINEARIZABLE` + commit wait) |
| Rogue clock response | Marzullo eviction of masters | Node self-termination vs. majority |

### 4.5 Why not vector clocks at scale

Neither system uses vector clocks despite their exact causality characterization, for two reasons: the *O(n)* per-message overhead, and a mismatch of purpose. A serializable database needs a *total* order of commits consistent with real time or causality — not post-hoc concurrency *detection* between arbitrary event pairs. HLC and TrueTime supply exactly the needed order at *O(1)* size. The lesson generalizes: *choose the weakest clock that supplies the order your consistency model requires.*

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof inventory

We have established, with proof sketches, the four load-bearing properties of the design space:

1. **HLC monotonicity** (§4.1): per-process HLC timestamps strictly increase even under backward physical-clock steps — what lets CockroachDB use HLC values directly as MVCC version stamps.
2. **HLC clock condition** (§4.1): *a* → *b* ⇒ *HLC(a) < HLC(b)*; message exchange advances the receiver past the sender at zero cost beyond the timestamp itself.
3. **TrueTime interval correctness** (§4.2): *earliest ≤ t_abs ≤ latest*, maintained by sawtooth ε accounting.
4. **External consistency via commit wait** (§4.3): the chaining argument *s(T₁) < t₁^abs < t₂^abs ≤ s(T₂)* — the entire theoretical content of Spanner's transaction protocol.

A fifth property deserves explicit statement, as it is the bridge between the two halves of this thesis:

> **Theorem (HLC physical boundedness):** If physical clocks are synchronized within ε, then for every process *j* and real time *t*, *l.j(t) ≤ t + ε + δ*, where *δ* bounds the in-flight logical skew from messages carrying future timestamps.

*Proof sketch.* *l.j := max(l.j, pt.j, m.l)* with *pt.j ≤ t + ε*, and every received *m.l* was itself bounded by its sender's physical time plus ε; induction over the message chain bounds *l.j*. ∎ Hence HLC timestamps stay *interpretable* as wall-clock time, unlike Lamport timestamps.

### 5.2 Quantitative uncertainty analysis

The commit-wait latency is not a constant but a function of the ε sawtooth. From the OSDI 2012 measurements [2][5]:

- ε ranges **1–7 ms** (6 ms from worst-case local drift at 200 μs/s over a 30 s poll interval, plus ~1 ms communication delay).
- Mean ε ≈ **4 ms**; mean commit wait ≈ **2ε ≈ 8–10 ms**.
- This wait is on the critical path of *every* read-write commit — the dominant latency term for single-shard OLTP writes in Spanner.

CockroachDB's trade is the mirror image: zero commit-wait latency in the common case, paid with occasional **uncertainty restarts** whose frequency grows with skew and contention. Under tight synchronization restarts are rare and HLC wins on tail latency; under degraded sync, restart storms can exceed the cost of waiting. The crossover — *E[restarts] × restart_cost = 2ε* — has never been published as a systematic benchmark, a gap we flag in §6.

```tla+
---- MODULE CommitWait ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
VARIABLES s1, t1vis, t2start, s2, eps
\* s(T1) < t1vis < t2start <= s2  (external consistency)
TypeOK == s1 \in Real /\ t1vis \in Real /\ t2start \in Real /\ s2 \in Real
CommitWaitOK == t1vis > s1          \* TT.after(s1) held before visibility
AssignOK     == s2 >= t2start       \* s2 = TT.now().latest >= t_abs
OrderOK      == t1vis < t2start     \* T1 visible before T2 starts
THEOREM ExternalConsistency ==
    TypeOK /\ CommitWaitOK /\ AssignOK /\ OrderOK => s1 < s2
====
```

### 5.3 Logical-clock overhead comparison

For *n = 1000* nodes, a vector clock piggybacked on every RPC costs ~16 KB per message versus ~16 bytes for an HLC pair. At 100k RPCs/s that is 1.6 GB/s of causality metadata versus 1.6 MB/s — the three-order-of-magnitude gap behind the "constant-size" claim.

## 6 Limitations

**The bounded-skew assumption is load-bearing.** Every guarantee here rests on clocks staying within ε. When it fails, failures are *silent and semantic*: timestamps remain monotonic and totally ordered but *wrong* about real time, violating linearizability with no error raised. Marzullo voting and self-terminating clock monitors are mitigations, not proofs.

**HLC's logical counter is unbounded adversarially.** A faulty node broadcasting far-future timestamps inflates every recipient's *l*; timestamps stay monotonic and causal but lose physical interpretability — snapshot reads "as of now" see versions stamped minutes ahead. CockroachDB's max-offset guard is a heuristic, not a theorem.

**Commit wait is a static cost.** Spanner's 2ε wait is paid even with zero conflicting transactions anywhere — adaptive commit wait remains unexplored in production.

**The causal-reverse anomaly is user-visible.** In CockroachDB's default mode, a client can observe *T₁*'s commit, causally issue *T₂*, and find *T₂* ordered *before* *T₁* by timestamp — single-key linearizability is not whole-database linearizability.

**No benchmark of the wait-vs-restart trade exists.** The §5.2 crossover analysis is absent from the literature; both systems chose on engineering judgment.

**Leap seconds, VM pauses, and container migration** remain the practical enemies: a VM paused 30 s resumes with a stale clock; HLC's *max(l, pt)* snaps forward harmlessly, but TrueTime must book the pause as uncertainty, inflating ε.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Distributed time is a *design space* parameterized by what uncertainty a system names. HLC fuses causality and physical time into a constant-size timestamp, monotonic even under oscillator misbehavior. TrueTime makes uncertainty explicit — an interval, not a point — and commit wait converts the interval into external consistency at ~2ε per commit. CockroachDB reaches the same ends optimistically: HLC timestamps with uncertainty restarts, commit wait as an opt-in.

The unifying lesson is methodological: "known unknowns are better than unknown unknowns" [2]. Systems that *quantify* clock uncertainty build protocols correct conditional on a checkable bound; systems that ignore it inherit undetectable failures. Whether hardware drives ε toward zero or scale drives it up, the interval algebra, the five HLC receive cases, and the chain *s₁ < t₁ < t₂ ≤ s₂* remain the vocabulary of distributed time.
