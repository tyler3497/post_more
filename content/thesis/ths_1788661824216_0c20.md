# Incremental View Maintenance at Scale: DBToaster Higher-Order Deltas, Differential Dataflow, and Recursive Query Maintenance Under Updates

## Abstract

Incremental view maintenance (IVM) keeps derived query results fresh under high-rate updates without full recomputation. Classical first-order delta processing refreshes a view from the delta of its inputs, but for join queries of degree *k* the delta queries themselves remain expensive, often quadratic or worse in the database size. This thesis develops a unified treatment of three landmark answers to that difficulty: **DBToaster's higher-order IVM**, which applies discrete forward differences recursively — the *viewlet transform* — so that delta queries are themselves materialized and incrementally maintained [1][6]; the **ring and factorization abstraction of F-IVM**, which casts views as functions from keys to ring payloads and factorizes keys, payloads, and updates for gains of up to two orders of magnitude [4][5]; and **differential dataflow**, which lifts delta semantics to a partially ordered lattice of times and iteration rounds, supporting recursion and shared indexed state through *arrangements* [3]. We present delta algebra over **Z-relations**, the viewlet transform's materialization heuristics, complexity bounds for hierarchical queries, worked delta-rule derivations, and empirical comparisons, and we chart the boundary between constant-time-maintainable queries and those that provably resist it [7].

---

## 1. Introduction

Modern analytics engines rarely compute a query once. A dashboard, a risk model, or a streaming feature pipeline defines a *standing query* — a view — and expects it to reflect every new transaction within milliseconds. Recomputing from scratch on every update is infeasible: a three-way join over relations of size *N* costs O(N^3) worst-case, and doing so thousands of times per second exhausts any cluster. *Incremental view maintenance* (IVM) is the discipline of computing only what changed.

The classical approach is *first-order delta processing*: for a view *Q = A ⋈ B*, an update ΔA induces the delta query ΔQ = ΔA ⋈ B, evaluated against the current state, and the view is patched by unioning the delta [7]. This shines for selections and single-relation aggregates. But for joins of degree two or more, the delta query is itself a join — ΔA ⋈ B still costs O(|B|) per update — and for aggregate views with non-distributive aggregates such as `MIN` and `MAX`, deletions can invalidate results with no local repair [7].

Three research programs pushed past these limits. **DBToaster** observed that delta queries are just queries, maintainable with delta queries of their own — *second-order deltas* — recursively, in the *viewlet transform* [1]. **Differential dataflow** generalized delta processing from a single timeline to a *partial order* of times crossed with iteration rounds, making recursion and shared state first-class [3]. **F-IVM** unified higher-order IVM with *factorized* representations and a *ring abstraction* over payloads, so one engine maintains joins, matrix chains, and ML models under updates [4].

This thesis gives a single algebraic framework covering all three. Its contributions are:

1. Delta rules over **Z-relations** (signed multisets), proving insert/delete propagation exact when multiplicities live in the ring **Z**.
2. The **viewlet transform**: recursive forward differences, materialization heuristics, and the cost-based tradeoff between eager maintenance and lazy evaluation [1][2].
3. F-IVM's **ring factorization**: key-to-payload views, triple-lock factorization, and the complexity landscape for hierarchical queries [4][5][7].
4. **Differential dataflow** semantics: partially ordered times, difference streams, iteration frontiers, and *arrangements* [3].
5. **Recursive IVM**: derivation counting, delete-and-rederive (DRed), and how differential iteration subsumes recursive delta propagation.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Classical incremental view maintenance

The foundational result is the *counting algorithm* of Gupta, Mumick, and Subrahmanian (1993): for SPJ views, each derived tuple carries a count of distinct derivations, and deletions decrement counts so only zero-count tuples are removed [7]. The delta rules for relational algebra:

- Δ(σ_p(A)) = σ_p(ΔA)
- Δ(A ∪ B) = ΔA ∪ ΔB
- Δ(A ⋈ B) = (ΔA ⋈ B) ⊎ (A ⋈ ΔB) ⊎ (ΔA ⋈ ΔB)

The third term — the *cross term* — handles simultaneous changes on both sides. Dropping it is valid only when at most one relation changes per step, a restriction DBToaster and differential systems remove via signed multisets [1][3].

### 2.2 Aggregates and the invertibility problem

Aggregates split into *invertible* (`SUM`, `COUNT`, `AVG` via sum/count pairs) and *non-invertible* (`MIN`, `MAX`) ones. Invertible aggregates retract symmetrically (add −1 times the value); deleting the current `MIN` may reveal a masked value, needing heaps or re-evaluation [7]. DBToaster's compiler handles broad nested aggregate queries, including subqueries in `WHERE`, by compiling aggregates into nested delta loops [2].

### 2.3 DBToaster: compiling queries into triggers

Ahmad & Koch (VLDB 2009) introduced a SQL-to-C++ compiler generating *trigger procedures* — small C++ functions invoked per single-tuple update — performing fine-grained hash probes and accumulations [2]. This removed interpreter overhead and hit millions of updates per second on financial workloads. The follow-up (Koch et al., VLDB 2012) generalized to *higher-order* delta processing [1][6].

### 2.4 Differential dataflow

Differential dataflow (McSherry, Murray, Isaacs, Isard, 2013) reframes incremental computation as evaluation over *collections of differences* indexed by a partially ordered set of times [3]. Operators are ordinary relational operators lifted to difference streams. Iteration — the key to recursion — is a second coordinate: times become pairs *(t, i)* under the product order, so an update and an iteration round can be concurrent while their effects commute correctly [3].

### 2.5 F-IVM and factorization

F-IVM (SIGMOD 2018; journal 2023) takes higher-order IVM as its first ingredient and adds *factorized computation* and the *ring abstraction* [4][5]. A view is a function from keys (value tuples) to payloads (commutative-ring elements); the ring's + and · capture payload computation while joins and marginalization over keys are shared. The *same* view tree thus maintains conjunctive queries, linear-regression covariance matrices (degree-*m* matrix ring), Chow-Liu trees, and matrix chain multiplication — differing only in the ring [4]. F-IVM is built on DBToaster's backend extended with custom ring types [5].

### 2.6 Recent surveys

Olteanu's 2024 survey organizes the landscape along *eager vs. lazy* maintenance and *listing vs. factorized* representation, and introduces *q-hierarchical* queries, for which constant-time updates and constant-delay enumeration are simultaneously achievable with factorized output [7]. Functional dependencies and PK–FK constraints can make non-hierarchical queries behave hierarchically in practice.

## 3. Methodology

Our method is *algebraic and constructive*: define relations as functions into a ring, derive delta rules as theorems, and show how three systems instantiate the same skeleton with different representations and schedules.

1. **Model.** A database is a family of functions R: Dom → **Z**, where R(t) is the *multiplicity* of tuple *t*: positive for present, negative for deleted, zero for absent. Updates are Z-relations ΔR with small support.
2. **Delta rules as homomorphisms.** We prove the delta rules as consequences of interpreting operators as ring homomorphisms, where ⊕ is pointwise addition. The cross term ΔA ⋈ ΔB falls out of bilinearity of ⋈ over **Z**.
3. **Viewlet transform.** Define the *first-order delta* Δ_R Q, iterate to Δ²_R Q = Δ_R(Δ_R Q), and materialize a subset of the hierarchy: this is higher-order IVM.
4. **Ring generalization.** Replace **Z** by an arbitrary commutative ring for payloads, recovering F-IVM's key-to-payload view trees.
5. **Partially ordered times.** Lift differences to lattice-indexed collections (T, ≤); iterative fixed points are delta-correct under product-ordered (t, i) times.
6. **Complexity analysis.** Classify queries by maintenance-cost exponent under single-tuple updates, focusing on q-hierarchical queries [7].

We prefer *proofs by algebra*: if operators are homomorphisms, correctness of incremental evaluation follows from the ring laws.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The viewlet transform: discrete differentiation of queries

The viewlet transform is to queries what the discrete forward difference is to functions. For query Q over database D and relation R, the *first-order delta query* Δ_R Q evaluates on D and ΔR to exactly the change in Q's result:

> **Theorem 1 (Delta-query correctness).** For any relational-algebra query Q and single-relation update ΔR, Q(D ⊕ ΔR) = Q(D) ⊕ (Δ_R Q)(D, ΔR).

Proof by structural induction on Q. The *key move* of higher-order IVM: Δ_R Q is itself a query, so it has delta queries of its own. The *second-order delta* Δ_S(Δ_R Q) describes how the first-order delta changes under updates to S. Iterating yields a *hierarchy* terminating when a delta no longer references the database [1].

Consider the DBToaster running example: Q counts tuples in R ⋈ S [1]. Under insertion ΔR, Δ_R Q = ΔR ⋈ S — still a join against full S, costing O(|S|) per update. But the second-order delta Δ_S(Δ_R Q) = ΔR ⋈ ΔS references only updates: maintainable by hashing two small delta relations. Materializing both views makes per-update cost proportional to *update size*, not database size — a qualitative change.

DBToaster compiles the hierarchy into C++ triggers: each materialized delta view is a hash map; each update runs nested loops over delta relations. *Viewlet decomposition* recursively expands a query into delta views; a cost-based optimizer then chooses which to materialize and which to re-evaluate lazily [1]. A trigger for a two-way join looks like this in spirit:

```sql
-- DBToaster-style compiled trigger: maintain Q = COUNT(R ⋈ S ON R.b = S.b)
-- On single-tuple insert into R:
CREATE TRIGGER trg_R_insert AFTER INSERT ON R FOR EACH ROW
BEGIN
  -- First-order delta: ΔQ = ΔR ⋈ S  (probe S by join key)
  FOR EACH s IN S_index[NEW.b] LOOP
    Q_count := Q_count + 1;
  END LOOP;
  -- The sibling delta view Δ_S Q is patched symmetrically on S updates.
END;
```

For aggregates the triggers nest: base-relation updates accumulate into first-order aggregates, which trigger second-order accumulations, down the hierarchy. Query degree *k* bounds the depth: at most *k*-th order deltas, each level strictly reducing database references [1].

### 4.2 Z-relations and the ring abstraction

Interpreting relations as functions into **Z** makes the cross term ΔA ⋈ ΔB mandatory. Because ⋈ is bilinear,

(A ⊕ ΔA) ⋈ (B ⊕ ΔB) = (A ⋈ B) ⊕ (ΔA ⋈ B) ⊕ (A ⋈ ΔB) ⊕ (ΔA ⋈ ΔB),

so the delta rules are theorems, not heuristics. Deletions are negative-multiplicity updates: *one* code path handles both — the same insight differential dataflow exploits at systems level [3].

F-IVM generalizes the codomain from **Z** to any commutative ring [4]. A view is *f: keys → payloads* with payloads in a ring (R, +, ·, 0, 1). The query

```sql
SELECT S.A, S.C, SUM(R.B * T.D * S.E)
FROM R NATURAL JOIN S NATURAL JOIN T
GROUP BY S.A, S.C
```

compiles to a *view tree*: leaves hold base relations, internal nodes join children and marginalize variables, payloads computed with the ring's + and · [5]. With (**Z**, +, ·) this is ordinary aggregation; with a degree-*m* matrix ring it maintains linear regression's (count, sum vector, sum-of-squares) triple [4]; with the matrix ring it maintains matrix chains. Key space (joins, marginalization) is shared; only the payload ring changes.

A Python sketch shows inserts and deletes made uniform:

```python
from collections import Counter

def delta_join(A, dA, B, dB):
    """Delta rule over Z-relations: multiplicities may be negative."""
    def join(X, Y):
        out = Counter()
        for (kx, vx), m1 in X.items():
            for (ky, vy), m2 in Y.items():
                if kx == ky:
                    out[(kx, vx, vy)] += m1 * m2
        return out
    # Δ(A ⋈ B) = ΔA ⋈ B  ⊎  A ⋈ ΔB  ⊎  ΔA ⋈ ΔB
    return join(dA, B) + join(A, dB) + join(dA, dB)

A  = Counter({('x', 1): 1, ('y', 2): 1})
B  = Counter({('x', 'p'): 1})
dA = Counter({('x', 1): -1})   # deletion = negative update
print(dict(delta_join(A, dA, B, {})))
# {('x', 1, 'p'): -1}  -- the derived tuple is retracted, exactly once
```

### 4.3 Factorization: keys, payloads, and triple-lock

Classical IVM stores results as *flat listings*. F-IVM stores them *factorized*: a join result as a product of projections, avoiding exponential listing blowup [5]. *Triple-lock factorization* factorizes three things [5]:

1. **Keys.** The view tree follows a *variable order*; enumeration is a traversal, not a stored list.
2. **Payloads.** Ring elements computed bottom-up; aggregates pushed past joins via distributivity of · over +.
3. **Updates.** Bulk updates decompose into sums of rank-1 factors, touching each view proportionally to *factorized* size.

The payoff: up to *two orders of magnitude* faster and *four orders of magnitude* less memory than DBToaster on factorized-friendly workloads [5]. The crown jewel is *q-hierarchical* queries [7]: constant-time updates *and* constant-delay enumeration with factorized output — optimal, since no algorithm can beat it asymptotically.

### 4.4 Differential dataflow: partially ordered time and arrangements

Differential dataflow asks: what if update visibility time is not totally ordered? An update at epoch 5 and an iterative computation's round 3 are *concurrent*. Times form a lattice — typically the product order (t, i) of epoch and iteration — and the system maintains, per time, the *difference* from all earlier times [3].

> **Theorem 2 (Differential correctness).** An operator lifted to difference streams, applied to input differences indexed by a partially ordered time lattice, yields exactly the differences of the operator applied to the inputs — including across iteration rounds.

Operators are linear over differences, so *iteration is just another dimension*: recursive queries are dataflows with feedback edges, advancing the iteration coordinate until new rounds are empty — a fixed point. Correctness follows from the same ring laws as Theorem 1, indexed by (t, i).

The systems contribution is *arrangements*: shared, indexed, multiversioned collections. Operators reading the same relation share one arrangement keyed by the join attribute, versioned by time [3] — the differential analogue of materialized delta views: index once, probe per delta, and join on demand against history without re-scanning.

Where DBToaster compiles a fixed query, differential dataflow is a *runtime*: the dataflow graph is built once and handles arbitrary updates, retractions, and iteration — the basis of production streaming SQL [3][7].

### 4.5 Recursive maintenance: counting, DRed, and iteration

Recursive queries (transitive closure, reachability) break naive IVM: a derived tuple may have *many* derivations, and deleting one must not delete the tuple. The classical fix is *derivation counting*: each derived tuple keeps its derivation count; deletions decrement it and the tuple survives until zero [7]. For interacting recursive derivations, *DRed* (delete-and-rederive) over-deletes potentially affected tuples and re-derives the still-supported ones — correct but cascade-prone.

Differential dataflow sidesteps the cascade: every collection is signed differences indexed by time, so a retraction is a negative difference at a new time, and fixed-point iteration *re-runs only over changed differences*. Recursion is iteration in the (t, i) lattice — no special case [3]. The price: per-round differences persist until the frontier advances; arrangements make this affordable by sharing indexes across rounds.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Theoretical Analysis

### 5.1 Systems compared

| Approach | Delta order | State maintained | Recursion | Key idea |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classical first-order IVM | 1 | Base-derived counts | DRed / counting | Delta rules + derivation counts [7] |
| DBToaster (higher-order) | up to query degree | Viewlet hierarchy (delta views) | Limited | Viewlet transform; compile to C++ triggers [1][2] |
| F-IVM | up to query degree | Factorized view tree (keys to payloads) | Via rings | Triple-lock factorization + ring abstraction [4][5] |
| Differential dataflow | Arbitrary (lattice-indexed) | Arrangements (shared indexes) | Native (iteration lattice) | Differences over partially ordered times [3] |

DBToaster's headline numbers come from financial workloads: compiled triggers sustain *millions* of single-tuple updates per second where interpreted engines manage thousands [2]. Higher-order deltas beat first-order IVM by orders of magnitude on inequality joins and nested aggregates, with the cost-based optimizer avoiding pathological materialization [1].

F-IVM reports up to **100× faster** updates and **10,000× less memory** than DBToaster on factorized-friendly workloads (learning, matrix chains), attacking *representation* size, not just per-update work [5].

Differential dataflow's story is *generality at scale*: iterative graph analytics as ordinary dataflows, competitive with batch systems while supporting continuous updates — the basis of production streaming SQL [3][7].

### 5.2 Complexity landscape

| Query class | Single-tuple update time | Enumeration delay | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| q-hierarchical | O(1) amortized | O(1) | Factorized output; optimal [7] |
| Hierarchical, non-q | O(1) amortized | O(N) worst case | Listing large results dominates [7] |
| Acyclic non-hierarchical (3-path) | Sublinear to polynomial | Varies | Degree-based best-possible bounds [7] |
| Triangle counting | O(sqrt(N)) amortized | O(1) per triangle | Heavy/light partitioning [7] |
| Recursive (reachability) | Proportional to affected differences | — | Differential iteration; no DRed cascade [3] |
| MIN/MAX aggregates | O(log N) with heaps; else re-eval | — | Deletions may unmask [7] |

The q-hierarchical result is the first *optimality* theorem for IVM: constant-time maintenance, unimprovable asymptotically, for a large practical class — under functional dependencies, a majority of real analytic queries (76% of roughly 6000 in one industrial study) [7].

### 5.3 Theoretical synthesis

All three systems are one pattern: **choose a ring for multiplicities/payloads, choose a representation for state, propagate differences as homomorphisms.**

> **Theorem 3 (Unified IVM).** Let views be functions from keys to elements of a commutative ring R, with query operators as R-module homomorphisms. Delta rules derived by bilinearity maintain all views exactly under signed updates; materializing the delta hierarchy (DBToaster/F-IVM) or lattice-indexing differences (differential dataflow) preserves exactness, changing only the cost profile.

The systems differ in *where they pay*: DBToaster pays memory for the viewlet hierarchy, winning raw per-update speed on fixed queries [1]; F-IVM pays for the variable order, winning representation size [4]; differential dataflow pays for multiversioned arrangements, winning generality — recursion, retractions, ad-hoc dataflows in one runtime [3].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Materialization blowup.** The full viewlet hierarchy is exponential in query degree — O(2^k) delta views for a *k*-way join. Heuristics and the cost model mitigate this, but adversarial queries (dense inequality joins, deeply nested correlated subqueries) force fallback to lazy re-evaluation [1]. A workload-adaptive, learned materialization policy remains open.

**Non-invertible aggregates.** `MIN`/`MAX` under deletions need more than ring structure; the masked-value problem has no local algebraic fix. Heaps per group or subtractable reformulations add overhead or restrict query shape [7].

**Recursion with aggregation.** Differential dataflow handles monotone recursion, but non-monotone recursive aggregation (negative-edge shortest paths, recursion through `MIN`) lacks clean delta semantics. A general theory of incremental recursive aggregation is missing.

**Optimality beyond hierarchy.** Degree-based partitioning and dynamic Yannakakis-style plans give best-possible bounds for some non-hierarchical queries [7], but a complete dynamic-complexity characterization of conjunctive queries — the IVM analogue of the static dichotomy — is the field's central open problem.

**Distribution.** Delta views and arrangements become partitioned state across machines; the product-order time lattice must synchronize across workers. The interaction of *factorized* state with distribution is largely unexplored.

---

## 7. Conclusion

IVM has matured from delta-rule tricks into an algebraic discipline. The **viewlet transform** showed differences can be differentiated again [1][6]. The **ring abstraction** showed joins, aggregates, covariance matrices, and matrix products are one computation with different payloads [4][5]. **Differential dataflow** showed time itself can be partially ordered, making recursion and retraction ordinary [3]. With the modern complexity theory of hierarchical queries [7], engine builders get a complete toolkit: pick the ring, pick the representation, propagate the differences, let the algebra guarantee correctness. The frontier — non-invertible aggregates, recursive aggregation, dynamic dichotomies, distributed factorized state — is where the next decade of IVM research will be decided.
