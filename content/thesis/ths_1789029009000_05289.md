# Tropical Geometry and Phylogenetics: Tree Space as the Tropical Grassmannian

## Abstract

Tropical geometry — algebraic geometry over the min-plus semiring — converts polynomial equations into piecewise-linear combinatorics, and in doing so it places one of the central objects of computational biology on rigorous geometric footing: the space of phylogenetic trees. We survey how the tropical Grassmannian $\mathcal{G}_{2,n}$, the tropicalization of the classical Grassmannian of 2-planes, *is* the Billera–Holmes–Vogtmann (BHV) tree space, with the tropical Plücker relations recovering Buneman's four-point condition; how the subspace of equidistant trees (ultrametrics) forms a tropically convex tropical linear space; and how the tropical metric enables statistical tools — tropical principal component analysis, tropical density estimation, and tropical imputation — that respect the non-Euclidean, non-convex structure of tree space where classical methods fail. We present the structure and fundamental theorems of tropical geometry, the polyhedral combinatorics of tropical linear spaces, the algorithmic pipeline from distance matrices to tropical polytopes, and the empirical performance of these methods on gene-tree data. We close with open problems on complexity, geometry, and statistical theory.

##1 Intro

Phylogenetic inference rarely ends with a single tree. Bootstrapping, Bayesian posterior sampling, and multi-locus gene-tree analysis all produce *collections* of trees, and the natural statistical questions — What is the mean? What are the principal directions of variation? Is this tree an outlier? — demand a geometry for the space of trees in which these collections live. The Billera–Holmes–Vogtmann (BHV) tree space [4] provides that geometry: a piecewise-Euclidean cubical complex, glued from $(2n-5)!!$ orthants, that is a CAT(0) space with unique geodesics. Yet BHV geometry is awkward for statistics: the space is not classically convex, Fréchet means can be sticky, and the convex hull of three trees can have arbitrarily high dimension.

Tropical geometry offers a second, complementary geometry on the same object. Speyer showed that the tropical Grassmannian $\mathcal{G}_{2,n}$ — the tropicalization of the Grassmannian of 2-planes under the Plücker embedding — parametrizes exactly the phylogenetic trees studied by Billera, Holmes, and Vogtmann [2]. Under max-plus (or min-plus) arithmetic, the tropical Plücker relations are precisely Buneman's four-point condition for tree metrics. The equidistant trees (ultrametrics), which arise under the molecular-clock and multispecies-coalescent models, form a *tropically convex* subset: a tropical linear space on which the tropical metric is well-behaved and geodesics are intrinsic tropical line segments [7].

This piecewise-linear shadow of algebraic geometry thus does two things at once: it explains *why* tree space has the combinatorics it does (as the tropicalization of something classical), and it equips tree space with an intrinsic metric — the tropical metric — under which modern machine-learning methods (PCA, density estimation, imputation) can be transplanted to sets of trees [6][7]. This article develops the theory from the tropical semiring to tropical statistics, with proofs, algorithms, and empirical results.

##2 Background

### The tropical semiring

The **tropical semiring** is the set $\mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}$ equipped with two operations [1]:

- **tropical addition:** $x \oplus y := \min(x, y)$,
- **tropical multiplication:** $x \odot y := x + y$.

Here $\infty$ is the additive identity ($x \oplus \infty = x$) and $0$ is the multiplicative identity ($x \odot 0 = x$). There is no additive inverse: the tropical semiring is *idempotent*, since $x \oplus x = x$. An equivalent convention uses max-plus algebra; we follow the min-plus convention of [1] and note that all statements dualize.

A **tropical polynomial** is a tropical linear combination of tropical monomials:

$$f(x) = \bigoplus_{\alpha} c_\alpha \odot x_1^{\odot \alpha_1} \odot \cdots \odot x_d^{\odot \alpha_d} = \min_\alpha \big(c_\alpha + \alpha \cdot x\big).$$

Classically this is a *piecewise-linear convex function* $\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$. Its **tropical hypersurface** $\mathcal{T}(f)$ is the locus where the minimum is attained at least twice — the non-differentiability locus, a polyhedral complex of pure codimension one [3].

### From classical to tropical: valuations

Tropical varieties arise as *logarithmic degenerations* of classical varieties. Let $K$ be an algebraically closed field with a non-trivial valuation $\mathrm{val}: K^* \to \mathbb{R}$ (e.g., Puiseux series). For an ideal $I \subseteq K[x_1^{\pm 1}, \dots, x_d^{\pm 1}]$, the **tropical variety** is the closure of the coordinatewise valuation of the classical variety $V(I)$ [3]:

$$\mathrm{trop}(V(I)) = \overline{\{(\mathrm{val}(x_1), \dots, \mathrm{val}(x_d)) : x \in V(I)\}}.$$

The fundamental results of the subject are:

> **Theorem:** *(Fundamental Theorem of Tropical Geometry [3].)* For an ideal $I$, the following coincide: (i) the closure of the valuation image of $V(I)$; (ii) the intersection of all tropical hypersurfaces $\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{trop}(f))$ for $f \in I$; (iii) the polyhedral complex dual to the Gröbner complex of $I$.

> **Theorem:** *(Structure Theorem [3].)* $\mathrm{trop}(V(I))$ is the support of a *balanced* weighted polyhedral complex of pure dimension $\dim V(I)$, connected through codimension one.

The **balancing condition** — at each vertex, $\sum_i m_i u_i = 0$ for the primitive integer edge directions $u_i$ with weights $m_i$ — is the combinatorial residue of the classical variety's geometry.

### Phylogenetic trees: the classical picture

A phylogenetic tree on a leaf set $[n] = \{1, \dots, n\}$ is a weighted tree with labeled leaves. Its **tree metric** $w \in \mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}$ records pairwise leaf distances. Buneman's theorem characterizes tree metrics by the **four-point condition**: for all quadruples $i,j,k,\ell$, among the three sums

$$w_{ij} + w_{k\ell}, \qquad w_{ik} + w_{j\ell}, \qquad w_{i\ell} + w_{jk},$$

the *maximum is attained at least twice* [2].

Billera, Holmes, and Vogtmann constructed the **BHV tree space** $\mathcal{T}_n$ by taking one orthant $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{\,n-3}$ per fully resolved unrooted binary topology — $(2n-5)!!$ of them — parametrized by internal edge lengths, and gluing orthants along faces where edges contract to zero [4]. The resulting space is CAT(0): any two trees are joined by a unique geodesic, computable in $O(n^4)$ time (Owen–Provan).

An **equidistant tree** (rooted, ultrametric) is a tree in which every root-to-leaf path has the same total length — the molecular-clock hypothesis. Its distance vector satisfies the **three-point condition**: for all triples $i,j,k$, the two largest of $w_{ij}, w_{ik}, w_{jk}$ are equal [6]. Multispecies-coalescent models produce exactly such trees, making ultrametrics the natural object of study in phylogenomics [7].

##3 Methodology

Our methodology is the *tropicalization pipeline*: classical algebraic objects → valuations → polyhedral combinatorics → algorithms → statistical estimators. Concretely:

1. **Embed the phylogenetic model in an algebraic variety.** Tree metrics embed in the Grassmannian $\mathrm{Gr}(2,n)$ via Plücker coordinates; graphical models embed as algebraic varieties whose Newton polytopes control inference [5].
2. **Tropicalize.** Apply coordinatewise valuation to obtain a tropical variety — a balanced polyhedral complex whose cells are indexed by combinatorial types (tree topologies, Gröbner cones).
3. **Read off the combinatorics.** The cells of $\mathcal{G}_{2,n}$ correspond to trivalent trees; the tropical Plücker relations are the four-point condition [2].
4. **Equip with the tropical metric** $d_{\mathrm{tr}}$ on the tropical projective torus $\mathbb{R}^e / \mathbb{R}\mathbf{1}$, and define tropical analogues of classical estimators (Fréchet-type means, principal polytopes, kernel density estimators) as optimization problems over tropically convex sets [6][7].
5. **Compute.** Mixed-integer programming, tropical projection formulas, and MCMC samplers estimate these objects on gene-tree data, evaluated by Robinson–Foulds distance to ground truth and by outlier-detection accuracy.

The bridge between the two geometries of tree space is the following dictionary:

| Classical object | Tropical shadow |
|---|---|
| Grassmannian $\mathrm{Gr}(2,n)$ | Tropical Grassmannian $\mathcal{G}_{2,n}$ = BHV tree space |
| Plücker relation $p_{ij}p_{k\ell} - p_{ik}p_{j\ell} + p_{i\ell}p_{jk} = 0$ | Four-point condition: max of the three sums attained twice |
| Linear subspace | Tropical linear space (Bergman fan) |
| Ultrametrics (three-point condition) | Tropical linear space, tropically convex |
| Amoeba | Piecewise-linear polyhedral complex |
| Degree of a curve | Lattice length / balancing weights |

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The tropical Grassmannian and the space of trees

Let $\mathrm{Gr}(d,n)$ be the Grassmannian of $d$-planes in $K^n$, embedded in projective space via Plücker coordinates $p_J$ for $J \subseteq [n]$, $|J| = d$. The **tropical Grassmannian** $\mathcal{G}_{d,n}$ is the tropicalization of this embedding: a fan of dimension $d(n-d)$ in $\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{d}} / \mathbb{R}\mathbf{1}$ [2].

For $d = 2$, Speyer proved the landmark identification [2]:

> **Theorem:** *(Speyer [2].)* The tropical Grassmannian $\mathcal{G}_{2,n}$ parametrizes all tropical linear spaces of dimension 2 — that is, all phylogenetic trees. It equals the space of phylogenetic trees studied by Billera, Holmes, and Vogtmann.

The proof runs through the quadratic Plücker relations. Tropicalizing

$$p_{ij}p_{k\ell} - p_{ik}p_{j\ell} + p_{i\ell}p_{jk} = 0$$

replaces products by sums and the equation by the condition that the *minimum* (or maximum, in the max-plus convention) of the three resulting sums is attained at least twice. That condition is exactly Buneman's four-point condition for tree metrics. Hence a point of $\mathcal{G}_{2,n}$ is a tree metric, and the fan structure of the tropical Grassmannian refines BHV space by its Gröbner fan: each cone corresponds to a fixed tree topology, and crossing a wall performs a nearest-neighbor interchange [2][8].

Higher Grassmannians $\mathcal{G}_{d,n}$ then offer a natural *generalization* of tree space — a hierarchy of phylogenetic moduli spaces whose combinatorics (facets corresponding to binomial initial ideals of the Plücker ideal) remains largely unexplored [2].

### 4.2 Tropical linear spaces and ultrametrics

A **tropical linear space** is the tropicalization of a classical linear subspace — equivalently, the Bergman fan of a matroid [3]. The space of ultrametrics on $[n]$ is the tropical linear space obtained by tropicalizing the linear relations $x_{ij} - x_{ik} + x_{jk} = 0$; its points are exactly the distance vectors satisfying the three-point condition [1][6].

Crucially, this space is **tropically convex**: for any two ultrametrics $u, v$, the tropical line segment

$$[u, v]_{\mathrm{trop}} = \{\, a \odot u \oplus b \odot v : a, b \in \mathbb{R} \,\}$$

stays inside the space of ultrametrics. Monod, Lin, Kang, and Yoshida showed that this tropical line segment is *intrinsically geodesic* and unique [7]. This is the decisive advantage over BHV geometry: BHV convex hulls of three trees can have arbitrarily high dimension, while the tropical convex hull of $s$ points has dimension at most $s - 1$ [6]. Tropical convexity is what makes statistics on tree space tractable.

The **tropical metric** on $\mathbb{R}^e / \mathbb{R}\mathbf{1}$,

$$d_{\mathrm{tr}}(u, v) = \max_i (u_i - v_i) - \min_i (u_i - v_i),$$

is well-defined modulo the all-ones vector and turns the space of ultrametrics into a metric space in which nearest-point projections admit closed-form tropical formulas [6].

### 4.3 Tropical principal component analysis

Classical PCA is unavailable on tree space: the space is not Euclidean, not classically convex, and has no linear structure to project onto. Yoshida et al. introduced **tropical PCA** in 2019, with the definitive tree-space formulation by Page, Yoshida, and Zhang [6]:

> **Definition:** *(Tropical principal polytope [6].)* Let $S = \{u_1, \dots, u_m\}$ be a sample from the space of ultrametrics. The $(s-1)$-st order tropical principal polytope is the tropical polytope $\mathcal{P} = \mathrm{tconv}(D^{(1)}, \dots, D^{(s)})$ with $s$ vertices minimizing $\sum_i d_{\mathrm{tr}}(u_i, \Pi_{\mathcal{P}}(u_i))$, where $\Pi_{\mathcal{P}}$ is the tropical projection onto $\mathcal{P}$.

Two formulations exist: the **tropical principal linear space** (best-fit Stiefel tropical linear space of fixed dimension) and the **tropical principal polytope** (best-fit tropical polytope with a fixed number of vertices) [6]. The latter reduces to a mixed-integer linear program; Page et al. developed an MCMC sampler to estimate the vertices and proved a **tropical cell decomposition**: the principal components partition tree space into regions of fixed tree topology, giving the components a direct phylogenetic interpretation [6]. A projected-gradient-descent method using tropical subgradients now scales this to larger genomic datasets.

### 4.4 Tree topologies along a tropical line segment

Because the tropical line segment between two trees is intrinsically geodesic [7], a natural question arises: as one travels tropically from tree $T_1$ to tree $T_2$, which tree topologies are visited, and in what order? Yoshida and collaborators showed that the tropical line segment decomposes into finitely many cells, each labeled by a tree topology, with transitions governed by explicit combinatorial rules — a tropical analogue of the BHV geodesic's orthant-crossing sequence, but with the transitions controlled by the four-point condition rather than by edge contraction [8].

This has practical content: **tropical imputation** of missing trees and **tropical interpolation** between gene trees can be performed by walking the tropical line segment, and the visited topologies are themselves informative about the reticulation or incomplete lineage sorting separating the endpoints [8].

### 4.5 Tropical density estimation and statistical models

Weyenberg et al. built a kernel density estimator (KDE) on BHV tree space, but the BHV normalizing constant of the kernel varies with the center — an expensive obstruction. Yoshida, Miura, Barnhill, and Howe introduced a **tropical KDE** using the tropical symmetric distance $d_{\mathrm{tr}}$: because the space of ultrametrics is tropically convex with a homogeneous metric structure, the normalizing constant is *constant*, and bandwidth selection reduces to nearest-neighbor estimation [7]. The tropical KDE discovers outlier gene trees (e.g., horizontally transferred loci) in housekeeping-gene datasets, visualized through tropical PCA projections.

The statistical thread runs deeper. Pachter and Sturmfels showed that graphical models are algebraic varieties and that parametric inference — how MAP estimates and sum-product outputs vary with parameters — is governed by the tropicalization of the model, with the Newton polytope playing the central role [5]. Their work on the hidden Markov model and the general Markov model on a binary tree is the direct ancestor of the tropical phylogenetics program: inference functions are piecewise-constant on the chambers of a tropical variety [5].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### Proof sketch: $\mathcal{G}_{2,n}$ = tree space

We sketch Speyer's argument [2]. The Plücker ideal $I_{2,n}$ is generated by the quadrics $p_{ij}p_{k\ell} - p_{ik}p_{j\ell} + p_{i\ell}p_{jk}$. A weight vector $w \in \mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}$ lies in the tropical variety iff its initial ideal $\mathrm{in}_w(I_{2,n})$ contains no monomial. For a generic $w$ on a maximal cone, $\mathrm{in}_w(I_{2,n})$ is binomial; the condition that no monomial appears is exactly that, for every quadruple, the maximum of $w_{ij} + w_{k\ell}$, $w_{ik} + w_{j\ell}$, $w_{i\ell} + w_{jk}$ is attained at least twice — the four-point condition. The maximal cones are in bijection with trivalent trees on $[n]$, and the fan glues them exactly as BHV glues orthants. ∎

### Proof sketch: tropical convexity bounds the hull dimension

For $s$ points in $\mathbb{R}^e / \mathbb{R}\mathbf{1}$, the tropical convex hull is generated by tropical linear combinations $\bigoplus_i a_i \odot v^{(i)}$. Each coordinate of a tropical combination is a min-plus polynomial in the coefficients $a_i$, and the resulting polyhedral complex is the image of the $(s-1)$-simplex under a piecewise-linear map — hence of dimension at most $s-1$ [6]. Contrast with BHV geometry, where three points can span arbitrarily high-dimensional convex hulls.

### Algorithms

The core computational primitive is tropical projection. The following Python implements the tropical metric, tropical addition, and the tropical line segment between two ultrametrics, and verifies the four-point condition on a distance matrix:

```python
import itertools
import numpy as np

def trop_add(u, v):
    """Tropical (min-plus) sum of two vectors."""
    return np.minimum(u, v)

def trop_scalar_mul(a, u):
    """Tropical scalar multiplication: a . u = a + u."""
    return a + u

def tropical_distance(u, v):
    """Tropical metric on R^e / R1: max(u-v) - min(u-v)."""
    d = u - v
    return float(np.max(d) - np.min(d))

def tropical_line_segment(u, v, n=11):
    """Sample the tropical line segment [u, v]_trop = {a.u (+) b.v}."""
    seg = []
    for t in np.linspace(0, 1, n):
        # min-plus: a (.) u (+) b (.) v with a+b fixed is a 1-param family
        seg.append(trop_add(trop_scalar_mul(t, u),
                            trop_scalar_mul(1 - t, v)))
    return np.array(seg)

def four_point_condition(w, n):
    """Check Buneman's four-point condition for a distance dict w[(i,j)]."""
    for quad in itertools.combinations(range(n), 4):
        i, j, k, l = quad
        s = sorted([w[(i, j)] + w[(k, l)],
                    w[(i, k)] + w[(j, l)],
                    w[(i, l)] + w[(j, k)]])
        if not np.isclose(s[1], s[2]):
            return False, quad
    return True, None

# Example: an ultrametric on 4 leaves (rooted tree, equal root-leaf paths)
w = {(0,1): 2.0, (0,2): 4.0, (0,3): 4.0,
     (1,2): 4.0, (1,3): 4.0, (2,3): 2.0}
ok, bad = four_point_condition(w, 4)
print("four-point condition holds:", ok)
```

### Empirical results

Page, Yoshida, and Zhang applied tropical PCA to three empirical datasets — Apicomplexa genomes, African coelacanth genomes, and New York influenza hemagglutinin sequences [6]. Key findings:

- **Topology-aware decomposition.** The estimated tropical principal polytopes induce cell decompositions whose cells correspond to fixed tree topologies, so each principal component is interpretable as a *direction of topological variation* rather than an abstract axis [6].
- **Outlier detection.** The tropical KDE of [7] flags outlier gene trees in housekeeping-gene datasets; projecting onto the tropical principal polytope visualizes outliers as points far from the polytope in $d_{\mathrm{tr}}$.
- **Imputation accuracy.** Tropical-polytope imputation of held-out trees achieves lower mean Robinson–Foulds distance to ground truth than baseline BHV-based methods across a range of missing-data ratios [7].
- **Scalability.** The MCMC estimator of [6] handles hundreds of gene trees; projected gradient descent with tropical subgradients further reduces runtime on the Apicomplexa dataset.

##6 Limitations

1. **The molecular-clock restriction.** Tropical convexity — the property that makes everything work — holds for *equidistant* trees (ultrametrics), not for general BHV tree space [7]. Data violating the clock (rate heterogeneity, non-clocklike evolution) must be transformed or restricted, and the tropical metric is not the biologically natural metric for non-clocklike trees.
2. **No orthogonality or nestedness.** Tropical principal polytopes lack the nested, orthogonal structure of classical PCA: the 2-vertex polytope need not be contained in the 3-vertex polytope, and there is no spectral theorem behind the vertices [6].
3. **Computational hardness.** Exact tropical PCA is a mixed-integer program; the MCMC and projected-gradient estimators come with no global optimality guarantees, and tropical Gröbner computations scale poorly with $n$ [3][6].
4. **Two geometries, one space.** BHV geodesics and tropical line segments generally disagree. There is no canonical choice between the CAT(0) geometry and the tropical geometry of tree space, and statistical conclusions can depend on the choice [4][8].
5. **Statistical theory is young.** Consistency, rates of convergence, and valid confidence sets for tropical estimators are largely open; the constant normalizing constant of the tropical KDE is established computationally rather than by a general theorem [7].
6. **Unexplored higher Grassmannians.** $\mathcal{G}_{d,n}$ for $d \ge 3$ generalizes tree space, but its statistical meaning — what data would live there? — is unknown [2].

---

##7 Conclusion

Tropical geometry has given phylogenetics something it previously lacked: an *intrinsic algebraic explanation* of tree space. The BHV complex is not an ad hoc construction but the tropical Grassmannian $\mathcal{G}_{2,n}$, the four-point condition is the tropical Plücker relation, and the space of ultrametrics is a tropical linear space — tropically convex, metrized by $d_{\mathrm{tr}}$, and hospitable to PCA, density estimation, and imputation [2][6][7]. The pipeline from Pachter–Sturmfels parametric inference [5] through Speyer's Grassmannian [2] to Yoshida's tropical statistics [6][7] shows a single idea — replace $+$ by $\min$ and $\times$ by $+$ — reorganizing an entire applied field.

The frontier is now statistical and computational: proving consistency of tropical estimators, scaling them to thousands of loci, understanding higher tropical Grassmannians as phylogenetic moduli, and deciding, for each biological question, which of tree space's two geometries is the right one. The tropical shadow, at least, is here to stay.
