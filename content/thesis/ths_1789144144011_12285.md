# Certified Unsatisfiability: DRAT Proof Logging, LRAT Elaboration, and the Verified Checking of Conflict-Driven SAT Solvers

## Abstract

Modern conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL) solvers are among the most consequential artifacts of automated reasoning, routinely discharging formulas with millions of variables and clauses arising in hardware verification, software analysis, and combinatorial mathematics. Yet the sheer sophistication of a modern solver — inprocessing, phase saving, clause database reduction, aggressive restart schedules — is also a liability: every heuristic is a potential source of unsoundness, and a single bug can invalidate a published mathematical result. This thesis develops the theory and engineering of *certified unsatisfiability*: emitting, trimming, and independently checking clausal proofs in the **DRAT** (Delete Reverse Asymmetric Tautology) format, elaborating them into **LRAT** proofs annotated with unit-propagation hints, and verifying them with formally verified checkers. The result is a coherent account of how SAT solving earned the trust it needs for computer-assisted mathematics at the terabyte-proof scale.

## 1. Introduction

The Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) occupies a singular position in computer science: the first problem proven NP-complete, and the workhorse of formal verification, planning, cryptography, and combinatorial search. The practical story of SAT is a triumph of engineering over pessimism. Early DPLL solvers [1] of the 1960s handled a few hundred variables; modern conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL) solvers — Glucose, CaDiCaL, Kissat, MapleSAT — routinely solve industrial instances with millions of variables and tens of millions of clauses [2].

SAT solvers have become instruments of mathematical discovery: the Boolean Pythagorean triples result produced a 200-terabyte unsatisfiability proof [3]. But this raises an uncomfortable question: *how do we know the solver was right?* An unsatisfiability verdict is a universal claim over an exponential search space. The solver's internal search, with its inprocessing transformations, clause deletions, and heuristic branching, is far too complex for human audit, and SAT solver bugs are real and historically documented. A result that cannot be checked independently is not a proof; it is a press release.

Certified SAT solving resolves this tension. Instead of trusting the solver, we require it to *log* its reasoning as a clausal proof in a standardized format — today, the de facto standard **DRAT** format [4] — which an independent, simpler checker validates. Since the 2014 SAT Competition, DRAT has been the mandatory proof format for unsatisfiability claims [5]. The pipeline has evolved: the fast, unverified `drat-trim` tool [6] backward-checks and emits an **LRAT** proof annotated with hints [7], validated by a formally verified checker [8]. The trusted base shrinks from tens of thousands of lines of heuristic C++ to a few hundred lines of verified code.

Section 2 reviews the foundations: CNF, resolution, unit propagation, and CDCL with first-UIP learning, VSIDS, Luby restarts, and phase saving. Section 3 develops proof logging: the RUP and RAT rules, DRAT with deletion, and backward checking. Section 4 dives into the CDCL engine, restarts and phase saving, DRAT as generalized extended resolution, and the verified LRAT pipeline. Section 5 reports empirical results, Section 6 limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

> **Thesis statement.** Certified unsatisfiability is achieved not by verifying the solver, but by requiring the solver to produce a proof whose checking is simple enough to verify. The DRAT/LRAT pipeline reduces the trust required for a computer-assisted mathematical result to a mechanically checked kernel, at a checking cost comparable to the original solve time.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Propositional Logic, CNF, and Resolution

A SAT instance is a formula in conjunctive normal form (CNF): a conjunction of clauses, each a disjunction of literals. The standard input format is DIMACS CNF, where variables are positive integers, negated literals negative integers, clauses terminated by `0`, and a header `p cnf n m` declares variable and clause counts [4].

The *resolution rule* is the fundamental inference step:

> **Theorem (Resolution soundness).** From clauses $C \lor x$ and $D \lor \lnot x$ one may infer the *resolvent* $C \lor D$; the resolvent is a logical consequence of its parents.

Resolution is refutationally complete: every unsatisfiable formula has a resolution proof deriving the empty clause $\bot$. Exponential lower bounds (e.g., for the pigeonhole principle) motivate stronger proof systems.

**Unit propagation** (BCP) is the atomic inference of every SAT solver: if all literals of a clause but one are falsified, the remaining literal is *forced*. Nearly all practical proof logging is built on unit propagation as the checkable primitive.

### 2.2 From DPLL to CDCL

DPLL [1] performs depth-first backtracking search: branch, propagate, backtrack on conflict. Conflict-driven clause learning, introduced in GRASP [9] and industrialized in Chaff [10], adds three transformative ideas:

1. **Conflict analysis.** On conflict, the solver analyzes the *implication graph* and derives a *learned clause* via the *first unique implication point* (first UIP) scheme; backjumping makes it unit, forcing progress.
2. **Non-chronological backtracking.** The solver jumps directly to the second-highest decision level in the learned clause, pruning vast search regions.
3. **Clause database management.** Periodic deletion of low-quality learned clauses (modern solvers use the *LBD* metric [11]) keeps unit propagation fast.

Each learned clause is a resolution consequence of the input, so the learned-clause trace is in principle a resolution proof — but naive logging produces proofs too large to check. DRAT and backward checking (Section 3) make the pipeline practical.

### 2.3 Branching Heuristics: VSIDS

**VSIDS** [10] maintains an *activity* score per variable, bumped when the variable appears in a learned clause and periodically decayed. Branching selects the highest-activity unassigned variable — a *conflict-focused* intensification strategy retained by every competitive solver [2].

### 2.4 Restarts and Phase Saving

**Restarts** abandon the search tree and restart from the top, retaining learned clauses. The *Luby sequence* (1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 4, …) [12] is optimal up to constants for Las Vegas algorithms with unknown runtime distributions; modern solvers use dynamic policies, e.g., restarting when recent LBD averages degrade [11].

**Phase saving** [13] caches each variable's last polarity; post-restart branching assigns the saved phase, recovering most of the abandoned trail's progress and making frequent restarts nearly free.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Proof-Logging Problem

A solver reporting `UNSAT` must produce evidence. Logging the full resolution DAG fails twice over: modern *inprocessing* (variable elimination, subsumption, blocked-clause elimination) uses non-resolution steps preserving only satisfiability, and the antecedent graph of a large solve is enormous.

The community's answer is **clausal proof logging**: the solver emits a sequence of clause *additions* and *deletions*, each addition required to preserve satisfiability in polynomially checkable fashion. The checker maintains an *active multiset* of clauses — initialized to the input, modified by the proof — with the invariant that it is *at least as satisfiable* as the input. If the proof ends by adding the empty clause, the input is unsatisfiable [5].

### 3.2 RUP: Reverse Unit Propagation

The weaker DRAT addition rule is **RUP** (Reverse Unit Propagation), originating in BerkMin's proof traces [14]:

> **Definition (RUP).** A clause $C$ has the RUP property with respect to $F$ if unit-propagating $F \land \lnot C$ derives a conflict.

Every CDCL learned clause is RUP with respect to the clauses present when learned — conflict analysis literally performs this derivation. RUP checking is polynomial, and RUP already captures all of CDCL's learning.

### 3.3 RAT: Resolution Asymmetric Tautologies

RUP cannot express inprocessing steps — notably *blocked clause* additions — that preserve satisfiability without being logical consequences. The **RAT** rule [15] generalizes RUP:

> **Definition (RAT).** A clause $C = l \lor C'$ has the RAT property with respect to $F$ on pivot $l$ if for *every* clause $D \lor \lnot l \in F$, the resolvent $C' \lor D$ has the RUP property with respect to $F$.

Adding $C$ is safe: any model of $F$ falsifying $C$ is repaired by flipping $l$ to true, and the RAT condition guarantees the flipped assignment still satisfies $F$. RAT strictly generalizes RUP: DRAT is a *generalization of extended resolution* [4], enabling short proofs where resolution needs exponential size.

### 3.4 The DRAT Format and Backward Checking

A **DRAT proof** is a sequence of clause additions (each required to be RAT, covering RUP) and deletions (prefixed `d`, unchecked — deletion trivially preserves the satisfiability invariant) [4]. Deletion steps are essential engineering: they let the checker discard clauses the solver deleted, keeping each unit-propagation check cheap [6].

Forward checking is expensive — every addition needs a RAT scan over the active database. **Backward checking** [14, 16, 6] works *backwards* from the empty clause:

1. Mark the empty clause as *needed*.
2. Process additions in reverse; a step matters only if its clause was marked needed.
3. For relevant steps, verify the RAT/RUP property and mark as needed the clauses unit propagation actually used (the *antecedents*).
4. Skip irrelevant steps entirely.

Backward checking simultaneously *validates* and *trims*: the output is a minimal proof core, typically far smaller than the raw solver log. DRAT-trim also emits the dependency information used to elaborate the proof into **LRAT** [7].

### 3.5 LRAT Elaboration and Verified Checking

DRAT checking requires the checker to *rediscover* unit-propagation derivations. **LRAT** augments each addition with *hints* — the clause IDs used in the derivation — making checking a deterministic linear-time replay with no search.

This enables the modern trust architecture [5, 7, 8]:

| Stage | Tool | Trust status |
|---|---|---|
| Solving + DRAT emission | CDCL solver (Kissat, CaDiCaL, …) | Untrusted |
| Backward check + LRAT elaboration | `drat-trim` (unverified C) | Untrusted; output re-checked |
| LRAT validation | Verified checker (mechanically proved) | Trusted kernel |

Because the LRAT proof is *independently re-checked*, `drat-trim` bugs can only reject valid proofs, never produce false UNSAT verdicts. The trusted base is the verified LRAT checker — the *de Bruijn criterion* applied to SAT: trust the checker, not the prover [7, 8].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The CDCL Engine: Implication Graphs, First-UIP Learning, and VSIDS

The CDCL main loop in stylized form:

```python
def cdcl(F):
    trail, level = [], 0
    while True:
        conflict = unit_propagate(F, trail)
        if conflict is not None:
            if level == 0:
                emit_drat_empty_clause(); return UNSAT
            learnt, bt = analyze_conflict(conflict)  # first-UIP
            F.add(learnt); emit_drat_addition(learnt) # RUP by construction
            backjump(bt); bump_vsids(learnt)
        elif all_assigned(F, trail):
            return SAT
        else:
            level += 1
            v = select_vsids_unassigned()
            assign(v, phase_saving[v]); decay_activities()
```

Conflict analysis walks the implication graph from the conflict clause, resolving away current-level literals until one remains — the *first unique implication point*. The learned clause is *asserting*: after backjumping it is unit and propagates immediately. Each analysis step is a sound resolution, so the learned clause is a logical consequence of the active formula, hence RUP [2].

VSIDS is invisible to proof logging — the checker never sees branching decisions — which is precisely why clausal logging is robust: *any* branching heuristic is certified by the clauses it produces.

### 4.2 Restarts, Phase Saving, and the Luby Schedule

Restarts add *no* proof steps: every learned clause was already emitted as a DRAT addition. Clausal logging certifies the clause-database evolution, not the search tree.

The **Luby sequence** [12] gives a universal restart schedule

$$t_i \in \{1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 4, 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 4, 8, \dots\}$$

whose expected runtime is within a logarithmic factor of the optimal fixed-cutoff strategy for Las Vegas algorithms with unknown runtime distributions. Glucose-style dynamic policies trigger restarts when recent LBD averages degrade [11].

**Phase saving** [13] is proof-theoretically invisible to DRAT: it changes only *which* clauses get learned, never their validity.

> **Theorem (Restart transparency).** Inserting restart events (trail resets preserving the clause database) into a CDCL solver's execution does not affect the validity of its emitted DRAT proof, because every addition was RAT with respect to the database at emission time, and restarts do not modify the database.

### 4.3 DRAT as Generalized Extended Resolution

DRAT's proof-theoretic content is best seen through *extended resolution* (ER), which adds the *extension rule*: introduce a fresh variable $x$ defined as a Boolean function of existing variables via defining clauses. ER admits polynomial-size pigeonhole-principle proofs, exponentially separating it from resolution.

DRAT generalizes ER [4]: the RAT rule simulates extension — defining clauses for $x \leftrightarrow (a \land b)$ are each RAT — and every DRAT proof converts to an ER proof with polynomial overhead. DRAT is thus *as powerful as extended resolution* up to polynomial factors: strong enough for all practical inprocessing, yet polynomially checkable per step.

The clausal proof-system hierarchy:

$$\text{Resolution} \subsetneq \text{RUP} \subseteq \text{DRAT} \equiv_p \text{Extended Resolution}$$

RUP captures CDCL learning without inprocessing; DRAT adds the RAT steps needed for preprocessing/inprocessing. The binary DRAT format (variable-byte encoded literals) compresses proofs roughly threefold over text [4].

A concrete taste of the format — logging the learned clause $(x_{129} \lor \lnot x_{63} \lor x_{8191})$ and deleting an obsolete clause:

```
129 -63 8191 0
d -63 -8193 0
```

each addition verified by reverse unit propagation against the active database [4].

### 4.4 The Verified Pipeline: From DRAT-trim to Mechanically Checked LRAT

The standard certification pipeline [5, 7]:

1. **Solve and log.** The CDCL solver emits a DRAT proof (additions for learned/inprocessing clauses, deletions mirroring clause-database reductions).
2. **Backward check and elaborate.** `drat-trim` [6] validates backwards from $\bot$, trims irrelevant steps, extracts the unsatisfiable core, and emits an **LRAT** proof with unit-propagation hints.
3. **Verify.** A formally verified LRAT checker — soundness mechanically proved in a proof assistant — replays the hints deterministically [7, 8].

Cruz-Filipe et al. [7] developed a verified RAT checker in ACL2; the elaborator–checker split keeps unverified `drat-trim` *outside* the trusted base. Later verified checkers in Coq and Isabelle/HOL achieved competitive performance [8]. The methodology extends beyond SAT: DRAT-style certificates have been adapted to SAT modulo monotonic theories [17] and to SAT+CAS combinations settling open problems in quantum foundations [18].

```haskell
-- Sketch of a verified LRAT checker core (in the style of [7,8])
checkLRAT :: Formula -> LRATProof -> Bool
checkLRAT f proof = go (initDB f) proof
  where
    go db []                          = False   -- must end with empty clause
    go db (Delete c : rest)           = go (remove c db) rest
    go db (AddRUP c hints : rest)     =
        unitPropagatesToConflict db hints (negate c)
        && go (insert c db) rest
    go db (AddRAT c pivot rhints : rest) =
        all (\(d, h) -> unitPropagatesToConflict db h
                         (negate (resolve c d pivot)))
            (zip (clausesWithNeg pivot db) rhints)
        && go (insert c db) rest
```

The checker's soundness theorem: *if `checkLRAT f proof = True` and the final addition is the empty clause, then `f` is unsatisfiable* — proved by induction over proof steps, maintaining the invariant that the active database is equisatisfiable-or-stronger than the input.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Cost of Certification

DRAT-trim's checking time is typically comparable to solver runtime, with similar memory consumption [6] — a dramatic improvement over resolution-based checkers whose antecedent graphs could exhaust memory. LRAT elaboration adds a modest constant factor; final verified checking is essentially linear in proof size [7].

The SAT Competition has required DRAT proofs for UNSAT results since 2014 [5] — a decade-long large-scale experiment with thousands of industrial benchmarks certified annually. The format's dominance ("the only supported proof format … since 2014" [5]) reflects rare consensus in a competitive community.

### 5.2 Landmark: The Boolean Pythagorean Triples Proof

The celebrated application: can the naturals be 2-colored with no monochromatic Pythagorean triple $a^2+b^2=c^2$? Heule, Kullmann, and Marek [3] encoded $\{1,\dots,7824\}$ (~3,700 variables, ~30,000 clauses), solved it via Cube-and-Conquer, and produced a **200-terabyte DRAT proof** — establishing 7825 as the threshold. Validated with a verified checker, it is among the largest mechanically checked proofs ever built; without DRAT logging, the result would rest on trusting ~30,000 CPU-hours of heuristic search.

### 5.3 Proof Complexity Perspective

DRAT polynomially simulates extended resolution [4], so short proofs in strong systems can actually be found and logged — e.g., polynomial DRAT proofs of the pigeonhole principle via RAT-based variable introductions. No *known* complexity barrier separates DRAT from the strongest feasible proof systems.

| Proof system | Captures | Check cost per step | Simulates ER? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tree resolution | DPLL | Polynomial | No |
| RUP (clausal) | CDCL learning | Unit propagation | No |
| DRAT (RUP+RAT+deletion) | CDCL + inprocessing | Polynomial | Yes |
| LRAT | DRAT + hints | Linear replay | Yes |

## 6. Limitations

**SAT instances have no certificates.** DRAT certifies only UNSAT verdicts; SAT claims are witnessed by the checkable assignment itself.

**Proof size.** The 200 TB Pythagorean-triples proof [3] shows checkable proofs can be astronomically large; compression and shorter-proof emission remain active research [5].

**The elaborator is unverified.** `drat-trim` bugs can only *reject* valid proofs, never *accept* invalid ones [7] — but rejecting a terabyte proof after days of checking wastes enormous compute. Verified elaborators remain an open challenge.

**Expressiveness.** DRAT captures all *currently used* SAT techniques [6], but SMT, QBF, and pseudo-Boolean solving need extended formats ([17], QRAT), each requiring its own verification story.

**Trust is concentrated, not eliminated.** The verified checker still depends on the proof assistant's kernel, the hardware, and the DIMACS parser — a small auditable kernel, but not zero.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Certified SAT solving runs from a simple observation — *a solver that cannot explain itself cannot be trusted with mathematics* — to a mature discipline. CDCL solvers are extraordinarily effective search procedures; DRAT logging captures everything they do, including inprocessing, in a polynomially checkable artifact; backward checking trims the log to its core; LRAT elaboration adds machine-checkable hints; and verified checkers shrink the trusted base to a mechanically proved kernel.

Computer-assisted results like the Boolean Pythagorean triples theorem [3] now carry the epistemic status of traditionally proved theorems: checkable by an independent party with a minimal verifier. Theoretically, DRAT matches extended resolution in power while remaining practical. Open frontiers include verified elaborators, terabyte-scale compression, and extending certification to SMT, QBF, and beyond: in automated reasoning, *trust is a deliverable*, and the DRAT/LRAT pipeline is how we manufacture it.
