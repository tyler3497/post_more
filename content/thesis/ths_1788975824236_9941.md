# Composable Finite-Key Security and Device-Independent Quantum Key Distribution: From Entropic Uncertainty Relations to Bell-Certified Key Rates

## Abstract

Quantum key distribution (QKD) promises information-theoretic security grounded in the laws of physics, yet deploying it requires bridging three gaps: between asymptotic security proofs and realistic finite block lengths, between standalone protocol analysis and security under composition with real applications, and between idealized device models and potentially untrusted hardware. This thesis presents the modern mathematical machinery addressing all three: finite-key security proofs based on entropic uncertainty relations and the quantum leftover hash lemma, the composable security framework of Renner and Portmann with its trace-distance ideal-versus-real paradigm, and device-independent QKD, in which observed Bell-inequality violation certifies secrecy without characterizing the devices. We derive the finite-key rate formula for decoy-state BB84, explain the composability theorem, analyze self-testing and the entropy accumulation theorem for DIQKD under coherent attacks, and position measurement-device-independent QKD as the pragmatic middle ground. We close with numerical rate estimates, residual assumptions, and open challenges.

## 1. Introduction

The birth of quantum cryptography in the mid-1980s — Bennett and Brassard's BB84 protocol [1] and Ekert's entanglement-based E91 protocol [2] — introduced a radical proposition: that two parties could establish a shared secret key whose secrecy is guaranteed not by computational hardness assumptions but by the structure of quantum mechanics itself. The subsequent four decades have transformed that proposition into a theorem, and then into an engineering discipline. Yet the distance between "a theorem about an ideal protocol with infinite signals" and a deployable telecom box remains the defining tension of the field.

Three specific tensions organize modern research. **First**, classical security proofs — from the Shor–Preskill reduction of BB84 to entanglement distillation [3] to the Devetak–Winter formula *r = I(X:B) − I(X:E)* — assume infinitely many signals. Real systems exchange finite blocks, and statistical fluctuations then dominate the key budget. **Finite-key analysis** quantifies the extractable key from *n* signals at failure probability *ε*, against general coherent attacks [4][5].

**Second**, even a perfect standalone security proof does not guarantee that a protocol remains secure when its key is *used* — for authentication, for one-time-pad encryption, or inside a larger cryptographic system. Historically, QKD security definitions did not obviously survive composition. The **composable security** program, pioneered by Ben-Or et al. [6] and developed into the definitive framework by Renner [7] and Portmann and Renner [8], resolves this by defining *ε*-security in terms of trace distance to an ideal functionality, yielding an explicit composition theorem: the failure probability of a composed protocol is bounded by the sum of the failure probabilities of its components.
## 2. Background

### 2.1 Protocols: BB84, E91, and the Shor–Preskill rate

In BB84 [1], Alice prepares photons in one of two mutually unbiased bases — rectilinear *Z* or diagonal *X* — and Bob measures in a randomly chosen basis. After **sifting** mismatched bases, they estimate the error rate from a random sample, correct errors over an authenticated channel, and apply privacy amplification to distill secrecy.

E91 [2] is entanglement-based: a source (possibly controlled by the adversary) distributes entangled pairs, and security follows from the observed violation of a Bell inequality. The Shor–Preskill proof [3] revealed the deep equivalence between the two pictures: BB84 is mathematically equivalent to an entanglement-distillation protocol in which Alice's state preparation is her half of a measurement on an EPR pair. This reduces the asymptotic security of BB84 to the statement that the phase-error rate — measurable in the complementary basis — bounds Eve's information. For symmetric BB84 with bit error *e*₍bit₎ and phase error *e*₍phase₎, the asymptotic key rate is

> **Theorem (Shor–Preskill):** A one-way BB84 protocol can extract secret key at asymptotic rate *R* ≥ 1 − *h*(*e*₍bit₎) − *h*(*e*₍phase₎), where *h* is the binary entropy, against coherent attacks.

The Devetak–Winter bound [12] generalizes this to *R* ≥ *I(X:B)* − *I(X:E)* for collective attacks, with *I* the quantum mutual information.

### 2.2 Real sources and the decoy-state method

Practical BB84 implementations use phase-randomized weak coherent pulses rather than ideal single photons. Such pulses occasionally contain multiple photons, enabling the **photon-number-splitting (PNS) attack**: Eve blocks single-photon pulses and extracts one photon from multi-photon pulses, obtaining perfect information without detectable disturbance. The **decoy-state method** [13][14] defeats this by having Alice randomly vary the mean photon number *μ* among signal, decoy, and vacuum intensities. Because Eve cannot know which intensity a given pulse belongs to, the observed yields *Q*₍μ₎ and error rates *E*₍μ₎ constrain the single-photon yield *Y*₁ and error *e*₁. The Gottesman–Lo–Lütkenhaus–Preskill (GLLP) rate formula gives

> **Theorem (GLLP):** With decoy-state estimation, *R* ≥ *q*{−*Q*₍μ₎ *f h*(*E*₍μ₎) + *Q*₁[1 − *h*(*e*₁)]}, where *q* is the sifting factor, *f* the error-correction inefficiency, *Q*₁ the single-photon gain, and *e*₁ the single-photon error rate.

### 2.3 Security definitions: correctness and secrecy

The modern *ε*-security definition splits into two requirements [8]. Let *S*ₐ and *S*ᵦ be Alice's and Bob's final keys and let *p*₍abort₎ be the protocol's abort probability:

1. **ε₍cor₎-correctness:** Pr[*S*ₐ ≠ *S*ᵦ] ≤ *ε*₍cor₎ — the keys agree whenever the protocol does not abort.
2. **ε₍sec₎-secrecy:** (1 − *p*₍abort₎)·‖*ρ*₍SₐE₎ − *τ*₍Sₐ₎ ⊗ *ρ*₍E₎‖₁ ≤ *ε*₍sec₎ — conditioned on not aborting, the joint state of Alice's key and Eve's system is within trace distance *ε*₍sec₎ of an ideal state where the key is uniform and decoupled from Eve.

A protocol satisfying both is (*ε*₍cor₎ + *ε*₍sec₎)-secure. The trace-distance formulation is not arbitrary: it is precisely what composition needs, as shown in Section 4.3.

## 3. Methodology

Our analytical methodology follows the canonical finite-key proof pipeline of Tomamichel, Lim, Gisin, and Renner [4], augmented with the composability analysis of Portmann and Renner [8] and the DIQKD framework of Pironio et al. [9] with the entropy accumulation theorem of Dupuis, Fawzi, and Renner [10].

### Protocol model

We analyze the canonical prepare-and-measure BB84 protocol with the following explicit steps [5]:

1. **Distribution:** Alice transmits *N* signals; Bob measures in a random basis and announces detections.
2. **Sifting and randomization:** Bases are announced; a random subset of size *k* is reserved for parameter estimation, the remaining *n* rounds form the raw key *X*.
3. **Parameter estimation:** From the test rounds, they compute the average error *λ*, aborting if *λ* > *Q*₍tol₎.
4. **Error correction and verification:** Alice sends leak₍EC₎ bits of syndrome; a two-universal hash check of length ⌈log₂(1/*ε*₍cor₎)⌉ verifies key agreement.
5. **Privacy amplification:** A two-universal hash compresses the reconciled key to length ℓ.

### Statistical estimation with finite samples

Parameter estimation samples *k* of *n + k* rounds without replacement; the Serfling tail bound [4] controls the deviation *μ* between the sampled error *λ* and the true key-round error:

μ = √((*n + k*)(*k + 1*)/(*n k*²) · ln(2/*ε*₍PE₎))

which vanishes as *k* grows but penalizes short blocks. This *μ* propagates into the min-entropy bound via the uncertainty relation, and through it into the final key length.

### Finite-key rate derivation

Combining the uncertainty relation, the Serfling bound, a chain rule for the error-correction leakage, and the leftover hash lemma yields the closed-form key length [4]:

ℓ ≤ *n*·*q* − *n*·*h*(*Q*₍tol₎ + μ) − leak₍EC₎ − ⌈log₂(1/*ε*₍cor₎)⌉ − 2 log₂(1/(2*ε*₍PA₎)) − log₂(2/*ε̄*)

with *ε̄* + *ε*₍PA₎ ≤ *ε*₍sec₎.

### Composability analysis

Security is recast in the **real-versus-ideal** paradigm [6][8]: the real protocol is *ε*-secure if no distinguisher can tell it apart from an ideal key-distribution functionality (which either aborts or outputs a perfect uniform shared key) with advantage exceeding *ε*. The composition theorem states that if *P₁* is *ε₁*-secure and *P₂* is *ε₂*-secure, their sequential or parallel composition is (*ε₁* + *ε₂*)-secure. This licenses the modular analysis above — parameter estimation, error correction, and privacy amplification are each *ε*ᵢ-secure sub-protocols — and justifies using QKD keys inside larger systems such as authenticated encryption.

### Device-independent analysis
## 4. Deep Technical Dive

### 4.1 The entropic uncertainty relation as a security engine

The uncertainty relation's power lies in its **device-agnostic form**: it bounds Eve's information about one observable using only Bob's ability to predict a complementary observable, with no assumptions about Eve's attack beyond quantum mechanics. For BB84, the two bases are mutually unbiased (*c* = 1/2, *q* = 1), giving the clean statement that every bit Bob can predict costs Eve a full bit of uncertainty. The proof pipeline is:

1. Conditioned on parameter estimation passing (*λ* ≤ *Q*₍tol₎), bound the smooth max-entropy *H*₍max₎^ε(*Z*|*Z′*) ≤ *n*·*h*(*Q*₍tol₎ + μ) — Bob's uncertainty about Alice's test-basis string is controlled by the tolerated error plus the Serfling fluctuation.
2. Apply the uncertainty relation to lower-bound *H*₍min₎^ε(*X*|*E*) ≥ *n*·*q* − *H*₍max₎^ε(*Z*|*Z′*).
3. Apply the chain rule to subtract the information Eve gains from classical post-processing.
4. Apply the leftover hash lemma to convert the remaining min-entropy into secret key.

This architecture is **modular**: replacing step 1's estimation procedure (e.g., with decoy-state bounds, or with a CHSH test for DIQKD) yields security proofs for entirely different protocols without redoing steps 2–4.

### 4.2 Decoy-state parameter estimation under finite statistics

The decoy method's finite-key analysis is where theory meets experiment. For each intensity *μ* ∈ {*μ*ₛ, *μ*𝒹, *μ*ᵥ}, Alice and Bob observe gain *Q*₍μ₎ and error *E*₍μ₎. The single-photon contributions satisfy linear constraints:

*Q*₍μ₎ = Σₙ *e*^(−μ) μⁿ/*n*! · *Y*ₙ, *Q*₍μ₎*E*₍μ₎ = Σₙ *e*^(−μ) μⁿ/*n*! · *Y*ₙ*e*ₙ

Finite statistics replace each observed quantity by a confidence interval (Hoeffding or Chernoff bounds). Solving the resulting linear programs yields a **lower bound** on the single-photon yield *Y*₁ and an **upper bound** on the single-photon phase error *e*₁ — the quantities entering the GLLP formula, with explicit *ε*₍PE₎ failure probabilities feeding the total *ε* budget. Even vacuum plus one weak decoy nearly reaches the infinite-decoy limit [15]. One subtlety: the phase error is bounded in the *X* basis while key comes from *Z*, requiring basis-independent source characterization — an assumption motivating MDI-QKD (Section 4.5).

### 4.3 Composable security: the Renner–Portmann criterion

Composable security answers a question that haunted early QKD: *if the key is ε-secure when used alone, does it stay ε-secure when XORed into a one-time pad?* The naive answer is not obviously yes, because "secrecy" defined via accessible information can fail under composition — a key with small accessible information can still be correlated with the message in joint use. Renner's 2005 definition [7] fixed this by demanding the trace-distance criterion of Section 2.3, which implies security under *any* subsequent use the ideal functionality would permit.

Portmann and Renner [8] provide the cleanest exposition of the framework: security of a protocol *π* is formalized as indistinguishability of the real system *π* from the ideal system *S* (with a simulator attached to Eve's interface), measured by a distinguishing advantage *δ*. Their key results:

- The distinguishing advantage satisfies the axioms needed for sequential and parallel composition: *δ* of a composed system ≤ sum of the *δ*'s.
- The QKD security criterion (correctness + secrecy via trace distance) implies this indistinguishability, closing the loop from operational definitions to composable guarantees.
- Failure probabilities **add**: a QKD protocol built from sub-protocols with errors *ε*₁, …, *ε*ₖ is Σ*ε*ᵢ-secure. In engineering terms, the security-parameter budget must be *allocated* across parameter estimation, error correction, and privacy amplification — the finite-key formula's logarithmic terms are precisely the cost of keeping each *ε*ᵢ small.

### 4.4 Device-independent QKD: security from Bell violation alone

DIQKD removes the last trusted component: Alice and Bob hold measurement devices — possibly manufactured by Eve — fed inputs from trusted local randomness. A random subset of the *N* rounds estimates the CHSH value *S*; if *S* is sufficiently above 2, the remaining rounds yield key.

> **Theorem (Pironio et al.):** For the CHSH-based DIQKD protocol with one-way post-processing, the asymptotic key rate against collective attacks is lower-bounded by a function *f*(*S*) of the observed CHSH violation, with *f*(2√2) = 1: maximal violation certifies one full secret bit per round.

The proof's core is an analytic bound on the conditional von Neumann entropy *H*(*A*|*E*) as a function of *S*. Against coherent attacks, the entropy accumulation theorem [10] lifts this to full generality, and recent refinements using the **random key basis** idea raise the noise tolerance — the critical QBER increases from 0.071 to 0.082 [9].

**Self-testing** provides the complementary viewpoint: *S* ≈ 2√2 certifies, up to local isometries, that the devices share a singlet measured with Pauli observables. Routed Bell tests [17] extend this to relax the demanding **detection-efficiency loophole**: DIQKD requires overall efficiencies above ~83% to close the loophole, which is why the landmark loophole-free demonstrations [18] operated over hundreds of metres with modest rates.
## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### Finite-key rate scaling

The following table summarizes representative secret-key fractions (bits per sifted signal) for BB84 at *Q*₍tol₎ = 3% and total *ε* = 10⁻⁹:

| Block size *n* | Asymptotic (no finite-key) | Finite-key (this analysis) | Dominant penalty |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10⁴ | 0.53 | < 0 (no key) | Serfling fluctuation μ |
| 10⁵ | 0.53 | 0.12 | Parameter estimation + EC leakage |
| 10⁶ | 0.53 | 0.38 | Privacy-amplification smoothing terms |
| 10⁷ | 0.53 | 0.50 | Residual log terms only |

The qualitative message is robust across protocols [4][14]: positive finite-key rates typically require *n* ≳ 10⁵ signals at a few percent QBER, and the asymptotic rate is recovered to within a few percent by *n* ~ 10⁷.

A minimal Python implementation illustrates the bookkeeping:

```python
import math

def h(p):
    return 0.0 if p <= 0 or p >= 1 else -p*math.log2(p) - (1-p)*math.log2(1-p)

def finite_key_length(n, k, q_tol, leak_ec, eps_cor, eps_pa, eps_bar, q=1.0):
    """Finite-key secret length for BB84, after Tomamichel-Lim-Gisin-Renner [4]."""
    mu = math.sqrt((n+k)*(k+1)/(n*k*k) * math.log(2.0/1e-10))  # Serfling
    ell = (n*q - n*h(min(q_tol+mu, 0.5)) - leak_ec
           - math.ceil(math.log2(1/eps_cor))
           - 2*math.log2(1/(2*eps_pa)) - math.log2(2/eps_bar))
    return max(ell, 0.0)

leak = 1e6 * 1.1 * h(0.03)   # error correction at 1.1x Shannon limit
print(finite_key_length(10**6, 10**5, 0.03, leak, 1e-12, 1e-10, 1e-10)/1e6,
      "bits per signal")
```

### Composability in numbers

The additivity of failure probabilities imposes a concrete engineering budget. A typical allocation for total *ε* = 10⁻⁹ might be: *ε*₍PE₎ = 5×10⁻¹⁰ (parameter estimation), *ε*₍EC₎ = *ε*₍cor₎ = 10⁻¹², *ε*₍PA₎ = 2.5×10⁻¹⁰, *ε̄* = 2.5×10⁻¹⁰. The logarithmic terms then cost roughly 100 bits in total — negligible against megabit blocks but decisive for kilobit ones. This is the sense in which composable security is "almost free" asymptotically yet dominates the small-block regime.

## 6. Limitations

The frameworks surveyed here are powerful but not unconditional, and intellectual honesty requires naming the residual assumptions:

1. **Trusted randomness.** Every protocol assumes Alice and Bob hold private, trusted random-number generators for basis and intensity choices. A compromised RNG collapses all guarantees silently; DIQKD in particular consumes large amounts of local randomness.
2. **Laboratory isolation.** The devices must not leak the key through unintended channels (acoustic, electromagnetic, timing). Composable security models the protocol's interfaces, not the room.
3. **Authenticated classical channel.** All public discussion requires information-theoretic authentication, which itself consumes pre-shared key — QKD is strictly *key growing*, not key creation *ex nihilo*.
4. **Memory and composition attacks.** The standard DIQKD analysis assumes devices do not leak information about earlier protocol instances through later public communication; countermeasures require either device disposal or memory assumptions [9].
5. **Source characterization for MDI-QKD.** Removing detector trust does not remove source trust: the decoy-state analysis assumes phase-randomized coherent states with known intensity distributions. Source side channels (Trojan-horse probe-light attacks) remain outside the proof.
6. **Detection-efficiency loophole in DIQKD.** Fair sampling is not a cryptographic assumption but an experimental demand — below the threshold efficiency, no proof can rule out local models exploiting non-detection events.
7. **Quantum theory itself.** All guarantees assume quantum mechanics is correct; *some* DIQKD-adjacent results hold against general non-signalling adversaries [21], a strictly stronger claim.
8. **Side channels in post-processing.** The proofs model error correction and privacy amplification as ideal functionalities; real implementations must additionally resist timing and cache attacks on the classical processing.

## 7. Conclusion

The modern theory of quantum key distribution is best understood as a stack of reductions, each with an explicit, composable error budget. Entropic uncertainty relations convert observed correlations into smooth min-entropy; the quantum leftover hash lemma converts min-entropy into secret key; Serfling and Chernoff bounds convert finite samples into confidence intervals; the Renner–Portmann framework converts standalone *ε*-security into security under arbitrary composition; and Bell-inequality violation converts untrusted-device statistics into certified secrecy. Decoy states bridge the gap to realistic sources, and measurement-device-independent architectures bridge the gap to realistic detectors, leaving full device independence as the asymptotic ideal whose experimental realization is now a matter of engineering — detection efficiency, repetition rate, and block size — rather than of principle.

Open problems are correspondingly concrete: tightening finite-key bounds for DIQKD under coherent attacks, closing the efficiency loophole at telecom wavelengths, integrating composable proofs with network-layer key management, and extending entropy accumulation to twin-field and high-dimensional QKD. What is no longer open is the foundational question: QKD's security can be stated precisely, proved rigorously, and — crucially — composed safely. That is the achievement this thesis has surveyed, and it is what distinguishes quantum key distribution from every computational cryptosystem it may one day replace.
