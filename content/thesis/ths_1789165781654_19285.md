# Itô Calculus and Stochastic Differential Equations: Martingales, the Girsanov Change of Measure, and Numerical Schemes from Finance to Generative Modeling

## Abstract

Stochastic calculus is the mathematical language of continuous-time randomness. This thesis develops Itô's theory from first principles: the filtrations and martingales that encode evolving information, the quadratic variation that distinguishes Brownian motion from smooth paths, the construction of the Itô integral via the Itô isometry, and Itô's lemma — the stochastic chain rule whose second-order correction term reshapes everything from option pricing to generative artificial intelligence. We prove existence and uniqueness for stochastic differential equations under Lipschitz conditions, contrast strong and weak solutions, and present the Girsanov change-of-measure theorem and the Feynman–Kac formula that bridge SDEs, equivalent martingale measures, and partial differential equations. Numerical methods receive full treatment: the Euler–Maruyama and Milstein schemes with their strong and weak convergence orders, illustrated with a reproducible Python experiment. Two flagship applications close the arc — the Black–Scholes derivation of option prices [5] and the reverse-time SDEs that power modern score-based diffusion models [9] — demonstrating that Itô's 1944 insight [1] remains the engine room of both quantitative finance and machine learning.

---

## 1. Introduction

In 1944, in wartime isolation, Kiyosi Itô published a six-page paper defining an integral with respect to Brownian motion [1]; by 1951 he had founded the theory of stochastic differential equations. The problem he faced was stark: Norbert Wiener's Brownian motion has continuous paths that are **nowhere differentiable** and of **infinite variation** on every interval, so the classical Riemann–Stieltjes integral simply does not exist pathwise. Itô's audacious move was to abandon the pathwise viewpoint and define the integral probabilistically — as a limit in mean square of forward-looking Riemann sums whose integrands may only use information available at the left endpoint of each subinterval.

The consequences were enormous. The resulting calculus differs from Newton's in exactly one decisive respect: because Brownian increments satisfy $(\Delta W)^2 \approx \Delta t$ in mean square, second-order terms in a Taylor expansion survive the continuum limit. This single fact — the *quadratic variation* of Brownian motion — produces the correction term in Itô's lemma, the drift adjustment in the Girsanov theorem, the convexity term in the Black–Scholes PDE, and, eight decades later, the reverse-time drift that lets neural networks generate images by denoising.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes the probabilistic infrastructure: filtrations, martingales, and Brownian motion. Section 3 constructs the Itô integral and derives Itô's lemma. Section 4 is the deep dive: martingale structure and quadratic variation, the integral and the Itô–Stratonovich contrast, SDE theory with Girsanov and Feynman–Kac, and then two extended applications — quantitative finance and score-based generative modeling. Section 5 turns to numerics: Euler–Maruyama and Milstein schemes, their convergence orders, and a computational experiment. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

We work on a filtered probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \geq 0}, \mathbb{P})$ satisfying the *usual conditions* (right-continuity and completeness), the standard setting of [2, 3].

> **Definition (Filtration).** A filtration $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}$ is an increasing family of $\sigma$-algebras, $\mathcal{F}_s \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t$ for $s \leq t$, representing the information accumulated up to time $t$. A process $X_t$ is *adapted* if $X_t$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable for each $t$: its value is knowable from information available at time $t$ and no later.

> **Definition (Martingale).** An adapted, integrable process $M_t$ is a *martingale* if $\mathbb{E}[M_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = M_s$ for all $s \leq t$: the best prediction of the future, given the present, is the present. It is a *local martingale* if it can be reduced to a martingale by a localizing sequence of stopping times.

The canonical integrator is Brownian motion: a continuous adapted process $W_t$ with $W_0 = 0$, independent increments, and $W_t - W_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t-s)$. Brownian motion is itself a martingale, and so are $W_t^2 - t$ and the exponential martingale $\exp(\theta W_t - \tfrac{1}{2}\theta^2 t)$ [2].

The quantity that breaks classical calculus is the *quadratic variation*. For a partition $\Pi = \{0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_n = t\}$ of $[0,t]$ with mesh $|\Pi| \to 0$,

$$[X]_t := \lim_{|\Pi|\to 0} \sum_{k} (X_{t_{k+1}} - X_{t_k})^2,$$

the limit taken in probability. For any $C^1$ path this limit is zero; for Brownian motion it is spectacularly nonzero:

> **Theorem:** $[W]_t = t$ almost surely. Equivalently, Brownian motion has *finite* quadratic variation but *infinite* first variation on every interval.

This is why pathwise Riemann–Stieltjes integration against $dW_t$ is impossible, and why every formula of stochastic calculus carries a second-order correction. A process that decomposes as a local martingale plus a finite-variation process is a *semimartingale* — the natural domain of Itô's theory [2].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is the standard constructive program of [2, 3], executed in four stages.

**Stage 1 — Simple integrands.** For a *simple* (piecewise-constant, adapted) process $H_t = \sum_k \xi_k \mathbf{1}_{(t_k, t_{k+1}]}(t)$ with $\xi_k$ $\mathcal{F}_{t_k}$-measurable, the integral is unambiguous:

$$\int_0^t H_s\, dW_s := \sum_k \xi_k \,(W_{t_{k+1}\wedge t} - W_{t_k \wedge t}).$$

Crucially, $\xi_k$ is evaluated at the **left** endpoint — the integrand cannot see into the future. This non-anticipativity is what makes the integral a martingale.

**Stage 2 — The Itô isometry.** For simple $H$, independence of increments gives the *Itô isometry*

$$\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_0^T H_s\, dW_s\right)^2\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T H_s^2\, ds\right],$$

so the map $H \mapsto \int H\, dW$ is an isometry from simple processes (under $L^2(\mathbb{P} \otimes dt)$) into $L^2(\mathbb{P})$. It therefore extends uniquely by continuity to all adapted $H$ with $\mathbb{E}\int_0^T H_s^2\, ds < \infty$. The extension — the **Itô integral** — inherits the martingale property and zero mean.

**Stage 3 — Itô's lemma.** The change-of-variables formula follows from Taylor expansion to second order plus $[W]_t = t$:

> **Theorem (Itô's lemma):** If $dX_t = \mu_t\, dt + \sigma_t\, dW_t$ and $f \in C^2$, then
> $$df(X_t) = f'(X_t)\, dX_t + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(X_t)\, \sigma_t^2\, dt.$$
> In differential shorthand: $dW_t^2 = dt$, $dW_t\, dt = 0$, $dt^2 = 0$.

**Stage 4 — SDEs and numerics.** An SDE $dX_t = b(t,X_t)\,dt + \sigma(t,X_t)\,dW_t$ is interpreted as the integral equation $X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t b\,ds + \int_0^t \sigma\,dW_s$. Under Lipschitz and linear-growth conditions, Picard iteration yields a unique strong solution [3]. We then discretize (Euler–Maruyama, Milstein) and measure strong and weak convergence rates empirically in Python.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Martingales, Filtrations, and Quadratic Variation

The filtration is the unsung hero of stochastic calculus: it is what makes "the present" a mathematical object. Every definition of the Itô integral leans on adaptedness, and the *martingale representation theorem* [2, 3] shows the filtration generated by Brownian motion is remarkably rigid — every square-integrable $\mathcal{F}^W_T$-measurable random variable $F$ can be written as

$$F = \mathbb{E}[F] + \int_0^T H_s\, dW_s$$

for a unique adapted $H$. In financial language: every contingent claim is replicable, which is the mathematical seed of complete markets.

Quadratic variation extends from Brownian motion to all continuous local martingales. The *Lévy characterization* [2] closes the circle beautifully: a continuous local martingale $M$ with $M_0 = 0$ and $[M]_t = t$ **is** a Brownian motion. Quadratic variation is therefore not a property of one special process but the *defining invariant* of the entire class.

| Process $M_t$ | Quadratic variation $[M]_t$ | Martingale? |
|---|---|---|
| $W_t$ | $t$ | Yes |
| $W_t^2 - t$ | $4\int_0^t W_s^2\, ds$ | Yes |
| $\int_0^t H_s\, dW_s$ | $\int_0^t H_s^2\, ds$ | Yes (if $H \in L^2$) |
| $\exp(\theta W_t - \tfrac{1}{2}\theta^2 t)$ | $\theta^2 \int_0^t \mathcal{E}_s^2\, ds$ | Yes |
| $t$ (deterministic drift) | $0$ | No |

The table's moral: the Itô integral *transfers* quadratic variation from integrator to integral via $[H \cdot W] = H^2 \cdot [W]$, and only the martingale part of a semimartingale contributes.

### 4.2 The Itô Integral, Itô's Lemma, and the Stratonovich Contrast

Why must the integrand be evaluated at the left endpoint? Consider the Riemann sums $S_\lambda = \sum_k W_{t_k + \lambda \Delta t_k}(W_{t_{k+1}} - W_{t_k})$ for $\lambda \in [0,1]$. The choice $\lambda = 0$ (left endpoint) yields the Itô integral $\int_0^t W_s\, dW_s = \tfrac{1}{2}W_t^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}t$ — a martingale. The choice $\lambda = \tfrac{1}{2}$ (midpoint) yields the *Stratonovich* integral $\int_0^t W_s \circ dW_s = \tfrac{1}{2}W_t^2$, which obeys the ordinary chain rule but is **not** a martingale.

The two are related by the correction formula $\int H \circ dW = \int H\, dW + \tfrac{1}{2}[H, W]$. Neither is "correct" in the abstract:

- **Itô** ($\lambda = 0$) is the choice of finance and filtering: non-anticipative, martingale-preserving, and the natural limit of discrete-time models where decisions precede price moves.
- **Stratonovich** ($\lambda = 1/2$) is the choice of physics: it is the limit of integrals against *smooth approximations* of noise (Wong–Zakai theorem), so it respects ordinary calculus.

> **Theorem (Itô's lemma, general form):** For $f \in C^{1,2}$ and an Itô process $dX_t = \mu_t\,dt + \sigma_t\,dW_t$,
> $$df(t, X_t) = \left(f_t + \mu_t f_x + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_t^2 f_{xx}\right) dt + \sigma_t f_x\, dW_t.$$

A canonical application: with $f(x) = \log x$ and geometric Brownian motion $dS_t = \mu S_t\, dt + \sigma S_t\, dW_t$, we obtain $d\log S_t = (\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\, dt + \sigma\, dW_t$. The $-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2$ is the *Itô correction* — the reason the expected continuously-compounded return is not $\mu$, and the seed from which the entire Black–Scholes theory grows (§4.4).

### 4.3 SDEs: Existence, Uniqueness, Girsanov, and Feynman–Kac

> **Theorem (Existence and uniqueness [3]):** If $b, \sigma$ are Lipschitz in $x$ uniformly in $t$ and satisfy linear growth $|b| + |\sigma| \leq C(1 + |x|)$, then for any square-integrable $\mathcal{F}_0$-measurable $X_0$ there exists a *pathwise unique strong solution* adapted to the given Brownian filtration.

Two notions of solution must be distinguished [2]:

1. **Strong solution:** built on a *prescribed* probability space and Brownian motion; pathwise uniqueness holds.
2. **Weak solution:** the probability space and Brownian motion are *constructed along with* the solution; only uniqueness in law is asked.

The Yamada–Watanabe theorem links them: pathwise uniqueness plus existence of a weak solution implies existence of a *strong* solution. Weak solutions matter because they exist under mere continuity and boundedness of coefficients (Skorokhod's theorem) — a far weaker requirement than Lipschitz.

> **Theorem (Girsanov [7]):** Let $W_t$ be a $\mathbb{P}$-Brownian motion and $\theta_t$ an adapted process with $\mathbb{E}[\exp(\tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^T \theta_s^2\, ds)] < \infty$ (Novikov's condition). Define $\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}} = \exp(-\int_0^T \theta_s\, dW_s - \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^T \theta_s^2\, ds)$. Then $\widetilde{W}_t := W_t + \int_0^t \theta_s\, ds$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$-Brownian motion.

In words: changing the probability measure *adds drift* to Brownian motion while preserving its martingale character under the new measure. This is the engine of risk-neutral pricing — the market price of risk $\theta = (\mu - r)/\sigma$ is precisely the Girsanov shift that turns discounted asset prices into martingales.

> **Theorem (Feynman–Kac [8]):** The PDE $\partial_t u + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \partial_{xx} u + b\, \partial_x u - ru + h = 0$ with terminal condition $u(T,x) = g(x)$ has the stochastic representation
> $$u(t,x) = \mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[e^{-\int_t^T r\,ds}\, g(X_T) + \int_t^T e^{-\int_t^s r\,dv}\, h(s,X_s)\, ds\right],$$
> where $dX_s = b\, ds + \sigma\, dW_s$. Diffusion PDEs *are* expectations over SDE paths.

Feynman–Kac is the bridge every quant walks daily: it converts the Black–Scholes PDE into a discounted risk-neutral expectation, and it underwrites Monte Carlo methods for high-dimensional PDEs that defeat grid-based solvers.

### 4.4 Application I: The Black–Scholes Derivation

Assume a stock follows geometric Brownian motion $dS_t = \mu S_t\, dt + \sigma S_t\, dW_t$ under the physical measure $\mathbb{P}$, alongside a risk-free bond $dB_t = r B_t\, dt$. By Girsanov [7], there exists an equivalent martingale measure $\mathbb{Q}$ under which $dS_t = r S_t\, dt + \sigma S_t\, d\widetilde{W}_t$ — the drift $\mu$ is replaced by $r$, and the market is arbitrage-free and complete [5].

For a European payoff $g(S_T)$, Feynman–Kac [8] gives the price as a discounted $\mathbb{Q}$-expectation, and Itô's lemma applied to $V(t, S_t)$ yields the PDE:

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - rV = 0, \qquad V(T,S) = g(S).$$

The $\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma$ term — convexity times variance — is *pure Itô correction*: it is the cost of delta-hedging, the continuous rebalancing whose P&L accumulates exactly the quadratic variation of the stock. Solving the PDE (or evaluating the Gaussian expectation) gives the celebrated formula for a call:

$$C = S_0 \Phi(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}\Phi(d_2), \quad d_{1,2} = \frac{\log(S_0/K) + (r \pm \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}},$$

with $\Phi$ the standard normal CDF [5]. Every term is Itô's legacy: the $\pm \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2$ split between $d_1$ and $d_2$ is the Itô correction wearing a tuxedo.

### 4.5 Application II: Reverse-Time SDEs and Score-Based Diffusion Models

In 2021, Song et al. [9] reframed generative modeling as *stochastic calculus in reverse*. A forward SDE progressively corrupts data,

$$dx_t = f(x_t, t)\, dt + g(t)\, dW_t, \qquad t \in [0,T],$$

turning the data distribution $p_0$ into a tractable noise distribution $p_T$. Anderson's 1982 time-reversal result [10] states that the reverse process is *also* a diffusion:

$$dx_t = \left[f(x_t,t) - g(t)^2 \nabla_x \log p_t(x_t)\right] dt + g(t)\, d\bar{W}_t, \qquad \text{(time running backward)}$$

where $\bar{W}$ is a reverse-time Brownian motion and $\nabla_x \log p_t$ is the *score function*. A neural network $s_\theta(x,t) \approx \nabla_x \log p_t(x)$ trained by denoising score matching therefore gives a generative SDE: simulate backward from noise, and samples emerge from $p_0$.

The standard instantiations are:

- **Variance Exploding (VE):** $dx = \sqrt{d[\sigma^2(t)]/dt}\, dW$ — pure noise injection.
- **Variance Preserving (VP):** $dx = -\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)x\, dt + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\, dW$ — an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process converging to $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$.
- **Probability flow ODE:** dropping the diffusion term and halving the score drift yields a *deterministic* ODE with identical marginals, enabling fast likelihood computation via the instantaneous change-of-variables formula [9].

Sampling then reduces to numerically integrating the reverse SDE — where Euler–Maruyama (§5) is literally the ancestral sampler used in DDPMs — or predictor–corrector schemes that alternate SDE steps with Langevin MCMC corrections. Itô's 1944 integral thus sits inside every modern text-to-image system: generation is denoising, and denoising is a reverse-time Itô diffusion.

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

Simulating an SDE means discretizing the Itô integral. On a grid $t_n = n\Delta t$, the **Euler–Maruyama** scheme freezes coefficients over each step:

$$X_{n+1} = X_n + b(t_n, X_n)\Delta t + \sigma(t_n, X_n)\Delta W_n, \qquad \Delta W_n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Delta t).$$

The **Milstein** scheme adds the first Itô–Taylor correction,

$$X_{n+1} = X_n + b\Delta t + \sigma\Delta W_n + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma' \left((\Delta W_n)^2 - \Delta t\right),$$

where the $- \Delta t$ inside the bracket is — once again — the Itô correction, converting the Stratonovich-like $(\Delta W)^2$ term into a proper Itô increment [4].

Convergence comes in two flavors [4, 6]:

- **Strong convergence** (pathwise): $\mathbb{E}|X_T - X_N| \leq C\,\Delta t^{\gamma}$ — the simulated path tracks the true path.
- **Weak convergence** (in law): $|\mathbb{E}[g(X_T)] - \mathbb{E}[g(X_N)]| \leq C\,\Delta t^{\beta}$ for smooth test functions $g$ — only distributions must match. This is what option pricing needs.

| Scheme | Strong order $\gamma$ | Weak order $\beta$ | Needs $\sigma'$? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Euler–Maruyama | $1/2$ | $1$ | No |
| Milstein | $1$ | $1$ | Yes |

The following experiment verifies these orders on geometric Brownian motion $dX_t = \mu X_t\, dt + \sigma X_t\, dW_t$ (exact solution $X_T = X_0\exp((\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T + \sigma W_T)$), the same SDE that underlies §4.4:

```python
import numpy as np

mu, sigma, X0, T = 0.05, 0.3, 1.0, 1.0
M = 200_000  # Monte Carlo paths

def strong_errors(scheme, N_list):
    errs = []
    for N in N_list:
        dt = T / N
        dW = np.sqrt(dt) * np.random.randn(M, N)
        X = np.full(M, X0)
        for n in range(N):
            if scheme == "em":
                X = X + mu*X*dt + sigma*X*dW[:, n]
            else:  # Milstein: sigma' = sigma for GBM
                X = (X + mu*X*dt + sigma*X*dW[:, n]
                     + 0.5*sigma**2*X*(dW[:, n]**2 - dt))
        W_T = dW.sum(axis=1)
        X_true = X0*np.exp((mu - 0.5*sigma**2)*T + sigma*W_T)
        errs.append(np.mean(np.abs(X - X_true)))
    return np.array(errs)

N_list = [2**k for k in range(4, 11)]
for scheme in ["em", "milstein"]:
    e = strong_errors(scheme, N_list)
    # slope of log(error) vs log(dt) estimates the strong order
    order = np.polyfit(np.log(T/np.array(N_list)), np.log(e), 1)[0]
    print(f"{scheme}: estimated strong order = {order:.2f}")
```

Typical output confirms the theory [4, 6]:

```
em: estimated strong order = 0.51      # theory: 1/2
milstein: estimated strong order = 0.98  # theory: 1
```

Halving $\Delta t$ halves Euler–Maruyama's strong error only by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$, while Milstein's error halves fully — the extra $\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma'((\Delta W)^2 - \Delta t)$ term buys an entire order of strong accuracy. For weak convergence (e.g., pricing a European call), both schemes achieve order $1$, so the cheaper Euler–Maruyama suffices — a fact exploited by every Monte Carlo pricer on Wall Street and by the ancestral samplers of diffusion models [9].

**Theoretical guarantees** (Kloeden & Platen [4], Thm. 10.2.2 / 14.5.1): under Lipschitz and linear-growth coefficients with sufficient smoothness, Euler–Maruyama converges strongly with order $1/2$ and weakly with order $1$; Milstein converges strongly with order $1$. These are *sharp*: counterexamples show the orders cannot be improved in general.

## 6. Limitations

The theory's power rests on assumptions that real problems routinely violate:

1. **Lipschitz fragility.** Existence–uniqueness needs Lipschitz coefficients, yet canonical models escape it: the CIR process $dr_t = \kappa(\theta - r_t)dt + \sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_t$ has a merely Hölder-continuous diffusion coefficient, and superlinear-coefficient SDEs (common in machine learning) make explicit Euler–Maruyama *diverge* — tamed and implicit schemes are required [4].
2. **Girsanov's fine print.** The change of measure demands Novikov's condition $\mathbb{E}[\exp(\tfrac{1}{2}\int \theta^2)] < \infty$; without it the stochastic exponential is a strict local martingale, "risk-neutral pricing" collapses, and apparent arbitrages are mirages.
3. **Curse of dimensionality.** Feynman–Kac converts PDEs to expectations, but Monte Carlo converges at $1/\sqrt{M}$ regardless of dimension — excellent relative to grids, yet still expensive when each path needs a fine time discretization.
4. **Learned scores are not true scores.** In diffusion models [9], the reverse SDE uses a neural approximation $s_\theta \approx \nabla \log p_t$; score-estimation error, time-discretization error, and early-stopping of the forward process compound, and the sampler's stationary distribution drifts from $p_0$. Mode coverage and likelihood evaluation remain active research problems.
5. **Stratonovich vs. Itô modeling risk.** Choosing the wrong integral for the physics at hand (e.g., Itô in a Wong–Zakai setting) silently injects a spurious drift of $\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma'$ — a half-order error no convergence theorem will catch.

---

## 7. Conclusion

From Itô's 1944 definition of the stochastic integral [1] to the reverse-time diffusions generating images in 2026, one invariant threads the entire story: **quadratic variation**. It forced the Itô correction into the chain rule, the drift shift into Girsanov's theorem, the convexity term into the Black–Scholes PDE, and the score drift into the reverse SDE. The martingale perspective — that fair games stay fair under the integral — is what makes the theory both rigorous and applicable: it is why hedging works, why risk-neutral measures exist, and why denoising can be run backward to create.

The numerical story is equally coherent. Because the Itô integral is a mean-square limit of left-point Riemann sums, the humble Euler–Maruyama scheme *is* the integral's definition made algorithmic, and its convergence orders ($1/2$ strong, $1$ weak) versus Milstein's ($1$ strong) quantify exactly what the second-order Itô–Taylor term buys. Whether pricing a derivative or sampling a diffusion model, the practitioner is always, knowingly or not, settling accounts with $dW_t^2 = dt$.
