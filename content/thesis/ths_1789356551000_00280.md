# Differential Privacy: Composition Theorems, Rényi Differential Privacy, and the Geometry of Privacy Loss

## Abstract

This thesis develops a unified treatment of *differential privacy composition*: the rules that govern how privacy degrades when a sensitive dataset is queried repeatedly. We begin with the classical framework of pure and approximate differential privacy [1], derive basic and advanced composition theorems through the lens of the *privacy loss random variable*, and prove that the optimal composition bound is characterized by the hockey-stick divergence of a canonical four-outcome mechanism [7][8]. We then introduce *Rényi differential privacy* (RDP) [2], a divergence-based relaxation whose linear composition law makes it the natural accounting language for iterative algorithms, and its close cousin *zero-concentrated differential privacy* (zCDP) [3][4]. The centerpiece application is differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) [5], where we work through the moments-accountant analysis of subsampled Gaussian mechanisms [6] and give numerical comparisons showing that RDP accounting converts a loose linear epsilon budget into a near-optimal logarithmic one. We close with the frontier: auditing, heterogeneous composition via FFT-based privacy-loss distributions, and the limits of what any composition theorem can guarantee.

## 1 Intro

*Differential privacy* is a promise about indistinguishability. Informally, a randomized computation over a dataset is differentially private when its output distribution barely changes if any single individual's record is added, removed, or replaced. That single sentence, formalized in 2006, turned privacy from a checklist of heuristics into a mathematical property with a calculus: definitions compose, mechanisms can be calibrated by sensitivity, and post-processing can never weaken the guarantee [1].

But real analyses are never single queries. A data release answers dozens of questions; a private machine-learning model runs thousands of gradient steps. Each access leaks a little, and the central operational question is how those leaks *add up*. This is the composition problem, and it is the load-bearing wall of every deployed differentially private system.

> **Theorem: The composition problem.** Given mechanisms M_1, ..., M_k, each satisfying a per-release privacy bound, characterize the tightest privacy guarantee satisfied by the joint release (M_1(x), ..., M_k(x)), both for fixed (non-adaptive) and adaptively chosen sequences of mechanisms.

The answer is subtler than it first appears. Naive composition adds per-release epsilons linearly, but a sharper *advanced composition* theorem shows that with a tiny failure probability delta, the total epsilon grows only as the square root of the number of releases times a logarithmic factor [7]. This square-root phenomenon is no accident: it is a central-limit effect in the distribution of the *privacy loss random variable*, and once that is understood, a whole family of divergence-based relaxations — concentrated DP, zero-concentrated DP, Rényi DP — falls into place as alternative geometries for measuring privacy loss [2][3][4].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes definitions: neighboring datasets, pure and approximate DP, sensitivity, and the Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms. Section 3 introduces the privacy loss random variable and adaptive composition. Section 4 is the deep dive: basic, advanced, and optimal composition, Rényi DP, and zCDP. Section 5 turns theory into numbers for DP-SGD [5][6]. Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Neighboring datasets and the definition

Fix a data domain X. Two datasets x and x' are *neighboring* (written x ~ x') when they differ in exactly one record — by addition, removal, or substitution, a choice that must be fixed before any guarantee is stated. A randomized mechanism M maps datasets to distributions over outputs.

> **Definition (Pure differential privacy, [1]).** A mechanism M is *epsilon-differentially private* if for every pair of neighboring datasets x ~ x' and every measurable output set S,
>
> Pr[M(x) in S] <= exp(epsilon) * Pr[M(x') in S].
>
> The parameter epsilon >= 0 is the *privacy budget*: smaller is stronger.

The definition is intentionally *adversarial*: it must hold for all neighbors and all events, so it bounds the worst case over both the data and the attacker's inference strategy. It is also *prior-free*: it constrains likelihood ratios, which means guarantees hold regardless of the adversary's auxiliary information.

Pure DP is demanding. Many natural mechanisms — most famously the Gaussian mechanism — do not satisfy it for any finite epsilon, because Gaussian tails assign positive density everywhere and likelihood ratios are unbounded. This motivates the relaxation to *approximate DP* [1]:

> **Definition (Approximate differential privacy).** M is *(epsilon, delta)-differentially private* if for all neighboring x ~ x' and all measurable S,
>
> Pr[M(x) in S] <= exp(epsilon) * Pr[M(x') in S] + delta.
>
> Here delta in the unit interval (typically cryptographically small, e.g. 1e-6 or smaller) is an additive failure probability.

The parameter delta permits a rare catastrophic event — a draw under which the likelihood-ratio bound fails entirely — so it must be kept far below the reciprocal of the dataset size to remain meaningful.

### 2.2 Sensitivity and the Laplace mechanism

For a vector-valued query f, the *global L1 sensitivity* is Delta_f = max over neighbors of the L1 distance between f(x) and f(x'). Sensitivity is the fundamental scale of the problem: it measures how much one person can move the answer.

> **Theorem (Laplace mechanism, [1]).** Releasing f(x) + Z, where Z is drawn from the Laplace distribution with scale Delta_f / epsilon, is epsilon-differentially private.

The proof is a one-line likelihood-ratio calculation: the Laplace density ratio between neighboring inputs is bounded by exp(epsilon) pointwise. This mechanism is *tight* for pure DP in a strong sense, but its linear composition behavior and L1 geometry make it suboptimal for high-dimensional iterative algorithms.

### 2.3 The Gaussian mechanism and hockey-stick divergence

The Gaussian mechanism releases f(x) + N(0, sigma^2 I) for a query with L2 sensitivity Delta. It satisfies (epsilon, delta)-DP when sigma >= c * Delta / epsilon with c^2 > 2 ln(1.25/delta) [1]. The analysis is less elementary than the Laplace case, because Gaussian likelihood ratios are unbounded; the delta parameter absorbs the tail event where the realized noise is small.

A cleaner language for approximate DP uses the *hockey-stick divergence*. For distributions P and Q and gamma >= 1, E_gamma(P || Q) is the supremum over events S of (P(S) - gamma * Q(S)). Then M is (epsilon, delta)-DP if and only if E_{exp(epsilon)}(M(x) || M(x')) <= delta for all neighbors [2][8].

### 2.4 Post-processing and group privacy

Two properties make DP a workable engineering primitive. *Post-processing immunity*: if M is (epsilon, delta)-DP, then so is g(M(x)) for any randomized function g. *Group privacy*: epsilon-DP degrades to k*epsilon-DP for datasets differing in k records.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The privacy loss random variable

All composition analysis in this thesis flows through a single object. For neighboring datasets x, x' and an output y drawn from M(x), the *privacy loss* is

L(y) = ln( Pr[M(x) = y] / Pr[M(x') = y] ),

the log-likelihood ratio of the observed output under the two worlds. The *privacy loss random variable* (PLRV) is L(Y) with Y ~ M(x) [3][4]. Pure epsilon-DP is the statement that the PLRV is bounded by epsilon almost surely; approximate (epsilon, delta)-DP is the statement that the PLRV exceeds epsilon with probability at most delta.

> **Theorem: Composition as convolution.** For non-adaptive composition, the PLRV of the joint mechanism is the *sum* of the independent per-release PLRVs. Composition theorems are therefore tail bounds on sums of random variables — which is why martingale and central-limit machinery applies.

This is the methodological key. Basic composition is a union-style worst-case bound on the sum. Advanced composition is an Azuma/Chernoff bound on the sum of bounded variables [7]. The optimal composition theorem computes the exact tail of the sum for the worst-case single-release distribution [8]. And Rényi DP bounds not the tail but the *moment generating function* of the PLRV — which tensorizes multiplicatively under independence, giving linear composition for free [2].

### 3.2 Adaptive composition

In practice the j-th mechanism is chosen after seeing the first j-1 outputs: M_j may depend on the realized transcript. An *adaptive composition experiment* runs M_1(x), then selects M_2 based on the first output, and so on. Remarkably, all the composition theorems in this thesis hold in the adaptive setting with identical parameters [7]. The proof technique is a hybrid argument: replace the true dataset with its neighbor one release at a time, and observe that each step's privacy loss, conditioned on the transcript so far, is controlled by that step's individual guarantee. Adaptivity is therefore free — a fact of enormous practical importance for iterative algorithms like DP-SGD, where every gradient step depends on all previous ones.

### 3.3 What "tight" means

A composition theorem maps per-release parameters to a joint guarantee. It is *tight* (optimal) when some concrete family of mechanisms attains the bound. Tightness matters because a loose theorem forces practitioners to add more noise than necessary, and much of the modern literature is a campaign to close the gap between the linear bound and the true optimal curve [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Basic composition: linear, simple, and often too weak

> **Theorem (Basic composition).** If each M_i is (epsilon_i, delta_i)-DP, then the joint mechanism (M_1, ..., M_k) is (sum of epsilon_i, sum of delta_i)-DP, even adaptively.

*Proof sketch.* Condition on the transcript of earlier releases at each step; the i-th mechanism's guarantee bounds the conditional likelihood ratio. Multiplying the k conditional bounds gives a total ratio bounded by exp(sum epsilon_i) on the intersection of the good events, whose complement has probability at most sum delta_i. ∎

Basic composition is tight for pure DP in the worst case. But for approximate DP it is dramatically loose: releasing the same Gaussian query 10,000 times costs epsilon on the order of sqrt(k) under advanced composition, not k.

### 4.2 Advanced composition: the square-root phenomenon

> **Theorem (Advanced composition, Dwork-Rothblum-Vadhan [7]).** For epsilon in (0, 1], the k-fold adaptive composition of (epsilon, delta)-DP mechanisms is (epsilon', k*delta + delta')-DP for any delta' > 0, where
>
> epsilon' = sqrt(2 k ln(1/delta')) * epsilon + k * epsilon * (exp(epsilon) - 1).
>
> For small epsilon the second term is approximately (1/2) k epsilon^2.

The theorem has a characteristic *privacy-utility* moral: the delta' parameter is spent to buy concentration, and the optimal split between the per-step delta and the composition delta' is itself an optimization problem that privacy accountants solve numerically. The proof views the cumulative privacy loss as a martingale: each step's conditional expected loss is small (order epsilon^2) and each step's loss is bounded, so Azuma's inequality concentrates the total around its mean. The sqrt(k) scaling is the central limit theorem doing its work — and it is the reason iterative private algorithms are feasible at all.

### 4.3 Optimal composition: Kairouz, Oh, and Viswanath

Kairouz, Oh, and Viswanath proved that the composition bound for approximate DP can be made *exactly tight* [8]. Their insight: for fixed (epsilon, delta), there is a single canonical worst-case mechanism — a randomized response over four outcomes with carefully tuned probabilities — such that the optimal k-fold composition parameters are obtained by analyzing k-fold composition of *that* mechanism alone. Every (epsilon, delta)-DP mechanism is, in a precise simulation sense, dominated by it.

The exact optimal curve has no simple closed form; computing it is #P-hard in general [8], which motivated a line of work on numerically tight accounting via *privacy loss distributions* (PLDs) and fast Fourier transforms [8].

### 4.4 Rényi differential privacy: composition made linear

> **Definition (Rényi DP, Mironov [2]).** For alpha > 1, the *Rényi divergence of order alpha* between distributions P and Q is
>
> D_alpha(P || Q) = (1/(alpha-1)) * ln E_{y ~ Q}[(P(y)/Q(y))^alpha].
>
> A mechanism M is *(alpha, epsilon)-RDP* if D_alpha(M(x) || M(x')) <= epsilon for all neighboring x, x'.

RDP generalizes pure DP: as alpha -> infinity, D_alpha becomes the max log-likelihood ratio and RDP recovers epsilon-DP; as alpha -> 1 it becomes the KL divergence, bounding the *expected* privacy loss.

1. **Adaptive composition is linear.** If M_1 is (alpha, epsilon_1)-RDP and M_2 (chosen adaptively) is (alpha, epsilon_2)-RDP, the pair is (alpha, epsilon_1 + epsilon_2)-RDP. No square roots, no extra deltas — the Rényi divergence tensorizes exactly over product distributions.

2. **Post-processing immunity.** Like all DP variants, RDP survives arbitrary post-processing, by the data-processing inequality for Rényi divergence.

3. **Conversion to approximate DP.** If M is (alpha, epsilon)-RDP, then it is (epsilon + ln(1/delta)/(alpha-1), delta)-DP for every delta in the open unit interval [2]. The conversion is optimized over alpha: one tracks the whole *RDP curve* epsilon(alpha) and converts at the end, choosing the alpha that minimizes the final epsilon for the target delta.

The Gaussian mechanism is the showcase: adding N(0, sigma^2 I) noise to a query of L2 sensitivity Delta yields (alpha, alpha * Delta^2 / (2 sigma^2))-RDP for every alpha > 1 [2][4].

### 4.5 Zero-concentrated DP and the subgaussian view

Dwork and Rothblum's *concentrated DP* [3] and Bun and Steinke's *zero-concentrated DP* (zCDP) [4] take the PLRV view one step further: they demand that the privacy loss be *subgaussian*.

> **Theorem (zCDP composition and conversion [4]).** The composition of rho_1-zCDP and rho_2-zCDP mechanisms is (rho_1 + rho_2)-zCDP. A rho-zCDP mechanism is (rho + 2 sqrt(rho ln(1/delta)), delta)-DP for all delta > 0.

The Gaussian mechanism with noise variance sigma^2 on sensitivity-Delta queries is exactly (Delta^2/(2 sigma^2))-zCDP [4].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The moments accountant for DP-SGD

DP-SGD [5] modifies stochastic gradient descent in two ways: per-example gradients are clipped to L2 norm C, and Gaussian noise N(0, sigma^2 C^2 I) is added to the averaged clipped gradients. Each training step samples a minibatch by including each example independently with probability q (Poisson subsampling), which *amplifies* privacy: a subsampled mechanism's RDP curve is strictly better than the base mechanism's [6].

Abadi et al.'s *moments accountant* [5] tracks, for each order alpha, the log moment of the per-step PLRV — equivalently, the RDP curve — and sums these across steps. The original analysis bounded the subsampled RDP curve numerically; Wang, Balle, and Kasiviswanathan later gave a closed-form *analytical* moments accountant with a tight bound on the subsampled Gaussian's RDP for all alpha [6], removing the numerical integration and the integer-alpha restriction.

### 5.2 Worked comparison: accounting methods on a realistic workload

Consider training for T = 10,000 steps with sampling probability q = 0.01, noise multiplier sigma = 4.0, and target delta = 1e-5. The table below compares the final epsilon reported by four accounting strategies (values are representative of published accountant benchmarks [5][6][7]):

| Accounting method | Final epsilon (delta = 1e-5) | Scaling in T |
|---|---|---|
| Basic composition | ~ 63.2 | linear |
| Advanced composition | ~ 7.9 | sqrt(T log T) |
| RDP / moments accountant | ~ 2.6 | near-optimal |
| FFT-based PLD (optimal) | ~ 2.4 | optimal |

The qualitative lesson is robust across hyperparameters: linear accounting is unusable for iterative training, advanced composition rescues it, and Rényi accounting nearly attains the information-theoretic optimum. The gap between RDP and the FFT optimum is small because the subsampled Gaussian's PLD is close to Gaussian, and Gaussians are exactly characterized by their RDP curve.

### 5.3 Proof: RDP composition

We prove the linear composition law, the engine of RDP accounting [2].

> **Theorem.** Let M_1 be (alpha, epsilon_1)-RDP and let M_2(·; y_1) be (alpha, epsilon_2)-RDP for every fixed first output y_1. Then the adaptive composition M = (M_1, M_2) is (alpha, epsilon_1 + epsilon_2)-RDP.

*Proof.* Write P, P' for the output distributions on neighbors x, x'. Decompose over the first output:

E_{y ~ P'}[(P(y)/P'(y))^alpha] = E_{y_1 ~ P'_1}[ (P_1(y_1)/P'_1(y_1))^alpha * E_{y_2 ~ P'_{2|1}}[(P_{2|1}(y_2)/P'_{2|1}(y_2))^alpha] ].

The inner expectation is at most exp((alpha-1) epsilon_2) by the RDP guarantee of M_2, uniformly in y_1. Factoring it out leaves the M_1 moment, bounded by exp((alpha-1) epsilon_1). Taking (1/(alpha-1)) ln of the product gives epsilon_1 + epsilon_2. ∎

The proof's crux is that the exponential moment *factorizes* over the transcript — the same factorization that fails for tail probabilities, which is precisely why approximate DP needs the messier advanced-composition machinery.

### 5.4 Proof sketch: RDP to approximate DP conversion

> **Theorem (Mironov [2]).** (alpha, epsilon)-RDP implies (epsilon + ln(1/delta)/(alpha-1), delta)-DP.

*Proof sketch.* For any event S, write P(S) = E_{Q}[1_S * (P/Q)]. Apply Hölder's inequality with conjugate exponents alpha and alpha/(alpha-1) to split the indicator from the likelihood ratio; the ratio's alpha-moment is controlled by RDP, and optimizing the resulting bound over the decomposition yields the claimed epsilon inflation of ln(1/delta)/(alpha-1). ∎

In practice one does not convert per step. One accumulates the RDP curve over all T steps and converts once, minimizing over alpha — the minimization typically lands at alpha ~ 1 + sqrt(ln(1/delta)/(T * rho_step)) for zCDP-like curves.

### 5.5 Privacy amplification by subsampling

Subsampling is the quiet workhorse of private ML. If M is (alpha, epsilon)-RDP, then running M on a Poisson-q subsample yields a mechanism whose RDP parameter is roughly q^2 * epsilon for small q — a *quadratic* amplification in the sampling rate [6]. The analytical moments accountant makes this precise without numerical quadrature, and it is the reason DP-SGD can train for thousands of steps at single-digit epsilon.

## 6 Limitations

**Composition theorems are worst-case.** Every bound in this thesis holds for *all* mechanisms with given per-release parameters, so the tight curves are attained by adversarially constructed mechanisms (like the Kairouz four-outcome canonical mechanism). A concrete mechanism — say, DP-SGD on a well-conditioned loss — may leak far less than the bound permits. Composition gives ceilings, not measurements; *auditing* (empirically lower-bounding leakage via membership-inference attacks) is the complementary discipline, and the gap between upper bounds and audit lower bounds remains large.

**The delta problem.** Approximate DP, advanced composition, and the RDP conversion all spend a delta. Deltas compose additively, and a delta of 1e-5 is meaningless against a dataset of 1e9 records. Practitioners must keep delta well below the reciprocal of the population size, which constrains how aggressively the advanced-composition and conversion optimizations can be pushed.

**Computational cost of optimal accounting.** The FFT-based PLD accountant attains the Kairouz optimum but requires discretizing and convolving distributions; for heterogeneous compositions with thousands of distinct mechanisms this becomes expensive, and discretization error must itself be bounded rigorously [8].

**Side channels and the neighboring relation.** Composition theorems assume the *mechanism* is the only observation channel. Timing, floating-point artifacts, and data-dependent stopping rules (e.g., unaccounted early stopping on a private validation metric) all break the formal guarantees. Adaptivity is free only when every adaptive choice is part of the accounted transcript.

**RDP's blind spot.** RDP controls all exponential moments of the privacy loss but does not directly bound the probability of a single catastrophic output. Moreover, RDP composition requires a *common* alpha across releases, and the optimal alpha for the final conversion is not the optimal alpha for intermediate reasoning — a subtlety that heterogeneous FFT accounting handles more gracefully.

## 7 Conclusion

The story of differential privacy composition is the story of the privacy loss random variable growing up. Basic composition treats it as a worst case; advanced composition discovers its concentration; the optimal composition theorem computes its exact distribution for the hardest case; and Rényi differential privacy changes the subject from tails to moments, where composition becomes linear and accounting becomes arithmetic. Zero-concentrated DP completes the picture by identifying subgaussianity as the property that makes Gaussian noise compose so cleanly.

For the practitioner, the takeaways are concrete: account in Rényi space (or with FFT-based PLDs), convert once at the end, exploit subsampling amplification, and keep delta cryptographically small. For the theorist, the open problems are sharper: closing the audit gap and extending tight composition to fully heterogeneous adaptive streams. The budget is finite; the mathematics of spending it is still being written.
