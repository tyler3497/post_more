# From Wilson Lines to the Proton Mass: Lattice Quantum Chromodynamics as a First-Principles Framework for Non-Perturbative Strong Interactions

## Abstract

Quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the SU(3) gauge theory of quarks and gluons, predicts the hadron spectrum and dynamics, yet its low-energy regime is intractable in perturbation theory: the strong coupling grows to order unity at hadronic scales, and phenomena such as confinement and dynamical chiral symmetry breaking are intrinsically non-perturbative. This thesis develops lattice QCD — the discretization of the Euclidean QCD path integral on a four-dimensional hypercubic lattice — as the first-principles tool for computing non-perturbative observables with controlled uncertainties. We derive the Wilson gauge action from parallel-transporter link variables, formulate the area-law criterion for confinement via the Wilson loop, and confront fermion doubling and the Nielsen–Ninomiya no-go theorem. We analyze the Hybrid Monte Carlo algorithm as the engine of gauge-ensemble generation, gradient-flow scale setting, and the correlator machinery by which hadron masses — including the 938 MeV proton — emerge from Euclidean correlation functions. Finally, we assess the exascale era — physical-point ensembles on Frontier- and Aurora-class machines — and the sign problem barring the lattice from the finite-density frontier of the QCD phase diagram.

## 1 Introduction

The Standard Model's strong sector is a deceptively compact theory. With $N_f$ flavors of Dirac fermions $\psi_f$ in the fundamental of SU(3) and gauge fields $A_\mu^a$, the continuum Euclidean action reads

$$S_{\mathrm{QCD}} = \int d^4x\,\left[\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}^a F_{\mu\nu}^a + \sum_f \bar{\psi}_f(\gamma_\mu D_\mu + m_f)\psi_f\right],$$

with $D_\mu = \partial_\mu + igA_\mu$. Asymptotic freedom renders high-energy scattering computable order by order in $\alpha_s$ [1], but the same beta function makes QCD strongly coupled in the infrared: at scales of order $\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}} \approx 300$ MeV, perturbation theory fails outright. Two of the most consequential facts about the visible universe — that quarks are permanently confined inside hadrons, and that the proton mass of 938 MeV exceeds its valence quark masses by two orders of magnitude — lie squarely in this non-perturbative regime.

In 1974, Kenneth Wilson supplied a non-perturbative *definition* of the theory [2]: formulate the gauge theory on a four-dimensional Euclidean lattice, replacing the continuum path integral by a finite-dimensional integral over compact group-valued variables. The lattice spacing $a$ acts as a gauge-invariant ultraviolet cutoff, and the continuum limit $a \to 0$ is recovered where the correlation length diverges in lattice units. Because the resulting partition function,

$$Z = \int \prod_{x,\mu} dU_\mu(x)\,\det D[U]\,e^{-S_G[U]},$$

is an ordinary integral with a positive-definite weight at zero chemical potential, it can be evaluated by Monte Carlo importance sampling. Lattice QCD is thus not a model of the strong interaction — it *is* QCD, regulated.

## 2 Background: Continuum Symmetries and Their Discretization

### The gauge principle on a lattice

The central difficulty of discretizing a gauge theory is preserving exact gauge invariance at finite lattice spacing. Wilson's insight was to place the fundamental degrees of freedom on *links*: to each directed edge from site $x$ to $x+a\hat{\mu}$ assign a parallel transporter

$$U_\mu(x) = \mathcal{P}\exp\left(ig\int_x^{x+a\hat{\mu}} A_\nu\,dx_\nu\right) \in \mathrm{SU}(3),$$

transforming as $U_\mu(x) \to g(x)\,U_\mu(x)\,g^\dagger(x+a\hat{\mu})$. Any traced product of links around a closed loop is then exactly gauge invariant. The smallest such loop — the $1\times 1$ square or *plaquette* —

$$U_{\mu\nu}(x) = U_\mu(x)U_\nu(x+a\hat{\mu})U_\mu^\dagger(x+a\hat{\nu})U_\nu^\dagger(x)$$

yields, upon expanding $U_\mu(x) = \exp(igaA_\mu(x))$, the Wilson gauge action [2]:

$$S_G = \beta\sum_{x}\sum_{\mu<\nu}\left[1 - \frac{1}{N_c}\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,U_{\mu\nu}(x)\right],\qquad \beta = \frac{2N_c}{g^2}.$$

Because the measure $\prod dU_\mu$ is the compact Haar measure, no gauge fixing is required and no Faddeev–Popov ghosts appear.

### The Wilson loop and the confinement criterion

Confinement acquires a sharp, computable definition through the Wilson loop $W(C) = \mathrm{Tr}\,\mathcal{P}\exp(ig\oint_C A\cdot dx)$, whose expectation value encodes the static quark–antiquark potential:

$$V(R) = -\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\ln\langle W(R\times T)\rangle.$$

> **Theorem:** *(Wilson's area-law criterion.)* If $\langle W(C)\rangle \sim \exp(-\sigma A(C))$ decays with the minimal area $A(C)$ spanning $C$ (area law), then $V(R) = \sigma R$ grows linearly and static quarks are confined with string tension $\sigma$. If instead $\langle W(C)\rangle \sim \exp(-\kappa P(C))$ decays with the perimeter (perimeter law), the potential saturates and charges are screened.

At strong coupling ($\beta \to 0$), the area law follows combinatorially: expanding $e^{-S_G}$, the only graphs surviving group integration are those that *tile* the loop's interior with plaquettes, each contributing one power of $\beta$, so $\langle W\rangle \sim \beta^{A/a^2}$ and $\sigma = -\ln\beta/a^2$ [2]. Confinement is thus *automatic* in the strong-coupling limit — the first demonstration of quark confinement in a field theory above two dimensions. The deep question is whether the area law persists to weak coupling, where the continuum limit lives. Numerical work beginning with Creutz's SU(2) simulations in 1979 answered affirmatively: the string tension scales per asymptotic freedom, with no deconfining bulk transition for SU(3) [9].

## 3 Methodology: Fermions, Ensembles, and Physical Units

### 3.1 The doubling problem and fermion discretizations

Discretizing the Dirac operator with a symmetric difference produces sixteen species where one was intended: the propagator $S(p)^{-1} = i\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\sin(p_\mu a)/a + m$ has poles at every corner of the Brillouin zone. This is no mere artifact; it is topology.

> **Theorem:** *(Nielsen–Ninomiya no-go theorem.)* No lattice fermion action can simultaneously be (i) local, (ii) translationally invariant, (iii) Hermitian with a real action, and (iv) chirally symmetric while describing a single Weyl fermion species [8].

One condition must be sacrificed, and each choice defines a research program:

| Formulation | Sacrificed property | Doublers | Chiral symmetry | Cost (relative) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wilson (clover) | chiral symmetry | none | explicitly broken | 1 (baseline) |
| Staggered (HISQ) | single species | 4 "tastes" | remnant U(1) | $\sim$1/8 |
| Twisted mass | flavor/chiral | none | broken, auto $O(a)$-improved | $\sim$1 |
| Domain wall | 4D locality | none | approximate, controlled by $L_s$ | $\sim$10–30 |
| Overlap | ultralocality | none | exact (Ginsparg–Wilson) | $\sim$100 |

Wilson fermions add an irrelevant dimension-five operator giving doublers masses of order $1/a$, at the price of explicit chiral breaking and $O(a)$ artifacts (removable by the clover term). Staggered fermions [3] spin-diagonalize the naive action to leave four degenerate *tastes*; the fourth-root trick recovers one physical flavor per field, validated empirically by the precision staggered program [6]. Domain-wall and overlap fermions satisfy the Ginsparg–Wilson relation $\gamma_5D + D\gamma_5 = aD\gamma_5D$, realizing an exact lattice chiral symmetry at steep computational cost.

### 3.2 The Hybrid Monte Carlo engine

Integrating out the Grassmann fields yields $\det D[U]$, a highly nonlocal functional of the links. The Hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC) algorithm of Duane, Kennedy, Pendleton, and Roweth [4] samples $P[U] \propto \det D[U]\,e^{-S_G[U]}$ exactly (up to integration error removed by a Metropolis step):

1. Represent $\det D$ via pseudofermions: $\det D = \int\mathcal{D}\phi^\dagger\mathcal{D}\phi\,\exp(-\phi^\dagger(D^\dagger D)^{-1}\phi)$.
2. Introduce conjugate momenta $\pi_\mu(x)$ and Hamiltonian $H = \tfrac{1}{2}\pi^2 + S_G[U] + \phi^\dagger(D^\dagger D)^{-1}\phi$.
3. Evolve $(U,\pi)$ with a reversible, area-preserving leapfrog integrator.
4. Accept or reject with probability $\min(1, e^{-\Delta H})$, guaranteeing exactness.

Modern refinements — Hasenbusch mass preconditioning, multiple time scales, and adaptive multigrid deflation of the Dirac operator's low modes — have cut the cost of physical-point simulations by orders of magnitude.

### 3.3 Scale setting and the continuum limit

The lattice computes only dimensionless ratios; the spacing $a$ must be fixed by matching one dimensionful quantity to experiment. The modern standard is the gradient-flow scales $t_0$ and $w_0$, with small statistical error and mild quark-mass dependence. A complete calculation then requires three extrapolations, each with a controlled effective-theory description:

- **Continuum:** $a \to 0$ at fixed physics, artifacts parameterized by the Symanzik effective theory ($O(a^2)$ for improved actions).
- **Chiral:** $m_{ud} \to m_{ud}^{\mathrm{phys}}$ via chiral perturbation theory — or eliminated by simulating directly at the physical pion mass.
- **Infinite volume:** $L \to \infty$, with finite-size corrections suppressed as $e^{-m_\pi L}$ for stable hadrons.

### 3.4 Spectroscopy from Euclidean correlators

Hadron masses are extracted from two-point functions $C(t) = \langle O(t)O^\dagger(0)\rangle = \sum_n |Z_n|^2 e^{-E_n t}$, whose long-time falloff is dominated by the ground state. The effective mass $m_{\mathrm{eff}}(t) = \ln[C(t)/C(t+a)]$ plateaus at the ground-state energy; variational (GEVP) analyses over operator bases resolve excitations, and smeared sources suppress excited-state contamination:

```python
import numpy as np

def effective_mass(corr, a=1.0):
    """Effective mass from a Euclidean two-point correlator."""
    c = np.asarray(corr, dtype=float)
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
        m = np.log(c[:-1] / c[1:]) / a
    return m

def plateau_average(m_eff, tmin, tmax):
    """Correlated-constant fit over the plateau window [tmin, tmax]."""
    window = m_eff[tmin:tmax + 1]
    return np.mean(window), np.std(window) / np.sqrt(len(window))

# Example: C(t) = A exp(-m t) + A' exp(-m' t) on synthetic data
t = np.arange(48)
C = 1.0 * np.exp(-0.42 * t) + 0.6 * np.exp(-0.85 * t)
m_eff = effective_mass(C)
m0, dm0 = plateau_average(m_eff, 20, 30)
print(f"ground state mass = {m0:.4f} +/- {dm0:.4f} (lattice units)")
```

## 4 Deep Dive: The Machinery of the Lattice

### 4.1 Strong-Coupling Confinement Revisited

The tiling argument of Section 2 exhibits the lattice's fusion of field theory and statistical mechanics. At $\beta = 0$ the weight is unity and link integrals factorize; $\int dU\,U = 0$ kills every untiled graph. Each link of the loop's perimeter must pair with a conjugate link from the action expansion, and consistency propagates inward: the minimal surface spanning $C$ must be tiled completely, at cost $\beta$ per plaquette. The area law follows *combinatorially*, with no reference to perturbation theory. Crucially, the confining phase connects smoothly to weak coupling — SU(3) has no deconfining bulk transition separating strong coupling from the continuum limit [9]. Confinement is not a strong-coupling artifact; it is a property of the theory.

### 4.2 Doubling, the Nielsen–Ninomiya Theorem, and Chiral Fermions

The Nielsen–Ninomiya theorem [8] is a topological statement: a chirally symmetric lattice Dirac operator defines a vector field on the Brillouin torus whose zeros — the fermion species — carry chiral charges summing to zero by the Poincaré–Hopf theorem. Doublers are as unavoidable as the impossibility of combing a hairy ball. The most satisfying escape is the Ginsparg–Wilson relation, which deforms rather than abandons chiral symmetry: the lattice action is invariant under $\delta\psi = i\epsilon\gamma_5(1 - aD)\psi$, and the Ward identities reproduce the axial anomaly *exactly* at finite spacing, with the index theorem $\mathrm{Tr}\,\gamma_5(1-aD) = n_+ - n_-$ counting topological zero modes. Overlap fermions realize this via $D_{\mathrm{ov}} = 1 + \gamma_5\,\mathrm{sgn}(H_W)$ — the reason chiral fermions remain a premium product in large-scale simulation.

### 4.3 Hybrid Monte Carlo and the Cost of the Vacuum

Generating one independent configuration at physical quark masses on a $96^3\times192$ lattice costs on the order of $10^5$–$10^6$ Dirac inversions, each a sparse solve on $\sim 10^8$ unknowns. The cost scaling is brutal — empirically $\propto a^{-7}$ or worse as $a \to 0$ — driven by the condition number $\kappa(D) \sim 1/(am_q)$ diverging toward the chiral limit and by *critical slowing down* of long-wavelength modes. Two algorithmic revolutions tamed this: *mass preconditioning* and *multiple time scales* split the determinant into cheap and expensive factors, and *adaptive multigrid* exploits the local coherence of low Dirac eigenmodes to deflate the near-null space, cutting iteration counts by an order of magnitude. A subtler obstruction is *topology freezing*: as $a \to 0$, HMC trajectories cease tunneling between topological sectors; open boundary conditions and metadynamics are active countermeasures. The net result: $N_f = 2+1+1$ simulations at the physical point — unthinkable in 2005 — are now routine production.

### 4.4 From Correlators to the Proton: the BMW Spectrum

The landmark demonstration that this machinery reproduces reality came from the Budapest–Marseille–Wuppertal (BMW) collaboration in 2008 [5]. Simulating $N_f = 2+1$ clover-improved Wilson fermions at pion masses down to 190 MeV, three lattice spacings, and $m_\pi L \gtrsim 4$, they performed controlled chiral, continuum, and infinite-volume extrapolations with all systematics quantified. Using $m_\pi$, $m_K$, and $m_\Omega$ to fix the quark masses and the scale, every other mass was a *prediction*:

| Hadron | Lattice QCD (MeV) | Experiment (MeV) |
|---|---|---|
| $\rho$ | 775 | 775 |
| $K^*$ | 895 | 892 |
| $N$ (proton/neutron) | 940 | 939 |
| $\Lambda$ | 1116 | 1116 |
| $\Sigma$ | 1190 | 1193 |
| $\Xi$ | 1320 | 1318 |
| $\Delta$ | 1230 | 1232 |
| $\Sigma^*$ | 1383 | 1385 |
| $\Xi^*$ | 1533 | 1530 |
| $\Omega$ | 1672 (scale input) | 1672 |

*(Central values rounded; the full results agree with experiment within quoted statistical and systematic uncertainties [5].)*

The proton mass — 99% of the mass of ordinary matter — is thus computed from the QCD Lagrangian at percent-level precision, emerging almost entirely from gluon field energy and chiral symmetry breaking rather than Higgs-generated quark masses. The FLAG reviews [7] now aggregate such calculations into world averages — quark masses, $\alpha_s$, decay constants, CKM form factors — each graded on continuum, chiral, volume, and renormalization systematics.

### 4.5 Exascale Computing and the Finite-Density Sign Problem

State-of-the-art ensembles — MILC's HISQ, CLS's Wilson, BMW's programs — run on exascale hardware: Frontier (Oak Ridge) and Aurora (Argonne) deliver $10^{18}$ flop/s, and a single campaign consumes tens of millions of GPU-hours. The frontier has moved from "can we compute the spectrum?" to precision electroweak physics: the hadronic vacuum polarization contribution to the muon $g-2$, where BMW's 2021 result sharpened a tantalizing tension with dispersive estimates; $K\to\pi\pi$ and direct CP violation; and nucleon structure — axial charge, form factors, and parton distributions via Large-Momentum Effective Theory.

One frontier remains closed. At nonzero baryon chemical potential $\mu_B$, the determinant becomes complex, $\det D(\mu) = [\det D(-\mu)]^*$, and the Monte Carlo weight loses positivity — the *sign problem*. Troyer and Wiese proved that solving the generic fermionic sign problem is NP-hard [10], so no universal algorithmic fix can exist. The lattice is therefore barred from direct simulation of neutron-star-core matter and much of the QCD phase diagram. Workarounds — Taylor expansion in $\mu_B/T$, imaginary chemical potential, reweighting, complex Langevin, Lefschetz thimbles — each work in limited regimes. Until this barrier falls, first-principles access to cold dense QCD remains the field's grandest open problem.

## 5 Empirical Results and Precision Tests

The lattice program is validated not by a single result but by a web of mutually consistent precision tests:

- **Light-hadron spectrum.** The BMW 2008 calculation [5] reproduces a dozen hadron masses at the percent level from first principles — the empirical anchor of this thesis.
- **Quark masses and $\alpha_s$.** FLAG 2019 [7] averages give $\overline{\mathrm{MS}}$ quark masses and $\alpha_s(M_Z) = 0.1182(12)$, competitive with or superior to any other method.
- **Decay constants and CKM physics.** Sub-percent $f_K/f_\pi$ yields $|V_{us}|$; $B$-meson decay constants and mixing bag parameters constrain the unitarity triangle.
- **Nucleon structure.** The lattice $g_A$ now matches experiment at the percent level, a nontrivial check given the historic struggle with excited-state contamination.
- **Muon $g-2$.** BMW's 2021 hadronic vacuum polarization result showed lattice QCD can adjudicate a multi-$\sigma$ experimental–theoretical discrepancy — the field's highest-profile confrontation with data to date.

Each result carries a complete error budget — statistical, continuum, chiral, volume, renormalization, and increasingly QED and strong-isospin-breaking corrections — the discipline that separates lattice QCD from model-building.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **The sign problem.** Finite-density QCD is inaccessible to standard importance sampling, and complexity theory suggests the obstruction is fundamental rather than technical [10].
2. **Critical slowing down and topology freezing.** Toward the continuum limit, autocorrelation times of topological and long-distance observables diverge; open boundaries and multiscale thermalization mitigate but do not cure the problem.
3. **The baryon signal-to-noise catastrophe.** Parisi–Lepage scaling dictates that nucleon correlator noise grows as $\exp[(m_N - \tfrac{3}{2}m_\pi)t]$, strangling multi-baryon calculations at large Euclidean times.
4. **Disconnected diagrams.** Flavor-singlet quantities (the $\eta'$ mass, nucleon sigma terms) require all-to-all propagators; stochastic estimators with variance reduction make them tractable but expensive.
5. **QED and isospin breaking.** Percent-level phenomenology demands QED formulations and $m_u \neq m_d$ simulations, roughly doubling ensemble-generation costs.
6. **Real-time dynamics.** Euclidean correlators cannot directly yield scattering amplitudes or transport coefficients without ill-posed analytic continuation, though the Lüscher finite-volume formalism keeps pushing this boundary.

## 7 Conclusion

Lattice QCD realizes Wilson's 1974 vision [2] in full: a mathematically well-defined, systematically improvable, first-principles formulation of the strong interaction in which confinement, chiral symmetry breaking, and the hadron spectrum are *computed*, not postulated. From link variables through the Nielsen–Ninomiya theorem [8], the Hybrid Monte Carlo revolution [4], and the BMW *ab initio* spectrum [5], each obstacle — doubling, critical slowing down, scale setting, renormalization — has yielded to theoretical insight and algorithmic ingenuity validated against experiment. The FLAG enterprise [7] now curates lattice results as precision Standard Model inputs on par with collider measurements. Two grand challenges define the coming decade: breaching the finite-density sign problem [10] to unlock the QCD phase diagram and neutron-star matter, and exploiting exascale architectures for fully controlled nuclear physics from quarks and gluons. The lattice has already shown that the mass of the visible universe is calculable; what remains is to calculate everything else.
