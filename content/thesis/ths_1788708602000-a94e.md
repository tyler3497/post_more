# From k-mers to Telomere-to-Telomere: The Graph-Theoretic Foundations and Modern Practice of De Novo Genome Assembly

## Abstract

*De novo* genome assembly — reconstructing an unknown genome sequence solely from sequencing reads — is among the most consequential computational problems in biology. This article presents a unified treatment of the field's two dominant graph-theoretic paradigms, de Bruijn graph assembly and overlap-layout-consensus (OLC), and explains how the long-read revolution transformed both. We derive the Eulerian-path formulation of de Bruijn assembly, Myers's string-graph formalism for OLC, and the role of *k*-mer spectra in error correction and quality assessment. We analyze how PacBio HiFi circular-consensus reads and Oxford Nanopore ultra-long reads jointly enabled the telomere-to-telomere era, with the hifiasm and verkko assemblers delivering haplotype-resolved, near-gapless human assemblies. Rigorous quality metrics — N50, Phred-scale quality values, BUSCO gene completeness, and reference-free *k*-mer completeness — are defined and interpreted. We survey the landmark T2T-CHM13 human assembly and the Human Pangenome Reference Consortium's graph-based reference, and close with the field's remaining limitations.

## 1 Introduction

A genome assembler receives billions of short strings — the reads produced by a sequencing instrument — and must return a small number of long strings that reproduce, as faithfully as possible, the genome of the sequenced organism. No reference sequence is available; the reconstruction is *de novo*, from nothing. The formal difficulty of the problem is easy to state and hard to solve: given a multiset of substrings of an unknown string *G*, recover *G*. Sequencing errors corrupt the substrings, repeats of *G* generate ambiguous orderings of them, and coverage fluctuates stochastically, so the unknown string is rarely recoverable uniquely. Assembly is therefore an exercise in **graph-based inference under uncertainty**, and its history is the history of two competing graph models of the same data [1][2].

Assembler output is organized into *contigs* — contiguous sequences built from overlapping reads — and, when linked information bridges gaps, *scaffolds*: ordered, oriented contigs separated by estimated gaps. The ideal is one contig per chromosome, telomere to telomere. For two decades this was unreachable for complex eukaryotes: every reference held thousands of gaps, most hiding the genome's most consequential regions — centromeres, telomeres, segmental duplications. The last decade changed this decisively: reads became simultaneously **long** and **accurate**, algorithms learned to exploit both at once, and the telomere-to-telomere (T2T) era began [4].

This article surveys sequencing technologies and the two great paradigms (Section 2), develops the methodology of de Bruijn graphs, string graphs, and *k*-mer spectra (Section 3), dives into graph simplification, repeat resolution, phasing, and quality metrics (Section 4), evaluates the landmark T2T-CHM13 and pangenome achievements (Section 5), and confronts remaining limitations (Section 6).

## 2 Background: Sequencing Technologies and the Two Great Paradigms

### 2.1 Read technologies

Every assembly algorithm is a negotiation with the error profile and length distribution of its input reads. The landscape is summarized below.

| Technology | Typical length | Accuracy | Dominant error mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sanger | 500–1,000 bp | Q20–Q30 | substitutions |
| Illumina short reads | 100–300 bp | >Q30 | substitutions |
| PacBio CLR | 10–25 kb | ~Q20 (85–90%) | indels, random |
| PacBio HiFi (CCS) | 10–25 kb | >Q30 (>99.9%) | rare, random |
| Oxford Nanopore (standard) | 10–100 kb | Q20–Q30 | indels, homopolymers |
| Oxford Nanopore ultra-long | >100 kb (N50 > 100 kb, up to Mb) | Q20–Q30 | indels, homopolymers |

The decisive development of the late 2010s was length *and* accuracy together. PacBio's circular consensus sequencing (CCS) reads each circularized template molecule repeatedly, collapsing random errors by majority vote into **HiFi reads** that are both long (~15–20 kb) and >99.9% accurate [3]. Oxford Nanopore pushed read *length* to the megabase scale with **ultra-long** reads at higher per-base error [4]. Modern assemblers consume both at once — the combination that finally defeated the repeats shielding 8% of the human genome.

### 2.2 Overlap–Layout–Consensus (OLC)

The older paradigm, dominant from the Human Genome Project through the Sanger and early 454 eras, is **overlap–layout–consensus**. Its three phases are conceptually transparent:

1. **Overlap** — compute all significant pairwise overlaps between reads (classically O(n²) with suffix-tree or seed-and-extend acceleration).
2. **Layout** — order and orient the reads into a tiling path consistent with the overlaps, discarding overlaps implied by longer ones (transitive reduction).
3. **Consensus** — derive the final sequence by majority vote over the multiple alignment of overlapping reads.

OLC preserves read coherence: each read is an intact string, and long reads carry long-range information through overlaps directly. Its cost is the overlap computation itself, which grows quadratically with read count, and the fact that layout over an overlap graph is, in general, a hard combinatorial problem.

### 2.3 De Bruijn graph assembly

The competing paradigm, introduced by Pevzner, Tang, and Waterman in 2001 [1], decomposes reads into *k*-mers and models assembly as an Eulerian path problem:

> **Theorem (Euler–Hierholzer):** A directed graph admits an Eulerian trail using every edge exactly once if and only if at most one vertex has out-degree minus in-degree equal to 1, at most one vertex has in-degree minus out-degree equal to 1, all other vertices are balanced, and every vertex with nonzero degree belongs to a single weakly connected component of the underlying undirected graph.

The theorem's algorithmic payload is that Eulerian trails are computable in **linear time** — unlike Hamiltonian paths through overlap graphs, which are NP-hard. Assembly is thus recast from an intractable search into a tractable graph traversal. That single reduction is the intellectual core of the de Bruijn assembly era, and it explains why de Bruijn assemblers (EULER, Velvet, ABySS, SPAdes, SOAPdenovo) dominated the short-read decade: billions of short reads collapse into a graph whose size depends on the genome, not the read count, since each distinct *k*-mer is stored only once.

## 3 Methodology: Graphs at the Heart of Assembly

### 3.1 The de Bruijn construction

Given a set of reads and an integer *k*, the de Bruijn graph *D* is constructed as follows. Each distinct (*k*−1)-mer appearing in any read becomes a **vertex**. Each distinct *k*-mer becomes a **directed edge** from its (*k*−1)-length prefix to its (*k*−1)-length suffix. A read of length *L* thus traces a walk of *L − k + 1* edges through *D*, and the genome itself corresponds to an Eulerian trail through the error-free graph. Assemblers then emit contigs by walking maximal non-branching paths — *unitigs* — through the simplified graph.

A minimal implementation of the construction is instructive:

```python
from collections import defaultdict

def debruijn_graph(reads, k):
    """Build a de Bruijn graph: vertices are (k-1)-mers, edges are k-mers."""
    edges = defaultdict(list)
    indeg = defaultdict(int)
    outdeg = defaultdict(int)
    for read in reads:
        for i in range(len(read) - k + 1):
            kmer = read[i:i + k]
            u, v = kmer[:-1], kmer[1:]
            edges[u].append((v, kmer))
            outdeg[u] += 1
            indeg[v] += 1
    return edges, indeg, outdeg

def unitigs(edges, indeg, outdeg):
    """Walk maximal non-branching paths; each is a contig candidate."""
    contigs, seen = [], set()
    for u in edges:
        if outdeg[u] != 1 or indeg[u] != 1:          # branch point: start here
            for v, kmer in edges[u]:
                if (u, kmer) in seen:
                    continue
                seq, seen_edge = kmer, (u, kmer)
                while outdeg[v] == 1 and indeg[v] == 1:
                    w, wk = edges[v][0]
                    seen.add((v, wk))
                    seq += wk[-1]
                    v = w
                seen.add(seen_edge)
                contigs.append(seq)
    return contigs
```

The parameter *k* controls the central trade-off of the method. Small *k* increases graph connectivity but merges distinct genomic repeats into single nodes, fragmenting the assembly. Large *k* resolves longer repeats but demands longer reads (a *k*-mer cannot span a repeat longer than *k*) and amplifies the effect of sequencing errors, since each error corrupts *k* overlapping *k*-mers. Assemblers like SPAdes and IDBA therefore iterate over multiple values of *k*.

### 3.2 The string graph and transitive reduction

Myers's 2005 **string graph** [2] showed that the Eulerian insight does not require *k*-mer decomposition at all. In the string graph, each read contributes two vertices (its two ends), and each overlap contributes two labeled directed edges encoding the non-overlapping overhang. Every path in the graph is a DNA sequence hypothesis consistent with the observed overlaps. Reads strictly contained in other reads — typically ~40% of a shotgun dataset — are discarded, since they carry no information beyond their containers.

The critical operation is **transitive reduction**: an edge *f → h* is removed whenever a read *g* exists such that *f → g* and *g → h*, because the direct edge is implied by the two-step path. Myers gave a linear-expected-time algorithm for this reduction in the assembly context, and proved that the reduced string graph is, in a precise sense, the *k*-mer-free analogue of the de Bruijn graph. Modern long-read assemblers (hifiasm, verkko's string-graph phase, Canu) are all descendants of this formalism: with accurate long reads, overlap graphs are sparse enough to build and reduce directly, recovering the read coherence that *k*-mer shredding destroys.

### 3.3 k-mer spectra and error correction

Before any graph is walked, the *k*-mer **multiplicity spectrum** — the histogram of how many times each distinct *k*-mer occurs in the reads — diagnoses the dataset. In a typical whole-genome shotgun experiment, the spectrum shows a Poisson-like peak at the mean coverage *C* (homozygous *k*-mers), a half-height peak at *C/2* (heterozygous *k*-mers in diploid samples), and a towering peak near multiplicity 1: the error *k*-mers, since a single-base error generates up to *k* novel *k*-mers. **Spectral error correction** exploits this: *k*-mers below a multiplicity threshold are deemed erroneous and corrected toward high-multiplicity neighbors within small Hamming distance. The same spectrum estimates genome size (*G ≈ K_total / C*, the GenomeScope principle) and heterozygosity before assembly begins. Reference-free quality assessment later reuses the identical machinery (Section 4.4) [7].

## 4 Deep Dive: The Mechanics of Modern Assembly

### 4.1 Graph simplification: tips, bubbles, and bulges

Real sequencing data produces tangled graphs. Three local structures dominate:

- **Tips** — short dead-end branches caused by errors near read ends or by coverage dropouts. They are pruned by removing short paths terminating in degree-0 vertices.
- **Bubbles** — pairs of divergent paths that reconverge, caused by sequencing errors or heterozygous variants. Assemblers *pop* bubbles by collapsing near-identical parallel paths, choosing the higher-coverage branch as the consensus.
- **Bulges and whirls** — short cycles and complex local tangles, often from tandem repeats, resolved or excised by coverage and length heuristics.

Haplotype-aware assemblers treat heterozygous bubbles differently from error bubbles: rather than popping them, they *phase* them, keeping both alleles and threading reads through each side — the central idea of hifiasm's phased string graph [3].

### 4.2 Repeat resolution: why long, accurate reads changed everything

Repeats are the fundamental obstruction to assembly. A repeat longer than the read length (or longer than *k* in a de Bruijn graph) collapses into a single graph node with multiple entrances and exits, and no information in the data orders them. Human segmental duplications span tens to hundreds of kilobases at >99% identity; centromeric alpha-satellite arrays span megabases. Short reads cannot resolve them — period. This is an information-theoretic statement, not an algorithmic one: the true genome is not identifiable from reads shorter than its longest near-identical repeat.

The remedy is reads that are **longer than the repeats** and **accurate enough** that near-identical repeat copies can be distinguished by their paralog-specific variants. The read length–accuracy scatter below shows why the HiFi + ultra-long combination was decisive: HiFi supplies base-level accuracy for distinguishing repeat copies, while ultra-long ONT reads supply the spanning length to order them.

![Figure 2. Sequencing technologies by read length and accuracy: the long-accurate quadrant is what defeated human repeats.](ths_1788708602000-a94e-1.webp)

In practice, assemblers resolve repeats through a hierarchy: *k*-mer or overlap graph first, then read threading (mapping long reads through graph tangles to disambiguate branches), then scaffolding with ultra-long reads or Hi-C, and finally gap filling. Verkko automates exactly this pipeline: a multiplex de Bruijn graph from HiFi reads provides base accuracy, and ultra-long ONT reads are threaded through the graph to untangle the longest repeats [5].

### 4.3 Haplotype phasing: hifiasm and verkko

Diploid assembly must *assemble* the sequence and *phase* alleles to parental haplotypes simultaneously. Collapsing both haplotypes into one consensus — the historical default — fabricates a mosaic genome that exists in no cell.

**hifiasm** (Cheng et al., 2021) [3] attacks the problem inside the graph. Its three innovations are: (i) *haplotype-aware error correction*, which corrects sequencing errors while deliberately preserving heterozygous alleles, so that phase information survives correction; (ii) a *phased string graph* in which only reads from the same haplotype are connected, so the graph itself encodes phasing; and (iii) *graph trio binning* (or Hi-C integration), which uses parental or chromatin-contact data to label graph components by haplotype. On human and even hexaploid (~30 Gb) redwood data, hifiasm consistently produced the most contiguous haplotype-resolved assemblies of its era.

**verkko** (Rautiainen et al., 2023) [5] extends the strategy to the T2T regime. It builds a *multiplex de Bruijn graph* from accurate HiFi reads — capturing variation at multiple *k* scales simultaneously — then progressively simplifies it by integrating ultra-long ONT reads and haplotype-specific markers. On the HG002 human genome, verkko assembled **20 of 46 diploid chromosomes without gaps at 99.9997% accuracy**, fully automatically. The comparison below summarizes the modern assembler landscape:

| Assembler | Graph model | Input | Phasing | T2T-capable |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| hifiasm | phased string graph | HiFi (+Hi-C/trio) | yes, haplotype-resolved | yes, with ultra-long |
| verkko | multiplex de Bruijn + threading | HiFi + ultra-long ONT | yes, diploid | yes |
| Canu | overlap (adaptive *k*-mer weighting) | CLR/HiFi/ONT | trio-binning variants | partially |
| Flye | repeat graph | CLR/ONT | no (consensus) | partially |

### 4.4 Measuring quality: N50, QV, BUSCO, and k-mer completeness

An assembly without quality metrics is a rumor. The standard battery is fourfold:

- **N50 / NG50** — the contig length *L* such that contigs of length ≥ *L* cover half the assembly (N50) or half the estimated genome size (NG50). It measures contiguity only, not correctness; a single chimeric contig can inflate it.
- **Consensus quality value (QV)** — the Phred-scaled per-base error rate, *QV = −10·log₁₀(E)*, where *E* is the probability that a consensus base is wrong. QV 40 means 99.99% accuracy; QV 50, 99.999%. Merqury [7] estimates QV *reference-free*: *k*-mers present in the assembly but absent from accurate reads must be consensus errors.
- **k-mer completeness** — the fraction of reliable read *k*-mers (excluding the error peak) found in the assembly. Missing high-multiplicity *k*-mers mean missing sequence; the spectra-cn plot further reveals false duplications (assembly *k*-mers with inflated copy number) and haplotype collapse.
- **BUSCO** — the fraction of conserved single-copy orthologs recovered complete and single-copy, a gene-centric completeness check orthogonal to *k*-mers.

A compact N50 implementation clarifies the statistic:

```python
def n50(lengths, genome_size=None):
    """N50 (or NG50 if genome_size given) of a list of contig lengths."""
    target = (genome_size or sum(lengths)) / 2
    cumulative = 0
    for L in sorted(lengths, reverse=True):
        cumulative += L
        if cumulative >= target:
            return L

# Example: a human assembly with contig N50 of 50 Mb
contigs = [150_000_000, 90_000_000, 50_000_000, 20_000_000, 5_000_000]
print(n50(contigs))  # 90000000
```

The modern standard, set by the Vertebrate Genomes Project, is roughly: contig N50 in the tens of megabases, QV ≥ 40, *k*-mer completeness > 95%, and BUSCO completeness > 95% — with haplotype-resolved assemblies reported *per haplotype*, not merged.

## 5 Empirical Results and Case Studies: T2T-CHM13 and the Pangenome Era

The **T2T-CHM13** assembly (Nurk et al., 2022) [4] is the field's moon landing: the first complete, gapless human genome. Built from the effectively haploid CHM13 cell line with PacBio HiFi and Oxford Nanopore ultra-long reads, it spans **3.055 billion base pairs**, gapless on every chromosome except Y, adding **~200 million base pairs** of novel sequence (1,956 gene predictions, 99 predicted protein-coding) and resolving every centromeric satellite array, recent segmental duplications, and the short arms of all five acrocentric chromosomes. Five companion *Science* papers dissected centromeres, epigenetics, repeat transcription, and duplication variation. T2T-CHM13 exposed misassemblies in GRCh38 and is now the preferred reference for complex regions.

The **Human Pangenome Reference Consortium** (Liao et al., 2023) [6] took the next step: 47 phased, diploid assemblies from ancestrally diverse individuals (94 haplotypes), each >99% complete and accurate, combined via Minigraph-Cactus into a **pangenome sequence graph** — a bidirected graph whose nodes are oriented DNA strings and whose embedded paths are the individual haplotypes. The draft pangenome adds 119 Mb of euchromatic polymorphic sequence and 1,115 gene duplications relative to GRCh38; using it for short-read analysis cut small-variant errors by 34% and increased structural-variant detection by 104%. The pangenome graph is, conceptually, the assembly graph promoted to reference status: variation is represented as graph topology (bubbles, alternative paths) rather than as coordinates against a single linear string, eliminating the reference bias that made diverse genomes systematically harder to analyze.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Heterozygosity and polyploidy.** Phased assembly of highly heterozygous diploids remains imperfect, and autopolyploid genomes (e.g., potato, sugarcane) with four or more near-identical haplotypes still defeat routine pipelines.
2. **Cost and compute.** T2T-grade assemblies require both HiFi (~30×) and ultra-long ONT coverage plus terabytes of RAM-hours; the compute remains out of reach for many labs and for population-scale cohorts.
3. **The hardest repeats.** Centromeric higher-order repeat arrays and ribosomal DNA arrays are now *assemblable* but remain the least validated regions; QV estimates are least trustworthy exactly where the biology is most interesting.
4. **Graph reference immaturity.** Pangenome graphs exist, but the ecosystem of graph-based aligners, variant callers, and browsers is young, and coordinate systems for graph variation are still stabilizing.
5. **Sampling and equity.** The draft pangenome's 47 samples underrepresent global diversity; reference bias will persist until the pangenome reflects the species, not a subset of it.
6. **Validation circularity.** Reference-free metrics like Merqury depend on the same reads used for assembly; truly orthogonal validation (BACs, optical maps, Strand-seq) remains the gold standard and is rarely applied comprehensively.

## 7 Conclusion

Assembly has traveled from an NP-hard overlap puzzle to linear-time Eulerian traversal [1], to phased string-graph inference [2][3], to automated telomere-to-telomere diploid reconstruction [5] — while the reference itself dissolved from a linear string into a pangenome graph [6]. Each transition renegotiated read length, read accuracy, and graph theory against ever more rigorous metrics [7]. T2T-CHM13 [4] proves complete human genomes are now a production process, not a heroic effort. The remaining frontier is scale: thousands of phased, T2T-grade genomes, equitably sampled, graph-represented, bias-free. The algorithms exist; the sequencing is affordable; the graphs are waiting.
