# Optimal Transport in Machine Learning: Wasserstein Geometry, Entropic Regularization, and the Sinkhorn Algorithm at Scale

## Abstract

Optimal transport (OT) furnishes a geometric language for comparing probability measures that has traveled from eighteenth-century civil engineering into the algorithmic core of modern machine learning. This thesis develops the Wasserstein theory from the classical Monge and Kantorovich formulations, through Kantorovich duality and c-transforms, to the computational revolution inaugurated by entropic regularization. We analyze Cuturi's Sinkhorn distances, showing how entropic smoothing converts an intractable linear program into a strictly convex problem solved by diagonal matrix scaling, with dimension-independent linear convergence in Hilbert's projective metric. We survey near-linear-time complexity results, log-domain stabilization, and epsilon-scaling, then turn to structural variants — sliced, unbalanced, and Gromov–Wasserstein distances — and to applications in generative modeling, domain adaptation, Wasserstein barycenters, and single-cell genomics. The thesis closes by cataloguing the statistical and computational limits that bound the theory: entropic bias, the curse of dimensionality in sample complexity, and quadratic memory scaling.

## 1 Introduction

In 1781, Gaspard Monge posed a deceptively simple engineering question: given a pile of earth to be excavated (*déblais*) and a prescribed embankment to be built (*remblais*), what is the cheapest way to move the soil? The problem — minimize the total work of transporting mass from one distribution to another — lay mostly dormant for a century and a half until Leonid Kantorovich, studying the translocation of masses in the Soviet planned economy, relaxed Monge's rigid maps into *couplings* and recognized the whole problem as a linear program [3]. What followed was one of the great unifications of applied mathematics: the same formalism describes the Earth Mover's Distance in computer vision, the displacement interpolation of fluid mechanics via the Benamou–Brenier formula, the gradient flows of partial differential equations through the Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto scheme, and, since the mid-2010s, the training objectives of deep generative models [4][6].

Machine learning adopted optimal transport because it compares distributions supported on nearly disjoint low-dimensional manifolds — the generic situation for image generators — where the KL divergence is infinite and its gradients vanish. The Wasserstein distance instead measures the *work* of morphing one distribution into the other, degrading gracefully with geometric separation [4]. Its price was computational: exact OT is a cubic linear program, useless beyond a few hundred support points.

Cuturi (2013) added an entropic penalty to the transport objective, converting the linear program into a smooth, strictly convex problem solvable by the Sinkhorn–Knopp matrix-scaling iteration — orders of magnitude faster, GPU-parallel, and differentiable end to end [1]. A decade of work has sharpened every side of the idea: near-linear-time guarantees [2], accelerated alternatives [5], log-domain stabilization, debiased divergences [6], and structural variants relaxing OT's rigid assumptions. This thesis traces that arc: from Monge to Kantorovich, from duality to Sinkhorn, from complexity theory to the modern variants and applications that made Wasserstein geometry a default language of probabilistic machine learning.

Section 2 develops the classical background (Monge, Kantorovich, Wasserstein-$p$, duality, c-transforms); Section 3 presents entropic regularization and Sinkhorn as the computational methodology; Section 4 dives into convergence, complexity, stabilization, variants, and applications; Section 5 collects empirical results and proof sketches; Sections 6–7 catalogue limitations and conclude.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Measures, pushforwards, and couplings

Let $\mathcal{X}$ be Polish with lower semi-continuous cost $c: \mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_+$. For measurable $T$, the **pushforward** $T_\#\mu$ is the law of $T(X)$, $X\sim\mu$. A **coupling** $\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ is a joint distribution with marginals $\mu,\nu$; $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ is non-empty (it contains $\mu\otimes\nu$) and weakly compact under mild conditions.

### 2.2 Monge's problem and its defects

Monge (1781) sought a *transport map* $T$ minimizing total cost:

$$\inf_{T : T_\#\mu = \nu} \int_{\mathcal{X}} c(x, T(x)) \, d\mu(x).$$

This formulation is intuitive but analytically hostile:

- **Feasibility can fail.** If $\mu = \delta_0$ is a Dirac mass and $\nu = \tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{-1} + \tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{+1}$, no map can *split* the atom at $0$; the constraint set is empty.
- **Non-convexity.** The feasible set $\{T : T_\#\mu = \nu\}$ is not convex, and minimizers need not exist even when it is non-empty.
- **Asymmetry.** The formulation privileges the source measure in a way the symmetric problem does not deserve.

### 2.3 Kantorovich's relaxation

Kantorovich (1942) replaced deterministic maps by *randomized* transport plans [3]:

$$\min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}} c(x,y) \, d\pi(x,y). \tag{KP}$$

This is an infinite-dimensional **linear program**; weak compactness of $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ guarantees a minimizer always exists. For histograms $a\in\Delta_n$, $b\in\Delta_m$ with cost matrix $C$ it becomes the finite LP $\min_{P\in U(a,b)}\langle P,C\rangle$ over the **transportation polytope** $U(a,b)=\{P\geq 0: P\mathbf{1}_m=a,\;P^\top\mathbf{1}_n=b\}$. When $a=b=\mathbf{1}_n/n$, the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem says the extreme points are exactly the permutation matrices — the relaxation is *tight* for assignment, and Monge's maps reappear as vertices.

### 2.4 Wasserstein-$p$ distances

Choosing $c(x,y) = d(x,y)^p$ for a ground metric $d$ yields the **Wasserstein-$p$ distance**

$$W_p(\mu,\nu) = \left( \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int d(x,y)^p \, d\pi \right)^{1/p}.$$

Its key properties:

1. **Metric structure.** $W_p$ is a genuine metric on $\mathcal{P}_p(\mathcal{X})$ (finite $p$-th moments); on compact spaces it metrizes weak convergence.
2. **The $p=1$ miracle.** Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality gives $W_1(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\mathrm{Lip}(f)\leq 1}\int f\,d(\mu-\nu)$ — the formula powering Wasserstein GANs [4].
3. **The $p=2$ Riemannian structure.** $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)$ with $W_2$ is formally an infinite-dimensional Riemannian manifold (Otto calculus); geodesics are McCann's **displacement interpolation** $\mu_t=((1-t)\mathrm{id}+tT)_\#\mu$, and the Benamou–Brenier formula recasts $W_2^2$ as kinetic-energy minimization — the dynamic face of OT.

### 2.5 Duality, c-transforms, and Brenier's theorem

Every Kantorovich problem admits a dual [3]:

$$\sup_{\phi,\psi} \int \phi \, d\mu + \int \psi \, d\nu \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \phi(x) + \psi(y) \leq c(x,y) \; \forall x,y. \tag{DP}$$

The **c-transform** $\phi^c(y)=\inf_x[c(x,y)-\phi(x)]$ always improves a feasible pair, reducing the dual to a single **c-concave** potential. Complementary slackness concentrates the optimal plan on the *contact set* $\{(x,y):\phi(x)+\psi(y)=c(x,y)\}$. For quadratic cost with $\mu$ absolutely continuous, **Brenier's theorem** gives a unique optimal map $T=\nabla u$ with $u$ convex — Monge's problem is solvable after all under regularity.

> **Theorem (Kantorovich duality).** *If $c$ is lower semi-continuous and bounded below, then $\min(\mathrm{KP}) = \max(\mathrm{DP})$, and both extrema are attained. Moreover, at optimality $\psi = \phi^c$ and $\phi = \psi^c$.*

### 2.6 The discrete complexity wall

Exact discrete OT via network simplex or the Hungarian algorithm costs $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ on $n^2$ variables — a cubic wall that confined OT to histograms of a few hundred bins and motivated the regularized program that follows.

| Solver | Complexity | Scales to | Differentiable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Network simplex / Hungarian | $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ | $n \sim 10^2$–$10^3$ | No |
| Entropic + Sinkhorn [1] | $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^2/\delta^3)$ [2] | $n \sim 10^4$–$10^6$ (GPU) | Yes |
| Sliced Wasserstein | $\mathcal{O}(Ln \log n)$, $L$ projections | $n \sim 10^6$ | Yes |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Entropic regularization

Cuturi (2013) proposed to smooth the Kantorovich problem with an entropic penalty [1]:

$$\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) = \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int c \, d\pi + \varepsilon \, \mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \mu \otimes \nu), \tag{ROT}$$

where $\mathrm{KL}$ is the Kullback–Leibler divergence. Strict convexity yields a *unique* optimizer, differentiability in $(\mu,\nu,c)$ (so the distance can serve as a loss), and a closed-form structure from the optimality conditions:

$$\pi^\star = \mathrm{diag}(u)\, K\, \mathrm{diag}(v), \qquad K_{ij} = e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon},$$

with **scaling vectors** $u,v>0$ enforcing the marginals — a finite-dimensional reduction of the infinite-dimensional problem.

### 3.2 The Sinkhorn–Knopp iteration

Finding $u,v$ is **matrix balancing**, solved by alternating normalization (Sinkhorn–Knopp, 1967): $u \leftarrow a \oslash (Kv)$, $v \leftarrow b \oslash (K^\top u)$, each half-step enforcing one marginal exactly. This is block-coordinate ascent on the smooth dual $\max_{f,g}\langle f,a\rangle+\langle g,b\rangle-\varepsilon\sum_{i,j}e^{(f_i+g_j-C_{ij})/\varepsilon}+\varepsilon$ with **dual potentials** $f=\varepsilon\log u$, $g=\varepsilon\log v$. Every operation is a matrix–vector product: the algorithm is *GPU-native*, batchable, and differentiable, so gradients flow through unrolled iterations or implicit differentiation [1][6].

### 3.3 The Schrödinger bridge viewpoint

(ROT) is the **static Schrödinger bridge**: the coupling closest in KL divergence to independence — equivalently, the most likely Brownian evolution conditioned on its endpoints. This explains the dense, diffuse plans ($\pi^\star$ has full support) and links OT to stochastic control and diffusion models.

### 3.4 Debiased Sinkhorn divergences

A blemish: $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha,\alpha)\neq 0$ — an **entropic bias**. Genevay, Peyré, and Cuturi (2018) introduced the debiased **Sinkhorn divergence** $S_\varepsilon(\alpha,\beta)=\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha,\beta)-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha,\alpha)+\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\beta,\beta))$ [6], which restores $S_\varepsilon(\alpha,\alpha)=0$ and *interpolates* between exact OT ($\varepsilon\to 0$) and maximum mean discrepancy ($\varepsilon\to\infty$) — a smooth, positive, convex-in-each-argument workhorse loss for generative modeling [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Entropic smoothing as a geometric deformation

The parameter $\varepsilon$ continuously deforms the problem's geometry: as **$\varepsilon\to 0$**, $\Gamma$-convergence recovers unregularized OT, the limit plan being the *maximum-entropy* coupling among exact optimizers (Cominetti–San Martín) — sharpness regained, speed lost; as **$\varepsilon\to\infty$**, $\pi^\star\to a\otimes b$ and all geometric information washes out. At intermediate $\varepsilon$, plans are dense Gibbs measures with weights decaying exponentially in cost. This *blurring* is simultaneously the source of Sinkhorn's speed (no combinatorial support to discover) and its main modeling criticism (see §6) — and the maximum-entropy principle gives it a principled reading: among near-optimal transports, prefer the least committal.

### 4.2 Convergence analysis in Hilbert's projective metric

The elegant convergence analysis uses **Hilbert's projective metric** on the positive cone, $d_H(u,u')=\log\frac{\max_i(u_i/u'_i)}{\min_i(u_i/u'_i)}$, invariant under positive rescaling — exactly the gauge freedom of $(u,v)$. Birkhoff's theorem: a positive linear map $T$ contracts $d_H$ with ratio $\tanh(\Delta/4)$ for projective diameter $\Delta$ of the image cone. For the Sinkhorn map this yields an explicit linear rate [3]:

> **Theorem (Birkhoff–Hopf contraction for Sinkhorn).** *Let $\eta(K) = \max_{i,j,k,\ell} \frac{K_{ik}K_{j\ell}}{K_{i\ell}K_{jk}} \leq e^{2\|C\|_\infty/\varepsilon}$. Then one full Sinkhorn sweep contracts Hilbert's metric by $\lambda = \frac{\sqrt{\eta(K)}-1}{\sqrt{\eta(K)}+1} < 1$. In particular the number of iterations to reach precision $\delta$ is $\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\|C\|_\infty}{\varepsilon} \log \frac{1}{\delta}\big)$ — **independent of the dimension $n$**.*

The iteration count does not grow with problem size — but the rate degrades as $\varepsilon\to 0$, since $\eta(K)$ explodes when the kernel becomes nearly singular. Accuracy and speed are in direct tension through $\varepsilon$, the field's central trade-off. Franklin–Lorenz (1989) proved linear convergence earlier; the Hilbert-metric analysis gives its sharp, explicit, dimension-free form [3].

### 4.3 Complexity: near-linear time and acceleration

Altschuler, Weed, and Rigollet (2017) settled the open question — can Sinkhorn approximate *exact* OT in near-linear time? — affirmatively [2]: choose $\varepsilon\asymp\delta/\log n$ so the entropic bias is below target accuracy $\delta$; run Sinkhorn (or the greedy **Greenkhorn** variant, which updates only the most violated row/column and is markedly faster in practice) until marginal violation is small; then apply an $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ **rounding** projecting the near-feasible plan exactly onto $U(a,b)$. The result: a $\delta$-approximation of the OT *value* — and a feasible near-optimal *plan* — in $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^2/\delta^3)$ operations, with no metric assumption on $C$ [2]: the first near-linear-time guarantee for general OT.

Dvurechensky, Gasnikov, and Kroshnin (2018) showed Sinkhorn is not the end of the story: adaptive primal-dual accelerated gradient descent on the *dual* of the regularized problem improves the accuracy-parameter dependence beyond Sinkhorn's cubic $\delta^{-3}$, and their framework handles arbitrary strongly convex regularizers, not just entropy [5]. In practice Sinkhorn's simplicity, GPU-friendliness, and dimension-free iteration count keep it dominant in ML pipelines, while accelerated methods win the asymptotic race.

### 4.4 Stabilization: log-domain updates and epsilon-scaling

Naively forming $K=\exp(-C/\varepsilon)$ is catastrophic when $C_{ij}/\varepsilon\gtrsim 745$: double precision underflows to zero and updates divide by zero. The remedy is the **log-domain** formulation — iterate on dual potentials $f=\varepsilon\log u$, $g=\varepsilon\log v$ with log-sum-exp reductions, never forming $K$, stable for arbitrarily small $\varepsilon$ at the price of costlier reductions (Schmitzer's stabilized iteration; see [3, §4.4]). The complementary heuristic is **$\varepsilon$-scaling** (annealing): solve a coarse large-$\varepsilon$ problem, then warm-start successively finer ones — a continuation method that empirically slashes iteration counts, though its theory remains incomplete. Two rules of thumb: normalize $C$ (e.g., by its median) before choosing $\varepsilon$, and stop on the *marginal violation* $\|P\mathbf{1}-a\|_1$ rather than the objective, since that is what rounding needs [2][3].

### 4.5 Structural variants: sliced, unbalanced, and Gromov–Wasserstein

Vanilla OT assumes equal-mass measures on a common space with a meaningful ground cost. Three variants relax these assumptions:

| Variant | Relaxes | Complexity | Sample complexity | Cross-space? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sliced Wasserstein | dimension | $\mathcal{O}(Ln\log n)$ | $\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})$ (dimension-free) | No |
| Unbalanced OT | mass conservation | Sinkhorn-like $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ | $\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})$ | No |
| Gromov–Wasserstein | common ground space | $\mathcal{O}(n^2m^2)$ naive; non-convex | problem-dependent | **Yes** |

- **Sliced Wasserstein.** Average 1D Wasserstein distances over random projections: $SW_p^p(\mu,\nu)=\int_{S^{d-1}}W_p^p(P_{\theta\#}\mu,P_{\theta\#}\nu)\,d\sigma(\theta)$. In 1D, OT is sorting ($\mathcal{O}(n\log n)$), so Monte-Carlo over $L$ directions is blazingly fast, with dimension-free $\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})$ sample complexity — at the price of a weaker, projection-averaged geometry.
- **Unbalanced OT** (Chizat et al., Liero–Mielke–Savaré). Replace hard marginals by soft penalties, e.g. $\min_\pi\int c\,d\pi+\varepsilon\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\mu\otimes\nu)+\lambda_1D_\phi(\pi_1\|\mu)+\lambda_2D_\phi(\pi_2\|\nu)$: mass may be created or destroyed at a price — the right model for noisy data and partial matching. A generalized Sinkhorn handles it with minimal changes [3, §4.6].
- **Gromov–Wasserstein** (Mémoli). Compare measures on *different* metric spaces via internal pairwise distances: $\min_\pi\iint|d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x')-d_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,y')|^2d\pi d\pi$. Non-convex quadratic assignment, solved by conditional-gradient/entropic iterations — uniquely enabling cross-domain alignment of graphs, shapes, and embeddings with no correspondence given.

### 4.6 Applications at scale

- **Wasserstein GANs.** Arjovsky, Chintala, and Bottou (2017) replaced the Jensen–Shannon objective with $W_1$, estimated via the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual with a 1-Lipschitz critic [4]. Since $W_1$ is continuous under weak convergence where JS is not, the critic's loss becomes a *meaningful* training curve and mode collapse is mitigated. (Weight clipping was later superseded by gradient penalties — the dual is only ever approximated.)
- **Differentiable OT losses.** Debiased Sinkhorn divergences [6] give smooth, GPU-friendly generative-model losses: minibatches are compared geometrically, gradients flow through the Sinkhorn iterations, and the large-$\varepsilon$ MMD limit provides a safety net.
- **Domain adaptation.** Transport source samples onto the target distribution via the OT plan (optionally class-regularized), then train on transported source — geometry-aware alignment without adversarial training.
- **Wasserstein barycenters.** The Fréchet mean $\min_\mu\sum_k\lambda_kW_2^2(\mu,\nu_k)$ (Agueh–Carlier), computed by iterative Bregman projections (Cuturi–Doucet), averages distributions while preserving geometric structure — used in texture mixing and Bayesian posterior aggregation.
- **Single-cell genomics.** Waddington-OT applies *unbalanced* entropic OT across measurement time points to infer developmental trajectories, reconstructing ancestor and descendant cell distributions from snapshot data.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 What the experiments showed

Cuturi's MNIST benchmark remains canonical [1]: with $k$-nearest neighbors, Sinkhorn distances *outperformed* exact Earth Mover's distances while running **several orders of magnitude faster** — regularization as beneficial smoothing, not just a computational hack. The broader empirical picture:

1. **Speed.** GPU Sinkhorn on $n=10^4$–$10^5$ histograms runs in seconds where exact solvers cannot start; Greenkhorn's greedy updates typically halve wall-clock time [2].
2. **Accuracy control.** Entropic bias scales roughly as $\varepsilon\log n$; $\varepsilon$-scaling reaches $10^{-6}$ relative accuracy where fixed-$\varepsilon$ iterations stall or underflow [3].
3. **Statistical blessing.** Empirical $W_p$ converges at the cursed $\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/d})$ rate (Dudley; Fournier–Guillin), but *entropic* OT enjoys dimension-free $\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})$ sample complexity (Genevay et al., 2019) — regularization provably breaks the curse, at the cost of a smoothed estimand.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: linear convergence via Hilbert's metric

*Claim.* One Sinkhorn sweep contracts $d_H$ with ratio $\lambda=(\sqrt{\eta}-1)/(\sqrt{\eta}+1)$.

*Sketch.* A sweep is $u\mapsto T(u)=a\oslash(K(b\oslash(K^\top u)))$. The positive linear maps $u\mapsto K^\top u$, $w\mapsto Kw$ contract $d_H$ by Birkhoff's theorem with ratio $\tanh(\Delta/4)$ for projective diameter $\Delta$; entrywise division by fixed vectors is a $d_H$-isometry. A direct computation gives $\Delta\leq 2\log\eta(K)$ with $\eta(K)\leq\exp(2\|C\|_\infty/\varepsilon)$, whence the formula; Banach's theorem yields linear convergence to the unique (up to scale) fixed point. ∎

### 5.3 Proof sketch: near-linear-time approximation [2]

*Claim.* A $\delta$-approximation of $\min_{P\in U(a,b)}\langle P,C\rangle$ is achievable in $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^2/\delta^3)$.

*Sketch.* Set $\varepsilon\asymp\delta/\log n$, so entropic bias is $\mathcal{O}(\delta)$. Run Sinkhorn until marginal violation $\leq\delta'=\mathcal{O}(\delta/\|C\|_\infty)$ — $\mathcal{O}(\|C\|_\infty\varepsilon^{-1}\log(n/\delta'))$ iterations of $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ by the Hilbert-metric bound. Altschuler et al.'s $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ rounding (rank-one corrections) projects the near-feasible output into $U(a,b)$, increasing cost by at most $\mathcal{O}(\delta'\|C\|_\infty)=\mathcal{O}(\delta)$. ∎

### 5.4 Reference implementation: log-domain Sinkhorn

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import logsumexp

def log_sinkhorn(a, b, C, eps, n_iter=2000, tol=1e-9):
    """Entropic OT via stabilized log-domain Sinkhorn.

    Returns OT_eps(a, b) and the transport plan P.
    Never forms K = exp(-C/eps); stable for tiny eps.
    """
    log_a = np.log(a + 1e-300)
    log_b = np.log(b + 1e-300)
    f = np.zeros_like(a, dtype=float)  # dual potentials (log-domain)
    g = np.zeros_like(b, dtype=float)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        f_new = eps * (log_a - logsumexp((g[None, :] - C) / eps, axis=1))
        g = eps * (log_b - logsumexp((f_new[:, None] - C) / eps, axis=0))
        if np.max(np.abs(f_new - f)) < tol:
            f = f_new
            break
        f = f_new
    P = np.exp((f[:, None] + g[None, :] - C) / eps)  # Gibbs plan
    return float(np.sum(P * C)), P

def sinkhorn_divergence(a, b, C, eps, **kw):
    """Debiased Sinkhorn divergence S_eps [6]: zero on the diagonal."""
    ot_ab, _ = log_sinkhorn(a, b, C, eps, **kw)
    ot_aa, _ = log_sinkhorn(a, a, C, eps, **kw)
    ot_bb, _ = log_sinkhorn(b, b, C, eps, **kw)
    return ot_ab - 0.5 * (ot_aa + ot_bb)
```

| Regime | $\varepsilon$ heuristic | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Fast approximate geometry | $\varepsilon \approx \mathrm{median}(C)$ | kernel well-conditioned; few dozen iterations |
| Accurate OT value | $\varepsilon \approx 10^{-2}$–$10^{-3}\times \mathrm{median}(C)$ + $\varepsilon$-scaling | bias $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon\log n)$; needs log-domain |
| Differentiable loss [6] | $\varepsilon \approx 0.1$–$1.0 \times \mathrm{median}(C)$, debiased | smooth gradients; self-distance corrected |

## 6 Limitations

1. **Entropic bias.** $\mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon$ systematically overestimates transport cost and blurs plans; debiasing [6] repairs the diagonal but $S_\varepsilon$ is not a metric (no triangle inequality) and its geometry at large $\varepsilon$ is MMD-like, not Wasserstein-like.
2. **The $\varepsilon$ trilemma.** Accuracy wants $\varepsilon \to 0$, speed wants $\varepsilon$ large, stability wants $\varepsilon$ moderate. No universal selector exists; $\varepsilon$-scaling is a heuristic without a complete theory.
3. **Quadratic memory.** The kernel $K$ is dense $n \times n$; beyond $n \sim 10^5$ even forming $C$ is impossible. Remedies (online KeOps-style reductions, Nyström/low-rank kernels, minibatch OT) each introduce their own bias — minibatch OT in particular is a *biased* estimator of population OT.
4. **Statistical curse for the unregularized distance.** Estimating $W_p$ itself from samples converges at $\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/d})$ — regularization fixes the rate only by changing the estimand.
5. **Outlier sensitivity.** $W_p$ with $p \geq 1$ lets a single far-flung atom dominate the distance; unbalanced variants help but add penalty hyperparameters $\lambda_i$ with no canonical calibration.
6. **The WGAN gap.** The Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual is a supremum over *all* 1-Lipschitz functions; a neural critic with weight clipping or gradient penalty explores a tiny, poorly understood subset — what is optimized in practice is a lower bound of unknown tightness [4].
7. **Gromov–Wasserstein non-convexity.** The quadratic objective admits spurious local minima; entropic GW iterations lack global guarantees and scale poorly without further approximation.

## 7 Conclusion

Optimal transport traveled from Monge's earthworks through Kantorovich's linear programming to the center of machine learning because it measures what learning needs: the geometric work of transforming one distribution into another. Its enabling trick was entropic regularization — a single strictly convex perturbation making the problem smooth, differentiable, GPU-friendly, and solvable by the two-line Sinkhorn iteration, backed by a convergence theory (Hilbert's projective metric), a complexity theory (near-linear approximation [2], accelerated refinements [5]), and a stabilization toolkit (log-domain, $\varepsilon$-scaling) [1][3]. Debiased divergences made it a legitimate loss [6]; sliced, unbalanced, and Gromov–Wasserstein variants extended its reach to high dimensions, noisy data, and incomparable spaces. The limits are now well mapped: entropic bias, the $\varepsilon$ trilemma, quadratic memory, and the statistical curse of dimensionality. Within those bounds, Wasserstein geometry has earned its place as the default geometric language of probabilistic machine learning — and the open problems (principled $\varepsilon$ selection, linear memory, global GW optimization) define the next decade.
