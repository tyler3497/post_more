# Masked Hardware Implementations Against Power Side-Channel Attacks: Probing Models, Composable Gadgets, and Machine-Checked Assurance

## Abstract

*Power side-channel attacks such as Differential Power Analysis extract cryptographic secrets from the physical emissions of hardware, rendering otherwise secure algorithms vulnerable at the implementation layer. Masking counteracts this threat by splitting every sensitive variable into randomized shares, so that the adversary must combine information from multiple wires to recover any secret-dependent signal. This thesis develops the complete arc from attack to assurance: the correlation-based DPA methodology and the Hamming-weight leakage model; the Ishai–Sahai–Wagner probing model and its refinement through t-non-interference and t-strong-non-interference, the composability notions that let large circuits be assembled from verified gadgets; the treatment of glitches and transitions that separate provably secure software from secure hardware; threshold implementations and higher-order masked AES S-boxes as concrete constructions; and the automated verification frameworks maskVerif and SILVER that discharge security proofs at the gate-netlist level using type systems and reduced ordered binary decision diagrams. We prove core composition theorems, present Verilog and Python artifacts, and analyze the asymptotic and empirical cost of assurance across orders.*

## 1. Introduction

Modern cryptographic primitives rest on mathematical hardness. Yet a mathematically secure cipher is only as strong as its physical implementation. In 1999, Kocher, Jaffe, and Jun demonstrated that smart-card power consumption leaks enough information to recover secret keys statistically — **Differential Power Analysis (DPA)** [1]. DPA needs no invasive probing; the adversary measures current draw and correlates it against hypotheses of intermediate values.

**Masking** has emerged as the principal countermeasure. The core idea is simple: represent each secret-dependent value \(x\) as a tuple of shares \((x_1, \ldots, x_n)\) such that \(\bigoplus_i x_i = x\) and any proper subset of shares is statistically independent of \(x\). The adversary who observes at most \(t\) wires — the *t-probing adversary* of Ishai, Sahai, and Wagner [2] — learns nothing when \(n > t\). Yet physical effects such as *glitches* (spurious transient recombinations in combinational logic) and *transitions* (register overwrites leaking Hamming distances) can recombine shares and invalidate proofs conducted at the Boolean abstraction level [7].

This thesis presents a unified treatment. Section 2 develops the attack methodology and leakage models; Section 3 formalizes the probing model, the t-NI/t-SNI composability notions [4], and the robust probing model; Section 4 dives into gadget design — the ISW AND gadget, share refresh, threshold implementations [7], and higher-order masked AES S-boxes [3,8]; Section 5 covers verification via maskVerif [5] and SILVER [6]; Section 6 addresses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

> **Theorem (informal):** A masked circuit composed exclusively of t-SNI gadgets, separated by register stages that absorb glitches, is t-probing secure: any set of t adversarial probes can be perfectly simulated from at most t shares of each secret input, leaking no information about the underlying secrets [4].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Power side-channel attacks

A CMOS device consumes power whenever gates switch. In the *Hamming weight* (HW) and *Hamming distance* (HD) models, the instantaneous power \(P(t)\) satisfies

\[
P(t) \approx \alpha \cdot \mathrm{HW}(v(t)) + \beta + \eta,
\]

where \(v(t)\) is the processed data, \(\alpha, \beta\) are device constants, and \(\eta\) is noise. **Simple Power Analysis (SPA)** visually interprets single traces — for instance, distinguishing square from multiply in naive RSA exponentiation. **DPA** [1] is statistical: the adversary records \(N\) traces \(t_i\), formulates a key hypothesis \(k\), predicts a target intermediate \(v_i(k)\), and computes the *Pearson correlation*

\[
\rho(k) = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(t, \mathrm{HW}(v(k)))}{\sigma_t \cdot \sigma_{\mathrm{HW}(v(k))}}.
\]

The correct hypothesis produces a correlation spike; wrong hypotheses yield near-zero correlation. The attack complexity scales as \(O(\mathrm{SNR}^{-2})\) traces in the low-SNR regime.

| Attack class | Traces needed | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|
| SPA (visual) | 1 | Constant-time, balanced logic |
| First-order DPA | 10³–10⁶ | First-order masking (\(n=2\) shares) |
| Second-order DPA | 10⁵–10⁹ | Higher-order masking (\(n=3\) shares) |
| \(d\)-th order DPA | \(\propto \sigma^{2d}\) | Masking of order \(d\) (\(n=d+1\) shares) |

The fundamental masking result is that the data complexity of a \(d\)-th order attack grows *exponentially* in the masking order, provided shares are truly independent and uniformly random [2,3].

### 2.2 The t-probing model

Ishai, Sahai, and Wagner [2] abstract the adversary as follows:

- The circuit is a directed acyclic graph of Boolean gates over shares.
- The adversary selects up to \(t\) wires *adaptively* and observes their values.
- A masked circuit is **t-probing secure** if the joint distribution of the probed wires is independent of the secret inputs — equivalently, if a simulator can reproduce the probe distribution from at most \(t\) shares of each input, without knowledge of the secret.

The ISW construction transforms any Boolean circuit of size \(s\) into a \(t\)-probing-secure circuit of size \(O(s \cdot t^2)\) using \(n = 2t+1\) shares. The quadratic blowup in \(t\) is the price of generality; concretely instantiated gadgets (Rivain–Prouff [3], Coron et al. [8]) achieve far better constants.

### 2.3 From software proofs to hardware reality

Proving security in the abstract probing model does not guarantee security in silicon. Three physical effects break abstraction boundaries:

1. **Glitches.** In combinational logic, unequal path delays cause gates to evaluate multiple times before settling. A gate computing \(f(a_1, b_1)\) on masked shares may transiently depend on *all* shares if the cone of logic recombines them — leaking in the *robust probing model* [7].
2. **Transitions.** Writing a new value \(v'\) over an old value \(v\) in a register leaks \(\mathrm{HD}(v, v') = \mathrm{HW}(v \oplus v')\), correlating consecutive share assignments.
3. **Couplings.** Neighboring wires induce data-dependent crosstalk, effectively merging probes.

The **threshold implementation (TI)** approach of Nikova, Rechberger, and Rijmen [7] was the first provably glitch-resistant masking methodology. TI imposes three properties on the sharing \((F_1, \ldots, F_s)\) of a function \(F\):

- *Correctness:* \(\bigoplus F_i = F\).
- *Non-completeness:* every component function \(F_i\) misses at least one input share — so no single gate ever "sees" all shares, even under glitches.
- *Uniformity:* the sharing preserves uniform output distributions, enabling composability.

First-order TI requires \(s \geq td+1\) shares for a function of algebraic degree \(d\).

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Gadget-level security: t-NI and t-SNI

Proving whole-circuit probing security by enumerating \(\binom{w}{t}\) probe sets is infeasible for realistic circuits. The breakthrough of Barthe et al. [4] was to define *composable* gadget properties:

> **Definition (t-NI):** A gadget \(G\) with \(n\) input shares is *t-non-interfering* (t-NI) if for every set of \(t_1 \leq t\) internal probes and \(t_2\) output probes with \(t_1 + t_2 \leq t\), the probed values can be perfectly simulated from at most \(t_1 + t_2\) shares of each input.

> **Definition (t-SNI):** A gadget \(G\) is *t-strongly-non-interfering* (t-SNI) if the same probes can be simulated from at most \(t_1\) shares of each input — the number of *internal* probes only. Output probes are "free": the simulator receives the corresponding output shares as part of its interface.

The distinction is decisive for composition:

> **Theorem (t-SNI composition [4]):** Let \(G_1\) and \(G_2\) be t-SNI gadgets with \(G_2\) consuming the output shares of \(G_1\). Then the sequential composition \(G_2 \circ G_1\) is t-SNI.

*Proof sketch.* Consider \(t_1\) internal probes (split \(t_1'\) in \(G_1\), \(t_1''\) in \(G_2\)) and \(t_2\) output probes of the composition, with \(t_1' + t_1'' + t_2 \leq t\). The probes in \(G_2\) plus the \(t_2\) output probes simulate from \(t_1''\) shares of \(G_2\)'s inputs — i.e., of \(G_1\)'s outputs — by t-SNI of \(G_2\). These become \(t_1''\) output probes on \(G_1\), which by t-SNI of \(G_1\) simulate from \(t_1'\) input shares. Total: \(t_1' + t_1'' = t_1\) input shares. ∎

By contrast, t-NI gadgets compose only under restrictive conditions (e.g., separated by t-SNI refresh gadgets). The practical design pattern is therefore: **prove small gadgets t-SNI, refresh where needed, and assemble arbitrarily**.

### 3.2 The refresh gadget

The ISW refresh gadget on \(n\) shares uses fresh randomness \(r_1, \ldots, r_{n-1}\):

```python
def isw_refresh(shares):
    """t-SNI refresh for n shares. Consumes n-1 random bits."""
    n = len(shares)
    out = list(shares)
    for i in range(n - 1):
        r = random_bit()
        out[i] ^= r
        out[i + 1] ^= r
    return out
```

Refresh is t-SNI: any probe inside the gadget depends on at most one fresh random \(r\) XORed with input shares. Inserting refresh converts t-NI compositions into t-SNI ones at the cost of \((n-1)\) random bits per refresh — a primary optimization target (Coron et al. [8] pushed ISW multiplication randomness from \(O(n^2)\) toward the minimum).

### 3.3 Glitch-robust and robust probing models

The **robust probing model** extends the classical model: a probe on a combinational gate observes not only its stable output but the entire transient evaluation — i.e., all intermediate values in its input cone. A gadget secure in the probing model but not in the robust model is *hardware-insecure*. Two mitigations dominate:

1. **Register stages (domain separation).** Placing a register between the nonlinear share-cross-product computation and the compression layer ensures glitches cannot propagate across share domains [7].
2. **Non-complete component functions (TI).** Designing \(F_i\) so that no gate's input cone spans all shares of any secret — security holds *by construction* even with arbitrary glitch propagation [7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The ISW masked AND gadget

The ISW AND gadget [2] is the workhorse of masked hardware. For inputs \(a = \bigoplus_i a_i\), \(b = \bigoplus_i b_i\) with \(n\) shares, it computes the cross products \(a_i b_j\) for all \(i, j\), masks each with fresh randomness \(r_{i,j}\), and compresses:

\[
c_i = a_i b_i \;\oplus\; \bigoplus_{j \neq i} \bigl(r_{i,j} \oplus a_i b_j \oplus a_j b_i\bigr).
\]

```verilog
// ISW AND gadget, n=3 shares, first order (t=1)
// Registered cross-products prevent glitch recombination
module isw_and3 (
    input  wire a1, a2, a3, b1, b2, b3,
    input  wire r12, r13, r23,
    input  wire clk,
    output reg  c1, c2, c3
);
    wire p11 = a1 & b1, p22 = a2 & b2, p33 = a3 & b3;
    wire p12 = a1 & b2, p21 = a2 & b1;
    wire p13 = a1 & b3, p31 = a3 & b1;
    wire p23 = a2 & b3, p32 = a3 & b2;

    reg q12, q13, q23; // glitch barrier: register the cross terms
    always @(posedge clk) begin
        q12 <= p12 ^ p21 ^ r12;
        q13 <= p13 ^ p31 ^ r13;
        q23 <= p23 ^ p32 ^ r23;
        c1  <= p11 ^ q12 ^ q13;
        c2  <= p22 ^ q12 ^ q23;
        c3  <= p33 ^ q13 ^ q23;
    end
endmodule
```

The registered cross terms form the glitch barrier: no combinational path to a single output spans all shares within one clock cycle. Rivain and Prouff [3] generalized this gadget to \(\mathbb{F}_{2^8}\) for AES — the foundation of higher-order masked AES.

### 4.2 Threshold implementations: glitch security by construction

TI [7] achieves glitch resistance without inter-stage registers by enforcing **non-completeness**. For the AES S-box — a degree-7 function — a direct TI needs \(td + 1 = 8\) shares at first order. The standard optimization decomposes the S-box into stages of lower algebraic degree:

| Construction | Shares | Stages | Randomness | Glitch-secure? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Direct TI, degree 7 | 8 | 1 | 0 | Yes |
| Decomposed TI (stages of degree ≤ 3) | 4 | 2–3 | moderate | Yes |
| ISW + register barriers | 3 | per mult | high | Yes |
| DOM (domain-oriented masking) | \(t+1\) | 1–2 | low | Yes |

Bilgin et al. demonstrated an AES TI using a 4-share decomposition with optimized area–latency trade-offs, and subsequent work quantified the Pareto frontier between shares, stages, and randomness. The lesson: *non-completeness is a structural property of the netlist*, verifiable syntactically — which is exactly what automated tools exploit.

### 4.3 Higher-order masking of AES S-boxes

The AES S-box \(S(x) = A x^{-1} + c\) over \(\mathbb{F}_{2^8}\) is the dominant nonlinear cost in masked AES. Rivain and Prouff [3] mask the inversion via the Fermat identity \(x^{-1} = x^{254}\), evaluated as a square-and-multiply chain:

\[
x^{254} = x^{128} \cdot x^{64} \cdot x^{32} \cdot x^{16} \cdot x^8 \cdot x^4 \cdot x^2,
\]

where each multiplication is replaced by a \(d\)-th order secure field multiplier and squarings are linear (sharewise). The basic scheme needs \(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\) random field elements per multiplication at order \(d\) (\(n = d+1\) shares). Coron, Prouff, Rivain, and Roche [8] identified a subtle higher-order leak in the original refresh placement and proposed a *refresh-free* multiplication variant consuming only \(\frac{n(n-1)}{4} + O(n)\) randoms, roughly halving randomness cost, with a full proof of \(d\)-probing security for the composed AES.

A masked S-box datapath therefore has four stages: (1) **input sharing** \(x \mapsto (x_1, \ldots, x_n)\) with uniform random masks; (2) **inversion chain** of \(\approx 4\) secure multiplications with optional refresh; (3) **affine layer** \(A x_i + c_i\) sharewise with \(\bigoplus c_i = c\); (4) **register barrier** before the next round.

### 4.4 Formal verification: maskVerif and SILVER

Manual proofs do not scale to full AES netlists with hundreds of thousands of gates. Two automated frameworks dominate:

**maskVerif** [5] encodes masked programs in a small imperative language and applies a *type system* for non-interference: each variable is typed as *uniform*, *secret-dependent*, or *public*, and the checker tracks how probe sets depend on inputs. Crucially, maskVerif models **glitches** (each intermediate expression is observable) and **transitions** (sequential assignments leak Hamming distance), so verified programs are secure in the *robust* probing model. It verified masked AES at orders previously out of reach.

**SILVER** [6] ingests the *synthesized gate-level netlist* directly — the artifact that will be manufactured — and checks **statistical independence** of probe distributions using **ROBDDs**. For each set of up to \(t\) probes, SILVER constructs the joint probability distribution over secrets and masks symbolically and tests independence exactly, avoiding the false positives/negatives of heuristic type systems. It also verifies *uniformity* of sharings and operates in glitch-extended models. The cost: ROBDD sizes can blow up exponentially, so SILVER is most effective at gadget level and moderate orders.

> **Theorem (SILVER soundness [6]):** If SILVER reports that a netlist is t-probing secure (resp. uniform), then the joint distribution of any t probed wires is statistically independent of the secrets (resp. the sharing is a uniform encoding), under the modeled glitch behavior.

The two tools are complementary: maskVerif scales to large routines, while SILVER gives bit-precise assurance on synthesized hardware, including tool-inserted optimizations invisible at source level.

### 4.5 Putting it together: a verified masked AES pipeline

A modern assurance pipeline for masked hardware proceeds in stages:

1. **Gadget design** — ISW/TI/DOM multiplication and refresh gadgets, proved t-SNI by hand or by tool.
2. **Composition** — assemble S-box, round, and full cipher from t-SNI gadgets with register barriers [4].
3. **Source verification** — maskVerif [5] checks the RTL model in the robust probing model.
4. **Netlist verification** — SILVER [6] re-verifies the *synthesized* netlist, catching synthesis-introduced share recombinations.
5. **Empirical validation** — TVLA with millions of traces confirms no first-order leakage; higher-order TVLA confirms higher orders.

Skipping stage 4 is a classic failure mode: synthesis tools aggressively optimize, and an optimization that merges \(a_1 \oplus a_2\) into a single gate *before* masking with randomness destroys the security proof while preserving functionality.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Composition theorem (restated with proof)

> **Theorem (End-to-end t-probing security):** Let \(C\) be a circuit composed of t-SNI gadgets whose inter-gadget boundaries are register stages, with uniform input sharing and independent fresh randomness per gadget evaluation. Then \(C\) is t-probing secure in the robust (glitch-extended) probing model.

*Proof.* We simulate by induction over the gadget DAG in reverse topological order. For gadget \(G\) with \(t_1\) internal probes (glitch-extended to input cones) and \(t_2\) output-register probes, t-SNI yields \(\leq t_1\) input shares reproducing the probes with fresh randomness. Output probes of \(G\) become input probes of successors; register stages keep glitch cones inside gadgets, so each successor's simulation costs at most its own internal probe count. The simulator needs at most \(\sum t_1 \leq t\) shares per input — the t-probing security condition. ∎

### 5.2 Verification performance (reported figures)

| Tool | Target | Order | Result | Runtime |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| maskVerif [5] | AES (Rivain–Prouff) | \(d=2\) | t-SNI verified | seconds |
| maskVerif [5] | Full AES rounds | \(d=3\) | verified | minutes |
| SILVER [6] | ISW AND gadget | \(t=2\) | secure + uniform | seconds |
| SILVER [6] | DOM AND, Keccak \(\chi\) | \(t=1,2\) | secure | minutes–hours |
| SILVER [6] | Full AES S-box netlist | \(t=1\) | secure (glitch-extended) | hours |

The pattern is consistent: type-based verification (maskVerif) scales further but can reject secure-by-structure gadgets; exact statistical verification (SILVER) is definitive but exponential in the worst case. In practice, designers verify *gadgets* with SILVER and *compositions* with maskVerif, bridged by the t-SNI composition theorem.

### 5.3 Attack-complexity scaling

For a \(d\)-th order masked implementation with independent uniform masks, the traces needed for a successful \(d\)-th order DPA scale as \(N \propto \sigma^{2d} / \epsilon^2\) — exponential in \(d\) [1,2]. At \(d=3\) with realistic noise, attacks need \(>10^9\) traces, beyond practical acquisition: the quantitative justification for higher-order masking despite quadratic area and randomness costs.

## 6. Limitations

1. **The probing model is not the physical world.** Real adversaries observe noisy, continuous leakages — not noiseless wire values. The *noisy leakage model* and *random probing model* give tighter bounds but are harder to verify; probing security is a conservative, qualitative abstraction that can both over- and under-approximate reality (e.g., it ignores horizontal attacks combining many operations within one trace).

2. **Verification scalability.** SILVER's ROBDD-based exact analysis is exponential in the worst case; full-cipher netlists at order \(d \geq 3\) remain out of reach. maskVerif's type system is incomplete for some secure constructions (false negatives force manual proof or redesign). Compositional reasoning mitigates but does not eliminate the gap.

3. **Randomness cost and generation.** Higher-order masking consumes randomness at \(O(n^2)\) per multiplication; a masked AES at \(d=3\) needs tens of thousands of random bits per encryption. The TRNG is a single point of failure — biased or observable randomness voids every proof, and RNG leakage itself lies outside the probing model.

4. **Transitions and micro-architecture.** Register-overwrite leakage (Hamming distance) and memory-bus recombinations require explicit modeling; maskVerif handles transitions in its sequential semantics, but coupling effects and layout-dependent crosstalk remain largely unverifiable pre-silicon.

5. **Fault + side-channel composition.** Masking provides no integrity: combined attacks (e.g., faulting a share to bias distributions, then applying SCA) defeat probing-secure designs. Combined countermeasures are active research.

6. **Synthesis trust gap.** Verification of the *source* RTL does not imply security of the *manufactured* netlist unless the synthesis flow is constrained (no share-merging optimizations) or the final netlist is re-verified with SILVER.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Masked hardware has matured from the theoretical ISW construction [2] into an engineering discipline with machine-checked assurance. The intellectual spine of this thesis is the compositionality program: **t-SNI gadgets compose freely** [4]; **glitch robustness comes from register barriers or non-complete sharings** [7]; **higher-order AES S-boxes are practical via Fermat-inversion chains with optimized refresh** [3,8]; and **maskVerif [5] and SILVER [6] discharge proofs no human could complete by hand**. The DPA threat [1] that motivated this field has been answered with a rigorous methodology whose guarantees are stated as theorems and checked by machines. Open challenges — noisy-leakage verification, randomness hardening, combined fault/SCA security, and post-quantum masked implementations — define the next decade of physically secure hardware.
