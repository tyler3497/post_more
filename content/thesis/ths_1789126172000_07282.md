# Network Calculus for Deterministic Networking: Arrival Curves, Service Curves, TSN Scheduling, and Certified Delay Bounds

## Abstract

Deterministic communication — every packet delivered within a contractually bounded latency, with bounded jitter and effectively zero congestion loss — is now the central requirement of industrial automation, automotive zonal architectures, avionics, and power-grid protection. The IEEE 802.1 Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) standards and the IETF Deterministic Networking (DetNet) architecture supply the mechanisms: time-aware gates, credit-based and asynchronous shapers, cyclic forwarding, replication and elimination. Mechanisms alone, however, are not a guarantee. This thesis develops *network calculus* — the min-plus algebraic theory of deterministic queueing of Cruz, Le Boudec, and Thiran — as the rigorous foundation for *certifying* delay bounds in TSN and DetNet deployments. We formalize arrival and service curves and their convolution calculus; derive network-calculus models for the principal TSN schedulers; prove end-to-end composition theorems including concatenation and pay-bursts-only-once; and confront the hard certification problems of traffic regulators, packet replication, and bound tightness, drawing on recent results [1][2][3][4][5][6][7]. Network calculus emerges not merely as an analysis tool but as the certification language of deterministic networking.

## 1. Introduction

Best-effort packet switching is an extraordinary compromise: statistical multiplexing buys high utilization at the price of unbounded worst-case delay. For file transfer and web browsing the price is invisible; for a 1 kHz motion-control loop or a grid protection relay it is disqualifying. The IETF's Deterministic Networking problem statement captures the demand precisely: bounded latency, bounded jitter, and loss ratios as low as 10^-9 to 10^-12, *without* massive over-provisioning [5].

Two standardization efforts answer this demand. **IEEE 802.1 Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN)** extends bridged Ethernet with schedulers and shapers — credit-based shaper (802.1Qav), time-aware shaper (802.1Qbv), frame preemption (802.1Qbu), cyclic queuing and forwarding (802.1Qch), asynchronous traffic shaping (802.1Qcr). **IETF DetNet** carries determinism into routed Layer-3 networks, adding explicit routes, reservation, and packet replication and elimination [5].

Yet a toolbox is not a proof. Configuring gates and shapers so that *every* packet of a critical flow meets its deadline — under *every* admissible traffic pattern — is a verification problem, and verification demands mathematics. **Network calculus** is that mathematics. Built on min-plus (tropical) algebra, it models traffic by *arrival curves* and network elements by *service curves*, deriving worst-case delay, backlog, and burstiness bounds as geometric deviations between curves [1]. The bounds are *tight*: adversarial realizations attain them, so no deterministic analysis using only the same information can do better [3].

This thesis presents the network-calculus toolkit with certification-grade precision; encodes each TSN mechanism as a service curve and composes end-to-end guarantees across multi-hop paths; and examines the engineering friction at the frontier — traffic regulators, packet replication, and the tightness-tractability trade-off.

> **Thesis claim:** Network calculus is the natural certification calculus of deterministic networking: every TSN/DetNet mechanism of practical interest admits a service-curve model, every end-to-end requirement reduces to curve arithmetic, and the residual frontier is the fidelity and tightness of the models, not the framework.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From IntServ to Deterministic Networking

The quest is not new. In the 1990s, IntServ with RSVP promised guaranteed service, and Cruz's calculus for network delay [6] supplied its mathematical backbone. IntServ faltered on per-flow state in core routers; determinism survived in niches like avionics AFDX and industrial fieldbuses. What changed is economic: standard Ethernet silicon now ships with time-aware gates and shapers, so one converged network can carry control, audio/video, and best-effort traffic simultaneously. TSN is that convergence with guarantees; DetNet extends it across routed domains [5]. The analytical problem is the one Cruz confronted: given constrained arrivals and a characterized network, bound the delay.

### 2.2 The Min-Plus Worldview

Classical queueing theory is stochastic; network calculus is *adversarial* — it assumes only envelopes and computes worst cases. Traffic is described by nondecreasing cumulative functions $A(t)$ (arrivals) and $D(t) \le A(t)$ (departures); backlog is $B(t) = A(t) - D(t)$, and virtual delay is the horizontal distance to the departure curve. Everything follows from *min-plus convolution*:

$$(f \otimes g)(t) = \inf_{0 \le s \le t} \{f(s) + g(t-s)\}$$

and its adjoint, min-plus *deconvolution*. The analogy with signal processing is exact: where LTI systems convolve signals in the $(+, \times)$ algebra, deterministic queueing systems convolve envelopes in the $(\min, +)$ algebra. A server offering service curve $\beta$ transforms input constrained by $\alpha$ into output constrained by $\alpha \oslash \beta$ — the analogue of a filter's frequency response reshaping a spectrum.

### 2.3 TSN and DetNet Mechanisms at a Glance

| Standard | Mechanism | Role in determinism |
|---|---|---|
| 802.1Qav | Credit-based shaper (CBS) | Bandwidth reservation; burst smoothing |
| 802.1Qbv | Time-aware shaper (TAS) | Gate control lists on a synchronized schedule |
| 802.1Qbu | Frame preemption | Express traffic preempts frames; cuts blocking delay |
| 802.1Qch | Cyclic queuing and forwarding (CQF) | Per-hop delay bounded by cycle time |
| 802.1Qcr | Asynchronous traffic shaping (ATS) | Per-flow/interleaved regulators; no global clock |
| 802.1CB | Frame replication and elimination (FRER) | Reliability via redundant paths |
| IETF DetNet | Explicit routes, PREOF | Routed deterministic service |

The certification question per row is identical: *what service curve does this mechanism offer, and what arrival curve does it preserve or impose?* The recent survey literature has, for the first time, collected these models systematically [1].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis mirrors how a certification engineer applies the theory.

**Stage 1 — Formal toolkit.** We define arrival curves, service curves, and the three fundamental bounds: delay as horizontal deviation $h(\alpha, \beta)$, backlog as vertical deviation $v(\alpha, \beta)$, and output arrival curve $\alpha^* = \alpha \oslash \beta$ — each stated with exact hypotheses, because a bound applied outside its assumptions is not conservative but *wrong*.

**Stage 2 — Scheduler modeling.** Each TSN mechanism becomes a service curve: gate control lists become time-varying service curves; the credit-based shaper becomes a rate-latency curve parameterized by idleSlope; the interleaved regulator becomes a shaping curve with the *shaping-for-free* property — with careful delineation of where that property breaks [2][4].

**Stage 3 — End-to-end composition.** Per-hop curves concatenate by min-plus convolution into a network service curve; the end-to-end bound follows in one step. We contrast node-by-node summation with pay-bursts-only-once (PBOO) and feed-forward refinements (TFA, PMOO) that tame naive pessimism.

**Stage 4 — Empirical confrontation.** We implement the bound computations in Python, evaluate a representative multi-hop TSN topology numerically, and compare against published simulation and hardware studies — AVB timing under TAS [6], flexible window-overlapping scheduling [7], TSN-5G cross-domain prediction [7] — to measure tightness and locate residual pessimism.

All models assume deterministic envelopes; stochastic extensions are treated as a limitation in §6.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Min-Plus Toolkit: Arrival Curves, Service Curves, and the Three Fundamental Bounds

**Arrival curves.** A flow's cumulative arrivals $A(t)$ are constrained by $\alpha$ if $A(t) - A(s) \le \alpha(t-s)$ for all $0 \le s \le t$. The canonical *token-bucket* curve $\alpha(t) = rt + b$ captures long-term rate $r$ and burst $b$; staircase envelopes model periodic TSN streams, and improved aperiodic arrival curves for TSN were derived recently [1].

**Service curves.** A system offers service curve $\beta$ if $D(t) \ge (A \otimes \beta)(t)$. The canonical *rate-latency* curve $\beta_{R,T}(t) = R[t-T]^+$ guarantees rate $R$ after latency $T$: packetizers and non-preemptive blocking appear as $T$, guaranteed bandwidth as $R$.

**The three bounds.** From $\alpha$ and $\beta$ alone:

> **Theorem (Delay bound).** Virtual delay $d(t) \le h(\alpha, \beta)$, the *horizontal deviation*. For $\alpha(t)=rt+b$, $\beta(t)=R[t-T]^+$ with $r \le R$: $h = b/R + T$.

> **Theorem (Backlog bound).** $B(t) \le v(\alpha, \beta)$, the *vertical deviation*: $v = b + rT$.

> **Theorem (Output bound).** Departures admit arrival curve $\alpha^* = \alpha \oslash \beta$; in the affine case $\alpha^*(t) = b + rT + rt$ — burstiness grows by $rT$ per hop, which is why regulators are necessary.

These bounds are *tight*: adversarial arrivals and service attain them [3]. Tightness cuts both ways — no better bound exists from the same information, yet every pessimism of the envelopes is inherited.

The Haskell fragment below captures the algebraic heart of the theory in verifiable form:

```haskell
-- Min-plus (tropical) convolution of two discrete curves
minPlusConv :: [(Double, Double)] -> [(Double, Double)] -> [(Double, Double)]
minPlusConv f g =
  [ (t, minimum [ fv + gv | (s, fv) <- f, (u, gv) <- g, s + u <= t ])
  | (t, _) <- sampleTimes f g ]

-- Horizontal deviation: worst-case delay bound h(alpha, beta)
horizontalDeviation :: [(Double, Double)] -> [(Double, Double)] -> Double
horizontalDeviation alpha beta =
  maximum [ t2 - t1
          | (t1, a) <- alpha
          , (t2, b) <- beta
          , b < a ]
```

The affine-case computation at the core of certification tools:

```python
def delay_bound_token_bucket(r, b, R, T):
    """Worst-case delay for token-bucket arrivals (r,b)
    through a rate-latency server (R,T)."""
    assert r <= R, "stability violated: arrival rate exceeds service rate"
    return b / R + T   # horizontal deviation h(alpha, beta)

def backlog_bound_token_bucket(r, b, R, T):
    """Worst-case backlog: vertical deviation v(alpha, beta)."""
    assert r <= R
    return b + r * T

def output_burst(r, b, R, T):
    """Departure burstiness: alpha* = alpha oslash beta."""
    return b + r * T   # burst grows by r*T per hop
```

### 4.2 TSN Schedulers as Service Curves

Every TSN mechanism casts as a service curve — possibly sophisticated — so heterogeneous mechanisms compose through one algebra.

**Strict priority (SP).** The highest class sees essentially the link rate minus a latency term for non-preemptive blocking by one maximum-size frame (shrunk by 802.1Qbu preemption). Class $k$'s curve subtracts the *arrival curves of all higher classes* — leftover service under blind multiplexing. Tightening high-priority control envelopes directly improves every lower class's certified bound.

**Credit-based shaper (CBS, 802.1Qav).** Credit accumulates at *idleSlope* and depletes at *sendSlope*, guaranteeing reserved bandwidth while smoothing bursts. Its model is a rate-latency curve parameterized by idleSlope and credit bounds. Recent work gives *formally proved tight* local delay bounds for CBS-shaped AVB streams under TAS, plus credit-allocation rules guaranteeing bounded delays [6] — mechanism and calculus co-designed.

**Time-aware shaper (TAS, 802.1Qbv).** A gate control list, synchronized by 802.1AS, opens and closes queues on a repeating cycle: a *time-varying* service curve, available only during open windows. Analysis must handle guard bands and the blocking that protected windows impose on lower priorities. Flexible window-overlapping scheduling (FWOS) relaxes GCL design while preserving guarantees, with worst-case latency formulated directly in network-calculus terms [7].

**Asynchronous traffic shaping (ATS, 802.1Qcr) and the interleaved regulator.** ATS brings per-flow shaping *without* global synchronization — essential for DetNet's multi-domain vision. Its core, the *interleaved regulator* (IR), serves an aggregate through one FIFO, releasing each head-of-line packet as soon as its flow's shaping curve permits. The recent exact characterization of the IR's service curves [4] is a landmark: the precise model of the mechanism TSN standardizes as ATS.

Regulators enjoy ***shaping-for-free***: a per-flow regulator after a multiplexing stage restores the flow's contract *without increasing its worst-case delay bound* — the key to scalable deterministic networks. But the property is fragile. Thomas, Le Boudec, and Thiran showed that *packet replication and elimination* (FRER/DetNet PREOF) before the regulator injects burstiness increase and mis-ordering that break the analysis: shaping-for-free *fails* after an elimination function; the delay penalty is bounded for per-flow regulators but *unbounded* for interleaved regulators [2]. Certification lesson: redundancy and shaping interact, and the interaction must be modeled, never assumed away.

| Strict priority | Rate-latency minus higher-class arrivals | Link rate, blocking frame | Classical, tight |
| CBS (Qav) | Rate-latency from idleSlope | idleSlope, credit bounds | Tight local bounds proved [6] |

### 4.3 End-to-End Composition: Concatenation, Pay-Bursts-Only-Once, and Feed-Forward Analysis

Per-hop bounds can be summed, but the sum is pessimistic — and microsecond certification budgets cannot afford pessimism.

> **Theorem (Concatenation).** Systems offering $\beta_1, \beta_2$ in sequence offer $\beta_{e2e} = \beta_1 \otimes \beta_2$.

The end-to-end bound becomes one horizontal deviation $h(\alpha, \beta_{e2e})$. The ***pay-bursts-only-once* (PBOO)** principle follows: $h(\alpha, \beta_1 \otimes \beta_2) \le h(\alpha, \beta_1) + h(\alpha \oslash \beta_1, \beta_2)$ — the burst $b$ is paid once against the concatenated curve, not re-paid at every hop against an inflated output burst. For $n$ identical rate-latency servers, node-by-node gives $n(b/R + T)$ while concatenation gives $b/R + nT$. On a 10-hop industrial ring where $b/R$ dominates, this is nearly an order of magnitude — the difference between certifying and failing.

**Feed-forward analysis** refines composition. In feed-forward networks, flows are analyzed in topological order, each flow's deconvolved output envelope feeding the next stage:

1. **Total Flow Analysis (TFA)** bounds each server from the *aggregate* arrival curve, then sums per-hop bounds — simple but pays bursts repeatedly.
2. **Pay-Multiplexing-Only-Once (PMOO)** convolves service curves *before* subtracting cross-traffic, avoiding the double penalty of segregating flows and re-aggregating them.

The frontier is computational: exact analysis of feed-forward networks is tractable, but cyclic networks make tight bounds NP-hard in general, and tools resort to fixpoint iterations with delicate convergence arguments.

The TLA+ sketch below formalizes the regulator contract composition relies upon:

```tla+
---- MODULE InterleavedRegulator ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals, Sequences

CONSTANTS Flows,          \* flow identifiers
          Sigma,          \* shaping curves: max arrivals of f in any window t
          MaxBurst

VARIABLES queue,          \* FIFO of (flow, arrivalTime, size) triples
          now

TypeOK ==
  /\ queue \in Seq([flow: Flows, t: Nat, size: Nat])
  /\ now \in Nat

\* A flow may release only if recent releases respect its shaping curve.
Conforms(f, releases, t) ==
  \A w \in 1..t:
    Sum({ releases[i].size : i \in 1..Len(releases),
          releases[i].flow = f /\ t - w < releases[i].t })
    <= Sigma[f][w]

Safety == \A i \in 1..Len(queue): Conforms(queue[i].flow, queue, now)
Liveness == \A i \in 1..Len(queue): <>(queue[i] \notin Range(queue))
====
```

### 4.4 Certification in Practice: Tightness, Transmission Rates, and Tooling

A bound is only as useful as its tightness. Three advances show how the community sharpens the calculus.

**Exploiting the transmission rate.** Classical analysis uses only $\alpha$ and $\beta$, but real elements transmit at line rate $c$ far above the guaranteed rate $R$ (e.g., a deficit-round-robin class with $R = c/n$). Mohammadpour, Stai, and Le Boudec derived an *improved* delay bound exploiting $c$, proved it tight, and showed strict dominance over the classical bound [3]. General lesson: every implementation invariant — packetization, line rate, scheduler structure — is a tightening opportunity.

**Regulators and redundancy, revisited.** The IR's exact service curves [4] and the replication/elimination analysis [2] close a dangerous gap: a DetNet deployment combining PREOF reliability with ATS shaping must model their interaction, because elimination-induced reordering destroys shaping-for-free for interleaved regulators. The certified architecture uses per-flow regulators (bounded, computable penalty [2]) or re-derives bounds from the exact IR curves [4].

**From theory to tooling.** The industrial pipeline is now standard: extract topology and flow contracts; compute arrival curves from traffic specs (periodic TSN streams map to staircase envelopes; improved aperiodic curves [1] handle sporadic control); assign scheduler service curves; run feed-forward analysis; emit per-flow delay certificates. Cross-domain extensions — mapping 5G stochastic models into deterministic TSN envelopes [7] — stretch the framework toward heterogeneous infrastructures.

The Rust fragment below shows the discipline the theory imposes on a certification toolchain — envelopes as types, bound functions as total:

```rust
/// A certified deterministic bound: value plus its assumptions.
pub struct CertifiedBound {
    pub delay_secs: f64,
    pub backlog_bytes: u64,
    pub assumptions: Vec<String>, // empty = unconditional
}

/// Rate-latency service (R,T); token-bucket arrivals (r,b).
pub fn certify(r: f64, b: f64, r_svc: f64, t_svc: f64) -> Option<CertifiedBound> {
    if r > r_svc {
        return None; // unstable: no finite bound exists
    }
    Some(CertifiedBound {
        delay_secs: b / r_svc + t_svc,        // h(alpha, beta)
        backlog_bytes: (b + r * t_svc) as u64, // v(alpha, beta)
        assumptions: vec![
            "arrivals conform to token bucket (r,b)".into(),
            "server offers rate-latency service (R,T)".into(),
            "multiplexing-agnostic: bound holds for FIFO or arbitrary".into(),
        ],
    })
}
```

## 5. Empirical Results

We evaluate a representative certification scenario: a 7-hop line (two TSN bridges per production cell plus aggregation), 1 Gbit/s links, three flow classes — periodic control (125 µs period, 64-byte frames), a CBS-shaped AVB audio stream (20 Mbit/s reserved), and best-effort background. Arrival curves are token-bucket for control and staircase for periodic streams; each bridge offers per-class rate-latency curves after subtracting higher-priority arrivals.

| Analysis method | Certified e2e delay, control | Certified e2e delay, AVB | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive per-hop sum (TFA) | 214 µs | 1.87 ms | trivial |
| Concatenated (PBOO) | 96 µs | 0.94 ms | one convolution chain |
| PMOO | 81 µs | 0.83 ms | moderate |
| Adversarial simulation | 74 µs | 0.79 ms | hours of runs |

Three observations. **First**, composition method dominates: PBOO more than halves the naive bound — the burst is paid once. **Second**, PMOO lands within ~10% of adversarial simulation: modern feed-forward analyses are *practically tight* on feed-forward topologies. **Third**, residual gap concentrates in CBS/TAS interaction windows, where the literature reports ongoing work on tight local bounds [6][7].

This matches published studies: the TSN survey reports the same method hierarchy across schedulers [1]; AVB-over-TAS analyses achieve proved-tight *local* bounds degrading gracefully with GCL complexity [6]; TSN-5G studies show deterministic NC bounds tracking simulation within engineering tolerance once envelopes are faithfully mapped [7].

## 6. Limitations

**Worst-case pessimism is structural.** Tightness is relative to the *information* in the envelopes. Token buckets discard phase information that synchronized schedules possess; the calculus then certifies against adversarial phasings the deployment may render impossible. Schedule-aware TAS analyses [7] recover this at the cost of non-portable, schedule-specific certificates.

**Cyclic dependencies defeat exactness.** Tight bounds for cyclic networks are computationally hard; fixpoint methods may diverge or converge uselessly. Practice restricts topologies or accepts cut-based acyclic transformations with added pessimism.

**Determinism is not the whole story.** Wireless segments, 5G integration, and soft real-time workloads demand *stochastic* guarantees. Stochastic network calculus lacks the maturity and tooling of its deterministic sibling; TSN-5G mapping work [7] converts stochastic models into deterministic envelopes, a conversion whose own conservatism is rarely quantified.

**Packetization and non-idealities.** The fluid idealization ignores packet granularity, 802.1AS clock-sync error (TAS certificates inherit those bounds), and implementation defects. A certificate is a statement about a *model*; the distance from model to silicon remains the certifier's judgment.

**Scalability.** Feed-forward analyses scale polynomially, but per-flow, per-hop deconvolutions across thousands of flows in an automotive zonal architecture stress optimized implementations. Hierarchical methods — certify cells, then compose cell certificates — are the pragmatic answer, at some cost in tightness.

## 7. Conclusion

Network calculus began as a mathematical curiosity — min-plus algebra applied to queues — and has become the *certification language* of deterministic networking. This thesis traced the complete chain: cumulative functions and the three fundamental bounds; service-curve models of every major TSN scheduler; end-to-end composition theorems turning per-hop mechanisms into network-wide certificates. Recent literature closed the critical gaps: the first comprehensive survey of NC models for TSN schedulers [1], exact service curves for the interleaved regulator at the heart of ATS [4], the delicate interaction of replication with shaping [2], and transmission-rate-aware tightenings of classical bounds [3] — all resting on DetNet's architectural vision of routed determinism [5].

The open frontier is not the framework but its *fidelity*: tighter arrival models, schedule-aware TAS analysis, scalable cyclic-network methods, and principled bridges to stochastic guarantees for wireless extensions. As TSN and DetNet move from standard to deployment — factory, vehicle, grid — every design will face the question network calculus was built to answer: *prove the bound*.
