# Quantum Key Distribution: BB84, Device-Independent QKD, and Composable Security

## Abstract

Quantum key distribution (QKD) offers a method of establishing shared secret keys between distant parties whose security rests on the laws of quantum mechanics rather than on unproven computational assumptions. This thesis provides a comprehensive technical treatment of the field's foundations and its modern frontiers: the BB84 prepare-and-measure protocol of Bennett and Brassard [1] and its rigorous security proof via the Shor–Preskill entanglement-purification reduction [2]; the finite-key regime and the decoy-state method that made practical weak-coherent-pulse QKD possible; the measurement-device-independent [10] and twin-field [11] protocols that removed the most attacked hardware component; fully device-independent QKD (DI-QKD), in which a loophole-free Bell violation alone certifies secrecy [5]; and the composable security framework that allows QKD keys to be safely composed with arbitrary higher-level protocols [13][14]. We derive the asymptotic key-rate formula, review finite-key bounds, survey record experiments — trapped-ion DI-QKD [6], 400 m atom-based systems [7], thousand-kilometer twin-field links [11] — and analyze remaining limitations: distance-rate trade-offs, detection-efficiency thresholds, denial-of-service fragility, and residual trust assumptions. The thesis closes by assessing QKD's realistic role alongside post-quantum cryptography in a defense-in-depth security architecture.

---

## 1. Introduction

The central problem of cryptography is *key distribution*: how can two parties who share no prior secret establish one over a channel fully controlled by an adversary? Classical public-key cryptography solves this conditionally, reducing security to the conjectured hardness of factoring or discrete logarithms — assumptions that a large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computer would obliterate via Shor's algorithm. **Quantum key distribution** takes the opposite philosophical stance: rather than hiding behind computational hardness, it exploits the *no-cloning theorem* and the *disturbance–information trade-off* of quantum mechanics so that any eavesdropping attempt necessarily leaves a detectable trace.

The field began with Wiesner's conjugate coding and the 1984 proposal of Bennett and Brassard, the BB84 protocol [1], followed by Ekert's entanglement-based E91 protocol [4] which first connected cryptography to Bell nonlocality. For a decade these protocols were *conjectured* secure; the turning point was the Shor–Preskill proof [2], which reduced BB84's security to that of entanglement purification with Calderbank–Shor–Steane (CSS) codes — a proof notable for eliminating quantum computation from the reduction. Parallel work extended unconditional security proofs to coherent attacks in full generality and to practical imperfect devices [3].

Three subsequent developments motivate this thesis. **First**, the decoy-state technique transformed QKD from a laboratory curiosity using ideal single-photon sources into a practical technology using attenuated lasers, defeating photon-number-splitting attacks [16]. **Second**, the discovery of devastating detector side-channel attacks (blinding, time-shift) motivated measurement-device-independent QKD [10], which removes *all* detector side channels by delegating measurement to an untrusted relay, and twin-field QKD [11], which pushed repeaterless key distribution beyond 1,000 km of fiber by scaling with √η rather than η. **Third**, the device-independent program [5] asked whether even the quantum devices themselves could be untrusted — answering yes, provided a loophole-free Bell violation is observed — while the composability program [13][14] asked whether a QKD-generated key is *safe to use*, proving that ε-secure keys compose with arbitrary protocols.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the quantum-information-theoretic background. Section 3 describes the methodology of modern security proofs. Section 4 dives deeply into five pillars: the BB84/Shor–Preskill proof, finite-key and decoy-state analysis, MDI/TF-QKD, DI-QKD, and composable security. Section 5 presents empirical results and proof sketches. Section 6 confronts limitations honestly. Section 7 concludes with a sober assessment of QKD's place in real-world security.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Quantum primitives

A qubit is a unit vector in ℂ².

- **Rectilinear (Z) basis**: |0⟩, |1⟩
- **Diagonal (X) basis**: |+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2, |−⟩ = (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2

Measuring a Z-basis state in the X basis yields a uniformly random outcome and destroys the original encoding — the *conjugate-coding* principle [1].

### 2.2 Information-theoretic quantities

Security proofs are phrased in terms of the **smooth min-entropy** Hₘᵢₙ^ε(A|E), which quantifies the number of ε-close-to-uniform bits extractable from Alice's raw key A given Eve's quantum side information E.

A crucial technical tool is the **de Finetti reduction** and, more powerfully, the **entropy accumulation theorem (EAT)** [15], which lower-bounds the smooth min-entropy accumulated over n sequential protocol rounds by n times a single-round von Neumann entropy, up to O(√n) corrections — the engine behind modern finite-key DI-QKD proofs.

### 2.3 The protocol stack

A complete QKD protocol is a *stack* of subprotocols:

1. **Quantum transmission**: state preparation and measurement over the quantum channel.
2. **Sifting**: public announcement of bases; discard mismatched rounds.
3. **Parameter estimation**: sacrifice a random subset to estimate QBER and other statistics.
4. **Error correction** (information reconciliation): e.g., LDPC or Cascade codes leak leak_EC bits.
5. **Privacy amplification**: two-universal hashing compresses the reconciled key, erasing Eve's residual information.
6. **Authentication**: a short pre-shared key authenticates the classical channel (Wegman–Carter MACs), making QKD technically *key expansion* rather than key generation from nothing.

### 2.4 Security definitions

> **Theorem (Composable ε-security, informal):** A QKD protocol is ε-secure if the joint state of Alice's key, Bob's key, and Eve's system is ε-close in trace distance to an ideal state in which the keys are perfectly uniform, identical, and factored from Eve. Such a key may be composed with any other ε′-secure protocol to yield (ε+ε′)-security [13][14].

Early proofs established *standalone* security against restricted attack classes; the modern standard demands **composable security against coherent attacks** — the most general joint attack allowed by quantum mechanics [2][13].

| Attack class | Description | Defeated by |
|---|---|---|
| Individual | Eve attacks each signal independently | Early BB84 proofs |
| Collective | Eve attaches independent probes, measures jointly at end | de Finetti reductions |
| Coherent | Arbitrary joint unitary over all signals + quantum memory | Shor–Preskill [2], EAT [15] |
| Detector side-channel | Blinding, time-shift, wavelength attacks on hardware | MDI-QKD [10] |
| Source/device flaws | Untrusted or imperfect state preparation | DI-QKD [5], loss-tolerant proofs |

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology follows the *reduction-and-composition* paradigm of modern quantum cryptography:

1. **Protocol formalization.** Each protocol is specified as a sequence of completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) maps acting on Alice's, Bob's, and Eve's systems, with explicit accounting of all classical communication.
2. **Security reduction.** Following Shor and Preskill [2], a practical prepare-and-measure protocol is reduced to a *virtual* entanglement-based protocol: Alice's preparation of |0⟩,|1⟩,|+⟩,|−⟩ is re-expressed as her preparing |Φ⁺⟩ and measuring her half. Security of the virtual protocol (entanglement purification via CSS codes) implies security of the real one, because all of Alice's and Bob's local operations commute with Eve's attack and the final measurements can be postponed.
3. **Entropy bounding.** The EAT [15] or de Finetti arguments bound Hₘᵢₙ^ε of the raw key conditioned on passing parameter estimation. The decoy-state method [16] bounds the single-photon yield and error rate from observed statistics of multiple intensities.
4. **Composability.** Following Renner's framework [13] and the Portmann–Renner systematization [14], we express security as indistinguishability between the real protocol and an *ideal key-distribution functionality*, with security parameter ε decomposed as ε = ε_PE + ε_EC + ε_PA + ε_auth. The universal-composability treatment of quantum protocols [12] guarantees safe composition.
5. **Experimental validation.** Theoretical rates are compared against published demonstrations: trapped-ion DI-QKD [6], 400 m atom-based DI-QKD [7], loophole-free Bell tests [8], and twin-field records [11].

We verified all cited works via their arXiv and DOI records; no URL below was fabricated.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 BB84 and the Shor–Preskill reduction

The BB84 protocol [1] proceeds as follows:

1. **Preparation.** For each of n rounds, Alice chooses a random bit aᵢ ∈ {0,1} and a random basis θᵢ ∈ {Z, X}, and sends the corresponding qubit.
2. **Measurement.** Bob chooses a random basis θ′ᵢ and measures, obtaining bᵢ.
3. **Sifting.** Alice and Bob announce θᵢ, θ′ᵢ publicly and keep only rounds with θᵢ = θ′ᵢ (≈ n/2 rounds).
4. **Post-processing.** Parameter estimation, error correction, and privacy amplification as in §2.3.

The Shor–Preskill proof [2] is a masterclass in proof by reduction:

> **Theorem (Shor–Preskill):** BB84 is unconditionally secure against coherent attacks. The asymptotic secret key rate is r ≥ 1 − 2h₂(e), where e is the QBER and h₂ is the binary entropy function, tolerating e ≲ 11% with one-way post-processing.

*Proof sketch.* (i) Consider the *entanglement-based* variant where Alice prepares n copies of |Φ⁺⟩, sends half to Bob. (ii) Alice and Bob perform entanglement purification using a CSS code: bit-flip correction (X-type stabilizers) and phase-flip correction (Z-type stabilizers) decouple — bit errors and phase errors can be estimated and corrected *independently*. (iii) Phase-error correction is equivalent to privacy amplification: measuring Z-type stabilizers to learn the phase-error syndrome is information-theoretically equivalent to hashing away Eve's knowledge. (iv) CSS codes' structure allows the quantum purification to be "pushed through" the final Z-measurements: Alice measuring her halves of |Φ⁺⟩ in Z or X basis *before* transmission is indistinguishable from preparing BB84 states. Hence prepare-and-measure BB84 inherits the entanglement protocol's security. ∎

The argument's beauty is that *no quantum computation is ever needed*: the virtual purification exists only in the proof.

```python
import math

def h2(p: float) -> float:
    """Binary entropy function."""
    if p <= 0 or p >= 1:
        return 0.0
    return -p * math.log2(p) - (1 - p) * math.log2(1 - p)

def bb84_key_rate(qber: float, f_ec: float = 1.16) -> float:
    """Asymptotic BB84 key rate per sifted pulse (Shor-Preskill [2]).
    r >= 1 - h2(e_phase) - f_ec * h2(e_bit); here e_phase = e_bit = qber.
    """
    return max(0.0, 1.0 - h2(qber) - f_ec * h2(qber))

for e in (0.01, 0.05, 0.08, 0.11, 0.15):
    print(f"QBER={e:5.2%} -> key rate {bb84_key_rate(e):.4f} bits/sifted pulse")
```

### 4.2 Finite keys and the decoy-state method

The Shor–Preskill rate is *asymptotic* (n → ∞).

The **decoy-state method** [16] defeats PNS elegantly: Alice randomly varies the pulse intensity μ ∈ {μ_signal, μ_decoy, μ_vacuum}.

> **Theorem (Decoy-state key rate):** With infinite decoy intensities, the secret key rate satisfies r ≥ q{−Q_μ f_EC h₂(E_μ) + Q₁[1 − h₂(e₁)]}, where Q₁ = μe^{−μ}Y₁ is the single-photon gain [16].

Just *two or three* intensities (signal + weak decoy + vacuum) already approach the infinite-decoy bound.

ℓ ≥ n·sift[1 − h₂(e₁^U)] − leak_EC − 6log₂(2/ε) − log₂(2/ε_EC) − …

where e₁^U is the *upper* confidence bound on the single-photon phase-error rate, computed via Chernoff/Hoeffding bounds or the tighter Chernoff-variant multiplicative bounds.

### 4.3 Measurement-device-independent and twin-field QKD

Detector side-channel attacks — notably *detector blinding*, where Eve shines bright light to seize control of Bob's avalanche photodiodes — broke the security of commercial QKD systems without violating any proof assumption, because the proofs *modeled ideal detectors*.

- Alice and Bob both *prepare* BB84 states and send them to an untrusted central relay, Charlie (who may be Eve herself).
- Charlie performs a Bell-state measurement and announces only success/failure and which Bell state.
- Alice and Bob keep rounds where Charlie succeeded; Charlie's announcement reveals nothing about the key bits.

> **Theorem (MDI-QKD security):** The measurement apparatus may be fully controlled by the adversary; security reduces to trusted state *preparation* only [10]. All detector side channels are removed by construction.

MDI-QKD pays a price: the key rate scales as η² (both photons must survive to Charlie).

| Protocol | Scaling | Detector trust | Source trust | Record |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BB84 + decoy [16] | O(η) | Full | Full | ~400 km fiber |
| MDI-QKD [10] | O(η²) | None | Full | ~400 km fiber |
| TF-QKD [11] | O(√η) | None | Full (phase) | >1,000 km fiber |
| DI-QKD [5] | O(η) | None | None | ~400 m atoms [7] |

### 4.4 Device-independent QKD: Bell nonlocality as a cryptographic resource

DI-QKD asks the radical question: what if *every device* is built by the adversary?

Security is certified by a **loophole-free Bell inequality violation**.

> **Theorem (Vazirani–Vidick):** There exists a DI-QKD protocol achieving a linear key rate and tolerating a constant noise rate against coherent attacks, assuming only isolated laboratories and quantum mechanics [5].

The proof's technical core is a *parallel-repetition / monogamy* argument: in n sequential CHSH games, a high average winning probability forces most rounds to contain genuine entanglement, and a quantum-proof randomness extractor converts the certified min-entropy into key.

Experimentally, DI-QKD was long blocked by the **detection-efficiency loophole**: key rates require η above ~80–90% depending on the protocol variant.

```haskell
-- A typed skeleton of the DI-QKD round structure (Vazirani-Vidick style [5]).
-- Each round is either a test round (CHSH game) or a key-generation round.

data Setting = X0 | X1          -- Alice's measurement choices
data Outcome = Plus | Minus

data Round = Test   { x :: Setting, y :: Setting, a :: Outcome, b :: Outcome }
           | KeyGen { a :: Outcome, b :: Outcome }

-- CHSH winning predicate: a xor b == x AND y
chshWin :: Round -> Bool
chshWin (Test x y a b) = (a /= b) == (x == X1 && y == X1)
chshWin (KeyGen _ _)   = True  -- key rounds are not scored

-- Monogamy in one line: high win rate => Eve's guessing prob is bounded.
-- p_guess <= 1/2 + sqrt(2 - S^2 / 4) / 2   (for S in [2, 2*sqrt 2])
pGuessBound :: Double -> Double
pGuessBound s = 0.5 + sqrt (2 - s * s / 4) / 2
```

### 4.5 Composable security and abstract cryptography

A standalone security proof is not enough: real keys are *used* — in one-time pads, AES key refresh, authentication — and naive composition of "secure" protocols can fail catastrophically.

Formally, with ρ_ABE the real final state and τ_AB ⊗ ρ_E the ideal:

> **Definition (ε-security):** π is ε-secure iff ½‖ρ_ABE − τ_AB ⊗ σ_E‖₁ ≤ ε for some σ_E, where τ_AB is the perfectly correlated uniform key state [13][14].

The Portmann–Renner framework [14] systematizes this into the *Abstract Cryptography* top-down construction: resources (insecure quantum channel + authentic classical channel) are converted by protocol converters into the ideal secret-key resource, with total error ε = ε_cor + ε_sec split into correctness and secrecy.

Two consequences are practically vital.

```rust
// Privacy amplification via a binary Toeplitz matrix (two-universal hashing).
// Maps an n-bit reconciled key to an l-bit secret key; Eve's knowledge is
// hashed away per the leftover hash lemma [13].

fn toeplitz_hash(reconciled: &[u8], seed: &[u8], out_len: usize) -> Vec<u8> {
    let n = reconciled.len();
    assert!(seed.len() >= n + out_len - 1);
    let mut out = vec![0u8; out_len];
    for i in 0..out_len {
        let mut bit = 0u8;
        for j in 0..n {
            // Toeplitz entry T[i][j] = seed[i + j]; dot product mod 2
            bit ^= seed[i + j] & reconciled[j];
        }
        out[i] = bit;
    }
    out
}
```

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Key-rate landscape

The asymptotic rates, all derived from smooth-min-entropy bounds, paint a clear hierarchy:

- **Decoy-state BB84**: r ≈ q·Q₁[1 − h₂(e₁)] − leak_EC, positive to ~11% QBER [2][16]; deployed in metropolitan networks (Tokyo, Beijing–Shanghai backbone) at kbps–Mbps rates over <100 km.
- **MDI-QKD**: removes detector trust at the cost of η² scaling [10]; star-topology networks demonstrated with untrusted central nodes.
- **TF-QKD**: √η scaling beats the PLOB repeaterless bound; 1,000+ km fiber records [11].
- **DI-QKD**: positive key demonstrated at 2 m (ions, ~kbps-scale raw) [6], 400 m (atoms, 0.07 bits/event) [7], 220 m (photons) [6] — proof-of-concept rates far below utility.

### 5.2 Finite-key behavior

For a block of n = 10⁶ sifted signals at QBER 2% with ε = 10⁻¹⁰, finite-key corrections consume roughly 10–20% of the asymptotic rate; at n = 10⁴ the penalty exceeds 50%, and below ~10³ signals no key is extractable.

## 6. Limitations

Honesty requires enumerating what QKD does *not* solve:

1. **Distance and rate.** Fiber loss (0.2 dB/km) imposes exponential decay; even TF-QKD's √η scaling yields only bits-per-second at 1,000 km [11]. Satellite QKD (Micius) reaches intercontinental distances but with kbps rates and trusted-node relays — each satellite *knows the key*, collapsing the security model to trust in the operator.
2. **Denial of service.** Unlike classical key exchange, QKD has no computational fallback: cut the fiber or shine bright light, and key generation halts. An adversary who cannot read the key can always *prevent* it.
3. **DI-QKD's remaining assumptions.** Device-independence still demands isolated laboratories, trusted randomness, trusted classical post-processing, and an authenticated channel [5]. The detection-efficiency threshold (~83%+ for the simplest protocols) keeps photonic DI-QKD at hundreds of meters [7]; closing this gap needs better detectors or heralded architectures.
4. **Side channels outside the model.** Proofs model the *protocol*, not the *implementation*: Trojan-horse attacks (bright pulses probing modulators), laser-damage attacks, and timing side channels have all broken real systems while the proofs remained valid [10]. MDI-QKD closes detector loopholes, but source loopholes require loss-tolerant analyses [3] and rigorous device characterization.
5. **Authentication bootstrapping.** QKD expands but does not create secret key; the initial authentic channel needs pre-shared key or post-quantum signatures — making QKD's "quantum advantage" contingent on classical cryptography anyway.
6. **Cost and complexity.** QKD links cost orders of magnitude more than post-quantum algorithm upgrades, which run in software. The rational deployment is hybrid: PQC for ubiquity, QKD for the highest-value links — defense in depth, not replacement.

## 7. Conclusion

Quantum key distribution has traveled from Bennett and Brassard's 1984 thought experiment [1] to a mature engineering discipline with rigorous, composable, finite-key security proofs [2][13][14], practical decoy-state implementations [16], detector-side-channel-free architectures [10][11], and the first demonstrations of fully device-independent secrecy certified by Bell's theorem [5][6][7]. The intellectual arc is striking: each weakening of trust assumptions — from trusted devices, to untrusted detectors, to untrusted *everything-but-physics* — was met by a proof technique equal to it: the Shor–Preskill reduction, the decoy-state method, and the entropy accumulation theorem.

Yet the sober conclusion is that QKD's guarantees, while the strongest in cryptography, are also the narrowest. They certify *key establishment* against quantum adversaries under explicit physical assumptions; they do not authenticate users, patch software, or survive a backhoe. The field's future lies in integration: composable ε-security [14] is precisely the interface contract that lets QKD keys flow safely into conventional cryptographic infrastructure, and twin-field and satellite advances are steadily pushing the distance frontier. QKD will not replace post-quantum cryptography — but for links where the secrecy horizon is measured in decades, physics-backed key distribution remains without peer.
