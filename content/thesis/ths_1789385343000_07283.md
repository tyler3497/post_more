# Quasi-Symmetry and Neoclassical Optimization in Stellarators: Three-Dimensional Magnetic Confinement from Wendelstein 7-X to Reactor Design

## Abstract

The stellarator, conceived by Lyman Spitzer in 1951, confines plasma with magnetic fields generated entirely by external coils, offering intrinsic steady-state operation without a toroidal plasma current and immunity to disruptions. Yet for decades classical stellarators suffered catastrophic neoclassical losses: helical field ripple traps particles whose vertical drifts go uncompensated, producing a 1/nu transport regime with no tokamak analogue. This thesis develops the modern resolution of that failure — three-dimensional magnetic field optimization. We review Boozer coordinates and the hidden-symmetry principle of quasi-symmetry, in which the field strength on a flux surface depends on a single helical combination of angles, restoring tokamak-like confinement; derive the neoclassical transport regimes and the effective-ripple figure of merit; trace the optimization lineage from Nührenberg and Zille through the Wendelstein 7-X HELIAS design; present the experimental confirmation of neoclassical optimization in W7-X; and compare the stellarator and tokamak paths to a reactor. We close with limitations: turbulence, fast-particle confinement, coil engineering, and the costs of three-dimensionality.

## 1 Intro

Magnetic confinement fusion has been dominated, since the late 1960s, by the tokamak. The results from T-3 announced in 1968 demonstrated energy confinement times an order of magnitude beyond anything the stellarator program — then centered on Princeton's Model C — had achieved, and the world pivoted. The tokamak's axisymmetry makes its physics tractable: neoclassical transport is modest, the equilibrium is described by the two-dimensional Grad–Shafranov equation, and confinement improves predictably with size and current.

But the tokamak's virtues are purchased with a toroidal plasma current, and that current is the source of nearly every operational pathology in the concept: disruptions, the need for non-inductive current drive in steady state, current-driven tearing modes, and the density limit. The stellarator avoids all of them by construction. Its rotational transform — the twist of field lines that provides confinement — is generated entirely by external coils, so the device is *intrinsically steady-state* and disruption-free. The price is three-dimensionality: without axisymmetry, the magnetic field strength varies along a field line in ways that trap particles in local helical mirrors, and those trapped particles drift radially outward. In classical stellarators this neoclassical transport was so large that reactor extrapolation was hopeless.

This thesis is about how that verdict was reversed. Beginning in the 1980s, theorists realized that confinement does not require geometric symmetry — it requires symmetry of the *field strength in flux coordinates*, a property called **quasi-symmetry** [1][2][3]. Combined with computational optimization of the full three-dimensional equilibrium [4], this insight produced Wendelstein 7-X (W7-X), the world's largest optimized stellarator, whose experiments have now confirmed that neoclassical transport can be reduced to tokamak levels [8]. We develop the theory, the optimization methodology, the experimental evidence, and an honest accounting of what remains unsolved.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The stellarator idea

In 1951, Spitzer proposed confining plasma in a figure-eight torus whose twisted geometry produced rotational transform without any plasma current. The Princeton stellarator program explored this through the 1960s, but energy confinement stubbornly refused to scale, and after the T-3 results the program was largely abandoned in favor of tokamaks.

The essential difficulty was understood through the neoclassical theory of Galeev and Sagdeev. In any toroidal device, particles trapped in magnetic mirrors execute banana orbits, and collisions scatter them across flux surfaces. In a tokamak, axisymmetry guarantees that the canonical toroidal angular momentum is conserved, so trapped-particle orbits remain close to their birth flux surface and the resulting transport — the banana regime — is small. In a classical stellarator, the field strength varies *helically* along a field line, creating local magnetic wells that trap a large fraction of particles. These helically trapped particles experience vertical grad-B and curvature drifts that do not average to zero over a bounce, so they step radially by large amounts with each collision. The result is the **1/nu regime**, in which the diffusion coefficient *increases* as collisionality *decreases*:

$$D_{1/\nu} \sim \epsilon_h^{3/2} \frac{v_d^2}{\nu}$$

where $\epsilon_h$ is the helical ripple amplitude, $v_d$ the magnetic drift speed, and $\nu$ the collision frequency. At reactor-relevant low collisionality, this scaling is devastating — orders of magnitude above tokamak levels. Classical stellarators were, in effect, *intrinsically* poor confiners at low collisionality, the exact regime a reactor requires.

### 2.2 Why three-dimensionality is both curse and opportunity

A tokamak's field is determined, up to the plasma current profile, by axisymmetric coils; the design space is small. A stellarator's field is determined by fully three-dimensional coil shapes, so the design space is enormous — thousands of degrees of freedom in the plasma boundary alone. For decades this was purely a curse, because the coils had to be built by hand calculation. But it is also an opportunity: with sufficient computational power, the field can be *shaped* to suppress exactly the transport mechanisms that plagued classical designs. The modern stellarator program is, at its core, an optimization program [4][5].

> **Theorem:** In a magnetic configuration with exact quasi-symmetry, the guiding-center drift orbits are confined to flux surfaces to the same order as in an axisymmetric tokamak, and the neoclassical transport assumes tokamak-like scalings.

This is the central theoretical result that the rest of this thesis unpacks: symmetry of the field *strength* in the right coordinates substitutes for symmetry of the *geometry* [2][3][6].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Equilibrium and coordinates

Stellarator equilibria with nested flux surfaces are computed with the VMEC code, which minimizes the MHD energy subject to fixed boundary or fixed coil currents. Analysis is performed in **Boozer coordinates** $(\psi, \theta, \varphi)$, introduced by Boozer [1][2], in which field lines are straight and the field strength admits the Fourier representation

$$B(\psi,\theta,\varphi) = \sum_{m,n} B_{mn}(\psi)\cos(m\theta - n\varphi).$$

Neoclassical transport in the low-collisionality regimes is determined almost entirely by this spectrum: only the variation of $B$ *on* a flux surface matters, not the geometric complexity of the surface itself.

### 3.2 Figures of merit

Optimization targets a vector of physics objectives:

- **Effective helical ripple** $\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}$: a geometric factor such that the 1/nu diffusion scales as $\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3/2}$. Reducing $\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}$ by an order of magnitude — as achieved in W7-X relative to classical stellarators — reduces 1/nu transport by a factor of roughly thirty.
- **Quasi-symmetry error**: the fraction of the $B_{mn}$ spectrum lying outside a single helicity family (see Section 4.1).
- **Fast-particle confinement**: proxies such as $\Gamma_c$ measuring the alignment of $B$ contours with flux surfaces, critical for alpha-particle confinement in a reactor.
- **MHD stability**: magnetic well depth and Mercier criterion, evaluated along the optimization path.
- **Bootstrap current**: minimized in W7-X so that the edge island structure — on which the island divertor depends — does not move as plasma pressure evolves.

### 3.3 Optimization machinery

Early optimization used fixed-boundary VMEC equilibria coupled to the STELLOPT suite [4]. The modern frontier is **single-stage optimization**, in which coils and plasma are optimized simultaneously rather than sequentially (first plasma boundary, then coils to reproduce it), and the **near-axis expansion**, which parametrizes quasi-symmetric fields analytically near the magnetic axis and has enabled the discovery of precise quasi-symmetric configurations [10].

Validation closes the loop: optimized designs are checked against the ISS04 empirical confinement scaling derived from the international stellarator database, and — decisively — against experiment.

```python
import numpy as np

def neoclassical_estimate(nu_star, eps_eff=0.01, rho_star=0.01, q=1.0, R=5.5):
    """Order-of-magnitude neoclassical diffusivities (m^2/s)."""
    v_th = 1e5                       # thermal speed
    v_d = rho_star * v_th * q        # grad-B drift speed, order of magnitude
    nu = nu_star * v_th / (q * R)    # collision frequency
    D_1_over_nu = eps_eff**1.5 * v_d**2 / nu
    D_banana = q**2 * (rho_star * R)**2 * nu   # tokamak-like reference
    return {"stellarator 1/nu": D_1_over_nu, "tokamak banana": D_banana}

for ns in [1e-3, 1e-2, 1e-1]:
    print(ns, neoclassical_estimate(ns))
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Hidden symmetry: Boozer coordinates and the quasi-symmetry principle

Boozer's 1981–1982 papers [1][2] established that any toroidal equilibrium with nested flux surfaces admits coordinates in which the magnetic field takes the remarkably simple contravariant form $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\psi \times \nabla\theta + \iota\,\nabla\varphi \times \nabla\psi$, with straight field lines. In these coordinates, the guiding-center drift equations depend on $B$ only through its flux-surface Fourier spectrum $B_{mn}(\psi)$.

**Quasi-symmetry** is the condition that this spectrum collapses onto a single helicity:

$$B(\psi,\theta,\varphi) = B(\psi, M\theta - N\varphi)$$

for integers $M, N$. The field *strength* then possesses a continuous symmetry even though the *geometry* does not. Noether's theorem applies to the symmetry that exists, not the one that is missing: the canonical angular momentum conjugate to the ignorable helical angle is conserved, trapped-particle orbits are confined exactly as in a tokamak, and neoclassical transport collapses to tokamak scalings [3][5].

Three families are distinguished by their helicity:

| Class | $(M, N)$ | Realization | Property |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quasi-axisymmetry (QA) | $(1, 0)$ | NCSX (PPPL, cancelled 2008) | Tokamak-like $B$ with stellarator coils |
| Quasi-helical symmetry (QH) | e.g. $(1, N_{fp})$ | HSX (Wisconsin, 1999) | Helical $B$ contours; reduced flow damping |
| Quasi-poloidal symmetry (QP) | $(0, N)$ | Theoretical | Smallest trapped fraction; hard to realize |

```haskell
-- Quasi-symmetry as a property of the field-strength function:
-- B depends only on the flux label and ONE helical angle combination.
type Flux    = Double
type Theta   = Double
type Phi     = Double
type Helical = Double   -- chi = M*theta - N*phi

fieldStrength :: (Flux -> Helical -> Double) -> Flux -> Theta -> Phi -> Double
fieldStrength b psi theta phi = b psi (m * theta - n * phi)
  where (m, n) = (1, 4)  -- quasi-helical symmetry, 4 field periods
```

A profound corollary was derived by Helander and Simakov [6]: in a quasi-symmetric stellarator, neoclassical transport is **intrinsically ambipolar** — the particle fluxes of each species vanish independently to leading order, exactly as in a tokamak. Classical stellarators instead require a radial electric field $E_r$ to enforce ambipolarity ($\sum_a Z_a \Gamma_a = 0$), and the resulting $E_r$ is typically large and sheared. Intrinsic ambipolarity frees the rotation profile, permitting the tokamak-like $E \times B$ shear that suppresses turbulence — a decisive advantage for reactor extrapolation.

### 4.2 Neoclassical transport in three dimensions: the 1/nu regime

The drift-kinetic equation in Boozer coordinates yields a hierarchy of collisionality regimes governed by $\nu^* = \nu qR / v_{th}$:

| Regime | Collisionality | Stellarator scaling | Tokamak analogue |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1/\nu$ | $\nu^* \ll \epsilon_h^{3/2}$ | $D \sim \epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3/2} v_d^2 / \nu$ | absent |
| $\sqrt{\nu}$ | very low $\nu^*$, finite $E_r$ | $D \sim \sqrt{\nu}$ | absent |
| Plateau | $\nu^* \sim 1$ | $D \sim v_d^2 qR / v_{th}$ | plateau |
| Pfirsch–Schlüter | $\nu^* \gg 1$ | $D \sim q^2 \rho^2 \nu$ | Pfirsch–Schlüter |

The $1/\nu$ regime is the stellarator's signature pathology: helically trapped particles with bounce frequency $\omega_b$ satisfying $\nu \ll \omega_b$ drift radially at $v_d$ and random-walk with step $\Delta r \sim v_d / \nu_{\mathrm{eff}}$, where only the fraction $\nu/\omega_b$ of particles is detrapped per bounce — hence diffusion *grows* as collisions *decrease*. The $\sqrt{\nu}$ regime appears when a strong $E_r$ poloidally precesses trapped particles faster than they drift radially, partially healing the loss [5].

Ambipolarity selects $E_r$. When electrons are in $1/\nu$ and ions are not, the plasma charges until an **ion root** ($E_r < 0$) suppresses ion losses; in hot-electron core plasmas an **electron root** ($E_r > 0$) can form, producing the core electron-root confinement (CERC) regime with strongly reduced transport — observed in W7-AS and predicted for W7-X high-performance scenarios.

```rust
/// A single Boozer Fourier harmonic of |B| on a flux surface.
struct BoozerHarmonic { m: i32, n: i32, amplitude: f64 }

/// Quasi-symmetry error: fraction of B-variation outside the (M, N) family.
fn qs_error(harmonics: &[BoozerHarmonic], m_qs: i32, n_qs: i32) -> f64 {
    let total: f64 = harmonics.iter().map(|h| h.amplitude.powi(2)).sum();
    let sym: f64 = harmonics.iter()
        .filter(|h| h.m * n_qs == h.n * m_qs)
        .map(|h| h.amplitude.powi(2)).sum();
    1.0 - sym / total
}
```

### 4.3 Coil optimization: from Nührenberg–Zille to single-stage design

The breakthrough came in 1988, when Nührenberg and Zille demonstrated numerically that realizable coil sets could produce quasi-helically symmetric fields [3] — Boozer's hidden symmetry made concrete. This single paper launched the optimized-stellarator era:

1. **Wendelstein 7-AS** (1988–2002): partially optimized; demonstrated improved confinement and the high-density H-mode, motivating a fully optimized successor.
2. **HSX** (1999, Wisconsin): the first quasi-helically symmetric experiment; confirmed reduced parallel flow damping in the symmetry direction and improved electron confinement — the first experimental proof that quasi-symmetry works.
3. **NCSX** (PPPL): quasi-axisymmetric design; cancelled in 2008 when cost and schedule overran — a cautionary tale about coil complexity that still shadows the field.
4. **Wendelstein 7-X**: the HELIAS line pursued a different optimum — **quasi-isodynamicity** rather than quasi-symmetry. Here the contours of $B$ close poloidally, so the bounce-averaged radial drift of trapped particles vanishes even without symmetry. The effective ripple $\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}$ was reduced by roughly an order of magnitude relative to the Large Helical Device [4].

The current frontier, reviewed in [10], combines the near-axis expansion with single-stage coil optimization to produce configurations with quasi-symmetry precise to parts in $10^4$ — and quasi-isodynamic power-plant studies (the SQuID family) that project competitive reactor economics.

### 4.4 The Wendelstein 7-X program: design and operation

W7-X is the largest stellarator ever built and the definitive test of the optimization paradigm:

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Major / minor radius | 5.5 m / 0.53 m |
| Field periods | 5 |
| Magnetic field | 2.5–3 T (NbTi superconducting) |
| Coils | 50 non-planar + 20 planar |
| Plasma volume | 30 m³ |
| Heating | up to ~10 MW ECRH at 140 GHz, plus NBI and ICRH |
| Divertor | Island divertor at $\iota = 1$ edge resonance |

Construction ran from the mid-1990s to 2014, with first plasma in December 2015.

### 4.5 The stellarator–tokamak comparison

With optimization validated, the comparison can be made on physics merits rather than historical accident:

| Criterion | Tokamak | Optimized stellarator |
|---|---|---|
| Steady state | Requires current drive (expensive, inefficient) | Intrinsic: no plasma current needed |
| Disruptions | Major threat; mitigation mandatory | Absent (no current-driven MHD) |
| Neoclassical transport | Banana regime; benign | $1/\nu$ tamed by optimization to tokamak levels |
| Turbulent transport | ITG/TEM; $E\times B$ shear from rotation | Similar; intrinsic ambipolarity aids rotation in QS |
| Shaping freedom | Limited by axisymmetry | Enormous: 3D shaping is the design tool |
| Coil engineering | Simple axisymmetric coils | Complex 3D superconducting coils; expensive |
| Startup | Inductive; transformer sets pulse length | ECRH breakdown; no transformer limit |
| Alpha confinement | Good (axisymmetry) | Must be explicitly optimized; residual risk |

The stellarator's fundamental bet is that *engineering complexity is a one-time cost* while the tokamak's *physics complexity — disruptions, current drive — is a permanent operating tax*.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 W7-X confirms neoclassical optimization

The decisive experimental result is reported by Pedersen *et al.* [8]: in pellet-fueled high-performance discharges, W7-X achieved energy confinement times exceeding 200 ms — values that would be *impossible* in a classical stellarator, because the neoclassical losses of an unoptimized field would exceed the applied heating power. The plasma can only exist in the observed state if the neoclassical transport has been reduced by optimization. This is as close to a direct proof of the optimization paradigm as an experiment can provide.

Supporting evidence accumulated across operation phases [7]:

- **OP1.1**: first plasmas confirmed vacuum flux surfaces matched design to within millimeters; the triple product set stellarator records.
- **Turbulence dominance**: heat-pulse propagation and phase-contrast imaging show electron heat transport well above neoclassical levels and consistent with gyrokinetic predictions for ITG/TEM turbulence — precisely the expected signature of a device whose *neoclassical* channel has been closed, leaving turbulence as the residual loss.
- **Pellet fueling**: by flattening density profiles and suppressing ITG turbulence, pellet injection accesses the regime where neoclassical optimization is directly visible in global confinement.
- **Island divertor**: stable detachment with good impurity screening sustained for tens of seconds, validating the exhaust concept.

### 5.2 HSX and the quasi-symmetry proof

The Helically Symmetric Experiment provided the cleanest test of quasi-symmetry itself: with quasi-helical symmetry, plasma flow damping in the symmetry direction is strongly reduced relative to the symmetry-breaking direction, and electron thermal confinement improves — exactly as the theory of Section 4.1 predicts. HSX remains the only operating quasi-symmetric stellarator and continues to validate optimization codes.

### 5.3 Scaling and extrapolation

W7-X confinement is broadly consistent with the ISS04 stellarator scaling, with the optimized configurations performing at or above the empirical trend. Gyrokinetic and drift-kinetic modeling with codes benchmarked against W7-X data now underpins extrapolation to reactor-scale HELIAS and quasi-isodynamic power-plant concepts [10], where the combination of reduced neoclassical transport and intrinsic steady state projects to competitive cost of electricity — contingent on the limitations below.

## 6 Limitations

An honest assessment must confront what optimization has *not* solved:

1. **Turbulence is now the dominant loss channel.** Closing the neoclassical leak exposed turbulent transport (ITG, TEM, ETG) as the performance limiter [9]. Turbulence optimization — shaping the field to reduce linear growth rates and nonlinear saturation — is far less mature than neoclassical optimization, though Xanthopoulos *et al.* showed it is addressable within the same framework [9].
2. **Fast-particle confinement remains uncertain.** Reactor alphas are collisionless on the slowing-down time; their confinement depends on the detailed 3D orbit topology. W7-X's high-mirror configuration studies show sensitivity, and no stellarator has yet confined a burning plasma's alpha population.
3. **Quasi-symmetry is always approximate.** Exact quasi-symmetry is incompatible with a fully three-dimensional toroidal equilibrium, so all practical designs carry residual symmetry-breaking harmonics. Optimization has driven these to remarkable smallness [10], but the *scaling* of residual transport to reactor parameters is not fully validated.
4. **Coil complexity and cost.** Non-planar superconducting coils with millimeter tolerances are expensive; NCSX's cancellation is the standing warning. Whether optimized stellarators can be built at reactor scale within economic constraints is unproven — though industrial ventures are now testing exactly this.
5. **Operational limits.** ECRH heating imposes density limits; turbulence-suppressed high-performance phases show signs of impurity accumulation [8]; and steady-state particle and heat exhaust at reactor power densities remains to be demonstrated in OP2 long-pulse campaigns.

## 7 Conclusion

The stellarator has traveled from Spitzer's 1951 figure-eight — through the wilderness years after T-3, when its neoclassical losses seemed a fundamental condemnation — to Wendelstein 7-X, where optimized three-dimensional fields confine plasma at tokamak levels without any plasma current. The intellectual arc is clean: Boozer's coordinates [1][2] revealed that confinement depends on the field-strength spectrum, not the geometry; Nührenberg and Zille [3] showed the hidden symmetry could be built; Helander [5][6] worked out the transport and ambipolarity consequences; and W7-X [7][8] proved it in hardware.

What remains is the harder second act: taming turbulence with the same optimization machinery that tamed neoclassical transport, demonstrating alpha confinement and steady-state exhaust at reactor scale, and building the whole thing affordably. The tokamak retains the advantage of incumbency and a vast database; the stellarator offers intrinsic steady state and freedom from disruptions — arguably the two properties a power plant needs most. With private ventures now pursuing compact optimized stellarators alongside the public program, the three-dimensional path to fusion is, for the first time, a genuine competitor rather than a curiosity.
