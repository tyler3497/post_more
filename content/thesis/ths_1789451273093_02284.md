# Secure Multi-Party Computation: Yao's Garbled Circuits, the GMW Compiler, and the SPDZ Framework from Semi-Honest to Malicious Security

## Abstract

Secure multi-party computation (MPC) lets mutually distrusting parties jointly evaluate a function of their private inputs while revealing nothing beyond the output. This thesis surveys the three foundational protocol families: Yao's garbled circuits for constant-round secure two-party computation [1], the Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson compiler reducing general multi-party computation to oblivious transfer [2], and the SPDZ framework attaining malicious security against a dishonest majority via offline preprocessing with information-theoretic message authentication codes [6]. We develop the ideal-versus-real simulation paradigm, dissect garbling, secret-shared circuit evaluation, and Beaver-triple multiplication [5], and analyze the optimizations — free-XOR [3], circular-correlation security [4], OT-based preprocessing [9] — that carried MPC from feasibility to deployable infrastructure, alongside the MP-SPDZ benchmarking framework [7] and information-theoretic honest-majority protocols [10]. We close with empirical considerations, the impossibility of fairness under dishonest majority, and open research directions.

---

##1 Intro

*Secure multi-party computation* is among the oldest problems in cryptography: parties P1 through Pn hold private inputs x1 through xn and wish to compute y = f(x1, ..., xn), with the guarantee that no corrupt coalition learns anything about honest inputs beyond what the output implies. Yao's millionaires' problem is the canonical special case [1]; Danish farmers ran the first large-scale deployment in 2008, computing the market-clearing price of sugar beets without revealing bids.

This thesis traces the field's intellectual arc through three landmark constructions, each answering a deeper question than the last:

1. **Can two parties compute any function in constant rounds?** Yao's garbled circuits answer yes under symmetric-key assumptions [1], with the first rigorous simulation-based analysis by Lindell and Pinkas [8].
2. **Can arbitrarily many parties compute any function from a minimal assumption?** The GMW compiler answers yes, reducing everything to oblivious transfer [2], while Ben-Or, Goldwasser, and Wigderson show honest majorities need no cryptographic assumptions at all [10].
3. **Can we have malicious security against a dishonest majority at practical speed?** SPDZ answers yes, splitting work into expensive input-independent preprocessing and a fast online phase authenticated by information-theoretic MACs [6], improved by OT-based preprocessing [9] and the MP-SPDZ framework [7].

Our method is conceptual and proof-driven: the real-versus-ideal paradigm, each protocol's mechanics and security argument, cost models, and where theory meets engineering. Three axes govern every MPC design: the *adversary model* (semi-honest, covert, malicious), the *corruption threshold* (honest versus dishonest majority), and the *circuit representation* (Boolean versus arithmetic).

##2 Background

### 2.1 The ideal functionality and the simulation paradigm

Modern MPC security is defined by comparison with an *ideal world*: all parties hand inputs to an incorruptible trusted party, which computes the function and returns each output. A real protocol is *secure* if, for every real-world adversary, some ideal-world simulator's interaction with the trusted party is computationally indistinguishable from the real execution — whatever the adversary achieves, it could equally have achieved given only its inputs and outputs.

The paradigm is parameterized by adversarial power: *semi-honest* adversaries follow the protocol but mine their views; *malicious* adversaries deviate arbitrarily; *covert* security deters rational cheaters by catching deviation with tunable probability. Cleve proved *fairness* — all parties receive output or none do — impossible against a dishonest majority, while honest majorities achieve guaranteed output delivery.

### 2.2 Secret sharing

*Secret sharing* is the workhorse of multi-party protocols. In Shamir's scheme, a secret s becomes the constant term of a random degree-t polynomial; any t+1 evaluation-point shares reconstruct s by Lagrange interpolation while any t reveal nothing. *Additive sharing* splits s into random summands, one per party, requiring all parties for reconstruction. Both schemes are *linear* — shares of a sum come from local addition, so linear operations cost no communication. Multiplication is where all the difficulty, and all the cryptography, lives.

### 2.3 Oblivious transfer as a complete primitive

In 1-out-of-2 *oblivious transfer* (OT), a sender holds two messages and a receiver a choice bit; the receiver learns exactly one message while the sender learns nothing about the choice. Kilian proved OT *complete* for secure computation: given OT, any function can be computed securely; Impagliazzo and Rudich showed OT cannot be built black-box from symmetric primitives alone, placing it at the foundation of the public-key world. *OT extension* (notably IKNP) reduces the amortized cost of many OTs to a few base OTs plus symmetric operations, making OT a nearly free resource in practice.

### 2.4 Corruption thresholds and the landscape

| Protocol family | Parties | Adversary | Threshold | Assumption | Circuit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yao garbled circuits [1] | 2 | semi-honest | 1 corrupt | symmetric crypto | Boolean |
| Yao with cut-and-choose | 2 | malicious | 1 corrupt | symmetric crypto | Boolean |
| GMW [2] | n | semi-honest | n−1 corrupt | OT | Boolean |
| BGW [10] | n | malicious | t < n/3 (t < n/2 with broadcast) | none (information-theoretic) | arithmetic |
| SPDZ [6] | n | malicious | n−1 corrupt | somewhat homomorphic encryption | arithmetic |

> **Theorem (BGW completeness, informal [10]):** Every n-party function can be computed with perfect, information-theoretic security against a malicious adversary corrupting fewer than one third of the parties — fewer than one half given a broadcast channel — using only Shamir secret sharing and no cryptographic assumptions.

The complementary GMW result is computational but threshold-optimal in the other direction, tolerating *any* number of corruptions at the price of computational assumptions [2]. The tension between these two extremes organizes the entire field.

##3 Methodology

This thesis is a *theoretical survey with proof sketches and empirical context* in four parts.

First, **formal definitions**: security stated in the real/ideal simulation paradigm, with each protocol's analysis reduced to explicit assumptions — pseudorandom functions for garbling, OT for GMW, somewhat homomorphic encryption for the original SPDZ preprocessing.

Second, **mechanistic dissection**: each protocol in enough detail to implement the core loop — garbled truth tables and point-and-permute, the GMW gate-evaluation round structure, the SPDZ share representation with MACs — omitting only routine details.

Third, **cost modeling**: asymptotic costs in circuit size, multiplicative depth, party count, and security parameter, connected to the concrete engineering artifacts (free-XOR, OT extension, preprocessing) that determine wall-clock performance. Qualitative claims are grounded in the published literature [3][6][7][9]; figures are illustrative unless attributed.

Fourth, **limitation analysis**: which guarantees each protocol *cannot* provide — fairness under dishonest majority, input validity, implementation side-channel resistance — and why these are inherent rather than incidental.

*Notation.* ⟦x⟧ denotes a secret-shared value; λ is the computational security parameter; arithmetic is over F_p unless stated otherwise.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Yao's garbled circuits: constant-round two-party computation

Yao's construction [1] is conceptually startling: the *garbler* encrypts the entire Boolean circuit so the *evaluator* can compute the output on encrypted inputs while learning nothing else — in a constant number of rounds regardless of circuit depth.

**Construction.** For each wire, the garbler samples two random λ-bit *wire labels*, one per truth value. For each gate, it encrypts every truth-table row: the row for input bits (α, β) holds the output label for g(α, β) encrypted under the two corresponding input labels, with the four ciphertexts randomly permuted. *Point-and-permute* adds a permutation bit to each label so the evaluator decrypts exactly one row per gate without trial decryption. The garbler sends the garbled tables and its own input labels; the evaluator fetches its input labels via oblivious transfer, one OT per input bit. Each step reveals only random-looking labels; semantic security hides unopened rows and OT hides the evaluator's choices. The first complete simulation-based proof — handling selective-failure attacks — is due to Lindell and Pinkas [8].

**Free-XOR and its security subtleties.** Kolesnikov and Schneider observed that XOR gates need not be garbled at all [3]: with one global secret offset Δ defining the label pair of every wire, an XOR output label is simply the XOR of the input labels — computed locally with zero ciphertexts and zero cryptographic operations, improving XOR-heavy circuits such as adders and AES by up to a factor of 4 [3].

The proof is delicate, however: the original analysis needed the random-oracle model, and the folklore claim that *correlation robustness* suffices proved false — Choi, Katz, Kumaresan, and Zhou showed free-XOR additionally demands a form of *circular security*, formalized as circular 2-correlation robustness [4].

**From semi-honest to malicious.** A malicious garbler may garble a *different* circuit; a malicious evaluator may exploit selective-failure in OT. *Cut-and-choose* counters both: the garbler produces many garbled circuits, the evaluator opens a random half to check and evaluates the rest, accepting only on unanimous agreement. Authenticated garbling and half-gates (two ciphertexts per AND gate) have made malicious-secure two-party computation genuinely practical.

### 4.2 The GMW compiler: multi-party computation from oblivious transfer

Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson generalized Yao's ideas to any number of parties [2], evaluating a Boolean circuit gate by gate with every wire value XOR-secret-shared among all n parties. XOR gates are free — each party locally XORs its shares. AND gates use 1-out-of-4 OT between each ordered pair of parties, costing O(n²) OTs per gate. Round complexity is proportional to the circuit's *multiplicative depth*, since same-depth AND gates evaluate in parallel but each layer needs a fresh round. Security against a semi-honest adversary corrupting any number of parties follows from a hybrid argument over the OT ideal functionality [2]. Since GMW's OTs are input-independent, they can be generated before inputs are known — foreshadowing SPDZ's offline/online paradigm.

### 4.3 SPDZ: malicious security with a dishonest majority

The SPDZ protocol of Damgård, Pastro, Smart, and Zakarias [6] is the landmark result in *practical* maliciously secure MPC against a dishonest majority — the strongest standard model, where all but one party may be corrupt.

**Share representation with information-theoretic MACs.** Values are shared additively, x = Σ_i x_i, and each share is *authenticated*: a global MAC key α is itself secret-shared, and party i holds for each x both its share x_i and a share m_i of the MAC m = α·x, denoted ⟦x⟧. Addition is local on both. The MACs are *information-theoretic* — forgery succeeds with probability only 1/p, negligible for large p — and no party ever knows α.

**Online multiplication from Beaver triples.** Multiplication consumes a preprocessed triple (⟦a⟧, ⟦b⟧, ⟦c⟧) with c = a·b [5]: parties locally compute ⟦d⟧ = ⟦x⟧ − ⟦a⟧ and ⟦e⟧ = ⟦y⟧ − ⟦b⟧, *open* d and e, and compute the product share locally via the identity

> **Identity (Beaver triple multiplication):** Since x = d + a and y = e + b, x·y = d·e + d·b + e·a + c. Every term is public (d, e) or shared, so each party computes ⟦z⟧ = d·e + d·⟦b⟧ + e·⟦a⟧ + ⟦c⟧ locally, with the public term d·e added by one designated party.

Opening d and e is safe because a and b are uniform masks, so the online phase costs one triple and a single communication round per multiplication.

```python
# Beaver-triple multiplication (2-party illustration)
p = 2**61 - 1

def beaver_mul(x_s, y_s, triple, pid, d_open, e_open):
    a_s, b_s, c_s = triple
    public = d_open * e_open if pid == 0 else 0
    return (c_s[pid] + d_open * b_s[pid] + e_open * a_s[pid] + public) % p
```

**The MAC check.** Before outputs are revealed, parties verify no share was tampered with. Opening every MAC would leak α, so SPDZ performs a *batched, randomized* check: a random linear combination of all opened values and MACs is verified against the shared key in one aggregated equation, with a commit-then-open round blocking adaptive cheating. A failed check aborts — the best achievable guarantee under dishonest majority.

**Preprocessing.** The offline phase generates input-independent Beaver triples and authenticated randomness. The original construction [6] used *somewhat homomorphic encryption* — parties encrypt shares, homomorphically compute product encryptions, and decrypt cooperatively with zero-knowledge proofs of plaintext knowledge — which was the bottleneck. Keller, Orsini, and Scholl's **MASCOT** replaced SHE with *oblivious transfer*-based triple generation [9]; successors (Overdrive, SPDZ2k over rings) continued the optimization. The offline/online split became the standard architecture for maliciously secure arithmetic MPC.

### 4.4 The malicious-security ladder

These results form a ladder of adversarial strength:

1. **Semi-honest security** (Yao [1], GMW [2], BGW [10]): adversaries follow the protocol — the model for parties bound by contract but curious.
2. **Covert security**: cheating caught with tunable probability, deterring rational adversaries below the cost of full malicious security.
3. **Malicious security with abort** (SPDZ [6], MASCOT [9]): arbitrary deviation is detected and the protocol aborts — the strongest notion achievable against a dishonest majority.
4. **Malicious security with guaranteed output delivery**: requires an honest majority (BGW [10] and descendants); every honest party receives correct output.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### Proof sketch: unforgeability of SPDZ MACs

Fix ⟦x⟧ with MAC shares summing to α·x, key shares summing to the unknown α. If the adversary shifts the reconstructed value by δ ≠ 0, it must add α·δ to the honest MAC contribution. Its view is independent of α, so α·δ is uniformly random and forgery succeeds with probability exactly 1/p — negligible for cryptographic-size p. The batched check amplifies this: one equation authenticates the entire transcript of opened values.

### Proof sketch: simulation for semi-honest GMW

The simulator proceeds gate by gate [2]: random shares for corrupt parties, honest shares consistent with corrupt shares and the ideal-world wire value. XOR gates need no simulation; for AND gates it invokes the OT simulator. Adjacent hybrids differ only in replacing one real OT with its simulation — indistinguishable by OT security.

### Empirical landscape

Structural findings from the literature:

- **Garbled circuits are bandwidth-bound, GMW is round-bound, SPDZ-online is triple-bound.** Yao sends a constant number of ciphertexts per AND gate (two with half-gates, zero for XOR with free-XOR [3]) and scales with circuit size, not depth — ideal for high-latency networks. GMW scales with multiplicative depth, punishing deep circuits on WANs. SPDZ's online phase is information-theoretically cheap per multiplication but consumes one preprocessed triple each, shifting the bottleneck offline [6][9].
- **Free-XOR is the single most impactful garbling optimization**, cutting computation and communication by up to a factor of 4 on XOR-heavy circuits such as adders and AES [3]; the later-discovered circular-security requirement [4] clarified the assumption stack without diminishing practical value.
- **Unified benchmarking changed the methodology.** MP-SPDZ [7] compiles the same program to more than thirty protocol variants on a common virtual machine, enabling the first apples-to-apples security-efficiency comparison and remaining the standard evaluation harness.

*Illustrative cost model* (n parties, security parameter λ, G gates, multiplicative depth D):

| Protocol | Communication per AND/mult gate | Rounds | Preprocessing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yao (half-gates + free-XOR) | 2λ bits (AND only) | O(1) | none |
| GMW (OT-based) | O(n²) OTs | O(D) | OTs (input-independent) |
| SPDZ online | 2 field elements opened | O(D) | 1 triple + MACs per mult |

The same mechanisms admit concise executable and formal models: Haskell captures sharing and MAC authentication at the type level, Rust models an authenticated share, and TLA+ specifies the commit-then-open MAC check.

```haskell
-- SPDZ-style authenticated shares over a prime field
newtype Field = F Integer deriving (Eq, Show)
data Share = Share { sval :: Field, smac :: Field } deriving Show

addShare :: Integer -> Share -> Share -> Share
addShare p (Share x mx) (Share y my) =
  Share (F ((unF x + unF y) `mod` p)) (F ((unF mx + unF my) `mod` p))
  where unF (F v) = v

-- MAC relation checked on open; the key is never revealed
verifyOpen :: Integer -> Integer -> Share -> [Share] -> Bool
verifyOpen p alpha opened allShares =
  sum [unF (smac s) | s <- allShares] `mod` p == (alpha * unF (sval opened)) `mod` p
  where unF (F v) = v
```

```rust
/// SPDZ authenticated share held by one party.
pub struct AuthShare {
    pub share: u64,      // share of x
    pub mac_share: u64,   // share of alpha * x
    pub key_share: u64,   // share of the MAC key
}

impl AuthShare {
    /// Local addition of authenticated shares: no communication.
    pub fn add(&self, other: &AuthShare, modulus: u64) -> AuthShare {
        AuthShare {
            share: (self.share + other.share) % modulus,
            mac_share: (self.mac_share + other.mac_share) % modulus,
            key_share: self.key_share, // key shares are fixed per party
        }
    }
}
```

##6 Limitations

**Fairness and guaranteed output are threshold-bound.** Cleve's impossibility is absolute in the plain model: with a dishonest majority, no protocol stops a corrupt party from learning the output while honest parties do not. SPDZ and MASCOT therefore provide *security with abort* [6][9] — the best possible — and applications needing fairness must assume an honest majority (BGW-style [10]) or add external enforcement such as blockchain penalties.

**Preprocessing is input-independent but not free.** The offline/online split moves cost rather than eliminating it: OT-based generation [9] improved greatly on SHE [6], but large workloads need billions of triples and preprocessing must be re-run per computation. *Silent preprocessing* via pseudorandom correlation generators is the active frontier addressing this cost.

**MPC authenticates the computation, not the inputs.** A malicious party can always lie about its own input — MPC computes the agreed function on the *provided* inputs. Input validity (e.g., range proofs that a bid is non-negative) needs auxiliary zero-knowledge proofs that compose with MPC but are not part of it.

**Selective-failure and side channels.** OT-based protocols must be hardened against selective-failure attacks [8]; garbled-circuit implementations face a physical attack surface too — the global free-XOR offset Δ can be extracted via side-channel analysis of the garbler's device with only a handful of nonlinear gates. Protocol-level security does not imply implementation-level security.

**Scaling with party count.** Most dishonest-majority protocols have per-gate communication quadratic in n (GMW's pairwise OTs) or linear with large constants (SPDZ's openings). Scaling malicious-secure MPC to hundreds of parties remains open, partially addressed by committee election and hierarchical techniques.

**Quantum vulnerability is assumption-dependent.** Information-theoretic protocols (BGW [10]) resist quantum adversaries unconditionally; OT-based and SHE-based constructions rely on computational assumptions not known to be quantum-safe, so post-quantum MPC needs lattice-based OT and heavier preprocessing.

##7 Conclusion

Four decades after Yao [1], the MPC landscape is remarkably coherent: the GMW compiler [2] established that oblivious transfer suffices for everything; BGW [10] that honest majorities need nothing beyond secret sharing; Beaver's triples [5] that nonlinear computation reduces to preprocessed randomness; and SPDZ [6] that these fuse into maliciously secure dishonest-majority protocols with an essentially information-theoretic online phase. Optimizations — free-XOR [3] with its circular-security subtlety [4], OT-based preprocessing [9], unified benchmarking [7] — carried the field from feasibility to deployment.

The trajectory points forward: silent preprocessing promises to collapse the offline bottleneck; mixed-protocol frameworks select garbled circuits, secret sharing, or homomorphic encryption per sub-computation; threshold cryptography has brought MPC into production wallets and signing infrastructure. The open problems are now as much about *systems* as *protocols* — scaling to many parties, hardening implementations, standardizing ideal functionalities. The theoretical foundations, however, are settled, and they rest on the three constructions surveyed here.
