# Marine Ice-Sheet Instability and the Shallow-Shelf Approximation in a Warming Climate: Grounding-Line Dynamics, Basal Sliding Laws, and Projections for the Amundsen Sea Sector

## Abstract

Marine ice sheets hold the largest uncertainty in sea-level projections. This thesis develops the mathematical foundations of their stability, from the Stokes equations through the shallow-ice and shallow-shelf (SSA) approximations. We present the Weertman-Schoof boundary-layer theory of grounding-line flux, proving that steady grounding lines are unstable on retrograde (inland-deepening) bed slopes -- the marine ice-sheet instability (MISI) -- with hysteretic bifurcation structure under parameter variation. We analyze the marine ice-cliff instability (MICI) hypothesis, in which tall unbuttressed ice cliffs exceed ice yield strength and collapse structurally, potentially accelerating retreat by an order of magnitude. We treat Weertman and Coulomb basal sliding laws and subglacial hydrology coupling as first-order controls on instability timescales. Finally, we synthesize evidence for the Amundsen Sea sector, where Thwaites and Pine Island glaciers show sustained, possibly irreversible retreat consistent with MISI onset, and clarify which predictions are theorems, which are modeled, and which remain hypotheses.

## 1. Introduction

Of all the uncertainties attending anthropogenic climate change, few carry larger societal stakes than the dynamical response of the Antarctic ice sheet. Greenland and Antarctica together contain approximately 65 m of sea-level equivalent, of which Antarctica accounts for roughly 58 m [6].  

The modern theory begins with a deceptively simple question: *where* does a marine ice sheet end? The **grounding line** is the locus where grounded ice detaches from the bed and begins to float, becoming an ice shelf. It is a free boundary of a moving-interface Stokes problem, and its motion controls the discharge of the entire upstream grounded catchment [1]. The ice shelf, though floating, matters crucially because it transmits **longitudinal stresses** back to the grounded ice — the phenomenon of *buttressing* [10].  

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 establishes the physical background: ice-sheet mass balance, Glen's flow law, and the full Stokes formulation. Section 3 presents the methodology of systematic shallow-layer reduction, deriving the shallow-ice approximation (SIA) and the shallow-shelf approximation (SSA) as distinguished asymptotic limits [7]. Section 4 develops the deep theory: the Weertman–Schoof flux boundary layer, the MISI criterion, the MICI hypothesis, basal sliding laws, and subglacial hydrology coupling. Section 5 synthesizes empirical evidence and theoretical guarantees, with quantitative focus on Thwaites and Pine Island glaciers. Sections 6 and 7 address limitations and conclusions. 

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Ice-sheet mass balance and sea-level contribution

The mass evolution of a grounded ice sheet is governed by the continuity equation

> **Continuity:** ∂H/∂t + ∇ · **q** = ȧ − ṁ,

where H is ice thickness, **q** = H**ū** the depth-integrated flux with **ū** the depth-averaged velocity, ȧ the surface accumulation (positive for snowfall), and ṁ basal melt. For Antarctica, ȧ totals roughly 2,000 Gt yr⁻¹ while grounding-line discharge accounts for comparable loss [6]. The imbalance between these terms — currently a net loss of approximately 150 Gt yr⁻¹ for the Antarctic ice sheet, with the Amundsen Sea sector dominating — is what raises sea level, at a rate of about 0.4 mm yr⁻¹ [8].

Ice is a shear-thinning, temperature-dependent viscous fluid described by **Glen's flow law**, an empirical power-law relation between strain rate έ and deviatoric stress τ:

```
έ = A(T) τ^(n−1) τ,
```

with n ≈ 3 and A the temperature-dependent rate factor [2]. This power-law rheology makes the full problem nonlinear and distinguishes ice dynamics from Newtonian lubrication problems. The three-dimensional field equations are the Stokes equations for a very viscous, incompressible, inertia-free fluid:

∇ · σ + ρᵢ**g** = 0,   ∇ · **u** = 0,

with σ = −p**I** + τ, closed by Glen's law.

### 2.2 The free boundary problem

The grounding line position x_g is not prescribed but determined by the flotation condition: ice floats where its thickness falls below the flotation thickness H_f = −(ρ_w/ρᵢ) b, with b the bed elevation (negative below sea level) and ρ_w, ρᵢ the densities of seawater and ice. 

## 3. Methodology

Our central methodological tool is **asymptotic reduction**: exploiting the small aspect ratio ε = H/L ≪ 1 of ice sheets (kilometer-thick, thousand-kilometer-wide) to derive vertically integrated approximations whose distinguished limits isolate the dominant stress balances in different regimes.

### 3.1 Derivation strategy

We non-dimensionalize the Stokes equations with the aspect ratio ε and expand in powers of ε. Two distinct balances survive at leading order, depending on the relative importance of basal drag versus membrane stresses:

| Approximation | Dominant balance | Valid regime | Governing equation type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shallow-Ice (SIA) | Vertical shear, basal drag balances driving stress | Slow, frozen-bed interior | Local algebraic flux law, q ∝ H^(n+2) |
| Shallow-Shelf (SSA) | Membrane (longitudinal/ lateral) stresses, weak basal drag | Ice streams, shelves, grounding zones | Elliptic PDE for velocity |

In the **SSA**, the depth-integrated momentum balance reads [7]:

∂/∂x [ 2ν̄H (2∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y) ] + ∂/∂y [ ν̄H (∂u/∂y + ∂v/∂x) ] − τ_b = ρᵢ g H ∂s/∂x,

where ν̄ is the depth-averaged effective viscosity, τ_b the basal traction, and s the surface elevation. 

> **Theorem (Schoof, 2007):** In the limit of small buttressing number, the steady-state flux across the grounding line of a two-dimensional marine ice sheet is a power-law function of the ice thickness at the grounding line alone, q_g = C · H_g^((n+m+3)/(m+1)) · |b|⁻¹ ... — that is, grounding-line flux increases *sharply* with grounding-line thickness, with exponent 4.75 for n = m = 3 [1].

The remainder of this thesis unpacks the profound consequences of this single scaling relation.

### 3.2 Numerical implementation

To make the boundary-layer theory concrete, we implement a one-dimensional flowline model in which the SSA momentum equation is solved on a domain with a moving grounding line, and Schoof's flux condition is imposed as an internal boundary condition. The following Python code sketches the flux law and a steady-state root-finding procedure:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

# Physical parameters (SI units)
rho_i, rho_w, g = 917.0, 1028.0, 9.81
n, m = 3.0, 3.0              # Glen exponent, Weertman sliding exponent
A = 4.0e-25                  # ice softness [Pa^-n s^-1]
C = 7.624e6                  # basal slipperiness [Pa s^(1/m) m^(-1/m)]
accum = 0.3 / 31557600.0     # accumulation rate [m s^-1]

def flotation_thickness(bed):
    """Flotation thickness H_f = -(rho_w/rho_i) * b for b < 0."""
    return -(rho_w / rho_i) * bed

def schoof_flux(Hg, buttressing=1.0):
    """
    Schoof (2007) grounding-line flux law:
    q_g proportional to Hg^((n+m+3)/(m+1)) for Weertman sliding.
    With n=m=3 the exponent is 19/4 = 4.75 -- extremely sensitive.
    """
    prefactor = (A * (rho_i * g) ** (n + 1) * (1 - rho_i / rho_w) ** n
                 / (4.0 ** n * C)) ** (1.0 / (m + 1.0))
    exponent = (n + m + 3.0) / (m + 1.0)
    return buttressing ** (n / (m + 1.0)) * prefactor * Hg ** exponent

def steady_grounding_line(bed_func, x_lo, x_hi):
    """Solve q_g(H_f(x_g)) = accum * x_g for the steady grounding line."""
    def residual(xg):
        Hg = flotation_thickness(bed_func(xg))
        return schoof_flux(Hg) - accum * xg
    return brentq(residual, x_lo, x_hi)

# Example: linear retrograde bed b(x) = b0 - s*x deepening inland
b0, slope = -500.0, 1.0e-3
xg = steady_grounding_line(lambda x: b0 - slope * x, 1e3, 2e6)
print(f"Steady grounding line at x = {xg/1e3:.1f} km")
```

The key lesson of the code is quantitative: because q_g ∝ H_g^4.75, a modest deepening of the bed at the grounding line multiplies the discharge by a large factor, and the steady-state equation can admit *multiple* solutions — the mathematical signature of hysteresis.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Weertman–Schoof boundary-layer theory of grounding-line flux

Prior to Schoof's 2007 work, the stability of marine ice sheets rested on a heuristic due to Weertman [2]: he argued, on the basis of a local force balance at the grounding line, that the flux through the grounding line increases with its thickness, and therefore that grounding lines on retrograde slopes (beds deepening inland) are unstable. 

Schoof [1, 3] resolved the controversy by constructing a **matched asymptotic expansion**.  Matching yields two boundary conditions at the grounding line: a thickness condition (flotation) and the flux law quoted in Section 3. The analysis proved:

1. Marine ice sheets possess **discrete** steady states, not the neutral equilibrium of earlier theories.
2. **No steady grounding line is stable on a retrograde bed slope.** Perturbations grow because retreat into deeper water increases H_g, which increases q_g (with exponent ~4.75), which thins the ice and drives further retreat — the *marine ice-sheet instability* (MISI).
3. Overdeepened beds (troughs) produce **hysteresis**: as forcing parameters vary, the grounding line jumps discontinuously between stable branches and cannot be restored by reversing the forcing [1].

> **Theorem (Schoof, 2007a):** Let q_g(H_g) be strictly increasing in grounding-line thickness. Then a steady grounding line on a bed with db/dx < 0 (retrograde, deepening inland) is unconditionally unstable, while on a prograde bed it is stable. The instability is intrinsic to the flux–thickness scaling and independent of the far-field accumulation pattern [1].

Crucially, the theory also explained the numerical artifact: grid spacings coarser than the boundary-layer width (~1 km) misrepresent the flux and can produce *false* stability — a warning that shaped all subsequent model-intercomparison protocols.

### 4.2 Marine ice-sheet instability (MISI): the retrograde-slope feedback

MISI is now the central paradigm of marine ice-sheet stability, and it is worth stating its mechanism with precision because popular accounts often blur it. The ingredients are:

1. **Archimedes:** at the grounding line, H_g = −(ρ_w/ρᵢ) b(x_g); thicker ice floats at deeper beds.
2. **Flux sensitivity:** q_g ∝ H_g^4.75 (Schoof) — discharge is a strongly convex function of thickness.
3. **Mass conservation:** upstream accumulation must balance grounding-line discharge in steady state.

On a retrograde slope, a small seaward perturbation of the grounding line places it in *shallower* water, reducing H_g and hence q_g; accumulation then exceeds discharge, thickening the ice and advancing the grounding line further seaward — runaway advance. A small inland perturbation does the reverse — runaway retreat. There is no restoring force: the equilibrium is a saddle in the dynamical-systems sense. The only arresting mechanisms are geometric (the bed shallowing again, a pinning point, or lateral buttressing from a confined shelf [10]) or dynamical (increased accumulation or reduced basal slipperiness).

An important subtlety: MISI is a statement about *steady states* of an idealized, unbuttressed, two-dimensional sheet. Real ice sheets are buttressed, three-dimensional, and transient. Buttressing, parameterized by a factor θ < 1 multiplying the flux law [10], can stabilize grounding lines even on retrograde slopes — which is precisely why the *loss* of buttressing through ice-shelf thinning is the trigger of concern [5, 8].

### 4.3 Marine ice-cliff instability (MICI): structural collapse at the calving front

MISI concerns viscous flow; **MICI** concerns *fracture*. DeConto and Pollard [4] proposed that once buttressing ice shelves are removed by hydrofracture (surface meltwater wedging crevasses open), the exposed grounding-line ice cliffs — potentially hundreds of meters tall where beds lie more than a kilometer below sea level — exceed the structural yield strength of ice and collapse under their own weight. Cliff failure exposes a new, equally tall cliff behind it, producing a self-sustaining calving front that can retreat far faster than viscous MISI alone.

The physics rests on the **yield strength envelope** of ice: laboratory and field estimates place the effective yield stress at roughly 0.1–1 MPa, corresponding to a maximum stable subaerial cliff height of order 80–100 m [9].  DeConto and Pollard's coupled ice–climate model, calibrated against Pliocene and Last Interglacial sea-level high stands, projected Antarctic contributions exceeding **1 m by 2100 and 15 m by 2500** under unabated emissions [4] — dramatically larger than contemporary IPCC estimates that excluded MICI.

MICI remains the most contested element of the theory.  The IPCC AR6 assessment treated MICI as a *deep uncertainty*: included in high-end storylines but excluded from the likely range [6]. The epistemic status is therefore clear: MISI is a theorem; MICI is a hypothesis awaiting decisive observational tests, likely from the ongoing disintegration of Antarctic ice shelves.

### 4.4 Basal sliding laws: Weertman, Coulomb, and the instability timescale

Everything above depends on how ice slides over its bed, because basal traction τ_b sets the discharge for a given geometry. Two end-member constitutive laws dominate:

- **Weertman sliding** (hard bed): τ_b = C |u|^(1/m − 1) u, a power law with m ≈ 3 arising from regelation and enhanced creep around bedrock obstacles [2]. Traction grows without bound with velocity.
- **Coulomb (plastic) sliding** (soft, deformable till): τ_b = μ N, capped by the effective pressure N = p_ice − p_water, independent of velocity at large speeds. This describes the weak, water-saturated tills beneath the Siple Coast ice streams.

The choice matters enormously.  Modern inversions of satellite velocities over Thwaites and Pine Island consistently favor **near-plastic** beds (low effective pressure, weak rate-dependence) [5], implying that these glaciers are more sensitive to forcing than Weertman-based theory predicts, and that their response times are shorter.

### 4.5 Subglacial hydrology coupling

The basal water system closes the loop.  This creates a **thermo-mechanical feedback**: faster sliding generates more frictional heat, producing more meltwater, raising p_w, lowering N, weakening the bed, and accelerating sliding further. 

Coupling hydrology to the SSA is an active frontier: the state of the art routes a Darcy-type drainage model beneath the ice-sheet model with two-way coupling through N.

## 5. Empirical Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 The Amundsen Sea sector: Thwaites and Pine Island glaciers

Theory becomes urgent where the bed geometry matches the instability criterion, and nowhere matches it better than the Amundsen Sea Embayment of West Antarctica. Satellite altimetry and interferometry show that Pine Island Glacier's grounding line retreated **31 km** inland between 1992 and 2011, Thwaites 14 km, with Smith/Kohler retreating 35 km [8] — all on retrograde slopes, all accelerating, all thinning from below by warm Circumpolar Deep Water intruding into sub-shelf cavities.

Thwaites Glacier — the "Doomsday Glacier" of popular media — drains roughly 4% of Antarctica, holds ~0.65 m of sea-level equivalent itself, and, critically, *buttresses* a much larger catchment of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet whose full loss would contribute ~3.3 m [6]. Joughin, Smith, and Medley [5] applied a finite-element SSA ice-stream model to Thwaites and found that the glacier's retreat has likely passed a point of no return: grounding-line retreat into the deepening Bentley Subglacial Trench triggers rapid, MISI-driven collapse on timescales of **200–500 years** in most scenarios, with the fast phase completing in decades once initiated. The mechanism is exactly the Schoof feedback: retreat into deeper water → thicker grounding-line ice → sharply higher flux → dynamic thinning → further retreat. Bedrock ridges can temporarily pin the grounding line, producing the observed *stepped* retreat pattern, but no ridge downstream is sufficient to halt the process permanently [5].

The observations also confirm a key theoretical prediction about *where* instability matters: retreat is fastest precisely where the bed deepens most steeply inland, and glaciers on prograde or flat beds in the same sector (e.g., parts of the Getz sector) remain comparatively stable — a natural experiment consistent with the MISI criterion [8].

### 5.2 What is guaranteed, what is modeled, what is unknown

We summarize the epistemic hierarchy in the following table:

| Claim | Status | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Grounding-line flux increases sharply with thickness (q_g ∝ H_g^4.75) | **Theorem** (within SSA + Weertman) | Matched asymptotics [1, 3] |
| No stable steady grounding line on retrograde slopes (unbuttressed) | **Theorem** | Stability analysis [1] |
| Hysteresis under forcing reversal on overdeepened beds | **Theorem** | Bifurcation analysis [1] |
| Buttressing can stabilize retrograde grounding lines | **Modeled** | 3-D SSA models with lateral drag [10] |
| Thwaites retreat is underway and likely irreversible | **Observed + modeled** | Satellite record + SSA projections [5, 8] |
| MICI amplifies 21st-century sea-level rise beyond 1 m from Antarctica | **Hypothesis** | Calibrated coupled model [4]; contested |
| Subglacial hydrology controls retreat *rates* | **Unknown** | Sparse borehole data; active research |

The IPCC AR6 likely range for 2100 Antarctic contribution under high emissions is 0.03–0.34 m, but the assessment flags *deep uncertainty* from MISI and MICI, with high-end storylines exceeding 1 m [6] — a gap reflecting disputed fracture and hydrology parameterizations, not disputed physics.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires enumerating what the theory does *not* establish.

1. **The SSA is itself an approximation.** It assumes small aspect ratio and negligible vertical shear; near the grounding line, where the boundary layer has aspect ratio of order unity, the SSA is formally invalid and only the matched inner solution rescues the flux law. Full-Stokes models confirm the results but remain too expensive for continental-scale, millennial simulations [1].
2. **Basal boundary conditions are inferred, not measured.** Inversions of surface velocity for basal traction are non-unique; the choice between Weertman and Coulomb laws changes flux exponents and timescales dramatically, and we have direct borehole access to the beds of only a handful of ice streams.
3. **MICI lacks a validated calving law.** No existing parameterization of cliff collapse has been tested against a full observed cycle of shelf disintegration followed by cliff retreat, because no such cycle has yet been observed at the relevant scale. Laboratory yield strengths may not scale to kilometer-scale, pre-fractured ice masses.
4. **Ocean forcing is the proximate trigger and is poorly observed.** Sub-shelf cavity circulation, melt-rate parameterizations, and future Southern Ocean warming are all uncertain; without accurate ocean boundary conditions, even a perfect ice model mistimes retreat.
5. **Timescale vs. commitment.** MISI theory predicts *instability*, not *speed*. Whether Thwaites collapses in 200 years or 1,000 depends on buttressing evolution, hydrology, and ocean heat delivery — quantities at the edge of current observability.

## 7. Conclusion

The shallow-shelf approximation, combined with Schoof's boundary-layer analysis of the grounding zone, transformed marine ice-sheet stability from a qualitative debate into a quantitative, predictive theory. Its central results — the steep flux–thickness scaling, the instability of grounding lines on retrograde slopes, and the hysteretic structure of steady states — are mathematical theorems within their stated assumptions and have survived two decades of numerical verification and observational testing. Applied to the Amundsen Sea sector, the theory provides the mechanistic explanation for the observed, accelerating retreat of Thwaites and Pine Island glaciers and underwrites the assessment that this retreat may be irreversible on human timescales.

What remains is not the *whether* of the instability but its *rate* and *ultimate extent* — questions controlled by basal sliding physics, subglacial hydrology, ocean heat delivery, and the contested marine ice-cliff instability. These are empirical questions, answerable in principle by the observing system now being deployed: high-resolution bed topography, autonomous sub-shelf ocean measurements, and continued satellite gravimetry and altimetry. The mathematics has done its job — it has told us exactly where to look and what to measure. The ice, meanwhile, is already moving.
