# Differential Dataflow and Timely Dataflow: Incremental Computation with Multidimensional Timestamps

## Abstract

This thesis presents a unified treatment of **differential dataflow** and **timely dataflow**, the complementary frameworks introduced by McSherry and collaborators that transformed incremental, iterative computation over changing data [1][3]. Differential dataflow generalizes incremental computation by indexing collection state over a *partially ordered* set of logical times: a collection is a function \(C : D \to \mathbb{Z}\) from records to multiplicities, updates are *differences* of collections, and operators lift pointwise while preserving a linearity law that makes differentiation exact. Timely dataflow supplies the execution substrate — a data-parallel runtime whose progress-tracking protocol of pointstamps, frontiers, and probes determines when all messages bearing a partially ordered timestamp have been delivered, even across nested loops and distributed workers. We formalize the timestamp lattice, the \(\delta\)-lifting of operators, arrangements with merge batches, and the bridge to the DBSP circuit calculus of Z-sets [4]. We prove the lifting correctness theorem, review empirical evidence from the Naiad prototype through Materialize's production deployment, and close with limitations: difference retention, the Abelian group boundary, and the cost of a fully general progress protocol.

## 1 Introduction

Consider the motivating query from McSherry's early talks: *what is the most popular hashtag within each connected component of the social mention graph, updated in real time?* [6]. The graph has billions of edges changing millions of times per second; the query is *iterative* (connected components need fixed-point computation) and *incremental* (edges arrive continuously). Traditional systems force an unhappy choice. Batch engines recompute the whole iterative algorithm per snapshot — prohibitively expensive. Stream processors handle updates but historically could not express nested iteration: their notion of time is a total order, and a loop inside the stream breaks the watermark protocol that tells operators when input is complete.

The breakthrough of differential dataflow is the observation that this conflict is artificial. If logical time may be a **partial order** rather than a total order — if a "version" of the data is a *multidimensional* timestamp \((t, i)\) where \(t\) is the epoch of the input change and \(i\) the iteration count — then streaming updates and loop iterations become two independent coordinates of one space. An update at epoch 5, iteration 0 and a refinement at epoch 4, iteration 3 are *incomparable*; the framework processes them concurrently and reconciles them exactly where they interact. This single move — generalizing versions from a sequence to a lattice — unifies incremental and iterative computation [1].

Timely dataflow is the runtime that makes this executable. A timely computation is a directed graph of operators exchanging timestamped messages; each operator reports, for each input, the frontier of timestamps it may still receive. A distributed protocol propagates these *pointstamps* through the graph — including through loop scopes, which remap timestamps — and each worker computes, for every timestamp, whether any message bearing it can still arrive. When the frontier advances past a timestamp, operators safely emit results for it, yielding deterministic, exactly-once semantics over out-of-order, partially ordered time [3].

The combination enabled incrementally maintained recursive queries — strongly connected components, transitive closure, PageRank — that were previously intractable, and became the foundation of industrial streaming SQL (Materialize) and the theoretical DBSP calculus [4].

This thesis contributes: (1) a self-contained formalization of collections, differences, and the difference-lifting \(\delta\) of operators over partially ordered times; (2) a precise account of timely dataflow's progress tracking; (3) a treatment of *arrangements* — shared, indexed, multi-versioned state with merge batches; (4) the bridge to DBSP's Z-set circuit calculus and its chain rule for automatic incrementalization [4][7]; and (5) a correctness proof for operator lifting plus a review of empirical evidence.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Incremental computation before partial orders

Incremental computation reuses prior results when inputs change slightly. The classic database formulation is *incremental view maintenance* (IVM): given query \(Q\) and change \(\Delta D\), compute \(\Delta Q\) with cost scaling in \(|\Delta D|\), not \(|D|\). Foundational work includes the counting algorithms and DRed for recursive Datalog (Gupta, Mumick, Subrahmanian, 1993) — correct under deletions via over-delete-and-rederive, but coarse — and higher-order IVM (DBToaster, Koch et al., 2014), which compiles queries into recursively maintained delta expressions and remains the single-node throughput champion for non-recursive SQL [2]. Parallel traditions include *self-adjusting computation* (Acar et al.) with dynamic dependence graphs, and functional reactive programming.

What all of these shared was a **totally ordered** notion of versions: version 7 follows version 6, updates consolidate into "the current state," and iteration — which needs many coexisting versions of the same logical state — was unsupported except through special cases (bulk-synchronous loop unrolling in Spark; systems handling iteration *or* incrementality but not both).

> **Theorem (folklore, pre-2013):** In any incremental system whose versions form a total order, an iterative computation whose \(i\)-th iterate depends on version \(t\) of the input must either re-execute all \(i\) iterations when \(t\) advances, or maintain one materialized state per \((t, i)\) pair with no sharing between incomparable pairs.

Differential dataflow dissolves this dilemma by making \((t, i)\) pairs partially ordered and sharing work across them systematically. Naiad, presented at SOSP 2013 by Murray, McSherry, Isaacs, and Isard, introduced *timely dataflow*: a distributed data-parallel runtime with timestamps drawn from a partially ordered set and explicit loop scopes [3]. The technical report MSR-TR-2012-105 documents the prototype's design and its evaluation on graph algorithms with shared-memory parallelism [3]. Differential dataflow, presented at CIDR 2013, supplied the programming model layered atop it: collections, differences, lifted operators [1]. The mathematical foundations — Möbius inversion over posets applied to incremental computation — were subsequently worked out in full, giving the theory its order-theoretic grounding [5].

### 2.3 The modern lineage: Materialize, DDlog, DBSP

The ideas escaped the laboratory. **Materialize** commercialized differential dataflow as a streaming SQL database: views compile to differential dataflows, and every write is processed as a difference with millisecond latency while views stay always-fresh. **Differential Datalog (DDlog)** applied the substrate to Datalog with semi-naïve evaluation generalized to partially ordered times. Most recently, **DBSP** (Budiu et al., VLDB 2022) reformulated the enterprise as a circuit calculus over Z-set-valued streams and proved a *chain rule* that automatically incrementalizes any DBSP circuit [4][7].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Collections, differences, and multiplicities

The primitive object is a **collection**: a function

\[
C : D \to \mathbb{Z}
\]

assigning each record \(d \in D\) an integer *multiplicity*. Multiplicity \(+1\) means present once; \(-1\) means retracted; \(0\) means absent. Collections form an **Abelian group** under pointwise addition — the entire secret of the framework.

A collection evolves over partially ordered *times*. For each time \(t\), the *difference* \(\delta C_t\) records what changed:

\[
\delta C_t(d) = C_t(d) - \sum_{s < t} \delta C_s(d), \qquad C_t = \sum_{s \le t} \delta C_s.
\]

This is **Möbius inversion** on the poset of times: the collection at \(t\) is the sum of differences at all \(s \le t\), and differences are recovered by subtracting strictly-earlier contributions [5]. Under a total order this collapses to the telescoping sum \(C_t = C_{t-1} + \delta C_t\). Under a partial order — e.g., product order on \((\text{epoch}, \text{iteration})\) — inversion attributes each record's multiplicity to the *least* times at which it became true, enabling sharing across epochs.

### 3.2 Lifting operators through differences

A dataflow operator \(op\) maps input collections to output collections. Its **lifted** version \(\uparrow\!op\) operates on differences:

\[
\uparrow\!op(\delta C)_t = \delta(op(C))_t.
\]

The central algebraic fact is the **linearity law**: the output difference at \(t\) depends only on input differences at \(s \le t\), and bilinear operators expand differences combinatorially:

| Operator | On collections | Lifted (differential) form |
|---|---|---|
| `map(f)` | \(\{ (f(d), m) \}\) | \(\uparrow\!map(f)(\delta C)_t = map(f)(\delta C_t)\) |
| `filter(p)` | \(\{ (d, m) : p(d) \}\) | pointwise on differences |
| `concat` | multiset union (add multiplicities) | **linear**: \(\uparrow\!concat = concat\) on differences |
| `join` | \((k,(a,b))\), multiplicities multiplied | \(\delta(A \bowtie B)_t = \sum_{r \lor s = t} \delta A_r \bowtie \delta B_s\) (convolution over the poset) |
| `reduce` / `group` | per-key aggregation | per-key difference accumulation |
| `iterate` | least fixed point of a step function | differences flow through loop scopes |

The join rule is the discrete product rule \((fg)' = f'g + fg'\): because multiplicities multiply, the difference of a join is a *convolution* of input differences over the time poset, and retractions — just negative multiplicities — need no special-case logic, unlike DRed-style over-delete-and-rederive.

### 3.3 Timestamp lattices in practice

In real dataflows, times are **product orders**. The canonical case is \(\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}\) with \((t_1, i_1) \le (t_2, i_2)\) iff \(t_1 \le t_2 \land i_1 \le i_2\): first coordinate the epoch of the input change, second the iteration count; nested loops yield \(\mathbb{N}^3\). Incomparable times proceed concurrently; a totally ordered system would serialize them.

### 3.4 Research method

This thesis is synthetic and formal: we reconstruct definitions from the primary sources [1][3][5], verify the lifting laws against the reference Rust implementations [6], and connect the framework to DBSP's published formalism [4]. The central lifting theorem is proved in full; the progress-tracking safety argument follows Murray et al.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Multidimensional Timestamps and the Lattice of Versions

A **timestamp** is an element of a poset \((T, \le)\); timely dataflow in practice uses products of chains, hence lattices. Consider iterative reachability: nodes \(N\) and edges \(E\) arrive over epochs, and we iterate \(R_{i+1} = R_i \cup (R_i \bowtie E)\). Differential dataflow expresses this as a *single* dataflow with a loop scope; timestamp \((t, i)\) means "state incorporating input changes up to epoch \(t\), after \(i\) iterations." Consequences:

- **Monotonicity of information.** If \((t_1, i_1) \le (t_2, i_2)\), the collection at \((t_2, i_2)\) incorporates everything known at \((t_1, i_1)\). Differences \(\delta C_{(t,i)}\) are genuinely *new* information at that lattice point.
- **Incomparability enables parallelism.** \((3, 7)\) and \((4, 2)\) are incomparable: the seventh iterate over epoch-3 data and the second iterate over epoch-4 data share no dependency and run concurrently.
- **Consolidation is Möbius inversion.** \(C_t = \sum_{s \le t} \delta C_s\). Implementations never materialize every \(C_t\); they maintain an *indexed* structure of differences (the trace, §4.3) and reconstruct on demand, compacting differences that cancel.

```rust
// Timestamps as product orders in timely dataflow (schematic)
use timely::order::Product;

// (epoch, iteration): a partial order, not a total one.
let t1: Product<usize, usize> = Product::new(3, 7);
let t2: Product<usize, usize> = Product::new(4, 2);
assert!(!t1.less_than(&t2) && !t2.less_than(&t1)); // incomparable: run concurrently
```

### 4.2 Timely Dataflow: Progress Tracking with Pointstamps and Frontiers

Timely dataflow's runtime contribution is a **distributed progress-tracking protocol** for arbitrary timestamp posets, nested loops, and dynamic graphs:

1. **Pointstamps.** Every message carries a pointstamp \((\text{location}, \text{timestamp})\): *where* in the graph and *when* in logical time. Operators also hold internal pointstamps for buffered-but-unemitted work and retained capabilities.
2. **Frontiers.** Each operator input maintains a frontier — an antichain of timestamps it may still receive. Frontiers flow *against* data: an operator summarizes its held pointstamps into implications for its inputs, propagating backwards to the sources.
3. **Scopes and timestamp remapping.** A loop is a *scope* with ingress/egress operators. Entering maps \(t \mapsto (t, 0)\); each iteration increments the inner coordinate. The tracker understands this remapping, so frontiers inside and outside loops stay comparable through monotone maps — this is what makes nested iteration *sound*.
4. **Probes.** A probe reports a stream's frontier — the timestamps for which computation is *complete*. A driver advances input epochs and blocks on the probe until the frontier passes the epoch, yielding deterministic, exactly-once batch semantics atop an asynchronous runtime.

> **Theorem (Progress safety, Murray et al. [3]):** If a probe's frontier advances beyond timestamp \(t\) (every frontier element strictly greater than \(t\)), then every message that will ever bear a timestamp \(s \le t\) on that stream has been delivered, and no operator will subsequently emit one.

The safety invariant behind it: *no operator emits output for \(t\) while any pointstamp with timestamp \(s \le t\) remains unprocessed anywhere in the system.* Downstream operators thus observe a *consistent cut* of the partially ordered computation — the distributed analogue of a Chandy–Lamport snapshot, computed continuously at fine granularity. Because tracking is *decentralized* (workers exchange pointstamp summaries and compute frontiers locally), the protocol scales without a central coordinator, and independent subcomputations advance independently.

### 4.3 Arrangements, Traces, and Merge Batches

Naïve difference maintenance would re-scan all differences per lookup. The implementation answer is the **arrangement**: a shared, indexed, multi-versioned representation of a collection's differences.

- **Trace.** The indexed history of differences: per key, a sorted list of \((value, time, diff)\) triples. It serves *reconstruction* ("accumulation of this key at time \(t\)?" — sum over the down-set via a seeking cursor) and *differentiation* ("what changed between frontiers \(f_1\) and \(f_2\)?") from one structure.
- **Merge batches.** Incoming differences arrive as small unsorted batches. Instead of merging each into the trace at \(O(n)\) cost, batches sit in an LSM-tree-like hierarchy of geometrically growing sizes, consolidated by a background *merger*. Each batch carries *descriptions* — the lower/upper frontiers of times it covers — so cursors skip irrelevant batches. The `arrange` operator builds these; `join` and `group` consume arranged inputs directly, **sharing one physical index among many logical readers** — a key economy, since base collections often feed several operators.

Amortized complexity is clean: each update participates in \(O(\log n)\) merges, and consolidation cancels opposite multiplicities, so trace size tracks the *logical* state plus uncompacted differences. Retention is proportional to the *distinguishable* versions still needed.

```rust
// Differential dataflow: arrange once, share across operators (schematic)
use differential_dataflow::operators::{arrange::ArrangeByKey, Join};

let arranged_edges = edges.arrange_by_key();

let two_hop = arranged_edges.join_core(&arranged_edges, |_k, &a, &b| Some((a, b)));
let triangles = two_hop.join_core(&arranged_edges, |_k, &a, &b| Some((a, b)));
// Both joins read the SAME trace; differences propagate incrementally.
```

### 4.4 From Differential Dataflow to DBSP: The Z-set Circuit Calculus

DBSP (Budiu et al., VLDB 2022, best paper [4]) is the algebraic retrospective differential dataflow deserved. *Streams* are functions \(s : \mathbb{N} \to A\) with \(A\) an Abelian group; three operators mediate streams and values:

- **Integration** \(\mathcal{I}\): \((\mathcal{I}s)[t] = \sum_{i \le t} s[i]\) — changes into states.
- **Differentiation** \(\mathcal{D}\): \((\mathcal{D}s)[t] = s[t] - s[t-1]\) — states into changes.
- **Lifting** \(\uparrow\!f\): apply \(f\) pointwise in time.

Databases are not groups (set union has no inverse), so tables become **Z-sets**: functions \(D \to \mathbb{Z}\) — exactly differential dataflow's collections. SQL over Z-sets: `UNION ALL` is addition, `UNION` is addition then `distinct`, `JOIN` multiplies weights. The incremental version of any query \(Q\) follows the **chain rule**:

\[
Q^{\Delta} = \mathcal{D} \circ \uparrow\!Q \circ \mathcal{I}.
\]

Integrate the change-stream into states, apply the query pointwise, differentiate back into changes. For *linear* operators (map, filter, concat), \(Q^{\Delta} = \uparrow\!Q\) — no state needed. For *bilinear* join, the chain rule yields the product-rule expansion: the convolution of §3.2. ## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 The lifting theorem

We prove the result justifying the framework: differentiation commutes with lifting, so computing on differences is *exactly* equivalent to computing on collections.

> **Theorem (Correctness of lifting):** Let \(op\) be any operator composed from the combinators of §3.2, and \(C\) an input collection over timestamp poset \(T\) with differences \(\delta C\) by Möbius inversion. Then for every \(t \in T\):
>
> \[
> \sum_{s \le t} \uparrow\!op(\delta C)_s = op\!\left(\sum_{s \le t} \delta C_s\right) = op(C_t).
> \]

*Proof.* By structural induction. **Base cases:** `map(f)` and `filter(p)` apply pointwise with multiplicities preserved — equality holds per \(s \le t\). `concat` is pointwise addition of group-valued functions; summation commutes with addition. **Join:** with weight multiplication, the lifted form is the poset convolution \(\uparrow\!J(\delta A, \delta B)_u = \sum_{r \lor s = u} \delta A_r \bowtie \delta B_s\). Summing over \(u \le t\):

\[
\sum_{u \le t}\; \sum_{r \lor s = u} \delta A_r \bowtie \delta B_s
= \sum_{r \le t}\sum_{s \le t} \delta A_r \bowtie \delta B_s
= \left(\sum_{r \le t}\delta A_r\right) \bowtie \left(\sum_{s \le t}\delta B_s\right) = A_t \bowtie B_t,
\]

by bilinearity of \(\bowtie\) and the lattice identity \(\{u : u \le t\} = \bigsqcup_{r \lor s = u \le t}\): each pair \((r, s)\) with \(r, s \le t\) appears exactly once, at \(u = r \lor s \le t\). **Reduce:** per-key accumulation in the trace (§4.3) plus the trace's reconstruction invariant. **Iterate:** loop timestamps are \((t, i)\); differences flow through the step function, and iterating differences reaches the difference of the fixed points by the Kleene argument relativized to each \(t\). ∎

**Corollary (Incremental complexity):** Work at time \(t\) scales with the *nonzero differences* at \(s \le t\) — the size of the *change*, not the collection — plus merge costs (§4.3). Retractions cost no more than insertions: both are signed multiplicities through one code path.

### 5.2 Empirical evidence

The Naiad prototype evaluation [3] applied differential dataflow to graph analytics on a single multicore machine — strongly connected components, shortest paths — over graphs with tens of millions of edges. The headline: after initial computation, *single-edge updates propagated to the output at interactive, millisecond-scale latencies*, versus minutes for batch recomputation, with near-linear shared-memory scaling.

The Rust reimplementation [6] hardened these ideas — worst-case-optimal joins, columnar arrangements, the merge-batcher hierarchy — to the point where Materialize productized them: thousands of concurrent SQL views over changing tables with millisecond freshness, the industrial existence proof that the lifting theorem survives contact with real workloads combining *joins, recursion, and retractions*. DBSP's evaluation [4] adds the compiler perspective: Feldera's runtime incrementalizes full SQL, recognized by the VLDB 2022 best paper and 2024 SIGMOD Research Highlights [7].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Memory retention of differences.** The trace retains differences until every reader's frontier advances past them. With *slow* readers — e.g., a long-converging iteration while new epochs arrive — uncompacted differences accumulate; pure insertions with ever-advancing epochs grow the trace monotonically. The framework trades space (indexed history) for time (incremental updates), and adversarial workloads can make space dominate.
2. **The Abelian group boundary.** Everything rests on \(\mathbb{Z}\)-valued collections. Non-group operators — `min`/`max` under retraction, `distinct` — need auxiliary structures. DBSP systematizes this [4], but the resulting circuits are more complex than the linear/bilinear core.
3. **Join skew.** A hot key serializes what the timestamp lattice parallelizes. Arrangements mitigate read costs, not the intrinsic work of a heavy hitter's delta.
4. **Analyzability.** The model is Turing-complete in ways that defeat static analysis: no general decision procedure exists for liveness (will frontiers advance?), and an operator that never releases capabilities stalls the computation silently. The type system guarantees memory safety, not progress.
5. **Cognitive load.** Partially ordered time — antichains, down-sets, Möbius inversion — is far from the "stream of batches" mental model. Tooling for explaining stalls and visualizing frontiers remains an active area.

## 7 Conclusion

Differential dataflow and timely dataflow jointly solved a long-standing problem: how to compute *iteratively* and *incrementally* in one framework. The solution restates in one sentence: **index state by a partially ordered set of times, represent change as signed differences in an Abelian group, lift operators through differences via the linearity law, and track progress with antichain frontiers so incomparable times proceed concurrently.** From this seed grow arrangements with merge batches, the probe-driven exactly-once runtime, and — a decade later — the DBSP circuit calculus with its chain rule for automatic incrementalization [1][3][4].

The trajectory from Microsoft Research prototype to production streaming database shows how the right abstraction compounds: once change is *algebraic* (signed multiplicities) and time is *order-theoretic* (posets, frontiers), iteration, streaming, retractions, and distribution become coordinates of one design space. Open problems remain — memory-bounded difference retention, automatic operator synthesis in the DBSP style, making partially ordered time *teachable* — but the core insight is secure: **time need not be a line, and change need not be recomputation.**
