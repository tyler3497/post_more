# The Sum-of-Squares Hierarchy: Semidefinite Programming Relaxations, Approximation Algorithms, and Integrality Gaps

## Abstract

The sum-of-squares (SoS) hierarchy is the most powerful known systematic method for converting hard nonconvex optimization problems into tractable convex ones. Independently discovered by Shor, Nesterov, Parrilo, Lasserre, and Grigoriev around the turn of the millennium, it organizes semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations of polynomial optimization into levels indexed by a degree parameter d, with the level-d relaxation solvable in n^{O(d)} time and converging to the global optimum as d grows. Its dual objects — pseudo-distributions — behave like genuine probability distributions on all low-degree polynomials, and its primal objects are sum-of-squares certificates of nonnegativity justified by Putinar's Positivstellensatz. This survey develops the hierarchy from first principles: the moment-matrix construction of Lasserre [1], the sum-of-squares programming framework of Parrilo [2], the interpretation of the Goemans–Williamson Max-Cut algorithm as degree-2 SoS, Raghavendra's optimality program under the Unique Games Conjecture, and SoS lower bounds — the integrality gaps of Khot and Saket and the pseudo-calibration framework of Barak et al. for planted clique.

## 1 Introduction

Most optimization problems of practical interest are nonconvex. Polynomial optimization — minimizing a multivariate polynomial subject to polynomial inequality constraints — is NP-hard in general, and the combinatorial problems of theoretical computer science (Max-Cut, Max-3SAT, graph coloring, unique games) are all special cases obtained by encoding discrete variables as polynomial equations such as x_i^2 = 1. For decades, each such problem demanded its own bespoke relaxation and its own rounding scheme, and algorithmic ingenuity was spent one problem at a time.

The sum-of-squares hierarchy proposes a radical alternative: a *single* parameterized family of semidefinite programs that, given any polynomial optimization problem and any degree bound d, mechanically produces a convex relaxation of strength d. Increase d and the relaxation tightens; at d = 2 one recovers the Goemans–Williamson SDP for Max-Cut; at large d the relaxations provably converge to the exact optimum under mild compactness assumptions. The hierarchy thus unifies Lasserre's moment relaxations [1] and Parrilo's sum-of-squares programming [2] into one object, and — through the lens of pseudo-distributions — it also unifies a growing list of algorithmic successes: subexponential algorithms for Unique Games, dictionary learning, tensor decomposition, robust statistics, and quantum information protocols, as surveyed by Barak and Steurer [5].

But the hierarchy's fame rests equally on its *limitations*. Because SoS is a proof system as well as an algorithm, every integrality gap — every instance where the SDP optimum strictly exceeds the true optimum — is a theorem about what SoS proofs cannot do at a given degree. Khot and Saket constructed integrality gaps for the basic SDP with local embeddability properties that constrain every rounding scheme [8]; Grigoriev showed that SoS cannot efficiently refute random 3XOR instances even though Gaussian elimination disposes of them in polynomial time; and Barak et al. proved a nearly tight lower bound for the planted clique problem, showing that degree o(log n) SoS cannot beat the trivial spectral threshold, via a new *pseudo-calibration* heuristic for designing hard instances [6]. This survey presents both faces of the hierarchy: the strongest general-purpose approximation paradigm known, and a proof system whose boundary with efficient computation is among the deepest open questions in complexity theory.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Semidefinite programming

A *semidefinite program* (SDP) optimizes a linear objective over the intersection of an affine subspace with the cone of positive semidefinite matrices:

> **Theorem:** (SDP duality). The primal-dual pair minimize ⟨C, X⟩ subject to ⟨A_i, X⟩ = b_i, X ⪰ 0 and its dual satisfy weak duality, and strong duality holds under Slater's condition (strict feasibility of either side).

SDPs are solvable to arbitrary accuracy in polynomial time by interior-point methods. Their expressive power comes from the fact that the PSD cone is a *spectrahedral shadow* of many nonconvex sets: optimizing over X ⪰ 0 with rank-one constraints X = xx^T recovers the original problem, and dropping the rank constraint yields a convex relaxation. The entire SoS hierarchy is the systematic exploitation of this single move.

### 2.2 Sums of squares and the Positivstellensatz

A polynomial p is a *sum of squares* (SoS) if p = Σ_i q_i^2 for polynomials q_i. Every SoS polynomial is globally nonnegative, but the converse fails — the Motzkin form x^4 y^2 + x^2 y^4 − 3x^2 y^2 + 1 is nonnegative yet not SoS. Deciding whether a degree-2d polynomial in n variables is SoS is, however, exactly an SDP: writing p(x) = z(x)^T Q z(x) with z(x) the vector of all monomials of degree at most d, p is SoS if and only if there exists Q ⪰ 0 satisfying the coefficient-matching linear equations [2].

For *constrained* nonnegativity, the right certificate is Putinar's Positivstellensatz: on a compact semialgebraic set K = {x : g_i(x) ≥ 0} satisfying an Archimedean condition, every polynomial strictly positive on K admits a representation p = s_0 + Σ_i s_i g_i with each s_i a sum of squares. Bounding the degrees of the multipliers s_i turns this infinite certificate into a finite SDP — this is Parrilo's sum-of-squares programming [2] and, dually, Lasserre's moment hierarchy [1].

### 2.3 The moment problem and duality

The dual viewpoint replaces the unknown point x by an unknown *moment sequence* y_α = E[x^α]. For a true probability measure, the *moment matrix* M_d(y) with entries (M_d(y))_{α,β} = y_{α+β} over multi-indices of degree at most d is positive semidefinite, as are the *localizing matrices* M_{d−d_i}(g_i y) for each constraint polynomial g_i. Lasserre's level-d relaxation minimizes the linearized objective over all y satisfying these PSD constraints [1]. By conic duality, the primal moment relaxation and the dual SoS-certificate SDP bound each other, and under the Archimedean condition the bounds converge to the global optimum as d → ∞. Nie and collaborators further showed that convergence is *generically finite*: for almost every problem instance, some finite level is exact.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The degree-d SoS relaxation

Given a polynomial optimization problem min{p(x) : g_i(x) ≥ 0, h_j(x) = 0}, the degree-d SoS relaxation proceeds mechanically:

1. **Linearize.** Introduce a variable y_α for every monomial x^α of total degree at most 2d, and replace the objective by the linear form L_y(p) = Σ_α p_α y_α.
2. **Moment constraint.** Require M_d(y) ⪰ 0, where M_d(y) is indexed by monomials of degree at most d.
3. **Localizing constraints.** For each inequality g_i ≥ 0 of degree 2d_i, require M_{d−d_i}(g_i y) ⪰ 0; for each equality h_j = 0, require L_y(q h_j) = 0 for all q of degree at most 2d − deg h_j.
4. **Normalize** y_∅ = 1 and solve the resulting SDP.

The SDP has n^{O(d)} variables and PSD blocks of size n^{O(d)}; for constant d it is polynomial-time solvable. Its optimum is a lower bound on the true optimum, monotone nondecreasing in d, and convergent under the Archimedean hypothesis [1].

### 3.2 Pseudo-distributions: the dual objects

A *degree-d pseudo-distribution* is a linear functional Ẽ: R[x]_{≤d} → R satisfying Ẽ[1] = 1 and Ẽ[q^2] ≥ 0 for all q of degree at most d/2. It behaves exactly like an expectation operator on all low-degree polynomials — it is linear, normalized, and nonnegative on squares — while potentially assigning "negative probability" to high-degree events. The fundamental duality is:

> **Theorem:** (SoS duality, [5]). For an explicitly bounded polynomial system, exactly one of the following holds: there exists a degree-d SoS proof of the desired bound, or there exists a degree-d pseudo-distribution satisfying all constraints and violating the bound.

This is the engine of both algorithm design (round the pseudo-distribution to a real solution) and lower bounds (exhibit a pseudo-distribution no low-degree proof can rule out). A pseudo-distribution that satisfies constraints x_i^2 = 1 but has Ẽ[objective] far above every true assignment is precisely an *integrality gap*.

### 3.3 Degree 2 recovers Goemans–Williamson

For Max-Cut with variables x_i ∈ {−1, 1}, the degree-2 SoS relaxation introduces y_{ij} = Ẽ[x_i x_j] and requires the matrix Y = (y_{ij}) to be PSD with unit diagonal — exactly the Goemans–Williamson SDP [4]. Random hyperplane rounding converts Y into a cut. The analysis reduces to the one-dimensional inequality:

> **Theorem:** (Goemans–Williamson [4]). For unit vectors v_i, random hyperplane rounding cuts each edge {i,j} with probability arccos(⟨v_i,v_j⟩)/π, and min over inner products of the ratio to the SDP contribution (1 − ⟨v_i,v_j⟩)/2 equals α_GW ≈ 0.8785672. Hence the algorithm is a 0.87856-approximation for Max-Cut.

Karloff showed this analysis is tight: no valid linear constraints added to the GW SDP can improve the ratio, so the degree-2 SoS proof system *exactly* captures the approximability of Max-Cut by this method.

### 3.4 From relaxations to algorithms: rounding

An SoS relaxation only yields a pseudo-distribution; extracting a genuine solution requires *rounding*. Barak, Kelner, and Steurer gave a general rounding framework: any *combining algorithm* that maps a distribution over solutions to a single solution can be simulated inside the SoS proof system, so that if a good solution can be obtained from samples, SoS finds one [5]. This explains the surprising power of constant-degree SoS on average-case problems — planted sparse vector, dictionary learning, tensor PCA — where the pseudo-distribution concentrates enough information for spectral or iterative rounding to succeed.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The geometry of the hierarchy: tightening spectrahedra

At each level d, the feasible set of the SoS relaxation is a *spectrahedron* — an affine section of the PSD cone — that contains the convex hull of the true feasible set. Increasing d intersects this spectrahedron with additional PSD constraints on higher moment matrices, producing a nested sequence:

conv(feasible) ⊆ S_∞ ⊆ ⋯ ⊆ S_6 ⊆ S_4 ⊆ S_2

where S_d is the projection of the level-d feasible set onto the original variables. Finite convergence means S_d = conv(feasible) for some d on generic instances. The *size* of the description grows as n^{O(d)}, so the hierarchy trades off strength against complexity in a smooth, quantifiable way. This is the precise sense in which SoS is a *proof system*: each level corresponds to proofs using only degree-d sum-of-squares reasoning, and the nested spectrahedra are the semantic counterpart of increasingly powerful syntactic proof rules.

### 4.2 SoS proofs as a proof system

A *degree-d SoS proof* that p(x) ≥ 0 on K = {g_i ≥ 0} is an identity p = s_0 + Σ s_i g_i with each s_i SoS and total degree at most d. This is a bona fide propositional-style proof system for real algebraic statements, extending both the Nullstellensatz (for equations) and Lovász–Schrijver/Sherali–Adams hierarchies (for 0/1 problems). Key structural facts:

- **Soundness** is immediate: every SoS proof certifies truth on K.
- **Completeness** follows from Putinar's theorem: every true strict inequality has *some* finite-degree proof.
- **Automatizability** is the SDP: finding a degree-d proof is polynomial-time in n^{O(d)}.

Grigoriev initiated the *proof complexity* of SoS, proving degree lower bounds for the knapsack problem and for refuting random 3XOR formulas — the first demonstrations that SoS, despite its algorithmic power, cannot simulate Gaussian elimination or count modulo small integers efficiently. These results locate SoS strictly between weak algebraic systems (polynomial calculus) and strong ones (Frege) in the proof-complexity landscape.

### 4.3 Unique Games, Raghavendra's theorem, and the optimality program

Raghavendra's 2008 STOC result is the philosophical centerpiece of the SoS research program: assuming the Unique Games Conjecture, for *every* constraint satisfaction problem there is a single canonical SDP whose integrality gap *equals* the optimal polynomial-time approximation ratio [7]. The algorithm is generic — solve the basic SDP, then round using the optimal rounding scheme derived from the gap instance itself — and the matching hardness comes from a generic reduction from Unique Games. In other words, *if* the UGC holds, then algorithm design for CSPs is finished: one SDP rules them all.

SoS enters because the basic SDP in Raghavendra's theorem is a weak fragment of SoS, and the natural question is whether *stronger* SoS levels beat it. For Max-Cut the answer is no (Karloff's tightness). For Unique Games itself, Arora, Barak, and Steurer gave a *subexponential* algorithm via higher SoS levels, showing the problem is easier than the UGC's hardest conjectured regime [5]. Whether constant-degree SoS refutes the UGC outright remains open — and is arguably the single most important open problem about the hierarchy. Khot and Saket's integrality gaps with local ℓ_1-embeddability [8] show that the basic SDP is already optimal among a natural class of relaxations for certain problems, sharpening the boundary of what stronger proofs could possibly add.

### 4.4 Lower bounds: pseudo-calibration and planted clique

The most sophisticated SoS lower-bound technology is *pseudo-calibration*, introduced by Barak, Hopkins, Kelner, Kothari, Moitra, and Potechin [6]. The idea is a computational analogue of Bayesian reasoning: to fool the degree-d SoS relaxation on a random instance, plant a solution, then define the pseudo-expectation Ẽ by *calibrating* low-degree moments to match the planted distribution — i.e., set Ẽ of each low-degree polynomial in the instance to its expectation under planting. Because the planted distribution is a genuine distribution, low-degree positivity is inherited automatically; the hard work is verifying the PSD constraints on high-degree moment matrices, which reduces to delicate random-matrix estimates.

Applied to the planted clique problem — distinguish G(n, 1/2) from G(n, 1/2) with a planted k-clique — pseudo-calibration yields a *nearly tight* lower bound:

> **Theorem:** (Barak et al. [6]). With high probability over G ∼ G(n, 1/2), the degree-d SoS relaxation of the k-clique problem attains value at least n^{1/2 − c(d/log n)} for some constant c > 0. In particular, for d = o(log n) the SoS value is n^{1/2 − o(1)}, matching (up to lower-order terms) the guarantee of the trivial spectral algorithm.

Thus SoS needs degree Ω(log n) — i.e., quasi-polynomial time — to improve on the simple eigenvalue bound for planted clique, even though information-theoretic recovery is possible at much smaller clique sizes. Pseudo-calibration has since become the standard template for average-case SoS lower bounds: tensor PCA, sparse PCA, community detection, and robust estimation all have matching upper and lower bounds proved in this framework. The emerging *low-degree likelihood* heuristic — that low-degree polynomial tests capture the power of all efficient algorithms on average-case problems — is the conjectural culmination of this line: SoS lower bounds would then imply hardness against *all* polynomial-time algorithms, not just SoS.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Goemans–Williamson ratio, numerically

The GW analysis reduces to a univariate optimization. The following snippet computes the exact worst-case ratio and locates where it is attained:

```python
import numpy as np

def gw_ratio(theta):
    # theta in (0, pi]: angle between SDP vectors
    sdp = (1.0 - np.cos(theta)) / 2.0
    rounded = theta / np.pi
    return rounded / sdp

thetas = np.linspace(1e-6, np.pi, 2000001)
r = gw_ratio(thetas)
i = int(np.argmin(r))
print(f"alpha_GW = {r[i]:.7f} at theta = {thetas[i]:.5f} rad")
# alpha_GW = 0.8785672 at theta = 2.33112 rad
```

The minimum α_GW ≈ 0.8785672 is attained at θ ≈ 2.33112 radians, strictly inside the open interval (0, π).

> **Proof:** (sketch of the GW ratio). For unit vectors u, v with angle θ, a random hyperplane with normal r ∼ N(0, I) separates them iff sign(⟨u,r⟩) ≠ sign(⟨v,r⟩), an event of probability θ/π by spherical symmetry. The SDP contribution of the edge is (1 − cos θ)/2. The ratio θ/π divided by (1 − cos θ)/2 is minimized over θ ∈ (0, π] by calculus at θ ≈ 2.33112, giving α_GW ≈ 0.8785672. Linearity of expectation over edges completes the argument. ∎

## 6 Limitations

The hierarchy's power is sharply delimited, and the delimitations are theorems, not conjectures.

**Complexity scaling.** Level d costs n^{O(d)} time and memory. Constant d gives polynomial time but fixed proof strength; d = Ω(log n) already costs quasi-polynomial time. The planted-clique lower bound [6] shows this tradeoff is *inherent*: beating the spectral threshold genuinely requires super-constant degree.

**Reasoning blind spots.** SoS proofs cannot efficiently simulate Gaussian elimination (Grigoriev's 3XOR lower bound), cannot count (knapsack integrality gaps), and cannot capture probabilistic arguments that are not "robust" in the low-degree sense. Any algorithm whose correctness proof uses these ingredients lies outside constant-degree SoS.

**Numerical and structural issues.** Large moment matrices are ill-conditioned; interior-point solvers struggle past a few thousand variables, which is why practical SoS stops at d = 2 or 4 despite the theory. Duality gaps can appear without strict feasibility, and extracting *exact* rational certificates from floating-point SDP solutions remains delicate.

**The UGC question.** If the Unique Games Conjecture is true, Raghavendra's theorem [7] says the basic SDP is already optimal for every CSP and higher SoS levels add nothing — but we cannot prove it. If it is false, SoS is the leading candidate for the refutation, yet after two decades no constant-degree SoS algorithm beats the basic SDP on Unique Games itself. Either resolution would be a landmark; the hierarchy sits exactly on the fault line.

## 7 Conclusion

The sum-of-squares hierarchy is best understood as three things at once: a *convergent discretization* of infinite-dimensional moment problems (Lasserre [1]); a *proof system* for real algebraic reasoning with a precise duality theory (Parrilo [2], Barak–Steurer [5]); and an *algorithmic paradigm* whose degree-2 case contains the Goemans–Williamson breakthrough [4] and whose higher levels capture the frontier of average-case algorithm design. Its successes — tensor decomposition, dictionary learning, robust statistics, subexponential Unique Games — demonstrate that "solve the SoS relaxation and round" is the closest thing theoretical computer science has to a universal algorithm. Its failures — planted clique [6], random CSPs, the Unique Games Conjecture itself [7][8] — are equally valuable, because each integrality gap is a theorem about the limits of efficient reasoning.

The central open question remains: does constant-degree SoS already achieve the optimal approximation ratio for every CSP, making Raghavendra's basic SDP redundant — or does the hierarchy stall exactly where the Unique Games Conjecture says it must? Pseudo-calibration and the low-degree likelihood heuristic suggest the hierarchy's limits may coincide with the limits of efficient computation itself. Whether that coincidence is real will decide if SoS is merely our best algorithm or, in a precise sense, the *only* algorithm that matters.
