# Information Geometry of Statistical Manifolds: The Fisher–Rao Metric, Dually Flat Structures, and Amari's Natural Gradient

## Abstract

Information geometry treats a parametric family of probability distributions as a Riemannian manifold equipped with the Fisher–Rao metric, and enriches it with a one-parameter family of affine connections — the Amari–Čencov α-connections — whose mutual duality generalizes the Levi-Civita calculus of classical differential geometry. This essay develops the subject from its statistical foundations: the Fisher information matrix as the unique (up to scale) monotone Riemannian metric on statistical manifolds, the dually flat structure of exponential families generated by the Kullback–Leibler divergence as a canonical Bregman divergence, and the α-divergences that interpolate between reverse KL, Hellinger, and χ² geometry. Central to the exposition is Amari's *natural gradient*, the Riemannian gradient induced by the Fisher–Rao metric, which renders optimization invariant under reparametrization and escapes plateaus that trap ordinary gradient descent. We present the key theorems — Čencov's characterization, the dual Pythagorean theorem, and Fisher efficiency of natural gradient learning — alongside proofs, empirical results from neural network training, and applications spanning variational inference, the Lp-generalized Fisher–Rao metrics of Bauer et al. [2], and curvature-aware deep learning [6].

##1 Introduction

Statistical inference is, at its core, a geometric activity. When we fit a parametric model $p(x \mid \theta)$ to data, we are selecting a point on a *manifold* of probability distributions; when we optimize a loss, we traverse that manifold; and when we compare two models, we measure a *distance* between distributions. Classical statistics treated these operations as if the parameter space $\theta$ were Euclidean. Information geometry, pioneered by Shunichi Amari and formalized through the work of Čencov, Nagaoka, and others, insists instead that the natural geometry of a statistical model is the Riemannian geometry induced by the Fisher information metric [1][7].

The consequences of this insistence are profound. The Fisher–Rao metric is the unique Riemannian metric (up to a constant factor) that is *invariant under sufficient statistics* — a theorem of Čencov that singles it out among all possible geometries on the probability simplex [2]. Around this metric, Amari constructed a one-parameter family of affine connections, the α-connections, that are pairwise *dual*: $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$ is dual to $\nabla^{(-\alpha)}$ with respect to the Fisher metric. For $\alpha = 0$ one recovers the Levi-Civita connection and the familiar Riemannian geodesic structure; for $\alpha = \pm 1$ one obtains the *exponential* and *mixture* connections, which are flat on exponential and mixture families respectively. When both members of a dual pair are flat, the manifold is *dually flat*, and the entire geometry is generated by a single potential function — a structure that underlies the dually flat geometry of the Kullback–Leibler divergence and its associated Bregman divergences.

This geometric edifice is not merely aesthetic. Amari's *natural gradient* — the Riemannian gradient with respect to the Fisher–Rao metric — transforms optimization on statistical manifolds into a coordinate-free descent that is invariant under reparametrization of the model and provably Fisher-efficient in maximum-likelihood learning [1]. The method escapes the plateau phenomena that stall ordinary gradient descent in multilayer networks, and it has seeded a generation of curvature-aware optimizers (K-FAC, adaptive natural gradient, and Riemannian score-matching variants) that remain central to modern deep learning [6][8].

In this essay we develop information geometry from first principles: the Fisher–Rao metric and its uniqueness, the α-connections and the Amari–Čencov tensor, dually flat manifolds and their potential functions, the α-divergences and their special cases, and finally the natural gradient with its theory and machine-learning applications. We include precise theorem statements, proof sketches of the central results, and empirical evidence from the literature on neural network optimization.

##2 Background

### 2.1 Statistical models as manifolds

Let $S = \{p(x \mid \theta) : \theta \in \Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^n\}$ be a parametric family of probability densities with respect to a dominating measure $\lambda$, where the parametrization $\theta \mapsto p_\theta$ is injective and sufficiently smooth. We regard $S$ as an $n$-dimensional differentiable manifold whose points are distributions and whose tangent space at $p_\theta$ is spanned by the *score functions* $\partial_i \ell = \partial \log p(x \mid \theta) / \partial \theta^i$. The fundamental random object is the log-likelihood $\ell(x; \theta) = \log p(x \mid \theta)$.

> **Theorem (Score identity):** Under standard regularity conditions permitting differentiation under the integral sign, $E_\theta[\partial_i \ell] = 0$ for every coordinate $i$.

The Fisher information matrix

$$
g_{ij}(\theta) = E_\theta[\partial_i \ell \, \partial_j \ell] = -E_\theta[\partial_i \partial_j \ell]
$$

defines a positive-definite bilinear form on each tangent space — the **Fisher–Rao metric** $g$. It measures the local distinguishability of neighboring distributions: to second order, the Kullback–Leibler divergence satisfies $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\theta \,\|\, p_{\theta + d\theta}) \approx \tfrac{1}{2} g_{ij} \, d\theta^i d\theta^j$.

### 2.2 Exponential families

The *exponential family* plays a privileged role. A family is exponential if

$$
p(x \mid \theta) = \exp\big(C(x) + \theta^i F_i(x) - \psi(\theta)\big),
$$

where $\theta$ are the *natural* (canonical) parameters, $F(x)$ the sufficient statistics, and $\psi(\theta) = \log \int \exp(C(x) + \theta^i F_i(x)) \, d\lambda$ the log-partition (cumulant) function. The *expectation parameters* $\eta_i = E_\theta[F_i] = \partial_i \psi$ form a dual coordinate system, related to $\theta$ by the Legendre transform of the convex potential $\psi$. Gaussians, Poisson, Bernoulli, multinomial, and von Mises–Fisher distributions are all exponential families, and as we shall see, they are precisely the manifolds that admit dually flat structure [7].

### 2.3 Divergences

A *divergence* $D(p \,\|\, q) \geq 0$ with equality iff $p = q$ generalizes squared distance without requiring symmetry or the triangle inequality. The Kullback–Leibler divergence

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) = \int p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \, d\lambda(x)
$$

is the canonical divergence of information geometry: Eguchi showed that any divergence induces, via its mixed second derivatives, a Riemannian metric and a pair of dual affine connections on the underlying manifold. Applied to the KL divergence this construction yields exactly the Fisher–Rao metric and the $(\pm 1)$-connections.

##3 Methodology

Our development follows the axiomatic route of Amari–Nagaoka [7], supplemented by modern extensions:

1. **Metric first:** define the Fisher–Rao metric via the score covariance; establish Čencov's uniqueness theorem (invariance under Markov morphisms).
2. **Connections second:** introduce the Amari–Čencov 3-tensor $T_{ijk} = E_\theta[\partial_i \ell \, \partial_j \ell \, \partial_k \ell]$ and the one-parameter family of α-connections $\Gamma_{ij}^{(\alpha)k} = \Gamma_{ij}^{(0)k} + \tfrac{\alpha}{2} T_{ij}{}^{k}$, where $\Gamma^{(0)}$ are the Levi-Civita symbols.
3. **Duality:** prove that $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$ and $\nabla^{(-\alpha)}$ are dual with respect to $g$, i.e. $X g(Y,Z) = g(\nabla^{(\alpha)}_X Y, Z) + g(Y, \nabla^{(-\alpha)}_X Z)$.
4. **Flatness:** characterize dually flat manifolds via potential functions and derive the generalized Pythagorean theorem.
5. **Optimization:** derive the natural gradient as the Riemannian gradient of the Fisher–Rao metric and analyze its invariance, efficiency, and practical approximations in deep learning [1][6][8].

Throughout, $\nabla^{(1)} = \nabla^{(e)}$ denotes the exponential connection, $\nabla^{(-1)} = \nabla^{(m)}$ the mixture connection, and $\nabla^{(0)}$ the Levi-Civita (metric) connection.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Fisher–Rao Metric and Čencov's Uniqueness Theorem

The Fisher–Rao metric is not one Riemannian metric among many — it is, in a precise sense, the *only* one compatible with the statistical meaning of the manifold. Čencov considered *Markov morphisms* (coarsenings of the sample space via sufficient statistics) and demanded that the geometry be *invariant* under them: compressing data through a sufficient statistic should not change distances between models, because no information is lost.

> **Theorem (Čencov's characterization):** Up to a positive constant factor, the Fisher–Rao metric is the unique Riemannian metric on finite probability simplices that is invariant under congruent embeddings by Markov morphisms (sufficient statistics).

This uniqueness result is the bedrock on which information geometry stands. It explains *why* the natural gradient uses the Fisher matrix rather than the Hessian or any other preconditioner: only the Fisher metric respects the invariance structure of statistical inference itself. Modern infinite-dimensional extensions, such as the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao gradient flows studied in [3], inherit this philosophy — the Fisher–Rao component treats *mass change* (multiplicative perturbation of densities) as the fundamental mode of variation, in contrast to the Wasserstein metric's focus on *transport* (displacement of mass).

A striking recent development is the $L^p$-Fisher–Rao family of Bauer, Le Brigant, Lu, and Maor [2], which generalizes the classical ($p = 2$) Fisher–Rao metric to a family of Finsler metrics $F_p$ on the space of densities. Remarkably, on positive densities the geodesic equations of $F_p$ coincide with those of the $\alpha$-connection for $p = 2/(1-\alpha)$, giving a new variational interpretation of α-geodesics as energy-minimizing curves — straight lines pulled back from $L^p$ space.

### 4.2 α-Connections and the Amari–Čencov Tensor

Given the Fisher metric $g$, there are infinitely many affine connections compatible with the statistical structure. Amari's insight was to organize them into a single one-parameter family. Define the totally symmetric **Amari–Čencov tensor**

$$
T_{ijk}(\theta) = E_\theta[\partial_i \ell \, \partial_j \ell \, \partial_k \ell], \qquad T_{ij}{}^{k} = g^{k\ell} T_{ij\ell}.
$$

The **α-connection** $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$ is then defined by its Christoffel symbols

$$
\Gamma_{ij}^{(\alpha)k} = \Gamma_{ij}^{(0)k} + \frac{\alpha}{2} T_{ij}{}^{k},
$$

where $\Gamma^{(0)}$ are the Levi-Civita symbols of $g$. The parameter $\alpha$ is real-valued, and the family interpolates between three distinguished geometries:

| $\alpha$ | Connection | Flat on | Geodesics |
|---|---|---|---|
| $+1$ | exponential $\nabla^{(e)}$ | exponential families | straight in $\theta$ (natural params) |
| $0$ | Levi-Civita $\nabla^{(0)}$ | — (metric geometry) | Fisher–Rao geodesics |
| $-1$ | mixture $\nabla^{(m)}$ | mixture families | straight in densities / $\eta$ |

Duality is the organizing principle:

> **Theorem (Mutual duality):** For every real $\alpha$, the connections $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$ and $\nabla^{(-\alpha)}$ are dual with respect to the Fisher–Rao metric $g$: $X\,g(Y,Z) = g(\nabla^{(\alpha)}_X Y, Z) + g(Y, \nabla^{(-\alpha)}_X Z)$ for all vector fields $X, Y, Z$.

In particular the exponential and mixture connections form a dual pair, and the Levi-Civita connection is self-dual. This is the precise sense in which information geometry *generalizes* Riemannian geometry: a statistical manifold is a quadruple $(M, g, \nabla, \nabla^*)$ with $\nabla, \nabla^*$ torsion-free and dual, and ordinary Riemannian geometry is the special case $\nabla = \nabla^* = \nabla^{(0)}$ [3].

### 4.3 Dually Flat Manifolds and Bregman Geometry

When *both* members of a dual pair are flat (vanishing curvature and torsion), the manifold is **dually flat** — the crown jewel of information geometry. A dually flat manifold admits:

- a pair of **dual affine coordinate systems** $\theta$ (affine for $\nabla$) and $\eta$ (affine for $\nabla^*$),
- a strictly convex **potential function** $\psi(\theta)$ with $g_{ij} = \partial_i \partial_j \psi$ and $\eta_i = \partial_i \psi$,
- a dual potential $\phi(\eta)$ related by the Legendre transform $\psi(\theta) + \phi(\eta) - \theta^i \eta_i = 0$.

Exponential families are the canonical examples: $\psi$ is the log-partition function, $\theta$ the natural parameters, $\eta$ the expectation parameters, and the Fisher metric is the Hessian $g_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j \psi$. On a dually flat manifold, the canonical divergence

$$
D(p \,\|\, q) = \psi(\theta_p) + \phi(\eta_q) - \theta_p^i \eta_{q,i}
$$

is a **Bregman divergence**, and for exponential families it coincides with the Kullback–Leibler divergence. The geometry then yields a generalized Pythagorean theorem of remarkable power:

> **Theorem (Dual Pythagorean theorem):** Let $p, q, r$ be points of a dually flat manifold. If the $\nabla$-geodesic from $p$ to $q$ is orthogonal (in $g$) at $q$ to the $\nabla^*$-geodesic from $q$ to $r$, then $D(p \,\|\, r) = D(p \,\|\, q) + D(q \,\|\, r)$.

This single identity underlies the *information projections* of statistics: the $m$-projection (mixture geodesic) and $e$-projection (exponential geodesic) onto submanifolds, which generalize maximum-likelihood estimation and the EM algorithm. Iterative $e$- and $m$-projections converge by repeated Pythagorean decomposition — a geometric explanation of why EM works.

### 4.4 α-Divergences

The α-connections are generated, in Eguchi's sense, by a one-parameter family of divergences — the **α-divergences**:

$$
D_\alpha(p \,\|\, q) = \frac{4}{1-\alpha^2}\left(1 - \int p(x)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}} q(x)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}} \, d\lambda(x)\right), \qquad \alpha \neq \pm 1,
$$

with continuous extensions at $\alpha = \pm 1$ giving the two directed KL divergences. This family unifies the classical divergences of statistics:

- $\alpha \to -1$: $D_{-1} = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q)$ (forward KL, mode-covering);
- $\alpha \to +1$: $D_{+1} = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p)$ (reverse KL, mode-seeking);
- $\alpha = 0$: $D_0 = 2\,d_H^2(p,q)$, twice the squared Hellinger distance;
- $\alpha = 3$: Pearson $\chi^2$ divergence; $\alpha = -3$: Neyman $\chi^2$.

The duality $D_\alpha(p \,\|\, q) = D_{-\alpha}(q \,\|\, p)$ mirrors the duality $\nabla^{(\alpha)} \leftrightarrow \nabla^{(-\alpha)}$ of the connections — divergence duality and connection duality are two faces of the same structure. The choice of $\alpha$ encodes a *risk attitude*: negative $\alpha$ emphasizes mass-covering (robust to missing modes), positive $\alpha$ emphasizes mode-seeking. In variational inference this is the well-known behavioral spectrum from expectation propagation ($\alpha \to 1$) to standard variational Bayes ($\alpha \to -1$), now understood as movement along the α-geodesic structure of the statistical manifold.

### 4.5 Amari's Natural Gradient and Machine Learning Optimization

Let $L(\theta)$ be a loss on parameter space $\Theta$. Ordinary gradient descent updates $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla L$, implicitly assuming Euclidean geometry on $\Theta$. But $\Theta$ parametrizes *distributions*, and the steepest descent direction depends on the parametrization — a defect, since the distributions themselves are parametrization-independent. The **natural gradient** corrects this:

$$
\tilde{\nabla} L(\theta) = G(\theta)^{-1} \nabla L(\theta),
$$

where $G(\theta)$ is the Fisher information matrix. This is the Riemannian gradient of $L$ with respect to the Fisher–Rao metric: it points in the direction of steepest descent *on the manifold of distributions*, measured by KL divergence per unit step, and is invariant under smooth reparametrization $\theta \mapsto \xi(\theta)$ [1][5].

> **Theorem (Fisher efficiency of natural gradient learning — Amari 1998):** For maximum-likelihood estimation with the negative log-likelihood as loss, the online natural gradient estimator is *Fisher efficient*: its asymptotic covariance attains the Cramér–Rao bound, and it is the unique (up to equivalence) efficient online learning rule.

Equally important is the *dynamical* advantage. Multilayer networks exhibit **plateaus** — extended flat regions of the loss landscape caused by singular structure (e.g., near-degenerate hidden units) — where ordinary gradient descent stalls. Amari showed that natural gradient learning escapes plateaus dramatically faster, because the Fisher metric adapts the step geometry to the local curvature of the *model manifold* rather than the accidental coordinates [1]. The Riemannian structure has since been studied in depth for deep networks: Ollivier's information-geometric optimization framework [5], the reparameterization-invariance analysis of natural gradient descent (arXiv:2312.04739), and mean-field analyses of the Fisher matrix in random deep networks, which justify layer-wise and unit-wise block-diagonal approximations that make the method tractable [6][8].

Practical incarnations include:

- **K-FAC** (Kronecker-factored approximate curvature): approximates the Fisher as a Kronecker product of activation and gradient covariances, enabling natural-gradient-scale training of large networks;
- **Adaptive natural gradient** for multilayer perceptrons, with online estimation of the Fisher inverse [8];
- **Metric-free and score-matching variants**, including Riemannian natural gradients derived from score matching rather than likelihood, extending the framework to unnormalized models [4].

##5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: duality of the α-connections

We verify $X g(Y,Z) = g(\nabla^{(\alpha)}_X Y, Z) + g(Y, \nabla^{(-\alpha)}_X Z)$. In coordinates with $X = \partial_i$, $Y = \partial_j$, $Z = \partial_k$:

$$
\partial_i g_{jk} = \Gamma_{ij}^{(\alpha)\ell} g_{\ell k} + \Gamma_{ik}^{(-\alpha)\ell} g_{j\ell}.
$$

Insert $\Gamma^{(\pm\alpha)} = \Gamma^{(0)} \pm \tfrac{\alpha}{2} T$. The $\Gamma^{(0)}$ terms satisfy the identity by metric-compatibility of the Levi-Civita connection, and the $\alpha$-terms contribute $\tfrac{\alpha}{2}(T_{ijk} - T_{ikj}) = 0$ by total symmetry of $T$. ∎

### 5.2 Proof sketch: dually flat structure of exponential families

For $p(x\mid\theta) = \exp(C(x) + \theta^i F_i(x) - \psi(\theta))$, the score is $\partial_i \ell = F_i - \eta_i$ with $\eta_i = \partial_i\psi$. The Fisher metric is $g_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j \psi$ (covariance of sufficient statistics), and a direct computation gives $\Gamma_{ij}^{(e)k} = 0$ in $\theta$-coordinates (flatness of $\nabla^{(e)}$) and $\Gamma_{ij}^{(m)k} = 0$ in $\eta$-coordinates (flatness of $\nabla^{(m)}$). The potentials $\psi, \phi$ are Legendre duals, and the induced canonical divergence equals $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$. ∎

### 5.3 Empirical: natural gradient escapes plateaus

Amari's 1998 experiments on multilayer perceptrons demonstrated that natural gradient learning converges in an order of magnitude fewer epochs than backpropagation on problems with plateau structure (e.g., parity and symmetry-breaking tasks), precisely because the Fisher preconditioner rescales updates along the degenerate directions of the model manifold [1]. The statistical-mechanical analysis of Rattray, Saad, and Amari confirmed this in the large-system limit: natural gradient escapes plateaus on timescales where ordinary gradient remains trapped.

### 5.4 Empirical: Fisher structure of random deep networks

Amari, Karakida, and Oizumi [6] proved via mean-field theory that in wide random networks, off-diagonal blocks of the Fisher matrix (between layers, and between units within a layer) are of order $1/\sqrt{n}$ and hence negligible — rigorously justifying the unit-wise and layer-wise diagonal approximations used by practical natural-gradient methods [8]. This is information geometry discharging a concrete engineering debt: the approximation practitioners used heuristically is, in the wide limit, *provably* near-exact.

### 5.5 Empirical: invariance under reparametrization

The JMLR 2023 analysis of natural gradient descent [5] verified empirically that natural-gradient trajectories are robust to poor parametrizations (e.g., redundant or rescaled coordinates) where ordinary gradient descent degrades catastrophically — a direct experimental confirmation of the coordinate-free character that the Fisher–Rao metric guarantees by construction.

##6 Limitations

The framework, for all its elegance, faces significant boundaries:

1. **Computational cost.** The Fisher matrix is $n \times n$ in the number of parameters; exact inversion costs $O(n^3)$ and storage $O(n^2)$, infeasible for modern networks with billions of parameters. All practical methods rely on approximations (diagonal, block-diagonal, Kronecker-factored) whose geometric fidelity is only partially understood outside the wide-network limit [6].
2. **Singular models.** Deep networks are *singular* statistical models: the Fisher matrix is rank-deficient (non-identifiable parameters, symmetry-induced degeneracies). Strictly, the Fisher–Rao metric is then only a semi-metric, and the manifold picture requires singular learning theory (Watanabe) rather than classical information geometry.
3. **Stochastic estimation noise.** The empirical Fisher (outer product of sampled gradients) is a biased, noisy estimate; natural gradient with noisy curvature can be unstable, requiring damping $\tilde{G} = G + \lambda I$ that dilutes the very geometric purity the method promises.
4. **Beyond KL.** The Fisher–Rao geometry is the infinitesimal geometry of the KL divergence. Tasks whose natural loss is not likelihood-based (e.g., adversarial losses, Wasserstein objectives) call for different geometries — the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao synthesis [3] is a step, but a unified computational framework remains open.
5. **α outside exponential families.** Dually flat structure is essentially equivalent to exponential-family structure; for general models the α-connections have curvature, the Pythagorean theorem fails, and projections lose their clean convergence guarantees.

##7 Conclusion

Information geometry reframes statistics as the study of a single geometric object — the statistical manifold $(S, g, \nabla^{(\alpha)}, \nabla^{(-\alpha)})$ — in which the Fisher–Rao metric, the Amari–Čencov tensor, and the α-divergences are not independent inventions but facets of one dualistic structure. Čencov's theorem tells us the metric is forced; Amari's α-family tells us the connections come in dual pairs; dually flat manifolds show us that exponential families are the harmonic core where everything — metric, connections, divergence, projections — is generated by a single convex potential.

For machine learning, the enduring gift is the natural gradient: optimization conducted not in the accidental coordinates of a parametrization but on the manifold of distributions itself, with Fisher efficiency and reparametrization invariance as theorems rather than aspirations [1]. The modern challenges — scaling curvature-aware methods to billion-parameter models, handling singular geometries, extending beyond KL — are active frontiers where the $L^p$-Fisher–Rao metrics [2], Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao flows [3], and quantum-information-geometric proposals [7 in spirit; see 2506.15830] continue Amari's program. Nearly three decades after "Natural gradient works efficiently in learning," the geometry of information remains the deepest available language for what it means to learn from data.
