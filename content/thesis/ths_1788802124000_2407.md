# Learned Cache Replacement via Belady's MIN Imitation Learning: Hawkeye and Glider Perceptron Predictors, PC-Correlated Dead-Block Prediction, and LLC Hit-Rate Analysis on SPEC CPU Workloads

## Abstract

The last-level cache (LLC) replacement problem has an exact optimal solution: Belady's MIN algorithm, which evicts the line whose next use is furthest in the future [2]. Because MIN requires an oracle of the future, practical policies have relied on heuristics that approximate it poorly. This thesis analyzes a fundamental shift: **imitating Belady's algorithm as a supervised learning problem**. We treat two policies embodying this idea — **Hawkeye** (Jain and Lin, ISCA 2016 [6]), which reconstructs MIN's decisions for past accesses with a hardware-efficient simulator called OPTgen and trains a per-program-counter (PC) predictor classifying lines as cache-friendly or cache-averse, and **Glider** (Shi et al., MICRO 2019 [9]), which trains an offline LSTM on oracle-labeled sequences, discovers via attention that reuse depends on *control-flow history*, and distills that into an online per-PC perceptron matching the LSTM at three orders of magnitude lower cost. We situate both within the lineage of dead-block prediction [3], RRIP [5], and SHiP [13][14], analyze their imitation-learning formulation and DAgger connection [8], summarize published SPEC CPU2006/2017 hit-rate results, and close with limitations: phase drift, distribution shift, and multi-core interference.

---

## 1 Introduction

Cache replacement is one of the oldest problems in computer architecture, yet the gap between practical policies and the theoretical optimum remains stubbornly large. In 1966, László Bélády published the algorithm that still anchors all replacement research: the optimal policy (MIN) evicts, on a miss, the block whose next reference lies furthest in the future [2]. MIN is *optimal* — no other policy achieves fewer misses on the same reference stream — but it is unrealizable, because it requires an oracle. Practitioners thus spent five decades building heuristics: LRU and its pseudo variants, frequency counters, scan-resistant insertion (BIP/DIP [7]), re-reference interval prediction (RRIP and its dynamic variants [5]), dead-block prediction driven by PC-correlated history tables [3], and signature-based hit prediction (SHiP [13]).

All of these approaches share a methodological weakness: they are designed by human intuition against a small catalog of *known* access patterns (scans, thrashing working sets, cyclic reuse). They extrapolate from heuristic rules rather than from the ground truth. The central thesis of this work is that **the ground truth is available offline** — any trace can be labeled exactly by simulating MIN — and that the right move is therefore to reframe replacement as *imitation learning*: given a set of features observable at miss time, learn the policy that reproduces MIN's decisions. This framing converts fifty years of heuristic design into a supervised classification problem where labels are exact, the expert is unambiguous, and the only remaining question is *representation*.

Two concrete systems realize this vision. **Hawkeye** [6], the winner of the 2nd Cache Replacement Championship (CRC2, 2017), introduces OPTgen, a hardware-feasible algorithm that reconstructs MIN's behavior over recent history by tracking overlapping *liveness intervals* in an occupancy vector, and uses the resulting hit/miss verdicts to train a per-PC saturating-counter predictor that partitions load instructions into *cache-friendly* and *cache-averse* classes. **Glider** [9] generalizes the representation: it trains an attention-based LSTM offline on MIN-labeled traces, inspects the attention weights, and discovers that reuse correlates with a small subset of *past PCs in the control-flow history* rather than with recency or frequency. From that insight it constructs a compact hardware design — a PC history register (PCHR) feeding an incremental SVM, provably equivalent in this setting to a perceptron of the kind long used in branch predictors — that matches the LSTM's accuracy online.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the foundations: MIN optimality, RRIP-class baselines, dead-block prediction, SHiP, and the ChampSim/SPEC evaluation infrastructure. Section 3 presents the methodology, formalizing replacement as imitation learning and analyzing the DAgger connection [8]. Section 4 gives the deep dive: OPTgen's construction and correctness argument, the Hawkeye predictor and RRPV mechanism, Glider's offline LSTM analysis and its online SVM distillation, and the PC-correlated dead-block prediction lineage. Section 5 summarizes the published empirical results on SPEC CPU2006/2017 and GAP workloads. Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Belady's MIN: the oracle

Consider a cache of capacity *C* lines and a reference sequence *r₁, r₂, …, rₙ*. On a miss, MIN evicts the resident line whose next reference is furthest in the future (or never). Belady proved this policy yields the minimum possible miss count [2]. Two refinements matter here. First, MIN is a *decision procedure*, not merely a lower bound: given a complete trace, the oracle action at every miss is computable, so every fill can be labeled "what the optimal policy did." Second, MIN's decisions induce a binary classification of usage intervals: an interval between two references to the same line is *cacheable* exactly when, over its duration, the number of *other* concurrently-live cacheable intervals never exceeds capacity. This interval-overlap characterization is what OPTgen mechanizes in hardware [6].

> Theorem: *Belady's optimality.* For any fixed reference stream and fully-associative cache, evicting the line with the furthest next use minimizes the total miss count. [2]

### 2.2 Heuristic baselines: LRU, RRIP, DIP

LRU fails on two patterns dominating LLC traffic: *scans* (streaming through more data than the cache, evicting the reusable working set) and *thrashing* (a working set slightly larger than the cache, where LRU yields zero hits but retaining a *subset* yields hits on every reuse of the retained portion). Qureshi et al.'s DIP [7] introduced *set dueling* and scan-resistant insertion (BIP); Jaleel et al.'s RRIP [5] introduced the re-reference interval prediction value (RRPV), a small counter per line where high values mark distant re-reference, with DRRIP selecting dynamically between scan-resistant (SRRIP) and thrash-resistant (BRRIP) insertion. Hawkeye and Glider reuse RRPV as the actuation mechanism: the learned binary classification drives RRPV insertion priorities [6][9].

### 2.3 Dead-block prediction and PC correlation

The idea that a line's future can be predicted from the *program counter* of the installing instruction dates to dead-block predictors (Lai et al., ISCA 2001 [3]): a small set of load PCs accounts for most dead blocks, and per-PC history tables predict death with actionable accuracy. The leap — from "this line will not be reused" to "lines installed by this PC are systematically not reused" — is the structure Hawkeye's predictor exploits: OPTgen's labels are aggregated *by PC*, so the predictor learns per-instruction reuse behavior. SHiP [13] (Wu et al., MICRO 2011) generalized the signature to {PC, memory-region} pairs to steer RRIP insertion; SHiP++ (CRC2) refined training to the *first* re-reference and handled writebacks specially [14]. In retrospect these are shallow imitation learners whose "label" is the observed reuse of sampler contents — a noisy proxy for MIN's verdict. Hawkeye replaces the noisy proxy with the oracle itself.

### 2.4 Evaluation infrastructure: ChampSim and SPEC

Replacement research is evaluated in cycle-level simulators such as **ChampSim**, the trace-driven simulator from Texas A&M that became the standard harness for cache research and the official platform of the Cache Replacement Championships. Workloads come from **SPEC CPU2006** and **SPEC CPU2017** (plus GAP graph workloads in the Glider paper), selected for *memory intensity*: `mcf`, `lbm`, `omnetpp`, `xalancbmk`, and `roms` exhibit high LLC misses per kilo-instruction (MPKI) and expose policy differences. Standard metrics are MPKI reduction relative to LRU and IPC improvement, measured with and without hardware prefetching, since prefetchers interact strongly with replacement [9][10][12].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Replacement as imitation learning

We formalize the learner as follows. Let *xₜ* be the feature vector available when line *ℓₜ* is installed or accessed: the installing PC, recent PC history, reuse counters, and address-derived signatures. Let *yₜ ∈ {friendly, averse}* be the oracle label computed by simulating MIN (or OPTgen's approximation) over the trace: *yₜ = friendly* iff MIN would retain *ℓₜ* through its current usage interval. The policy is a classifier *π(x) → y*, and at eviction time the implementation evicts lines classified *averse* (instantiated through RRPV priorities). Training minimizes the disagreement with the oracle:

```python
# Imitation-learning formulation of cache replacement
def train_policy(traces, features, oracle=belady_min):
    dataset = []
    for trace in traces:
        labels = oracle(trace)          # exact MIN labels, offline
        for t, access in enumerate(trace):
            x = features(access, history)   # PC, PCHR, signatures...
            dataset.append((x, labels[t]))
    return classifier.fit(dataset)      # SVM / perceptron / counters
```

This is *behavioral cloning* of MIN: the expert demonstrations are not collected from an expert agent but *computed* from the trace, sidestepping the usual data-collection cost while inheriting its central pathology — **distribution shift**. A classifier trained on states visited under MIN's occupancy will, at deployment, encounter states induced by *its own* imperfect decisions. This is the covariate-shift problem DAgger [8] addresses by aggregating states visited by the *learner* and re-querying the expert. Hawkeye's *online* training partially mitigates shift by construction: OPTgen labels the states the running system actually visits and the predictor updates continuously, so the training distribution tracks deployment — a hardware-realizable cousin of the DAgger loop, though without DAgger's no-regret guarantees [8].

### 3.2 Why this framing is legitimate

Two objections must be answered. First, MIN labels are *counterfactual*: had the policy cached a line MIN evicted, the occupancy — and hence the labels — would differ. OPTgen handles this by simulating MIN only over the recent past under the *observed* stream, the standard retrospective-oracle methodology [6]. Second, binary classification discards the *magnitude* of reuse distance; MIN's true preference is a total order by next-use distance. Both policies accept this coarsening because the actuation substrate (RRPV priorities) is itself coarse, and the empirical question — whether binary labels close the LRU–MIN gap — is answered affirmatively in Section 5 [6][9].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 OPTgen: reconstructing MIN in hardware

OPTgen is Hawkeye's core contribution [6]. For each *sampled set* (64 of the LLC's sets, per dynamic set sampling), OPTgen maintains an **occupancy vector** indexed by logical time, where entry *v[t]* counts the liveness intervals — periods between consecutive references to the same line — currently believed cacheable and spanning time *t*. When line *X* is re-referenced with previous reference at *t₀*, OPTgen scans *[t₀, now)*: if every entry is strictly below capacity *C*, MIN could have retained *X* throughout, the interval is marked cacheable (the vector is incremented over it, "reserving" a way), and the installing PC is trained toward *cache-friendly*; otherwise *X* is *cache-averse*. Prefetch-aware variants distinguish demand/prefetch interval endpoints so redundant prefetches do not consume simulated capacity [6].

```haskell
-- OPTgen, idealized (cf. Jain & Lin, ISCA 2016)
optgen :: Capacity -> [(Time, Line, PC)] -> [(PC, Label)]
optgen cap refs = go refs emptyOccupancy []
  where
    go [] _ labels = reverse labels
    go ((t, l, pc):rs) occ labels =
      case lookupPrev l occ of
        Nothing        -> go rs (record occ l t) labels
        Just t0 ->
          if all (< cap) (slice occ t0 t)
            then go rs (reserve occ l t0 t) ((pc, Friendly):labels)
            else go rs occ                   ((pc, Averse):labels)
```

The construction is an *O(1)*-amortized sliding-window simulation: the occupancy vector has *8·C* entries for a *C*-way sampled set, and only re-references in sampled sets trigger the scan, keeping the hardware budget near 4 KB [9]. Approximation error versus exact MIN comes from two sources: (i) the finite window discards intervals longer than the vector, conservatively labeled averse, and (ii) per-set simulation is exact for set-associative caches anyway, since sets are independent.

### 4.2 The Hawkeye predictor and RRPV actuation

OPTgen's labels train the **Hawkeye Predictor**: a per-core table of 8,192 entries, each a 3-bit saturating counter indexed by a hash of the load PC [6]. A low counter means the PC historically installs cache-friendly lines; a saturated counter means cache-averse. On a fill or hit, the line's 3-bit **RRPV** is set to 0 (friendly) or 7 (averse); the victim is the line with the highest RRPV, and friendly insertions *age* the rest of the set so stale friendly lines eventually yield to fresh ones. The design cleanly separates *learning* (the PC's reuse character, per the oracle) from *actuation* (RRPV priorities, the same substrate as RRIP). Total storage is roughly 12 KB per-line RRPV + 5.3 KB sampler + 4 KB OPTgen + predictor tables — comparable to the baselines it beats [9]. Hawkeye won CRC2 in 2017, and its per-PC, oracle-trained structure is the direct ancestor of every learned policy that followed [6][14].

### 4.3 Glider: from LSTM insight to online perceptron

Glider [9] attacks what Hawkeye leaves on the table: the *representation*. Hawkeye's features are a single PC; its model is a table of counters. Shi et al. begin at the opposite extreme — an **attention-based LSTM** trained offline on MIN-labeled traces, taking the *sequence of past PCs and addresses* as input (a sequence-labeling formulation: label each access "should be cached" or not). The offline model reaches ~82.6% accuracy vs. ~72.2% for prior art [9]. The decisive step is *interpretation*: attention weights reveal that (i) long PC histories help, but (ii) the decision depends on the *presence of a few PCs in the history*, not on the ordered sequence. The relevant function is approximately a sparse linear threshold over history-membership features, not a recurrent computation.

From this, the authors handcraft 16 binary features over a **PC History Register** (PCHR) holding the last 5 PCs — e.g., "the current PC appeared *k* positions ago" — and train an **incremental SVM (ISVM)** online in hardware. With these features, the ISVM update reduces to a perceptron update, the same learning rule used in commercial branch predictors:

```rust
// ISVM/perceptron update (Glider online predictor, per tracked PC)
fn update(w: &mut [i8; 16], x: &[bool; 16], label: bool) {
    let y: i32 = w.iter().zip(x).map(|(wi, &xi)| if xi { *wi as i32 } else { 0 }).sum();
    let pred = y >= 0;
    if pred != label {
        for (wi, &xi) in w.iter_mut().zip(x) {
            if xi { *wi = wi.saturating_add(if label { 1 } else { -1 }); }
        }
    }
}
```

Each ISVM tracks 16 8-bit weights (16 bytes); 2,048 PCs cost 32.8 KB, plus a 0.1 KB PCHR, for ~61.6 KB total including the Hawkeye-derived sampler and OPTgen — three orders of magnitude cheaper than the attention LSTM in storage and compute, with prediction latency hidden behind the LLC access itself [9]. The methodological lesson: **use deep learning as an analysis instrument to discover features, then deploy the simplest model that exploits them**.

### 4.4 The PC-correlated dead-block lineage

Hawkeye and Glider are the convergence of two threads. From the *architecture* side: dead-block prediction [3] established PC correlation; SHiP [13] and SHiP++ [14] built signature-indexed predictors trained on sampler observations; Hawkeye replaced the noisy training signal with the oracle. From the *learning* side: perceptron branch predictors showed simple linear models with the right features beat complex ones under hardware budgets; Glider showed deep models can *discover* those features offline. Mockingjay (ISCA 2022), the CRC successor, tracks per-address *temporal* reuse rather than binary friendliness — a reminder that the binary framing of Section 3.2 is a deliberate simplification, and finer-grained labels (reuse distance, liveness) are the natural next axis.

## 5 Empirical Results

All figures are reported from the primary sources [6][9]; the bar chart visualizes the Glider paper's headline comparison on 33 memory-intensive programs from SPEC CPU2006, SPEC CPU2017, and GAP.

| Policy | Miss-rate reduction vs. LRU (1 core) | IPC gain vs. LRU (4 cores) | Key mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Glider** [9] | **8.9%** | **14.7%** | Offline LSTM → PCHR + ISVM perceptron |
| **Hawkeye** [6] | 7.1% | 13.6% | OPTgen + per-PC 3-bit counters |
| **SHiP++** [14] | 7.5% | 11.4% | Signature hit predictor, CRC2 |
| **MPPPB** | 6.5% | 13.2% | Multi-path perceptron predictor, CRC2 |
| SRRIP/DRRIP [5] | ~4–5% | ~10% | Heuristic RRIP (baseline class) |

*Table 1: Published LLC results. Glider's online predictor also beats Hawkeye's prediction accuracy (88.8% vs. 84.9% online) and matches the offline LSTM (81.2% → 82.4% on the training subset) [9].*

Three observations matter. **First**, the percentage gaps look modest, but at the LLC a 1–2% miss-rate delta on memory-intensive SPEC workloads (`mcf`, `lbm`, `omnetpp`, `xalancbmk`) produces double-digit IPC swings in multi-core configurations because LLC misses serialize on DRAM latency [9][10]. **Second**, Hawkeye's 7.1% already captured most of the available gain over heuristics, which is why Glider's contribution is framed as *representation learning*: the LSTM analysis is the paper's durable artifact. **Third**, prefetching compresses the headroom available to any policy; both papers hold prefetching constant across compared policies [9][10]. Independent memory-hierarchy characterizations of SPEC CPU2006/2017 on Skylake-SP-class systems confirm that LLC MPKI is highly policy-sensitive for the memory-intensive subset without prefetching, and that aggressive prefetching flattens those differences — replacement research must be evaluated with realistic prefetchers to claim system-level relevance [10][12].

## 6 Limitations

**Phase behavior and staleness.** Programs exhibit time-varying phases: a PC that is cache-friendly in one phase may be averse in another. Online training adapts, but adaptation speed is bounded by the training rate, and offline-trained components cannot adapt at all.

**Distribution shift, formally.** Section 3.1 noted the DAgger connection [8]: cloning MIN suffers covariate shift because the learner's own errors change the occupancy states it encounters. Hawkeye's online labeling mitigates but does not eliminate this — the observed stream is itself a function of past mispredictions. No published analysis gives a regret bound for OPTgen-trained predictors analogous to DAgger's; a genuine theoretical gap.

**Multi-core interference.** In shared LLCs, one core's stream perturbs the occupancy observed by another's predictor. The published four-core results show Glider retaining its lead [9], but per-core predictor tables (Hawkeye) scale storage linearly with core count, and cross-core destructive interference in the sampler is unmodeled.

**Latency and the prediction critical path.** Glider needs two table lookups per decision, hidden behind LLC latency — acceptable. But richer features (longer PCHR, more weights) push toward the access critical path, and the perceptron's linearity is simultaneously its strength (cheap) and its ceiling (no feature interactions). Attention-LSTM-class models remain ~10³× too expensive [9].

**The binary-label ceiling.** Both policies discard reuse-distance magnitude. Follow-up work (e.g., Mockingjay's temporal reuse tracking) suggests the next gains come from finer-grained oracle labels, at the cost of more complex actuation than RRPV priorities.

## 7 Conclusion

The learned-replacement program reframes a fifty-year-old heuristic discipline as imitation learning against an exact oracle. Hawkeye showed that Belady's MIN can be *reconstructed* efficiently enough to train hardware predictors online, winning CRC2 and establishing the OPTgen → per-PC predictor → RRPV pipeline as the canonical architecture [6]. Glider showed that the *representation* matters as much as the labels: training an unconstrained LSTM offline and interpreting its attention revealed that control-flow history membership — not recency, frequency, or ordered sequences — is the informative signal, distilled into a perceptron-class online predictor matching the LSTM at a thousandth of the cost [9]. Together with their lineage in PC-correlated dead-block prediction [3] and signature-based hit prediction [13][14], they demonstrate a reusable methodology: **use the oracle to generate exact labels, use deep models offline as scientific instruments to find features, and deploy the simplest online model that exploits them**. The remaining gaps — distribution shift without regret bounds, phase staleness, multi-core interference, and the binary-label ceiling — define the next decade of the field.
