# Zero-Knowledge Succinct Arguments: From GMR to SNARKs and STARKs — Polynomial Commitments, Transparent Setups, and Post-Quantum Succinctness

## Abstract

Zero-knowledge succinct arguments have evolved from the abstract interactive proofs of Goldwasser, Micali, and Rackoff [1] into deployed cryptographic infrastructure that verifies arbitrary computation with proofs of constant or polylogarithmic size. This thesis traces that forty-year trajectory: the formalization of zero knowledge via simulation [1]; the pairing-based *succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge* (SNARKs) culminating in Groth16's three-element proofs [2]; the abstraction of *polynomial commitment schemes* such as KZG commitments [3] that underpin modern universal SNARKs including PlonK [4]; the elimination of per-circuit trusted setup through universal and recursive constructions such as Halo [5]; and the parallel *transparent, post-quantum* line exemplified by STARKs built on FRI low-degree testing [6][7]. We compare proof systems along the axes of setup assumptions, proof size, verifier complexity, and quantum resistance, formalize the central security notions (knowledge soundness, succinctness, zero knowledge), and present comparative asymptotics and concrete benchmarks. We conclude by identifying the open frontiers: concretely efficient lattice-based succinctness, post-quantum recursion, and the tension between transparency and proof size.

---

## 1 Introduction

In 1985, Goldwasser, Micali, and Rackoff introduced a radical idea: a *prover* could convince a *verifier* that a statement is true while revealing nothing else — not the witness, not even partial information about it [1]. Zero knowledge was defined not by listing which secrets must be protected, but by a sweeping simulation criterion: whatever the verifier learns in the interaction, it could have computed *without* the prover. For three decades this remained one of the most beautiful ideas in theoretical computer science, with applications ranging from identification schemes to the hardness of approximation.

Today, zero-knowledge proofs verify *billions of dollars* of blockchain state transitions. Systems such as Zcash, Tornado Cash, and the rollups that scale Ethereum produce *succinct* proofs — arguments whose size and verification time grow only polylogarithmically in the size of the computation being verified. The alphabet soup of deployed systems — zk-SNARKs, zk-STARKs, PlonK, Halo, Bulletproofs — all descend from the GMR paradigm, yet they differ sharply in their cryptographic assumptions, setup requirements, proof sizes, and resistance to quantum adversaries.

This thesis provides a unified account of the landscape. We organize the field into two great lineages:

1. **Pairing-based SNARKs** — building on bilinear maps and the common-reference-string model, from early quadratic arithmetic program (QAP) constructions to Groth16 [2], to universal SNARKs like PlonK [4] that separate the trusted setup from the circuit.
2. **Transparent, post-quantum arguments** — eliminating trusted setup and number-theoretic assumptions in favor of symmetric cryptography: collision-resistant hashes, Merkle trees, and interactive oracle proofs (IOPs), culminating in STARKs [6] and their low-degree testing engine, FRI [7].

Running through both lineages is the *polynomial commitment scheme* [3], the modular abstraction that made modern SNARKs possible: commit to a polynomial with a short digest, then later reveal its evaluation at a chosen point with a short proof. We also cover *proof recursion* — verifying proofs inside proofs — which enables incrementally verifiable computation and unbounded depth via constructions like Halo [5].

The central question guiding this work: **what must be assumed, and what must be sacrificed, to obtain succinct zero-knowledge arguments with the strongest possible security story?** The answer, as we will see, is a landscape of trade-offs with no single dominant point.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Interactive proofs and the GMR definition

An *interactive proof system* ⟨P, V⟩ for a language L consists of a (possibly unbounded) prover P and a probabilistic polynomial-time verifier V exchanging messages; V must accept with high probability when x ∈ L (completeness) and reject with high probability when x ∉ L regardless of the prover's strategy (soundness) [1]. Goldwasser, Micali, and Rackoff added the requirement that the interaction leak nothing:

> **Theorem:** (Zero knowledge, informally [1]). An interactive proof system ⟨P, V⟩ for L is *zero-knowledge* if, for every probabilistic polynomial-time verifier V*, there exists a probabilistic polynomial-time *simulator* S such that the output distribution of S(x) is computationally indistinguishable from the transcript of ⟨P(x), V*(x)⟩.

The power of this definition is its *obliviousness to the verifier's goals*: whatever any efficient verifier extracts from the interaction — witnesses, partial information, side-channel patterns — the simulator could have produced alone. A cornerstone result is that *every* language in NP admits a zero-knowledge proof, assuming one-way functions exist [1]. Zero knowledge thus became a free property of all of NP, at least in principle.

### 2.2 From proofs to arguments: succinctness

Two relaxations transform interactive proofs into practical systems. First, *computational soundness*: restrict the prover to polynomial time, converting "proofs" (statistically sound) into *arguments* (computationally sound) — a distinction formalized by Brassard, Chaum, and Crépeau. Second, *succinctness*: require that proof size and verification time be *sublinear* in the computation size, ideally polylogarithmic:

> **Definition:** (Succinct argument). An argument system for a relation R is *succinct* if for security parameter λ and computation of size N (in gates), the proof size and verifier running time are bounded by poly(λ, log N).

Succinctness is the property that makes verifiable computation practical: a verifier checks in milliseconds what would take hours to recompute. Combined with zero knowledge, this yields *zk-SNARKs*: zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge [2].

### 2.3 The non-interactive barrier and the CRS model

GMR-style proofs are interactive. Non-interactivity — a single message from prover to verifier — is essential for blockchains and asynchronous verification. The Fiat–Shamir heuristic transforms public-coin interactive protocols into non-interactive ones in the *random oracle model* by replacing verifier challenges with hashes of the transcript [6].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is *comparative and constructive*. Rather than presenting one system in depth, we reconstruct the field's major constructions from their shared algebraic skeleton, so that design differences become visible as explicit choices:

1. **Arithmetization** — how a computation is encoded: as a *quadratic arithmetic program* (QAP) [2], a *PlonKish* constraint system with permutation arguments [4], or an *algebraic intermediate representation* (AIR) over a trace table [6].
2. **Commitment** — how the prover binds itself to the witness encoding: pairing-based polynomial commitments (KZG [3]), Pedersen-style vector commitments, or Merkle commitments to Reed–Solomon codewords [7].
3. **Evaluation and consistency checks** — how the verifier confirms claimed evaluations: pairing equations [2][3], opening proofs combined with permutation or quotient checks [4], or FRI low-degree tests [7].
4. **Zero-knowledge masking** — how witness privacy is layered on: random blinding polynomials in KZG-based systems [3][4], or algebraic blinding of the execution trace with random shifts in STARKs [6].

For each system we state the asymptotic triple — *(proof size, prover time, verifier time)* — and the security reduction it rests on.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Pairing-based SNARKs: QAPs and Groth16

The pairing-based SNARK lineage reduces arbitrary computation to divisibility of polynomials. In the *quadratic arithmetic program* formulation, each multiplication gate of an arithmetic circuit becomes a constraint; the collection of constraints is interpolated into three polynomial families {uᵢ(x)}, {vᵢ(x)}, {wᵢ(x)} and a target polynomial t(x) = ∏(x − rⱼ) over the constraint roots. A witness assignment satisfies the circuit *if and only if* the polynomial identity holds:

$$ \left(\sum_i a_i\, u_i(x)\right)\!\left(\sum_i a_i\, v_i(x)\right) - \sum_i a_i\, w_i(x) = h(x)\, t(x) $$

for some quotient polynomial h(x). The prover's task collapses to: commit to A(x) = ∑aᵢuᵢ(x), B(x) = ∑aᵢvᵢ(x), C(x) = ∑aᵢwᵢ(x), and convince the verifier that the identity holds at a *secret* evaluation point τ embedded in the CRS — without revealing τ, which is the toxic waste of the setup [2].

> **Theorem:** (Groth16 [2]). There exists a pairing-based zk-SNARK for arithmetic circuit satisfiability with proof size 3 group elements (2 in 𝔾₁, 1 in 𝔾₂), verifier time O(1) pairings plus O(|x|) for the public input, and prover time O(N log N) group operations for N constraints, secure under knowledge-of-exponent-type assumptions in the generic group model.

Concretely, the Groth16 proof is π = (A, B, C) and verification is a *single equation* of pairings:

$$ e(A, B) = e(\alpha, \beta)\cdot e\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\ell} a_i\, \frac{\beta u_i(\tau) + \alpha v_i(\tau) + w_i(\tau)}{\gamma},\, \gamma\right) \cdot e(C, \delta) $$

The elegance of three group elements (~200 bytes on BN254) made Groth16 the workhorse of Zcash's shielded transactions and early zk-rollups. But it carries two structural costs: a **circuit-specific trusted setup** — new parameters per circuit, with per-circuit toxic waste — and reliance on **non-falsifiable knowledge assumptions** plus the hardness of discrete logarithms in pairing groups, which a large quantum computer would break.

```rust
// Groth16 verification skeleton (BN254): one equation, three pairings
fn verify(vk: &VerifyingKey, public_inputs: &[Fr], proof: &Proof) -> bool {
    let mut acc = G1::identity();
    for (i, a) in public_inputs.iter().enumerate() {
        acc += vk.ic[i + 1] * a;           // linear combination of public inputs
    }
    acc += vk.ic[0];
    let lhs = pairing(proof.a, proof.b);
    let rhs = pairing(vk.alpha, vk.beta)
        * pairing(acc, vk.gamma)
        * pairing(proof.c, vk.delta);
    lhs == rhs
}
```

### 4.2 Polynomial commitments: the KZG abstraction

Kate, Zaverucha, and Goldberg isolated the primitive that all modern pairing SNARKs share [3]. A *polynomial commitment scheme* has three algorithms:

- **Commit**(f) → C: bind to polynomial f with a short digest C.
- **Open**(f, z) → (y, π): reveal y = f(z) with an evaluation proof π.
- **Verify**(C, z, y, π): check the claimed evaluation against the commitment.

> **Theorem:** (KZG commitments [3]). Let e: 𝔾₁ × 𝔾₂ → 𝔾_T be a bilinear pairing and τ the setup trapdoor. Then C = g^{f(τ)} is a commitment to f with constant-size openings: for claimed evaluation y = f(z), the opening proof π = g^{q(τ)} where q(x) = (f(x) − y)/(x − z), verified by checking e(C / g^y, g) = e(π, g^τ / g^z).

Security reduces to the *t-SDH* (strong Diffie–Hellman) assumption, and the scheme is *homomorphic*: commitments add, so the verifier can form commitments to linear combinations of committed polynomials for free. Zero knowledge is obtained by *blinding*: the prover adds random multiples of the vanishing polynomial Z_H(x) to the committed polynomials, randomizing openings while preserving divisibility relations [3][4]. KZG is the engine inside PlonK, Marlin, and Ethereum's Danksharding blob commitments (EIP-4844) — the same mathematics now secures data availability for an entire ecosystem.

| Property | KZG [3] | Pedersen (vector) | Merkle (FRI-based) [7] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commitment size | 1 𝔾₁ element | 1 group element | 1 hash |
| Opening size | 1 𝔾₁ element | O(log n) | O(log² n) |
| Assumption | t-SDH / pairings | Discrete log | Collision-resistant hash |
| Trusted setup | Yes (powers of τ) | No (transparent) | No (transparent) |
| Quantum resistance | No | No | Yes (plausible) |

### 4.3 Universal SNARKs and recursion: PlonK and Halo

Groth16's per-circuit setup was operationally painful: every program change demanded a new multi-party ceremony. PlonK [4] restructured the arithmetization to make the setup *universal and updatable*. Instead of QAPs, PlonK uses a *permutation argument over Lagrange bases*: the circuit is a set of gate constraints plus *copy constraints* asserting that certain wire values are equal. The copy constraints are enforced by a grand-product permutation check — the prover commits to a permutation polynomial z(x) satisfying z(g·ωⁱ) = z(ωⁱ)·(numerator/denominator) ratios, and the verifier checks a single identity at a random challenge. The SRS (structured reference string) depends only on a bound on circuit size, so *one* ceremony serves *all* circuits up to that size, and the SRS is *updatable*: participants can sequentially contribute randomness, with security holding if *any one* contributor is honest [4].

Recursion goes further: what if the verifier's own computation is expressed as a circuit, so that a proof can verify *another proof*? Halo [5] achieved *recursive proof composition without a trusted setup* by exploiting the *nested amortization* of discrete-log-based accumulation schemes over cycles of elliptic curves (e.g., Pasta). Instead of verifying an entire inner proof inside the outer circuit — prohibitively expensive for pairing checks — Halo *accumulates* claims: each recursion step folds the previous instance into a running accumulator, deferring the expensive check to the final decider. This yields *incrementally verifiable computation*: a long computation is proved in streaming fashion, with proof size and verification staying constant regardless of the number of steps [5].

### 4.4 STARKs: transparency and post-quantum succinctness via FRI

STARKs — *Scalable Transparent Arguments of Knowledge* [6] — reject both the trusted setup and the number-theoretic assumptions of SNARKs. Their security rests on a single symmetric primitive: a collision-resistant hash function (instantiated with arithmetization-friendly hashes such as Rescue or Poseidon). The architecture has three layers:

1. **AIR (Algebraic Intermediate Representation)**: the computation is a *trace table* — rows are machine states over time, columns are registers — with polynomial constraints relating consecutive rows. This suits *uniform, sequential* computation (a CPU executing steps) far better than arbitrary circuits suit it.
2. **Low-degree extension**: the trace is interpolated into polynomials and evaluated on a much larger domain (the *blowup factor* ρ, typically 8–16×), producing Reed–Solomon codewords. If the prover cheated, the resulting function is *far* from any low-degree polynomial.
3. **FRI (Fast Reed–Solomon Interactive Oracle Proof of Proximity)** [7]: the verifier forces the prover to prove its committed function is close to the Reed–Solomon code. FRI works by *folding*: in each round, the prover commits to a function f_{i+1} on a domain half the size, where f_{i+1}(y) = (f_i(x) + f_i(−x))/2 + α·(f_i(x) − f_i(−x))/(2x) for a verifier challenge α. After log n rounds the function is constant, and the verifier spot-checks consistency of the folding at random queries via Merkle authentication paths.

> **Theorem:** (FRI soundness [7], informally). If f₀: L₀ → 𝔽 is δ-far from the Reed–Solomon code of rate ρ, then a cheating prover passes FRI with probability at most (1 − δ)^{q} + negl, where q is the number of queries; the protocol has O(log² n) proof size and O(log² n) verifier queries.

Zero knowledge in STARKs is achieved by *randomized trace blinding*: appending random rows and multiplying constraint polynomials by randomizers so that any bounded set of queries reveals nothing about the witness [6]. The payoff: **transparent setup** (just public randomness), **plausible post-quantum security** (only hashes and Merkle trees — no hidden-subgroup structure for Shor's algorithm to exploit), and *quasilinear* proving. The price: proofs of 40–200 KB — two to three orders of magnitude larger than Groth16 — and a verifier that, while polylogarithmic, does substantially more hashing work per proof.

```python
# FRI folding round, idealized: halve the domain each round
def fri_fold(f, domain, alpha):
    """Fold f: L -> F into f': L' -> F with |L'| = |L|/2."""
    half = {}
    for y in domain_halved(domain):
        x, neg_x = y**2_preimages(y)
        even = (f[x] + f[neg_x]) / 2
        odd  = (f[x] - f[neg_x]) / (2 * x)
        half[y] = even + alpha * odd
    return half, merkle_commit(half)   # commit; verifier later spot-checks
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Asymptotic comparison

The following table summarizes the asymptotic triple (proof size, prover time, verifier time) and the setup/security posture of the principal systems. Here N is the computation size in gates/steps and λ the security parameter [2][4][5][6]:

| System | Proof size | Prover time | Verifier time | Setup | Assumption | Post-quantum? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Groth16 [2] | O(1) (~200 B) | O(N log N) | O(\|x\|) | Circuit-specific trusted | Knowledge-of-exponent, pairings | No |
| PlonK [4] | O(1) (~400 B) | O(N log N) | O(\|x\| + log N) | Universal updatable trusted | t-SDH, pairings | No |
| Halo [5] | O(log N) (~5 KB) | O(N log N) | O(log N) | Transparent | Discrete log | No |
| STARK [6] | O(log² N) (45–200 KB) | O(N log² N) | O(log² N) | Transparent | Collision-resistant hash | Plausible yes |
| Bulletproofs | O(log N) | O(N) | O(N) | Transparent | Discrete log | No |

Two structural facts emerge. First, **succinctness is not free**: the polylogarithmic regime (STARKs) pays in proof size relative to the constant regime (Groth16/PlonK).

### 5.2 Concrete benchmarks (representative, 128-bit security)

Concrete numbers depend heavily on field choice, hash function, and implementation maturity, but representative orders of magnitude from the literature and deployed systems are [2][4][6]:

- **Groth16** (BN254, ~2²⁰ constraints): proof 196 bytes, verification ~3 ms, proving ~10–30 s. Used in Zcash Sapling [2].
- **PlonK** (BN254, universal SRS): proof ~400–500 bytes, verification ~6–10 ms, proving ~1.5–2× Groth16 [4].
- **Halo 2** (Pasta curves, recursion): proof ~5 KB, verification ~20 ms, recursion overhead per step small; powers Zcash's Orchard and the first production recursive SNARKs [5].
- **STARK** (e.g., Winterfell/Stone, 2²⁰-step trace): proof ~80–120 KB, verification ~10–25 ms, proving ~1–5 s on commodity hardware [6].

The crossover is instructive: STARK *proving* is often *faster* than pairing-based proving (no FFTs over pairing-unfriendly scalar fields, no multiexponentiations), while verification and bandwidth favor SNARKs.

### 5.3 Security reductions: what "knowledge" means

Knowledge soundness is formalized by *extraction*: for every efficient prover that convinces the verifier with non-negligible probability, there exists an *extractor* that recovers a valid witness (given the prover's code/randomness, or via rewinding in the IOP setting) [2][6]. The pairing-based line relies on *knowledge-of-exponent* assumptions — non-falsifiable assumptions asserting that any adversary producing certain group elements "must know" their discrete-log representation. The STARK line avoids knowledge assumptions entirely: soundness reduces to the *binding* of Merkle commitments and the *proximity* guarantees of FRI, with extraction via rewinding of the IOP prover [7]. This difference is philosophically significant: STARK security arguments are *falsifiable* and rest on better-understood symmetric primitives, which is the technical content of the claim that STARKs are "more conservative" cryptographically [6].

## 6 Limitations

**The setup–size–assumption trilemma.** No known system simultaneously achieves (i) fully transparent setup, (ii) constant-size proofs, and (iii) standard-model falsifiable assumptions. SNARKs achieve (ii) at the cost of (i); STARKs achieve (i) at the cost of (ii); Halo achieves (i) and near-(ii) but keeps discrete-log assumptions. Whether the trilemma is fundamental or an artifact of current techniques remains open.

**Prover scalability.** All known succinct arguments require the prover to do *superlinear* work — at minimum Ω(N log N) for FFT-based arithmetization, and often much worse in practice due to memory bandwidth. Proving remains the bottleneck: a computation that runs in seconds natively may take minutes to prove. Linear-time provers exist in theory but not in deployed, zero-knowledge form.

**Post-quantum succinctness is young.** STARKs are *plausibly* post-quantum, but the claim rests on the quantum security of specific hash functions and the (mostly classical) analysis of FRI under quantum queries. A fully quantum security proof of the Fiat–Shamir'd FRI in the quantum random-oracle model is an active research area, and hash-based proof sizes (hundreds of KB) remain heavy for bandwidth-constrained verifiers. Lattice-based succinct arguments exist in theory but are not yet concretely competitive [6][7].

**Arithmetization mismatch.** AIR excels at uniform sequential computation but is awkward for irregular, data-dependent control flow; R1CS/PlonKish circuits handle irregularity naturally but pay per-gate overhead and (for Groth16) per-circuit setup. The "right" arithmetization is workload-dependent, and compilers (e.g., Cairo, Noir, Circom) paper over differences that still leak into performance.

## 7 Conclusion

From the simulation paradigm of Goldwasser, Micali, and Rackoff [1] to the deployed proof systems of today, the arc of zero-knowledge succinct arguments is the arc of *assumptions made explicit and then removed*. Pairing-based SNARKs [2] bought constant-size proofs with trusted setup and knowledge assumptions; polynomial commitments [3] modularized the machinery; PlonK [4] universalized the setup; Halo [5] showed recursion needs no trusted setup at all; and STARKs [6][7] rebuilt the entire edifice on hash functions alone, gaining transparency and plausible post-quantum security at the price of larger proofs.

The field's open frontiers follow directly from its limitations: concretely efficient post-quantum succinctness with SNARK-like proof sizes, recursion-friendly transparent systems, linear-time zero-knowledge provers, and arithmetizations that adapt to workload structure. Whatever the eventual answers, the GMR insight endures: *verification need not imply revelation* — and succinctness shows it need not imply recomputation either.
