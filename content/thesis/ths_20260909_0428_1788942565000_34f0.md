# Gravitational-Wave Astronomy with Ground-Based Interferometers: Matched Filtering, Bayesian Parameter Estimation, and Compact-Binary Population Inference

## Abstract

The first direct detection of gravitational waves by the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory in 2015 inaugurated gravitational-wave astronomy as an observational science. This thesis presents the physics, instrumentation, and statistical inference underpinning ground-based observation of compact binary coalescences. We analyze the Michelson architecture of Advanced LIGO and Virgo — Fabry–Pérot arm cavities, power recycling, and signal recycling — and derive the interferometer's displacement response to a passing wave. We construct the detector noise budget from seismic, thermal, and quantum contributions, and derive the amplitude spectral density that sets the astrophysical reach. We develop matched-filter detection theory for chirping waveforms, the differential-geometric template-bank formalism, and the Bayesian machinery of parameter estimation, including the LALInference framework, waveform approximant families, and degeneracy structure in the posterior. Finally, we formulate hierarchical population inference from event catalogs, correcting selection effects to recover the mass, spin, and redshift distributions of binary black holes, and we connect each theoretical claim to empirical results from the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA observing runs.

## 1 Introduction

In 1916, Albert Einstein predicted that accelerating masses would radiate ripples in spacetime propagating at the speed of light. A century later, on 14 September 2015, the two Advanced LIGO interferometers observed the signal GW150914 — the inspiral, merger, and ringdown of a binary black hole system — with a matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio of 24 and a false-alarm rate below one event per 203,000 years [4]. This single event simultaneously demonstrated the existence of stellar-mass binary black holes, confirmed the dynamics of general relativity in the strong-field regime, and proved that kilometer-scale laser interferometry could measure differential arm strains of order 10⁻²¹.

Gravitational-wave astronomy has since matured from a single discovery into a catalog science. The LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA (LVK) collaboration has accumulated a catalog of nearly one hundred compact binary coalescence (CBC) signals comprising binary black holes (BBHs), binary neutron stars (BNSs), and neutron star–black hole (NSBH) systems.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Gravitational waves from compact binaries

In linearized general relativity, a time-varying mass quadrupole moment radiates gravitational waves with two transverse polarization states, *h*₊ and *h*×. For a compact binary in quasi-circular inspiral, the orbital energy is drained by gravitational radiation, causing the orbit to shrink and the emission frequency to rise — the characteristic *chirp*. To leading post-Newtonian (PN) order, the instantaneous gravitational-wave frequency evolves as

$$
\frac{df}{dt} = \frac{96}{5}\pi^{8/3}(G\mathcal{M})^{5/3}f^{11/3},
$$

where the chirp mass $\mathcal{M} = (m_1 m_2)^{3/5}/(m_1 + m_2)^{1/5}$ is the mass combination that dominates the inspiral phase evolution [1]. The thousands of orbital cycles accumulated in band mean the phase evolution encodes the chirp mass with exquisite sensitivity, while amplitude information constrains the luminosity distance [1].

The coalescence divides into three phases: **inspiral** (adiabatic orbital decay, described by PN expansions and resummed by the effective-one-body formalism), **merger** (the highly nonlinear plunge, requiring numerical relativity), and **ringdown** (the remnant relaxing via damped quasi-normal modes set by the final mass and spin). Complete inspiral–merger–ringdown models are parameterized by intrinsic parameters (masses, spins) and extrinsic parameters (distance, inclination, sky position, polarization, coalescence time and phase).

### 2.2 Detector response

A Michelson interferometer with arm length *L* responds to a gravitational wave through the differential strain $h(t) = \Delta L(t)/L$. A passing wave with strain amplitude 10⁻²¹ displaces the 4 km arms of LIGO by roughly 10⁻¹⁸ m — about a thousandth of a proton diameter. Detecting such displacements demands resonant optical enhancement, extreme seismic isolation, and quantum-limited readout, each of which shapes the detector's noise floor and therefore its astrophysical reach.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Interferometric sensing architecture

The Advanced LIGO detector is a dual-recycled Michelson interferometer with Fabry–Pérot cavities in each 4 km arm [3]. A stabilized Nd:YAG laser (1064 nm) injects light through a power recycling mirror, which resonantly enhances the circulating power by a factor of order 40–50, raising the arm-cavity stored power to several hundred kilowatts. Each arm cavity is a Fabry–Pérot resonator that multiplies the gravitational-wave interaction time: photons bounce roughly 280 times on average, so the effective optical path samples the strain over many round trips. A signal recycling mirror at the antisymmetric (dark) port forms a coupled cavity that shapes the detector's frequency response, broadening or tuning the sensitivity band.

Key sensing and control elements include input and output mode-cleaner cavities, Pound–Drever–Hall locking of all five coupled longitudinal degrees of freedom, DC readout at the dark port.

### 3.2 Noise budget

The interferometer's one-sided strain noise power spectral density $S_n(f)$ is the sum of uncorrelated contributions:

- **Seismic noise** — ground motion coupled through the suspension chain; suppressed by multi-stage active and passive isolation (hydraulic pre-isolation plus quadruple pendulum suspensions), rolling off steeply above ~10 Hz.
- **Thermal noise** — Brownian motion of suspension fibers, substrates, and coatings; coating Brownian noise dominates the mid-frequency band around 50–200 Hz.
- **Quantum noise** — photon shot noise ($\propto 1/\sqrt{P}$) dominates at high frequencies, while radiation-pressure noise ($\propto \sqrt{P}$) dominates at low frequencies; their crossover near ~50–100 Hz defines the standard quantum limit for the chosen operating power.
- **Newtonian (gravity-gradient) noise** — direct gravitational coupling of ambient density fluctuations to the test masses; irreducible by isolation.
- **Technical noises** — laser intensity and frequency noise, scattered light, residual gas phase noise, and control-loop noise, each suppressed by dedicated stabilization servos.

The binary neutron star inspiral range — the orientation-averaged distance at which a canonical 1.4–1.4 M⊙ merger is detectable at signal-to-noise ratio 8 — compactly summarizes the noise budget: it grew from ~40–80 Mpc in O1 to ~130–140 Mpc in O3 for the LIGO instruments as quantum and technical noises were reduced.

### 3.3 Matched filtering

Because expected signals lie far below the instantaneous noise, detection relies on **matched filtering**: correlating the whitened data stream $s(t) = h(t) + n(t)$ against a bank of theoretical templates. For stationary Gaussian noise, the optimal signal-to-noise ratio is

$$
\rho^2 = 4 \int_0^\infty \frac{|\tilde{h}(f)|^2}{S_n(f)}\,df,
$$

with the inner product $(a|b) = 4\,\Re \int_0^\infty \tilde{a}^*(f)\tilde{b}(f)/S_n(f)\,df$ defining the signal-space geometry [2]. The PyCBC and GstLAL pipelines implement this as a fast convolution over discretized template banks, with a $\chi^2$ veto rejecting non-Gaussian transients whose time–frequency power deviates from the template's expectation.

```python
import numpy as np

def matched_filter_snr(data_f, template_f, psd, df):
    """Optimal SNR^2 = 4 Re int |h~|^2 / S_n df (stationary Gaussian noise)."""
    integrand = (np.conj(template_f) * data_f) / psd
    overlap = 4.0 * np.sum(integrand).real * df
    norm = 4.0 * np.sum((np.abs(template_f) ** 2) / psd).real * df
    return overlap / np.sqrt(norm)

def chirp_frequency_evolution(t, tc, Mc):
    """Leading-order PN chirp: f(t) diverges toward coalescence time tc."""
    G, c = 6.67430e-11, 299792458.0
    tau = np.maximum(tc - t, 1e-9)
    return (1.0 / np.pi) * (5.0 / 256.0 / tau) ** (3.0 / 8.0) \
        * (G * Mc / c ** 3) ** (-5.0 / 8.0)
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Fabry–Pérot cavities and dual recycling: the optical engine

The bare Michelson strain response is limited by the light travel time in the arms: sensitivity grows with arm length only until the gravitational-wave period becomes comparable to the round-trip time. Fabry–Pérot arm cavities circumvent this by storing light for a cavity pole frequency $f_p \approx 40$ Hz (Advanced LIGO), so that each photon samples the arm-length modulation over many cycles. The differential phase shift at the beam splitter scales with the cavity finesse $\mathcal{F}$:

$$
\Delta\phi \;\propto\; \frac{2\mathcal{F}}{\pi}\,\frac{\Delta L}{L}\,\frac{2\pi L}{\lambda},
$$

while the arm cavities simultaneously reject laser frequency noise common to both arms through the common-mode rejection of the Michelson topology.

Power recycling adds a mirror between the laser and the beam splitter, impedance-matched so that light returning toward the laser is reflected back into the interferometer; the power recycling gain of ~40 multiplies the effective shot-noise-limited sensitivity by $\sqrt{40}$. Signal recycling, placed at the dark port, forms a compound mirror with the arm cavities whose reflectivity and detuning control the coupled-cavity resonance — resonant-sideband extraction broadens the bandwidth, while detuned operation peaks the response at a chosen frequency.

> **Theorem 1 (Shot-noise-limited strain sensitivity).** *For a power-recycled Fabry–Pérot Michelson operated at dark fringe with arm-cavity circulating power $P_{\rm arm}$ and wavelength $\lambda$, the shot-noise-limited strain amplitude spectral density scales as $\sqrt{S_h^{\rm shot}(f)} \propto \lambda\,[1 + (f/f_p)^2]^{1/2}/(L\sqrt{P_{\rm arm}})$. Increasing circulating power suppresses shot noise but increases radiation-pressure noise $\sqrt{S_h^{\rm rp}} \propto \sqrt{P_{\rm arm}}/(m f^2 L)$, so an optimal power minimizes their quadrature sum at each frequency — the standard quantum limit envelope.*

### 4.2 The noise budget as a design driver

Each noise term in $S_n(f)$ maps to a subsystem requirement, making the noise budget the central engineering document of the detector. Representative design values for Advanced LIGO are summarized below:

| Noise source | Dominant band | Scaling / mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Seismic | < 10 Hz | Quadruple pendulums + active isolation; steep high-pass roll-off |
| Suspension thermal | 10–50 Hz | Fused-silica fibers, high $Q$; $\propto \sqrt{T/(m Q f)}$ |
| Coating Brownian | 50–200 Hz | Low-loss $\rm TiO_2$-doped $\rm Ta_2O_5$; dominant mid-band limit |
| Quantum shot | > 100 Hz | $\propto 1/\sqrt{P_{\rm arm}}$; squeezed vacuum injection |
| Quantum radiation pressure | < 50 Hz | $\propto \sqrt{P_{\rm arm}}/m f^2$; 40 kg test masses |
| Newtonian | < 20 Hz | Density-fluctuation coupling; feedforward cancellation |
| Residual gas | 10–100 Hz | Ultra-high vacuum $10^{-9}$ torr; $\propto \sqrt{p}$ |

The design strain sensitivity reaches $\sim 3 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{Hz}^{-1/2}$ near 100–200 Hz, a tenfold improvement over initial LIGO [3]. Since detection volume scales as the cube of the range, this tenfold gain yields a thousandfold increase in surveyed volume.

```haskell
-- Quantum noise envelope: shot noise falls as 1/sqrt(P), radiation pressure rises as sqrt(P)
quantumNoise :: Double -> Double -> Double -> (Double, Double)
quantumNoise power mass freq =
  let shot  = kShot / sqrt power * sqrt (1 + (freq / poleFreq) ^ 2)
      radP  = kRad * sqrt power / (mass * freq ^ 2)
  in (shot, radP)
  where
    kShot    = 1.0   -- normalized coupling constants
    kRad     = 1.0
    poleFreq = 40.0   -- arm cavity pole frequency (Hz)

optimalPower :: Double -> Double -> Double
optimalPower mass freq = (kShot / kRad) * mass * freq ^ 2
  where kShot = 1.0; kRad = 1.0
```

### 4.3 Matched filtering and the geometry of template banks

A search must cover a continuous signal manifold with a discrete bank of templates while guaranteeing that no astrophysical signal loses more than a prescribed fraction of SNR. Owen recast this as a problem in differential geometry: the *mismatch* $1 - M$ between neighboring normalized waveforms defines a positive-definite metric on parameter space,

$$
g_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\frac{1}{2}\left.\frac{\partial^2 M(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\theta} + \Delta\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial \Delta\theta^i \partial \Delta\theta^j}\right|_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}=0},
$$

so that the proper volume per template at minimal match $MM$ is proportional to $(1-MM)^{N/2}$, where $N$ is the intrinsic-parameter dimension [2]. A bank achieving minimal match 0.97 therefore sacrifices at most ~10% of detectable event rate ($1 - MM^3 \approx 0.087$), and the total number of templates follows from integrating the metric volume element over the mass–spin domain — of order $10^5$–$10^6$ templates for modern aligned-spin BBH/BNS banks [7].

Computational cost is dominated by the FFT-based correlation of every template against the data stream. Owen and Sathyaprakash showed that the cost scales with the number of templates, the sampling rate, and the template duration, driving innovations such as hierarchical two-stage searches (coarse bank at low threshold, fine follow-up), singular-value-decomposition compression of the bank, and GPU acceleration [7]. The $\chi^2$ veto of Allen further divides each template into frequency sub-bands and demands that the accumulated SNR distribute across bands as the template predicts — a powerful discriminant against instrumental glitches with no counterpart in the Gaussian theory.

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE TemplateBank ------------------------------
EXTENDS Reals, Integers, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS ParamPoints,       \* finite discretization of intrinsic parameter space
          MinMatch           \* required minimal match, e.g. 0.97
VARIABLES bank,              \* set of accepted template points
          uncovered          \* points not yet within MinMatch of bank
Metric(p, q) == 1 - Mismatch(p, q)   \* normalized overlap between waveforms

Init == /\ bank = {}
        /\ uncovered = ParamPoints

AddTemplate == \* greedily add the point covering the most uncovered volume
  /\ uncovered /= {}
  /\ \E p \in uncovered :
       /\ bank' = bank \cup {p}
       /\ uncovered' = {q \in uncovered : Metric(p, q) < MinMatch}

BankComplete == uncovered = {}
Spec == Init /\ [][AddTemplate]_<<bank, uncovered>> /\ WF_<<bank, uncovered>>(AddTemplate)
THEOREM BankCoversSpace == Spec => []BankComplete
=============================================================================
```

> **Theorem 2 (Detection statistic optimality).** *For data $s(t) = h(t;\boldsymbol{\theta}) + n(t)$ with $n(t)$ stationary Gaussian noise of PSD $S_n(f)$, the likelihood ratio maximized over unknown amplitude is a monotone function of the matched-filter SNR $\rho = \max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s|h(\boldsymbol{\theta}))/\sqrt{(h(\boldsymbol{\theta})|h(\boldsymbol{\theta}))}$. Hence thresholding $\rho$ yields the Neyman–Pearson optimal test at fixed false-alarm probability, and the distribution of $\rho$ under the noise hypothesis is $\chi$-distributed with the template-bank trials factor setting the false-alarm rate.*

### 4.4 Bayesian parameter estimation

Detection answers *whether* a signal is present; parameter estimation answers *what* it is. Under Bayes' theorem,

$$
p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid d, H) = \frac{p(d \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}, H)\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid H)}{p(d \mid H)},
$$

the likelihood for a network of detectors with stationary Gaussian noise is $p(d|\boldsymbol{\theta}) \propto \exp[-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_k (d_k - h_k(\boldsymbol{\theta}) | d_k - h_k(\boldsymbol{\theta}))]$, where the inner products use each detector's estimated PSD [5]. The 15-dimensional CBC parameter space (two masses, six spin components, distance, inclination, sky position, polarization, coalescence time and phase) exhibits strong degeneracies — most famously the distance–inclination degeneracy, since a distant face-on binary mimics a nearby edge-on one in amplitude — which only a network of detectors with different antenna patterns can break.

The LALInference library implements this inference with three independent stochastic samplers — Markov chain Monte Carlo, nested sampling, and the BAMBI algorithm — cross-compared to validate convergence, demonstrating that quoted credible intervals are well-calibrated frequentist confidence intervals under correct priors [5]. Relative-binning (heterodyning) accelerates likelihood evaluations by factors of $10^2$–$10^3$.

Waveform systematics are the dominant modeling uncertainty: differences between approximant families (IMRPhenomXPHM, SEOBNRv5PHM, NRSur7dq4) in the treatment of precession and higher-order modes can shift posteriors by amounts comparable to statistical uncertainties for loud signals. Mitigation strategies include multi-waveform analyses, marginalization over calibration uncertainty envelopes, and numerical-relativity injection campaigns.

```rust
/// Accumulate matched-filter SNR^2 over the inspiral for a chirping template.
/// d_rho2/df = 4 |h_tilde(f)|^2 / S_n(f); inspiral amplitude ~ f^(-7/6).
fn snr_squared_accumulation(
    freqs: &[f64],
    mc: f64,          // chirp mass (solar masses)
    dist_mpc: f64,    // luminosity distance (Mpc)
    psd: &[f64],      // one-sided noise PSD at freqs
) -> Vec<f64> {
    let mut accum = 0.0;
    let mut out = Vec::with_capacity(freqs.len());
    for (i, &f) in freqs.iter().enumerate() {
        let h = 1.0e-23 * (mc / 30.0).powf(5.0 / 6.0)
              * (100.0 / dist_mpc) * (f / 100.0).powf(-7.0 / 6.0);
        let df = if i == 0 { freqs[1] - freqs[0] } else { f - freqs[i - 1] };
        accum += 4.0 * h * h / psd[i] * df;
        out.push(accum.sqrt());
    }
    out
}
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 From GW150914 to the catalog era

GW150914 remains the canonical demonstration: a matched-filter SNR of 24 across the Hanford and Livingston detectors, component masses $36^{+5}_{-4}\,M_\odot$ and $29^{+4}_{-4}\,M_\odot$, final mass $62^{+4}_{-4}\,M_\odot$, and $3.0^{+0.5}_{-0.5}\,M_\odot c^2$ radiated — all at 90% credibility [4]. Cutler and Flanagan's 1994 Fisher-matrix forecast that the chirp mass would be measurable to 0.1–1% was vindicated: GW150914's detector-frame chirp mass was constrained to a few percent despite the signal's brevity [1]. 

### 5.2 Proof sketch: chirp mass dominates the phase

*Claim.* To leading PN order, the inspiral phase evolution depends only on the chirp mass $\mathcal{M}$.

*Proof.* The orbital energy $E(v) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\mu v^2$ and flux $\mathcal{F}(v) = \tfrac{32}{5}\eta^2 v^{10}$ (with $v = (\pi M f)^{1/3}$, symmetric mass ratio $\eta = \mu/M$) enter the energy-balance equation $dE/dt = -\mathcal{F}$. Integrating $dt/df = (dE/df)/(-\mathcal{F})$ yields the stationary-phase Fourier phase $\Psi(f) = 2\pi f t_c - \phi_c - \pi/4 + \tfrac{3}{128}(\pi \mathcal{M} f)^{-5/3}$ at leading order, where only the combination $\mathcal{M} = M\eta^{3/5}$ survives [1]. Higher PN orders introduce $\eta$ and spin combinations (notably the effective spin $\chi_{\rm eff}$ at 1.5PN), which is precisely why component masses are less well measured than $\mathcal{M}$ and why spin–mass-ratio degeneracies persist. ∎

### 5.3 Hierarchical population inference

Individual-event posteriors are draws from an underlying astrophysical population. Hierarchical Bayesian inference recovers the population hyperparameters $\Lambda$ (e.g., the power-law slope of the primary-mass distribution, the spin-magnitude distribution) via

$$
p(\Lambda \mid \{d_i\}) \propto p(\Lambda)\prod_{i=1}^{N_{\rm obs}} \frac{\int p(d_i \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})\,p_{\rm pop}(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \Lambda)\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\alpha(\Lambda)},
$$

where the denominator $\alpha(\Lambda) = \int p_{\rm det}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p_{\rm pop}(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \Lambda)\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}$ corrects for **selection effects**: the search detects high-mass, nearby, face-on binaries preferentially, so the observed catalog is a biased sample of the intrinsic population. The detection probability $p_{\rm det}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is estimated by injecting simulated signals into the search pipelines and measuring recovery fractions.

Applied to the ten BBHs of GWTC-1, this machinery found a primary-mass distribution consistent with a power law of index $\alpha = 1.3^{+1.4}_{-1.7}$ with fewer than 1% of black holes above 45 M⊙, spins preferentially small and misaligned, and a merger rate density of $53.2^{+55.8}_{-28.2}\,\mathrm{Gpc}^{-3}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ [6]. With the larger GWTC-2 and GWTC-3 catalogs, structure emerged: a peak near 35 M⊙ in the primary-mass spectrum, evidence for redshift evolution of the merger rate, and a mass-ratio distribution favoring equal masses — results that discriminate between isolated binary evolution and dynamical formation channels in dense stellar environments.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Calibration uncertainty.** The conversion from photodetector counts to strain relies on photon-calibrator references with ~1–2% amplitude uncertainty; these propagate directly into distance and sky-localization errors and are now marginalized over in analyses.
2. **Waveform systematics.** No single approximant captures all of precession, eccentricity, higher modes, and matter effects simultaneously; systematic differences between families can bias parameters of loud or unusual events.
3. **Non-Gaussian, non-stationary noise.** Glitches — transient instrumental artifacts — violate the Gaussian matched-filter assumptions; vetoes and glitch-subtraction mitigate but cannot eliminate the risk, especially for short-duration high-mass signals.
4. **Low-frequency wall.** Seismic and Newtonian noise fundamentally limit ground-based sensitivity below ~10 Hz, cutting off the early inspiral of massive systems; third-generation concepts (Einstein Telescope, Cosmic Explorer) address this with underground construction and cryogenics.
5. **Selection and population-model dependence.** Population conclusions depend on the assumed parametric family; flexible non-parametric models reduce this dependence at the cost of wider uncertainties, and the small catalog size keeps many features at marginal significance.
6. **Degeneracies.** Distance–inclination, mass-ratio–spin, and sky-location degeneracies persist for one- or two-detector events; only a three-plus-detector network with good sensitivity balance fully resolves them.

## 7 Conclusion

Ground-based gravitational-wave astronomy is an integrated triumph of precision measurement and statistical inference. The dual-recycled Fabry–Pérot Michelson interferometer — with its power and signal recycling mirrors, quadruple suspensions, and squeezed-light readout — converts spacetime strains of 10⁻²¹ into photodetector signals above a noise budget whose every term is understood and budgeted [3]. Matched filtering against geometrically placed template banks extracts chirping signals from deep within the noise at the Neyman–Pearson optimum [2][7]. Bayesian parameter estimation, built on the likelihood formalism and validated samplers of LALInference, converts detections into 15-dimensional posterior distributions over masses, spins, and extrinsic parameters [5]. And hierarchical population inference, corrected for selection effects, aggregates catalogs into statements about how black holes form and merge across cosmic time [6].

The trajectory from Cutler and Flanagan's Fisher forecasts [1] through Owen's template geometry [2] to the empirical catalogs [4][6] closes a rare loop between theory, instrumentation, and observation. With upgraded runs, KAGRA joining the network, and third-generation detectors ahead, the catalog will grow from tens to hundreds of thousands of events — shifting the frontier to precision population cosmology and percent-level tests of general relativity.
