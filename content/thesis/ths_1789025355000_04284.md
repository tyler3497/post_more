# Social Choice and Voting Theory: Arrow's Impossibility, Gibbard–Satterthwaite, and Ranked-Choice Systems

## Abstract

This thesis investigates the fundamental tension between fairness and strategic robustness in collective decision-making, centered on three landmarks of social choice theory: Kenneth Arrow's impossibility theorem (1951) [1], the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem on strategy-proofness (1973/1975) [2], and the axiomatic evaluation of ranked-choice voting systems. We prove that no ordinal aggregation rule over three or more alternatives can simultaneously satisfy unrestricted domain, Pareto efficiency, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship; that no non-trivial deterministic rule is immune to strategic manipulation; and that widely deployed systems — instant-runoff voting, the Borda count, and Kemeny–Young — each purchase their strengths at the price of violating at least one desideratum. We supply rigorous proof sketches, computational complexity results from computational social choice [7], and worked empirical examples including the Condorcet paradox and the no-show paradox. The analysis concludes that the impossibility theorems are best read not as a counsel of despair but as an engineering specification: a principled catalog of the tradeoffs any democratic mechanism must make.

## 1 Introduction

Few questions in political economy are as deceptively simple as this: given a group of voters, each with a coherent ranking of the available candidates, how should society combine those rankings into a single collective decision? The stakes are enormous — the method chosen determines winners of presidential elections, parliamentary allocations, committee chairs, and, increasingly, the outputs of multi-agent AI systems and recommender platforms [7]. Yet the mathematical answer, developed over two centuries from Condorcet's *Essai* (1785) to the computational social choice literature of the 1990s, is uncompromising: *there is no perfect method*. Every procedure that aggregates individual rankings must fail at least one property we find intuitively indispensable.

The first crack appeared in 1785, when the Marquis de Condorcet observed that pairwise majority voting can cycle — *A* defeats *B*, *B* defeats *C*, *C* defeats *A* — even though every voter holds a transitive ranking [5]. Jean-Charles de Borda's 1781 positional scoring method resolves cycles but introduces vulnerabilities of its own. Kenneth Arrow broke new ground in *Social Choice and Individual Values* (1951), drawn from his doctoral thesis, by reversing the inquiry [1][3]: rather than proposing a rule and auditing its defects, he asked which rules could satisfy a set of elementary fairness axioms — and proved, with startling generality, that none can.

Allan Gibbard (1973) and Mark Satterthwaite (1975) then closed the strategic escape route, showing that every non-trivial deterministic voting rule with three or more candidates is *manipulable*: some voter can always do better by lying about their preferences [2][4]. Together, these results frame the design of voting systems as an exercise in choosing *which* failure to tolerate, and computational social choice — initiated by Bartholdi, Tovey, and Trick (1989), who distinguished constructive from destructive manipulation and measured its computational difficulty — added a new dimension to that choice: *complexity* as a protective barrier [7].

This thesis unifies these strands. Section 2 fixes the formal framework; Section 3 describes the axiomatic methodology; Section 4 develops the four core topics in depth; Section 5 supplies proof sketches and a computational simulation of paradox frequency; Section 6 confronts the limitations; and Section 7 draws the engineering conclusions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The formal framework

Let *N = {1, …, n}* be a finite set of voters and *A* a finite set of *m ≥ 3* alternatives. Each voter *i* holds a strict linear order *Rᵢ* over *A* (complete, transitive, antisymmetric). A **preference profile** is the *n*-tuple **R** = (*R₁, …, Rₙ*). Two aggregation objects are distinguished in the literature:

- A **social welfare function (SWF)** *F* maps each profile to a social ranking: *F: ℒ(A)ⁿ → ℒ(A)*, where *ℒ(A)* is the set of all linear orders on *A* [5].
- A **social choice function (SCF)** *f* maps each profile to a single winning alternative: *f: ℒ(A)ⁿ → A* (resolute rules); more generally to a non-empty choice set *f(**R**) ⊆ A*.

A voting rule is *resolute* if it always selects a unique winner (possibly via a fixed tie-breaking order). The central axioms are [1][3]:

- **Unrestricted domain (U):** *F* (or *f*) is defined on *all* logically possible profiles of linear orders.
- **Pareto efficiency (P):** if every voter ranks *a* above *b*, the social outcome ranks *a* above *b* (SWF) or *b* is never chosen while *a* is available (SCF).
- **Independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA):** the social ranking of any pair {*a, b*} depends only on voters' rankings of *a* versus *b*, never on their attitudes toward third alternatives *c*.
- **Non-dictatorship (D):** there is no voter *i* such that the social outcome always coincides with *i*'s preferences, regardless of others' ballots.

Two further properties govern the single-winner literature. A rule is **strategy-proof** if no voter can ever obtain a strictly better outcome by misreporting their preferences, holding others' reports fixed [2]. An alternative *a* is a **Condorcet winner** if it defeats every other alternative in pairwise majority contests; a rule is *Condorcet-consistent* if it elects the Condorcet winner whenever one exists [5].

### 2.2 The Condorcet paradox

Consider three voters and three alternatives {*a, b, c*}:

| Voter 1 | Voter 2 | Voter 3 |
|---|---|---|
| *a ≻ b ≻ c* | *b ≻ c ≻ a* | *c ≻ a ≻ b* |

Pairwise majorities yield *a ≻ b* (voters 1, 3), *b ≻ c* (voters 1, 2), and *c ≻ a* (voters 2, 3): a cycle, though every individual ranking is transitive [5][6]. No Condorcet winner exists. This is a property of the *profile itself* — Arrow's theorem shows it cannot be legislated away by clever aggregation design.

### 2.3 A brief history of rules

- **Plurality:** each voter names one favorite; the most-named wins. Simple but discards all information below the top rank.
- **Borda count (1781):** a voter's *k*-th ranked candidate receives *m − k* points; the highest total wins. It uses full ranking information but fails Condorcet consistency [6].
- **Instant-runoff voting (IRV):** repeatedly eliminate the candidate with the fewest first-place votes, transferring ballots until one holds a majority.
- **Kemeny–Young:** select the ranking minimizing total pairwise disagreement with the voters' ballots (equivalently, the maximum-likelihood ranking under Young's 1995 noise model) [6]. Condorcet-consistent but NP-hard to compute [7].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis adopts the **axiomatic method** pioneered by Arrow: rather than comparing rules by anecdote, we postulate desiderata, formalize them as axioms, and determine — by proof — which axiom bundles are jointly realizable [3]. The method is *normative* rather than empirical: it asks what any acceptable rule must satisfy, then maps the frontier of the possible. Its principal techniques are:

1. **Decisive-coalition arguments.** Arrow's proof shows that any SWF satisfying (U), (P), and (IIA) contains a *decisive* coalition — voters whose unanimous strict preference over a pair is always reflected socially — and that some singleton is decisive over *every* pair: a dictator. Modern expositions streamline this to the **pivotal-voter argument** sketched in Section 5 [4].
2. **Reduction between theorems.** Satterthwaite showed that Gibbard–Satterthwaite and Arrow's theorem are inter-derivable given Pareto: a strategy-proof SCF induces an Arrow-style SWF and vice versa [2]. Proving impossibility once and transferring it pervades the field.
3. **Computational complexity as a defense.** Bartholdi, Tovey, and Trick (1989) reframed manipulation as an algorithmic problem: given the other ballots, can voter *i* compute a ballot that yields a better outcome? They distinguished *constructive* manipulation (making a favored candidate win) from *destructive* manipulation (preventing a despised candidate from winning) and classified rules by the complexity of these problems [7]. If manipulation is NP-hard, the theorem goes, boundedly rational voters cannot exploit it — complexity becomes a *protective barrier*.
4. **Simulation.** Because worst-case theorems say little about typical elections, we complement proofs with a Monte Carlo study of paradox frequency under the impartial-culture model (Section 5).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Arrow's impossibility theorem

> **Theorem (Arrow, 1951):** If there are at least three alternatives, then there is no social welfare function satisfying unrestricted domain, Pareto efficiency, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship [1].

Formally: no *F: ℒ(A)ⁿ → ℒ(A)* with *|A| ≥ 3* satisfies (U), (P), (IIA), and (D) simultaneously. A few clarifications matter [1][3][8]:

- *Unrestricted domain* is load-bearing: without it, pairwise majority satisfies the remaining axioms by "giving up" (producing cycles) exactly on Condorcet profiles [1].
- Arrow's original 1951 statement included a *non-negative responsiveness* condition, later recognized as unnecessary to the proof and retracted by Arrow himself [3].
- The theorem concerns *ordinal* aggregation producing a full social ranking. Cardinal systems carry more information and escape this particular theorem — though Gibbard's broader 1973 theorem covers cardinal *mechanisms* too [2].

The philosophical sting is that IIA, the axiom doing most of the destructive work, is independently attractive: it forbids *spoiler effects*, where a candidate's standing relative to another shifts merely because voters reorder an irrelevant third candidate. Every ranked system in wide use violates IIA — including IRV and Borda — which is why spoiler candidates are a perennial feature of real elections rather than a bug of any particular one.

### 4.2 The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem and strategy-proofness

Arrow's theorem governs *aggregation*; Gibbard–Satterthwaite governs *incentives*. A resolute rule *f* is **strategy-proof** if truth-telling is always a weakly dominant strategy: for every voter *i*, every profile **R**, and every misreport *Rᵢ′*,

> *f(Rᵢ, **R**₋ᵢ) ⪰ᵢ f(Rᵢ′, **R**₋ᵢ)*.

> **Theorem (Gibbard, 1973; Satterthwaite, 1975):** Any resolute voting rule for more than two candidates that is surjective (every candidate can win under some profile) and strategy-proof is a dictatorship [2].

The result was first conjectured by the philosopher Michael Dummett and the mathematician Robin Farquharson in 1961, then proved independently: Gibbard's version in *Econometrica* 41(4) (1973), "Manipulation of Voting Schemes: A General Result," and Satterthwaite's in the *Journal of Economic Theory* 10 (1975), "Strategy-proofness and Arrow's Conditions" [2][4]. Gibbard's proof is the more general, covering non-ordinal mechanisms such as cardinal voting; its corollary for ranked systems is the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. The theorem was later extended to randomized rules (Gibbard, 1977 — only *random dictatorships* are strategy-proof on the full domain) and to multi-winner systems (Duggan–Schwartz).

The apparent counterexample of the **median voting rule** is instructive rather than contradictory [2]. On a one-dimensional policy space with *single-peaked* preferences, selecting the median voter's peak is strategy-proof — but single-peakedness is a *domain restriction*, violating unrestricted domain. Duncan Black (1948) had already shown that single-peaked profiles always admit a Condorcet winner, so Arrow's impossibility dissolves there too. The lesson: impossibility theorems map the cost of *generality*.

### 4.3 Ranked-choice systems: IRV, Borda, and Kemeny–Young

With impossibility established, the practical question is which violations to accept. The leading systems trade axioms differently:

| Rule | Condorcet-consistent | Monotonic | IIA | Strategy-proof | Polynomial winner |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plurality | No | Yes | No | No | Yes |
| Borda count | No | Yes | No | No | Yes |
| IRV (Hare) | No | **No** | No | No | Yes |
| Kemeny–Young | Yes | No | No | No | **No** (NP-hard) |

*Monotonicity* deserves emphasis: a rule is monotonic if additional support for a candidate can never harm them. IRV famously fails it — a candidate can *lose* by gaining votes, because extra first-place votes may alter the elimination order [6]. Moulin (1988) proved a deeper defect shared by *all* Condorcet-consistent rules: vulnerability to the **no-show paradox**, where voters obtain a better outcome by abstaining than by voting sincerely [5]. Plurality and Borda are immune to no-show — but both fail Condorcet consistency: with 499 voters *A ≻ B ≻ C*, 3 voters *B ≻ C ≻ A*, and 498 voters *C ≻ B ≻ A*, plurality elects *A*, yet *B* defeats both rivals pairwise and is the Condorcet winner [6].

**Kemeny–Young** occupies the opposite corner: it is the Condorcet-consistent rule with a maximum-likelihood justification under a natural noise model (Young, 1995), yet computing the Kemeny winner is NP-hard, and *controlling* such elections is hard as well [7]. The tradeoff is therefore three-way: *axiomatic virtue, manipulability, tractability* — pick at most two.

### 4.4 Computational social choice: complexity as a shield

Bartholdi, Tovey, and Trick's 1989 paper "The computational difficulty of manipulating an election" launched computational social choice by asking whether Gibbard–Satterthwaite's manipulability is *actionable* [7]. Their taxonomy:

1. **Constructive vs. destructive manipulation** — electing a target vs. blocking one.
2. **Single vs. coalitional manipulators**, weighted vs. unweighted voters.
3. **Worst-case complexity** of the decision problem "does a manipulating ballot exist?"

They showed, among other results, that constructive manipulation by a single unweighted voter is NP-complete for second-order Copeland, while it is polynomial for plurality, Borda, and many scoring rules; Bartholdi and Orlin (1991) extended NP-hardness to STV. The *normative spin* — the "protective barrier" thesis — is that hardness substitutes for the strategy-proofness Arrow's lineage proved unattainable.

The shield has cracks, and the literature catalogs them honestly [7]. NP-hardness is worst-case: typical instances may be easy, and a manipulator need not find the *optimal* lie, only a *better* one. Moreover, hardness protects only against *computational* manipulation — not the informational manipulation (polls, endorsements) dominating real campaigns. Nevertheless, tractability is now an explicit design criterion, which is why Kemeny–Young, despite its axiomatic credentials, remains a committee-room curiosity rather than a national electoral system.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: Arrow via the pivotal voter

We sketch the one-voter proof popularized by Geanakoplos (adapted in [4]). Fix three alternatives {*a, b, c*} and consider profiles where every voter ranks *a* either first or last. Start with all voters ranking *a* last; by Pareto, *a* is socially last. Move voters one by one to ranking *a* first. At the end, by Pareto, *a* is socially first. Some voter *i\** is **pivotal**: the first whose switch moves *a* from socially last to socially first. Claim: *i\** is a dictator over {*a, b*} — and by symmetric argument over every pair.

Fix a profile where *i\** ranks *a ≻ b* and all others are arbitrary. By pivotality and IIA, society ranks *a ≻ b* whenever all voters before *i\** rank *a* first and all after rank *a* last while preserving {*a, b*} comparisons. Moving every voter except *i\** arbitrarily while preserving their {*a, b*} order keeps *a ≻ b* social by IIA — so *i\**'s strict preference over {*a, b*} determines society's: decisiveness. The field-expansion lemma spreads this to all pairs, making *i\** a full dictator [1][3]. Hence any SWF satisfying (U), (P), (IIA) is dictatorial.

### 5.2 From Arrow to Gibbard–Satterthwaite

Satterthwaite's reduction: given a strategy-proof, surjective SCF *f*, define an SWF by lexicographic iteration — rank first *f(**R**)*, then the winner among remaining alternatives, and so on. Strategy-proofness plus surjectivity forces this SWF to satisfy (U), (P), and (IIA); Arrow then yields a dictator [2]. Manipulability is therefore the shadow Arrow's theorem casts on incentives.

### 5.3 Simulation: how common are Condorcet cycles?

Under the *impartial-culture* model (every profile equally likely), the probability of a Condorcet cycle with three candidates converges to approximately **8.8%** as the electorate grows — a classical result of Niemi and Weisberg (1968). We verify the finite-sample behavior:

```python
import random
from itertools import permutations

def majority_tournament(profile):
    """profile: list of rankings (tuples). Returns dict (x,y)->votes for x over y."""
    cands = profile[0]
    t = {}
    for x in cands:
        for y in cands:
            if x != y:
                t[(x, y)] = sum(1 for r in profile if r.index(x) < r.index(y))
    return t

def has_cycle(profile):
    cands = profile[0]
    n = len(profile)
    beats = {x: set() for x in cands}
    for x in cands:
        for y in cands:
            if x != y and sum(1 for r in profile if r.index(x) < r.index(y)) > n / 2:
                beats[x].add(y)
    # detect directed cycle via DFS
    def dfs(v, start, seen):
        for w in beats[v]:
            if w == start:
                return True
            if w not in seen and dfs(w, start, seen | {w}):
                return True
        return False
    return any(dfs(c, c, {c}) for c in cands)

def estimate(n_voters, trials=20000):
    rankings = list(permutations(['a', 'b', 'c']))
    cyc = sum(has_cycle([random.choice(rankings) for _ in range(n_voters)])
              for _ in range(trials))
    return cyc / trials

for n in (3, 11, 101, 1001):
    print(n, round(estimate(n), 4))
```

Typical output converges toward the theoretical ~0.088: cycles are rare in small committees but a persistent ~9% background risk in large electorates under impartial culture — real electorates with correlated preferences see far fewer, which is why pairwise-majority institutions survive despite the paradox [5].

### 5.4 The no-show paradox, concretely

Moulin's theorem states that every Condorcet-consistent rule is vulnerable to no-show [5]. A minimal illustration: 4 voters *A ≻ B ≻ C*, 3 voters *B ≻ C ≻ A*, 2 voters *C ≻ B ≻ A*. Pairwise, *B* beats *A* 5–4 and *C* 7–2 — *B* is the Condorcet winner and is elected. Yet the two *C ≻ B ≻ A* voters can obtain *C* — their favorite — by *abstaining*, since under several Condorcet extensions removing their ballots flips the outcome. Participation itself becomes strategically costly.

## 6 Limitations

The classical framework's strength — its generality — is also its boundary. Five limitations structure current research:

1. **Domain restrictions dissolve the impossibilities.** Single-peaked preferences (Black, 1948), single-crossing profiles, and dichotomous preferences each admit strategy-proof, non-dictatorial, Condorcet-consistent rules — notably the median-voter rule on a line [2][7]. Real electorates are rarely fully unrestricted, which bounds the theorems' practical bite.
2. **Two alternatives are trivial.** May's theorem (1952) characterizes simple majority rule for *m = 2* by anonymity, neutrality, and positive responsiveness. All impossibility results require *m ≥ 3*.
3. **Randomization escapes.** Gibbard (1977) showed that on the full domain, the only strategy-proof *randomized* rules are random dictatorships — but "only random dictatorships" is itself a characterization result, and approximate strategy-proofness (mechanisms where manipulation gains are bounded by *ε*) is achievable and practically relevant.
4. **Cardinal information helps — partially.** Range voting and approval voting escape Arrow's ordinal theorem, but Gibbard's general theorem still constrains cardinal *mechanisms*, and cardinal ballots introduce interpersonal comparability problems Arrow deliberately excluded [2][8].
5. **Empirics vs. worst cases.** Impartial-culture frequencies overstate paradox prevalence; spatial models of real electorates find cycles and manipulable profiles far rarer [5]. Complexity barriers likewise erode under typical-case analysis and heuristic search. The theorems describe what *can* go wrong, not what *usually* does.

These limitations do not refute the impossibility program — they *complete* it, by delineating exactly which assumptions must be sacrificed to recover possibility.

## 7 Conclusion

Social choice theory's central message is a disciplined humility. Arrow proved that no ordinal aggregation over three or more alternatives can be simultaneously universal, Paretian, independent of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorial [1][3]. Gibbard and Satterthwaite proved that no non-trivial deterministic rule can make honesty the best policy [2][4]. The ranked-choice systems actually deployed — plurality, IRV, Borda, Kemeny–Young — are best understood as *points on the Pareto frontier of these impossibilities*: Borda buys monotonicity at the price of Condorcet consistency; Kemeny–Young buys Condorcet consistency at the price of tractability; IRV buys majoritarian legitimacy at the price of monotonicity itself.

Computational social choice added the final design axis, showing that when strategy-proofness is unobtainable in principle, *computational hardness* can serve as a practical substitute — an imperfect shield, but the only one on offer [7]. The mature reading of these theorems is therefore not that democracy is impossible, but that mechanism design is *inescapably* a multi-objective optimization over axioms that cannot all be satisfied. The honest contribution of the field is the map of that frontier: knowing precisely which fairness property you are sacrificing, and why, when you choose how a group decides.
