# Onion Routing and Mix Networks: Tor Circuit Construction, Guard Discovery Attacks, Traffic Correlation Resistance, and the Anonymity Trilemma in Low-Latency Systems

## Abstract

This thesis examines the cryptographic and network-layer mechanisms by which low-latency anonymity systems — most notably Tor — achieve sender anonymity, and the fundamental limits constraining them. We analyze Tor's telescoping circuit construction, the transition from the Tor Authentication Protocol (TAP) to the ntor one-way authenticated key exchange over Curve25519, and the layered encryption model of fixed-size relay cells. We then survey the principal de-anonymization vectors: guard discovery through circuit fingerprinting, traffic correlation by AS- and IXP-level adversaries, and congestion-oracle attacks of the Murdoch–Danezis family. Our organizing principle is the **anonymity trilemma** of Das, Meiser, Mohammadi, and Kate [2], which proves no protocol can simultaneously achieve strong anonymity, low bandwidth overhead, and low latency overhead. Contrasting onion routing with Chaumian mix networks — where batching and artificial delay purchase anonymity at the cost of interactivity — we combine formal definitions, protocol analysis, and two decades of measurement literature to characterize precisely what Tor can and cannot guarantee.

---

## 1 Introduction

Anonymous communication is the problem of moving bits through a hostile network such that the *fact of communication* — who talks to whom — is hidden from the observer, even when payload confidentiality is trivially achievable by encryption. The distinction is crucial: an adversary who cannot read a message may still learn who sent it, to whom, and when, and in many threat models that metadata *is* the secret [5]. Onion routing, first described at the U.S. Naval Research Laboratory in the mid-1990s by Goldschlag, Reed, and Syverson [6], addresses this problem by routing each connection through a series of intermediaries, each of which knows only its immediate predecessor and successor. Tor, the second-generation onion routing system introduced by Dingledine, Mathewson, and Syverson in 2004 [1], is today the largest deployed anonymity network in the world, carrying millions of users' traffic over thousands of volunteer-operated relays.

Tor's design reflects a deliberate compromise. Classical *mix networks*, following Chaum's 1981 proposal, defeat traffic analysis by cryptographic transformation combined with batching, reordering, and artificial delay — powerful techniques that render them unsuitable for interactive applications such as web browsing. Tor instead adopts a **low-latency** circuit model: cells flow with minimal added delay, preserving interactivity while conceding vulnerability to *traffic correlation* attacks [3]. As Das et al. [2] formalized, this tradeoff is a mathematical necessity: strong anonymity against a global passive adversary is provably impossible unless the protocol pays in bandwidth or latency overhead.

We give a precise account of Tor's circuit construction — telescoping extension, the ntor handshake, relay cell layering, and guard selection. We analyze the three principal attack classes: (i) guard discovery and circuit fingerprinting; (ii) end-to-end traffic correlation by AS- and IXP-level adversaries; and (iii) active congestion oracles [4]. We sketch the anonymity trilemma's proof to situate Tor, mixnets, and dummy-traffic schemes on a common tradeoff surface, and synthesize two decades of measurement studies to identify open problems.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The threat model: sender and recipient anonymity

Following the AnoA framework of Backes et al. [2], we distinguish two canonical security goals. *Sender anonymity* requires that the adversary cannot determine which of two challenge users sent a given message; *recipient anonymity* requires that the adversary cannot determine to whom a message was sent. The adversary may be **local** (a single link or relay), **partially global** (an AS or IXP), or **global passive** (all traffic), and may control a subset of relays. Against a *global passive* adversary, Tor deliberately sacrifices strong anonymity for low latency — and the trilemma shows this sacrifice is unavoidable [2].

### 2.2 Onion routing vs. mix networks

In Chaum's original mix, each relay collects a *batch* of messages, decrypts and cryptographically transforms them, permutes their order, and forwards them after a random delay. Batching destroys timing correlations between inputs and outputs: the adversary learns only that the message set entered and (a transformed permutation of) the set left. The cost is latency measured in minutes and bandwidth consumed by cover traffic — acceptable for remailers, fatal for the web.

Onion routing inverts the priority. Tor builds a *circuit* — typically three relays — transmitting fixed-size 514-byte cells, each encrypted in layers such that every relay strips exactly one [1]. With no batching, reordering, or artificial delay, the sender's temporal signature survives to the exit. This is the architectural source of nearly every attack in this thesis: **low-latency systems preserve the traffic features that correlation attacks consume.**

### 2.3 Cryptographic foundations

Tor's security rests on three primitives. First, TLS (with forward secrecy) protects each relay-to-relay link against external eavesdroppers and provides link authentication [1]. Second, the **circuit-level handshake** — historically TAP, an RSA-based Diffie–Hellman exchange, and since Tor 0.2.4.x the **ntor** handshake over Curve25519 [5] — establishes per-hop symmetric keys with *one-way authentication*: the relay authenticates to the client, but the client remains anonymous. Third, AES in counter mode with per-cell integrity digests implements layered encryption. Ephemeral session keys, deleted after circuit teardown, give **perfect forward secrecy**: a relay compromised tomorrow cannot decrypt traffic recorded today [1].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds along three complementary axes. First, **protocol archaeology**: we reconstruct Tor's circuit construction from the original design paper [1], the Tor protocol specification, and proposal 216 defining ntor [5], verifying each claim against at least two independent sources. Second, **adversarial synthesis**: we unify the traffic-correlation literature — from Murdoch and Danezis's low-cost congestion oracle [4] through Johnson et al.'s realistic AS/IXP adversary study [3] to the NetFlow-based measurement campaigns [7] — into a single attack taxonomy, quantifying where possible the accuracy, cost, and required vantage of each attack. Third, **formal reasoning**: we present the anonymity trilemma of Das et al. [2] as a self-contained impossibility argument, stripped of full formalism but retaining the proof's essential structure — the challenge-message game, the counting argument over observed message sets, and the relationship between latency bound ℓ, bandwidth overhead β, and anonymity advantage δ.

> **Theorem: (Anonymity Trilemma, informal statement).** No anonymous communication protocol can simultaneously achieve (i) strong sender anonymity, (ii) low bandwidth overhead, and (iii) low latency overhead against a global passive adversary. Achieving any two requires sacrificing the third [2].

We conduct no new network measurements; the cited accuracies are reproduced from peer-reviewed results with their stated experimental conditions.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Telescoping circuit construction

The original onion routing design built each circuit with a single multiply-encrypted data structure — one "onion" — whose layers were peeled by each hop. Tor replaced this with an **incremental, telescoping construction** [1], which is both more reliable (the initiator knows exactly which hop failed and can retry) and more forward-secret (no long-lived onion material exists for a later compromise to expose).

The construction proceeds as follows:

1. The client (Alice) selects three relays from the directory consensus — a *guard*, a *middle*, and an *exit* — weighted by advertised bandwidth.
2. Alice opens a TLS connection to the guard and sends a `CREATE` cell containing ntor handshake material. The guard replies with a `CREATED` cell; both derive shared secret *K₁*.
3. Alice then sends a `RELAY_EXTEND` cell **through the existing circuit** (encrypted under *K₁*), instructing the guard to extend to the middle relay. The middle's `CREATED` response returns through the circuit, yielding *K₂*. The guard learns nothing about the handshake contents because they are encrypted under the end-to-end layer it cannot read.
4. Step 3 repeats for the exit relay, yielding *K₃*.

At the conclusion, Alice shares a distinct symmetric key with each hop, while no relay knows any hop beyond its neighbors. Handshake cost is *O(n)* messages for *n* relays — later work such as T0RTT reduces this to a single pass [8], at the expense of immediate forward secrecy.

```python
def build_circuit(client, path):
    """Telescoping circuit construction (simplified)."""
    keys = {}
    # Hop 0: direct handshake with guard
    keys[0] = ntor_handshake(client, path[0])
    for i in range(1, len(path)):
        # Extend through the already-built prefix
        extend_cell = relay_extend(path[i], keys[:i])
        response = send_cell(path[0], extend_cell)
        keys[i] = ntor_complete(client, path[i], response)
    return keys  # one shared key per hop, known only to client+relay
```

### 4.2 The ntor handshake: one-way authenticated key exchange

The original TAP handshake used 1024-bit RSA encryption of a Diffie–Hellman public value, which was slow, lacked forward secrecy of the authentication itself, and was eventually recognized as cryptographically fragile. Proposal 216 [5] replaced it with **ntor**, an instantiation of the Goldberg–Stebila–Ustaoglu one-way authenticated key exchange over Curve25519. In ntor:

- The client generates an ephemeral keypair *(x, X = x·G)* and sends *X* alongside the relay's identity fingerprint.
- The relay generates its own ephemeral keypair *(y, Y = y·G)* and computes two Diffie–Hellman shared secrets: *EXP(x, B)* with the relay's long-term public key *B* (providing **authentication** of the relay) and *EXP(x, Y)* (providing **forward secrecy** via ephemerality).
- Both parties derive the session key via HMAC-SHA256 with domain-separated tweaks (`:mac`, `:key_extract`, `:verify`), and the relay returns a MAC proving it derived the same keys.

These are exactly the properties an anonymity network needs: *unilateral entity authentication* (Alice verifies the relay; the relay learns nothing about Alice), *unilateral key authentication*, forward secrecy, and key freshness. The handshake is compact and fast, which matters because clients build circuits constantly — rotating roughly every ten minutes with several in parallel [1].

### 4.3 Layered encryption and the relay cell protocol

Once the circuit exists, data flows as **relay cells** of fixed 514-byte size. Each cell's payload is encrypted successively under *K₃, K₂, K₁* (innermost first). As the cell traverses the circuit, each relay decrypts one layer with the key it shares with Alice. Crucially, decryption is *recognized*: a relay cell carries a digest that is valid only after the correct number of layers have been removed, so each relay knows when a cell is addressed to it without learning anything about cells destined further along [1].

Two properties deserve emphasis. First, fixed cell size leaks *volume* only at cell granularity — a defense against packet-size fingerprinting that website-fingerprinting research has shown insufficient. Second, the "leaky pipe" topology lets streams exit at intermediate relays, aiding hidden services while complicating path inference [1].

### 4.4 Guard selection and the predecessor attack

In Tor's original design, the entry relay was chosen afresh for every circuit. This turned out to be a catastrophic vulnerability to the **predecessor attack**: an adversary controlling *c* of *N* relays is selected as the entry relay with probability roughly *c/N* per circuit; over many circuit rotations, the adversary is *eventually* the first hop of a target's circuit with probability approaching 1, at which point traffic confirmation against the exit (which the adversary may also observe) de-anonymizes the user. Øverlier and Syverson first demonstrated this in the hidden-service context, and subsequent analysis generalized it to all clients [3].

Tor's defense is the **entry guard**: each client pins a small set of relays and uses them as the first hop for *all* circuits over a rotation period of roughly 2–3 months [1]. The adversary must now be the client's *guard* — a one-time event with probability *c/N* — rather than winning a per-circuit lottery, converting a per-circuit compromise probability *p* into a per-rotation probability *p* and dramatically slowing the attacker.

Guards create a *fingerprinting* surface: an adversary controlling or observing the guard can perform **circuit fingerprinting** — distinguishing, with up to 99% laboratory accuracy, whether a circuit is for normal browsing, an introduction point, or a rendezvous point, purely from packet counts and directions [3]. This *guard discovery* attack breaks no encryption but narrows the search from "all circuits" to "circuits of this type through this guard," enabling targeted de-anonymization.

| Attack | Required vantage | What it reveals | Reported accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Predecessor attack | *c* malicious relays | Eventual entry-hop position | → 1 as circuits rotate |
| Guard circuit fingerprinting | Control/observe guard | Circuit type (HS vs. normal) | ~99% (lab) [3] |
| Murdoch–Danezis congestion oracle | 1 corrupt server + probes | Relays on target circuit | Scales with network size |
| AS/IXP traffic correlation | 1–3 IXPs or large AS | Entry↔exit flow linkage | Up to 80% of circuits [3] |
| NetFlow flow-record correlation | Router NetFlow export | Source of anonymous flow | 81.6%, 5.5% FP [7] |

### 4.5 Mix networks: the road not taken by Tor

A Chaumian mix defeats every attack in the table by construction. Batching destroys inter-arrival signatures; reordering destroys sequence correlation; artificial delay defeats congestion oracles, which rely on latency *sensitivity*; cover traffic defeats volume analysis. The trilemma [2] explains why: mixes purchase strong anonymity by paying the latency and bandwidth costs Tor refuses to pay. Modern high-latency designs — Mixminion, Nym, Katzenpost with Poisson mixing and Sphinx packets — accept delays of seconds to minutes and heavy cover traffic to sit at a different corner of the triangle. **Tor and mixnets are not competitors but different optimal points on the same provable tradeoff surface.**

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now sketch the trilemma's proof structure, following Das et al. [2], to make precise why Tor's vulnerability is structural. Consider a challenge game: the adversary picks two users *u₀, u₁*; the challenger picks a random bit *b* and has *u_b* send a message; the adversary observes all network traffic (global passive) and guesses *b*. **Strong anonymity** means the adversary's advantage is negligible in the security parameter.

Fix a protocol with latency bound ℓ (every message delivered within ℓ rounds) and bandwidth overhead β (at most βN noise messages per round for *N* users). The proof proceeds by an invariant over the set *T* of messages that could plausibly be the challenge message: any message sent by *u_{1−b}* in a round before *r − ℓ* (where *r* is the delivery round) cannot influence *T*, because the challenge message itself cannot be older than ℓ rounds when delivered. The adversary therefore only needs to consider messages from a sliding window of ℓ rounds. A counting argument then shows that if *2β < 1 − δ(η)* — i.e., the noise budget is insufficient to drown the challenge message in the window — the adversary can identify the challenge message's path through honest nodes with non-negligible advantage [2]. Formally:

> **Theorem: (Trilemma core).** For user distribution *U_B*, if the latency bound ℓ ∈ O(1) and bandwidth overhead β is constant, no protocol achieves strong anonymity. More generally, strong anonymity requires that *ℓ·β* exceed a threshold determined by the adversary's compromise budget [2].

Tor sits at small ℓ (sub-second) and β ≈ 0 (no systematic cover traffic): it maximally violates the necessary condition, offering only *probabilistic, diversity-based* anonymity — "the adversary probably can't watch both ends" — rather than a mixnet's *provable* anonymity.

The empirical literature corroborates the theory's predictions. Johnson et al.'s TorPS simulator, combining real Tor consensus data with AS-path inference, showed that a small number of compromised high-bandwidth relays plus IXP-level observation could de-anonymize up to **80% of circuit types within six months** [3]. The NetFlow study of [7] demonstrated **81.6% accuracy** with 5.5% false positives using only sampled flow records — no packet payloads, no timing finer than flow export. Murdoch and Zieliński extended the original congestion oracle to IXP adversaries, showing correlation possible even at packet sampling rates as low as 1:2000 [7]. And Sun et al.'s RAPTOR work showed that the *dynamic* nature of inter-domain routing — asymmetric paths, BGP churn — makes AS-level adversaries strictly stronger than the static models assumed in earlier work [3]. Each result is a different measurement of the same underlying fact: **in a low-latency system, the traffic's shape survives, and shape is identity.**

Defenses are partial. Tor's *adaptive padding* (WTF-PAD) injects dummy cells against website fingerprinting, but padding without delay cannot defeat inter-arrival-time correlation. Proposed countermeasures — constant-rate cover traffic, traffic morphing, multi-path routing — all move along the trilemma's edges, buying anonymity with bandwidth or latency exactly as the theorem predicts. None achieves strong anonymity at Tor's performance envelope, because the theorem says none can.

## 6 Limitations

First, the trilemma applies to a **global passive** adversary; real adversaries are weaker (partial observation) but also *active* — able to inject, delay, or drop traffic — and active attacks like congestion induction can exceed the passive model. Second, we assume standard Tor path selection; ongoing work on congestion-aware routing, Conflux multi-path circuits, and artificial-latency defenses changes the quantities, not the qualitative picture. Third, **endpoint security** is out of scope: Tor Browser fingerprinting defenses and operational-security failures dominate real-world de-anonymization statistics. Fourth, cited accuracies are laboratory or simulation results; live-network churn makes fielded accuracy lower and harder to measure ethically. Finally, "strong anonymity" is a demanding negligible-advantage definition; Tor's *practical* anonymity against resource-bounded adversaries without global vantage remains substantial, and is the right frame for most users' threat models.

## 7 Conclusion

Tor's telescoping circuit construction, ntor handshake, and layered relay-cell encryption constitute a mature, carefully engineered answer to the problem of *low-latency* anonymous communication: they hide routing information from every participant, provide forward secrecy against future compromise, and resist the predecessor attack through long-lived entry guards. Yet the system's residual vulnerabilities — guard discovery via circuit fingerprinting, end-to-end correlation by AS- and IXP-level observers, and congestion oracles — are not bugs to be patched but consequences of a proved impossibility. The anonymity trilemma [2] establishes that strong anonymity, low bandwidth overhead, and low latency cannot coexist; Tor's choice of the latter two is what makes it usable, and what makes it correlatable.

For interactive anonymity against realistic (non-global) adversaries, Tor remains the best-deployed option, and its defenses — guards, padding, path diversity — meaningfully raise the attacker's cost. For threat models including global passive observation, or metadata that is existentially sensitive, the mathematics points to high-latency mix networks that pay the trilemma's price in full. Understanding *which* corner of the triangle you stand in is the beginning of operational security; this thesis has mapped the triangle.
