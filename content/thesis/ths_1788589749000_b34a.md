# Register Allocation for Modern Compilers: From Chaitin's Graph Coloring through Linear Scan and PBQP to SSA-Based Optimal Allocation and Spill Code Generation in LLVM and GCC

## Abstract

Register allocation — mapping unbounded virtual registers onto the *k* physical registers of a target — remains one of the most consequential compiler backend phases: poor allocation inserts loads and stores into hot loops, while the underlying problems (coloring, spill minimization, coalescing) are NP-complete. This thesis unifies the four dominant algorithmic families: Chaitin-style **graph coloring** with simplify/select and iterated coalescing [1][2]; **linear scan** over live intervals, the JIT workhorse [3]; **Partitioned Boolean Quadratic Programming (PBQP)**, a cost-model formulation for irregular architectures [4]; and **SSA-based allocation**, exploiting chordal SSA interference graphs for polynomial-time optimal coloring [5]. Each family is grounded in its formal model with algorithms and worked examples, followed by complexity and empirical comparison, and a detailed account of production spill code generation: LLVM's greedy allocator with live-range splitting and rematerialization [6], and GCC's two-stage IRA/LRA pipeline [7].

## 1. Introduction

A processor exposes only a handful of general-purpose registers — sixteen on x86-64, thirty-one on AArch64 — while a function's IR may contain thousands of simultaneously live values. **Register allocation** decides which values reside in registers at each program point; **spilling** evicts the rest to stack slots at the price of explicit stores and reloads. Since memory is one to two orders of magnitude slower than registers, these decisions directly determine generated-code performance, especially inside loops.

The problem is deceptively hard. Chaitin showed that for *every* undirected graph there exists a program whose interference graph is exactly that graph, so allocation inherits the NP-completeness of graph coloring [1]. Spill minimization and copy coalescing are likewise NP-complete, which means production compilers live entirely in the world of heuristics. The central surprise of the last two decades is that one special case — programs in **static single assignment (SSA) form** — admits provably optimal coloring in polynomial time, because SSA interference graphs are chordal [5].

The remainder of this thesis develops each family formally (Sections 2-4), compares complexity and empirical results (Section 5), and discusses limitations (Section 6) before concluding (Section 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The field lay largely stagnant until 1981, when Gregory Chaitin and colleagues at IBM published **"Register Allocation via Coloring"** [1]: build an *interference graph* whose nodes are live ranges and whose edges connect simultaneously-live ranges, then *k*-color it. Chaitin's 1982 follow-up added the simplify/spill/select loop with a cost-per-degree spill heuristic [1] — the first scheme demonstrated to approach hand-written assembly quality.

The graph-coloring line was refined through the 1990s. Briggs, Cooper, and Torczon introduced **optimistic coloring** and a conservative coalescing test that made copy elimination safe without endangering colorability [1]. George and Appel's **iterated register coalescing** (1996) interleaved simplification and coalescing on worklists, removing the phase-ordering pathology of earlier designs [2]. Traub, Lucas, and Hicks (1998) developed **second-chance binpacking**, a linear-scan variant trading compile time for quality [3].

A different pressure — compile *time* — produced **linear scan** allocation. Poletto and Sarkar (1999) proposed allocating registers to live *intervals* in a single linear pass for dynamic and just-in-time compilers that could not afford graph coloring's quadratic behavior [3]. Linear scan runs several times faster at a modest quality cost and became the standard JIT choice (HotSpot's client compiler, LLVM's `-O0` "fast" allocator).

The third family addresses **irregular architectures** — DSPs with small, aliased register files where coloring's uniform-register assumption breaks down. Scholz and Eckstein (2002) formulated allocation as **Partitioned Boolean Quadratic Programming**: each symbolic register chooses among physical registers or spilling via boolean variables, with interference and coalescing as quadratic cost matrices solved by graph-reduction rules plus heuristics [4]; LLVM shipped this as the `RegAllocPBQP` allocator option.

The fourth family exploits program structure: around 2005–2006, Hack, Brisk, and Bouchez–Darte–Rastello independently proved that interference graphs of **SSA-form** programs are chordal, hence optimally colorable in linear time via perfect elimination orderings. Hack, Grund, and Goos (2006) built a complete allocator on this insight, fully decoupling coloring, spilling, and coalescing [5].

In production, **LLVM** offers four allocators: the fast linear-scan allocator for `-O0`, the basic allocator, the PBQP allocator for irregular targets, and the default **greedy** allocator — a global, splitting-based design that dominates `-O2` compilation [6]. **GCC** migrated from classic graph coloring to **IRA** (Integrated Register Allocator, region-based, since GCC 4.4) followed by **LRA** (Local Register Allocator, since GCC 4.8), which performs final assignment and reload generation [7].

## 3. Methodology

We fix a control-flow graph (CFG) whose nodes are instructions and consider a function already converted to a low-level IR with an unbounded set of **virtual registers**. A virtual register *v* is **live** at program point *p* if it holds a value that may be read on some path from *p* before being overwritten, computed by the standard backward dataflow equations:

> **Definition (Liveness).** For instruction *n* with successors *succ(n)*, definitions *def(n)* and uses *use(n)*:
> `live_in(n) = use(n) ∪ (live_out(n) ∖ def(n))`, `live_out(n) = ⋃_{s ∈ succ(n)} live_in(s)`.

Two live ranges **interfere** if simultaneously live at some program point. The **interference graph** *G = (V, E)* has one node per live range and an edge per interfering pair; a valid allocation is a *k*-coloring, and uncolorable nodes are **spilled** to stack slots. A **live interval** *[start, end)* linearizes a live range onto a total order of program points; linear scan operates on intervals rather than graphs, trading precision for speed.

The problem formulations used in this thesis:

| Formulation | Decision object | Objective | Complexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Graph coloring | Color per node | Minimize spill cost s.t. *k*-colorable | NP-complete [1] |
| Linear scan | Register per interval | Greedy, start-order | *O(n log n)* [3] |
| PBQP | Boolean vector per variable | Minimize quadratic cost | NP-hard; heuristic reductions [4] |
| SSA chordal | Color per SSA value | Exact min colors | Polynomial, *O(k·\|V\|)* [5] |

**Spill cost** estimation is itself a heuristic art. The classical metric, due to Chaitin, weights each definition and use by estimated execution frequency (typically *10^loop-nesting-depth*) and divides by the node's degree, on the intuition that spilling a low-degree node most cheaply restores colorability [1].

## 4. Deep Technical Dive

### 4.1 Chaitin's Graph Coloring: Simplify, Spill, Select

Chaitin's algorithm iterates four phases — **build**, **simplify**, **spill**, **select** — until the graph is colored [1]:

1. **Build.** Compute liveness, construct the interference graph, and add *move edges* between copy source and destination for later coalescing.
2. **Simplify.** Repeatedly remove a node of degree *< k*, pushing it onto a stack. Such a node is *guaranteed* colorable: when popped, at most *k−1* neighbors are colored, so a free color exists.
3. **Spill.** If no node has degree *< k*, remove a candidate minimizing *cost/degree* and continue. Spilling is optimistic — the node may still be colored during select.
4. **Select.** Pop nodes, assigning each the lowest free color. A marked node with no free color is *actually spilled*: spill code is inserted and the graph rebuilt.

```c
/* Chaitin simplify/select with optimistic spilling */
void allocate(Graph *g, int k) {
    Stack *s = new_stack();
    Node *n;
    while ((n = find_deg_lt(g, k)) || (n = spill_candidate(g))) {
        push(s, n); remove(g, n);   /* spill_candidate: min cost/degree */
    }
    while ((n = pop(s))) {
        int c = lowest_free_color(n, k);
        if (c < 0) spill_node(n); else assign_color(n, c);
    }
}
```

> **Theorem (Chaitin [1]).** For every undirected graph *G* there exists a program whose interference graph is exactly *G*. Hence *k*-coloring–based register allocation is NP-complete.

Briggs's **optimistic coloring** defers the spill decision until select actually fails, since optimistically-removed nodes frequently prove colorable [1]. **Coalescing** merges copy endpoints to eliminate moves, but aggressive coalescing can render a colorable graph uncolorable; the safe rules are:

- **Briggs's criterion:** coalesce *u, v* only if the merged node has fewer than *k* neighbors of *significant* degree (degree ≥ *k*).
- **George's criterion:** coalesce if every significant-degree neighbor of *u* already interferes with *v* (or vice versa).

**Iterated register coalescing** [2] replaces the rigid build–coalesce–color pipeline with a worklist algorithm cycling through *simplify*, *coalesce*, *freeze* (abandon coalescing for a node so it can be simplified), and *spill* phases until fixpoint, eliminating the phase-ordering problem where premature coalescing caused unnecessary spills.

### 4.2 Linear Scan: Intervals, Active Sets, and Spilling

Linear scan discards the graph [3]. Given live intervals sorted by start point, it sweeps once:

```python
def linear_scan(intervals, num_regs):
    active = []          # intervals live now, sorted by end point
    free = set(range(num_regs))
    for i in sorted(intervals, key=lambda x: x.start):
        # expire intervals that ended before i starts
        for j in [a for a in active if a.end <= i.start]:
            active.remove(j); free.add(reg[j])
        if len(active) == num_regs:
            spill_at_interval(i, active)   # spill farthest-ending interval
        else:
            r = free.pop(); reg[i] = r
            active.append(i)               # keep sorted by end
```

**`ExpireOldIntervals`** frees registers of ended intervals; **`SpillAtInterval`** spills the interval (among active ∪ {*i*}) with the *farthest* end point — a one-dimensional analogue of Belady's optimal page-replacement rule.

The naïve formulation above ignores **lifetime holes**: an interval may be dead across a call, and smarter allocators track *inactive* intervals — not live at the current point but live later — freeing their registers temporarily. **Interval splitting** goes further: when pressure is high only in a subrange, the interval is split and only the middle segment spilled, keeping the outer segments in registers.

| Allocator | Compile time | Code quality (vs. iterated coalescing) | Use case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Iterated coalescing [2] | Baseline (slowest) | 100% (reference) | Ahead-of-time `-O2` |
| Linear scan [3] | Several × faster | Within ~12% on most benchmarks | JIT, `-O0` |
| Second-chance binpacking | Between the two | Recovers much of the gap | Balanced JIT |

Poletto and Sarkar's evaluation is the empirical anchor of this family: their linear scan ran several times faster than even a *fast, non-coalescing* graph-coloring allocator, yet produced code within 12% of an aggressive iterated-coalescing allocator on all but two benchmarks — while a naive "top-*k* by usage count" allocator was several times slower at runtime [3]. *Liveness structure matters far more than usage frequency*, and a single ordered sweep captures most of it.

### 4.3 PBQP: A Boolean Quadratic Formulation

Graph coloring assumes interchangeable registers; on irregular architectures — DSPs with tiny aliased files, x86's `%eax`-constrained divide, 64-bit register pairs — they are not. **Partitioned Boolean Quadratic Programming** [4] replaces coloring with a cost model expressing arbitrary architectural constraints. For each symbolic register *v*, introduce a boolean vector *x_v ∈ {0,1}^{m+1}* over *m* physical registers plus a spill option, subject to:

```
x_sp + x_R1 + x_R2 + ... + x_Rm = 1        (exactly one choice per variable)
```

Each variable carries a **cost vector** *c_v* (spill cost; register biases), and each interfering pair *(u,v)* a **cost matrix** *C_{uv}* pricing the joint assignment. The objective is:

```
min   Σ_v  c_vᵀ x_v  +  Σ_{(u,v) ∈ E}  x_uᵀ C_{uv} x_v
```

Interference is encoded by placing **∞** on the diagonal entries *(R_i, R_i)* of *C_{uv}*: assigning two interfering variables the same register has infinite cost and is never chosen. Coalescing becomes a *negative* cost (a benefit) on *(R_i, R_i)* for copy-related pairs. Spilling selects the spill entry, paying the spill cost from *c_v*. Irregular constraints — "these variables must occupy an aligned register pair", "this operand must be `%eax`" — are additional matrix entries, which is why PBQP shines precisely where coloring needs hand-tuned special cases.

Solving PBQP exactly is NP-hard, but the *partitioned* structure admits **reduction rules** applied before any search [4]: **R0** removes edgeless variables at minimum cost; **R1** *provably optimally* eliminates degree-1 variables by folding costs into the neighbor's vector; **R2** eliminates degree-2 variables by folding costs into the matrix between their neighbors.

Reduction proceeds until no rule applies; the (usually small) irreducible core is solved by branch-and-bound or a heuristic, and the reductions are undone. In practice the rules collapse the vast majority of real functions, which is why PBQP's exponential worst case rarely bites. LLVM's **`RegAllocPBQP`** builds the instance through a `PBQPBuilder` class that backends extend with custom constraints (register pairs, subregister aliasing) [4] — new constraints become *data* (matrix entries) rather than new heuristic code, which is why PBQP remains the fastest route to allocation on irregular targets.

### 4.4 SSA-Form Allocation: Chordal Interference Graphs and Optimal Coloring

The deepest theoretical result in register allocation concerns programs in **static single assignment (SSA) form**, where each variable is defined exactly once and control-flow merges use φ-functions. Around 2005, three groups independently proved the following [5]:

> **Theorem (Hack; Brisk; Bouchez–Darte–Rastello).** The interference graph of a *strict* SSA program is **chordal**: every cycle of length greater than three has a chord.

Chordal graphs admit a **perfect elimination ordering (PEO)** — an ordering in which each vertex's later neighbors form a clique — and are *perfect*: chromatic number equals clique number *ω(G)*, so greedy PEO coloring is optimal. For SSA programs the dominance relation induces a PEO directly (a post-order walk of the dominance tree), so the interference graph is never built explicitly. Coloring runs in *O(k·|V|)* and uses *exactly* the register pressure — the maximum simultaneously-live count, a lower bound that is here achievable [5]. Once spilling lowers pressure below *k*, coloring introduces *no further spills*.

```python
def ssa_color(values_in_dominance_postorder, k, interferes):
    color = {}
    for v in values_in_dominance_postorder:   # a PEO of the chordal IG
        used = {color[u] for u in interferes(v) if u in color}
        # |used| < k is guaranteed once pressure <= k
        color[v] = min(c for c in range(k) if c not in used)
    return color
```

φ-functions require care: they are *parallel copies* on control-flow edges, representing register permutations between predecessor blocks and the join block. After coloring, SSA is **deconstructed** — φ-functions lowered to copies, with permutation cycles broken by temporaries or swaps; Pereira and Palsberg (2009) gave a *spill-free* deconstruction preserving the coloring [5].

Optimality of coloring does not imply optimality of the whole allocator: **spilling** (which values, at which points) and **coalescing** remain NP-complete even under SSA. But the SSA formulation *decouples* the three concerns — spill to reduce pressure, color optimally, coalesce afterward — replacing one entangled NP-complete problem with one polynomial core plus two smaller heuristic problems.

### 4.5 Spill Code Generation in Practice: LLVM's Greedy Allocator and GCC's IRA/LRA

Theory meets silicon in spill code generation: *where* loads and stores go, and how the allocator avoids drowning in them.

**LLVM's greedy allocator** (`RegAllocGreedy`, the `-O2` default [6]) is built around three ideas:

1. **Spill weights.** Every live range receives a weight from the estimated execution frequency of its uses and definitions. Allocation proceeds in decreasing weight order — important ranges first.
2. **Live-range splitting.** Instead of spilling a whole range, the allocator *splits* it around regions of high pressure: the range keeps a register where pressure is low and is spilled only inside hot regions, with copies at split boundaries. Splitting cascades, and is the primary reason greedy beats spill-everywhere designs.
3. **Rematerialization.** Values cheaper to *recompute* than reload — constants, stack addresses — are redefined at uses instead of spilled.

When eviction is unavoidable, greedy tries **last-chance recoloring** first, then emits spill code with **spill-slot coloring** (reusing non-overlapping slots) and shrink-wrapping of callee-saved restores.

**GCC** takes a two-stage approach [7]. **IRA** performs *regional* allocation — partitioning the function, computing per-region register costs, assigning each pseudo-register a hard register class or stack slot. **LRA** then generates *reloads* (spill code) instruction by instruction for operands left in memory or violating constraints, with reload inheritance and rematerialization. IRA optimizes globally; LRA guarantees correctness against operand constraints.

| Aspect | LLVM Greedy [6] | GCC IRA + LRA [7] |
|---|---|---|
| Granularity | Function-global, splitting-based | Regional (IRA), per-insn (LRA) |
| Spill decision | Weight-ordered eviction, split preferred | Cost-driven class assignment, reloads |
| Rematerialization | Yes, before spilling | Yes, via reload inheritance |
| Irregular constraints | PBQP allocator available | Constraint-driven reloads in LRA |
| Compile-time profile | Higher than fast, fine for `-O2` | IRA fast; LRA thorough |

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

**Coloring** a general interference graph is NP-complete (Chaitin [1]); **spill-cost minimization** and **copy coalescing** are NP-complete even under restrictions; but **coloring an SSA interference graph is polynomial** — greedy PEO coloring uses exactly *ω(G)* colors in *O(k·|V|)* time [5].

> **Theorem (Optimal SSA coloring).** Let *G* be the interference graph of a strict SSA program with register pressure *p ≤ k*. Greedy coloring in dominance post-order uses exactly *p* registers, which is optimal.

*Proof sketch.* Dominance post-order is a PEO of the chordal *G*: when *v* is colored, its colored neighbors form a clique of size ≤ *p − 1*, so a free color among *k ≥ p* always exists; *p* colors are necessary. ∎

Empirically, the families occupy distinct points on the compile-time/quality frontier:

- **Linear scan [3]:** several times faster than fast graph coloring; code within ~12% of iterated coalescing on all but two benchmarks.
- **PBQP [4]:** on irregular DSPs, the polynomial-time approximation performs close to exact solving (~3.9% vs. ~5.9% geometric-mean improvement over baseline) while modeling register pairs and aliasing that coloring handles only ad hoc.
- **LLVM greedy [6]:** the `-O2` default; live-range splitting and rematerialization sharply reduce spill traffic versus spill-everywhere designs.
- **GCC IRA/LRA [7]:** regional allocation scales to huge functions; LRA's constraint-driven reloads handle targets like x86.

*No single family dominates*: JITs and `-O0` choose linear scan for speed; `-O2` AOT compilers choose greedy or IRA/LRA for quality; irregular embedded targets choose PBQP for expressiveness; SSA chordality underwrites allocation directly on SSA IR.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

Serious limitations remain. **Coalescing versus colorability** is still managed by conservative heuristics; optimal coalescing is NP-complete, and the gap between heuristic and optimal copy elimination on real code is poorly quantified. **Spill placement granularity** — spill everywhere, split ranges, or partial spilling at individual points — interacts with instruction scheduling in ways no current allocator models jointly; integrated scheduling/allocation remains a research topic.

**Irregular register files** keep growing more irregular — x86-64's high-byte registers, AArch64 SVE predicates, GPU bank conflicts — which is PBQP's enduring niche. **Rematerialization** is underused: most allocators only rematerialize trivial constants.

**Compile-time budgets** in JITs cap allocator sophistication — WebAssembly and mobile JITs still use linear scan or simpler — while **machine-learned heuristics** (learned spill-cost models, learned eviction policies) have shown promise in research prototypes but have not yet displaced hand-tuned weights in production. Finally, **verified compilation** (à la CompCert) validates rather than reimplements allocation, because proving a heuristic allocator correct remains out of reach; the chordal SSA results are the closest the field has come to a *provably* well-behaved core.

## 7. Conclusion

Register allocation has traveled from Chaitin's 1981 insight — *allocation is coloring* [1] — through the pragmatic sweep of linear scan [3], the cost-model expressiveness of PBQP [4], to the structural elegance of SSA chordality and polynomial-time optimal coloring [5]. Each family answers a different question: coloring asks how to decide well, linear scan asks how to decide fast, PBQP asks how to decide under arbitrary constraints, and SSA allocation asks when the decision is easy. Production systems — LLVM's greedy allocator [6], GCC's IRA/LRA pipeline [7] — are hybrids borrowing from all four, because real compilers must simultaneously be fast, produce excellent code, and target irregular hardware. The field's central open challenge is unchanged since 1981: spilling and coalescing remain NP-complete, and every allocator is, at bottom, a carefully engineered bet about which heuristic loses least.
