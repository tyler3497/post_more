# Gromov–Witten Invariants: Quantum Cohomology, Virtual Fundamental Classes, and Mirror Predictions

## Abstract

Gromov–Witten invariants are rational intersection numbers on moduli spaces of stable maps that rigidly encode the enumerative geometry of holomorphic curves in a smooth projective variety. This article develops the theory from first principles: Kontsevich's compactification of the space of maps by nodal stable maps, the Behrend–Fantechi virtual fundamental class that supplies a cycle of the expected dimension when the moduli space is singular, and the Kontsevich–Manin axioms that organize the invariants into a cohomological field theory. We explain how genus-zero invariants assemble into the *quantum cohomology ring*, a deformation of ordinary cohomology whose associativity is the WDVV system of partial differential equations, and we trace the route from these equations through the mirror theorems of Givental and others to the celebrated predictions for rational curves on the quintic threefold. Tables of instanton numbers, proof sketches, and the theory's current limitations complete the exposition.

##1 Introduction

Classical enumerative geometry asked questions of the form: *how many* geometric objects of a given type satisfy prescribed incidence conditions? Schubert's calculus answered such questions for linear spaces, and the nineteenth century produced isolated counts of curves — for instance, the 27 lines on a smooth cubic surface, or the 2,875 lines on a smooth quintic threefold in projective four-space. These classical counts were obtained by ad hoc geometric arguments, and each new problem demanded a new construction.

The modern reformulation, initiated by physicists studying topological sigma models and the A-model of string theory, replaces ad hoc geometry with *intersection theory on a compact moduli space*. The Gromov–Witten invariant

$$\langle \gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_n \rangle_{g,n,\beta} = \int_{[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)]^{\mathrm{vir}}} \mathrm{ev}_1^*(\gamma_1) \smile \cdots \smile \mathrm{ev}_n^*(\gamma_n)$$

is the integral of pulled-back cohomology classes against a *virtual fundamental class* of the moduli stack of stable maps [7]. When the moduli space happens to be smooth of the expected dimension and the incidence conditions are generic, this rational number is literally the number of curves; in general it is a subtler invariant that continues to satisfy all the formal properties a curve count ought to satisfy [1].

Three developments elevated these numbers from a curiosity to a central object of modern geometry:

1. **Axiomatization.** Kontsevich and Manin isolated a complete list of axioms — the Kontsevich–Manin axioms — governing the behavior of the invariants under gluing, forgetting marked points, and degenerating the domain curve. These axioms assemble the invariants into a *cohomological field theory* (CohFT) [1].
2. **Virtual techniques.** Behrend and Fantechi constructed the intrinsic normal cone and the virtual fundamental class from a perfect obstruction theory, making the invariants well defined for arbitrary smooth projective targets in arbitrary genus [2], with the algebraic construction for stable maps carried out by Behrend [6].
3. **Mirror symmetry.** The genus-zero invariants were predicted, in spectacular cases, by period integrals on a *mirror* family of varieties. The prediction of all rational-curve counts on the quintic threefold by Candelas, de la Ossa, Green, and Parkes — later proved in the toric setting by Givental and by Lian, Liu, and Yau — remains the most famous application of the theory [4][5].

> **Theorem (Kontsevich–Manin reconstruction):** For a smooth projective variety, the entire tree-level (genus-zero) system of Gromov–Witten invariants is determined by the three-point invariants, and these are equivalent to the structure constants of an associative, commutative deformation of the cohomology ring — the *quantum cohomology* [1].

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews stable maps and their moduli. Section 3 describes the methodology: the definition of the invariants and the axiom system. Section 4 develops the four pillars of the theory — quantum cohomology and the WDVV equations, virtual fundamental classes, cohomological field theories, and mirror symmetry. Section 5 presents concrete numerical results and proof sketches, Section 6 the limitations, and Section 7 the conclusion.

##2 Background

### Stable maps and their moduli

Let $X$ be a smooth complex projective variety and $\beta \in H_2(X,\mathbb{Z})$ a curve class. A *stable map* of genus $g$ with $n$ marked points and class $\beta$ is a morphism $f: (C, p_1, \dots, p_n) \to X$ from a connected nodal curve $C$ of arithmetic genus $g$ with $n$ distinct smooth marked points, such that $f_*[C] = \beta$ and every contracted irreducible component of $C$ carries at least three special points (nodes or markings) — the stability condition, which guarantees a finite automorphism group [1].

Kontsevich's insight was that allowing the domain to acquire nodes *compactifies* the space of maps: the resulting moduli stack $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)$ is a proper Deligne–Mumford stack. It carries:

- **Evaluation maps** $\mathrm{ev}_i: \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta) \to X$ recording $f(p_i)$;
- **A forgetful map** $\pi: \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta) \to \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}$ to the Deligne–Mumford space of stable curves, forgetting the map and stabilizing the domain [1][7].

The *expected* or *virtual dimension* of the moduli stack is the index of the deformation complex of a map:

$$\mathrm{vd} = c_1(T_X)\cdot\beta + (\dim X - 3)(1-g) + n,$$

the Riemann–Roch count: deformations of the map contribute $c_1(T_X)\cdot\beta + \dim X\,(1-g)$, and deformations of the pointed domain contribute $3g - 3 + n$ [7]. When $X$ is *convex* — meaning $H^1(\mathbb{P}^1, f^*T_X) = 0$ for every genus-zero map, as holds for projective spaces, Grassmannians, and other homogeneous spaces — the genus-zero moduli space is smooth of exactly this dimension and ordinary intersection theory applies [6]. For general targets (notably Calabi–Yau threefolds), the moduli space is singular, reducible, and of the wrong dimension, and one must work *virtually*.

### Quantum cohomology: first glance

Fix a homogeneous basis $T_0 = 1, T_1, \dots, T_m$ of $H^*(X,\mathbb{Q})$ with Poincaré-dual basis $T^0, \dots, T^m$. The *quantum product* is defined on $QH^*(X) = H^*(X,\mathbb{Q}) \otimes \Lambda$, where $\Lambda$ is the Novikov ring tracking curve classes, by

$$T_i \star T_j = \sum_{\beta} \sum_{a} \langle T_i, T_j, T_a \rangle_{0,3,\beta}\, q^{\beta}\, T^a.$$

The three-point genus-zero invariants thus become the structure constants of a new ring. The deep fact — the content of the WDVV equations — is that this product is *associative*, and hence $QH^*(X)$ is a genuine commutative ring deforming ordinary cohomology [1].

##3 Methodology

The methodology of Gromov–Witten theory has three layers: **construction** of the invariants, **axiomatization** of their formal properties, and **computation** via the differential equations those axioms imply.

**Construction.** Given the proper stack $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)$, choose the virtual fundamental class $[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)]^{\mathrm{vir}} \in H_{2\,\mathrm{vd}}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta),\mathbb{Q})$ supplied by the perfect obstruction theory of the moduli problem [2][6]. Pairing pulled-back classes against this cycle yields the invariants. In the symplectic category, an equivalent construction via virtual moduli cycles was developed by Li–Tian and by Fukaya–Ono, and the resulting numbers agree with the algebraic ones [7].

**Axiomatization.** Kontsevich and Manin proved that the invariants satisfy the following axiom system, which we summarize in compact form [1]:

| Axiom | Statement |
|---|---|
| *Effectivity* | The invariant vanishes unless $\beta$ is effective (or zero) |
| *$S_n$-covariance* | Symmetric under permutation of the marked points |
| *Grading* | Vanishes unless $\sum \mathrm{codim}(\gamma_i) = \mathrm{vd}$ |
| *Fundamental class* | Inserting $\gamma_n = 1$ equals the pullback of the invariant with the point forgotten |
| *Divisor* | Inserting a divisor $D$ multiplies by the intersection number $D\cdot\beta$ |
| *Mapping to point* | For $\beta = 0$, reduces to classical intersection numbers on $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}$ |
| *Splitting* | Restriction to a boundary divisor with a separating node factors as a sum over intermediate states |
| *Genus reduction* | Restriction to a non-separating node contracts a loop with a sum over intermediate states |

The splitting and genus-reduction axioms are the engine of the theory: they express the behavior of the virtual class under the gluing maps

$$\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_1,n_1+1}(X,\beta_1) \times_X \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_2,n_2+1}(X,\beta_2) \longrightarrow \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_1+g_2,n_1+n_2}(X,\beta_1+\beta_2)$$

as a sum over a basis and its Poincaré dual, $\sum_a \langle \cdots T_a \rangle \langle T^a \cdots \rangle$ [1].

**Computation.** The axioms are not merely formal: applied to the geometry of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,4} \cong \mathbb{P}^1$, the splitting axiom yields the *WDVV equations*, a system of third-order PDEs for the genus-zero potential. For Fano varieties these equations essentially determine all invariants from finitely many initial data [1]. For Calabi–Yau targets the WDVV system is supplemented by *mirror symmetry*, which computes the potential from period integrals [4][5].

Recent work extends the methodology beyond ordinary cohomology: Abouzaid, McLean, and Smith construct Gromov–Witten-type invariants valued in complex-oriented generalized cohomology theories — including complex $K$-theory and Morava $K$-theories — satisfying analogues of the Kontsevich–Manin axioms, with global Kuranishi charts supplying the virtual geometry on the symplectic side [3].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Quantum Cohomology and the WDVV Equations

Let $F_0(t) = \sum_{n,\beta} \frac{1}{n!} \langle T,\dots,T \rangle_{0,n,\beta}\, q^{\beta}$ be the genus-zero Gromov–Witten potential, where $T = \sum t^i T_i$. The third derivatives $F_{ijk} = \partial_i\partial_j\partial_k F_0$ are the three-point functions. The **WDVV equations** assert

$$\sum_{a,b} F_{ija}\,\eta^{ab}\,F_{bkl} = \sum_{a,b} F_{ika}\,\eta^{ab}\,F_{bjl}\qquad\text{for all }i,j,k,l,$$

where $\eta$ is the Poincaré pairing. The left side computes $(T_i \star T_j) \star T_k$ paired against $T_l$; the right side computes $T_i \star (T_j \star T_k)$ paired against $T_l$. Hence WDVV is exactly **associativity of the quantum product** [1].

The geometric origin is transparent. The forgetful map $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}(X,\beta) \to \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,4}$ pulls back the linear equivalence of the two boundary divisors $D(12|34) \sim D(13|24)$ on $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,4} \cong \mathbb{P}^1$.

For $X = \mathbb{P}^2$, the WDVV equations collapse to **Kontsevich's recursion** for $N_d$, the number of degree-$d$ rational plane curves through $3d-1$ general points:

```python
from math import comb

def kontsevich_numbers(D):
    """N_d = number of rational degree-d plane curves through 3d-1 points."""
    N = {1: 1}
    for d in range(2, D + 1):
        total = 0
        for d1 in range(1, d):
            d2 = d - d1
            total += N[d1] * N[d2] * (
                d1**2 * d2**2 * comb(3*d - 4, 3*d1 - 2)
                - d1**3 * d2 * comb(3*d - 4, 3*d1 - 1)
            )
        N[d] = total
    return N

print(kontsevich_numbers(6))
# {1: 1, 2: 1, 3: 12, 4: 620, 5: 87304, 6: 26312976}
```

The recursion determines every $N_d$ from $N_1 = 1$ alone — a striking demonstration that the axioms, not geometry, do the enumerative work [1].

The genus-zero potential also equips $H^*(X)$ with the structure of a **Frobenius manifold**: the metric is the Poincaré pairing, the product is $\star_t$ (the big quantum product at parameter $t$), and flatness of the Dubrovin connection

$$\hbar\,\nabla_{\partial_i} = \hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t^i} - T_i \star_t$$

is equivalent to the WDVV system [1][5].

### 4.2 Virtual Fundamental Classes

When $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)$ fails to be smooth of the expected dimension, Behrend and Fantechi's machinery supplies a substitute cycle.

1. **The intrinsic normal cone.** To any Deligne–Mumford stack $M$ one attaches a purely zero-dimensional cone stack $\mathfrak{C}_M$, the *intrinsic normal cone*, which records the singularities of $M$ in a canonical way. It is constructed by gluing the normal cones of local embeddings into smooth stacks [2].
2. **The obstruction theory.** A *perfect obstruction theory* is a morphism $\phi: E^\bullet \to \mathbb{L}_M$ in the derived category, with $E^\bullet$ a two-term complex of vector bundles, inducing an isomorphism on $h^0$ and a surjection on $h^1$ of the cotangent complex. It embeds $\mathfrak{C}_M$ into the vector-bundle stack $\mathfrak{E} = h^1/h^0((E^\bullet)^\vee)$, and the **virtual fundamental class** is the refined intersection with the zero section:

$$[M, \phi]^{\mathrm{vir}} = 0^!_{\mathfrak{E}}[\mathfrak{C}_M] \in A_{\mathrm{vd}}(M).$$

> **Theorem (Behrend–Fantechi):** A perfect obstruction theory of virtual rank $r$ determines a virtual fundamental class in the Chow group of pure dimension $r$, compatible with smooth pullback, flat pullback, and Gysin maps in the relative setting [2].

For stable maps, the obstruction theory arises from the deformation theory of the map: the complex $R\pi_*(f^*T_X)^\vee$ relative to the stack of prestable curves, where $\pi$ is the universal curve and $f$ the universal map, is perfect of the correct rank.

A crucial subtlety: Gromov–Witten invariants are *rational* numbers, not integers, because the moduli stack has generic stabilizers and the gluing formulas involve division by automorphism orders.

### 4.3 Cohomological Field Theories

Kontsevich and Manin reformulated the axiom system as an algebraic structure.

$$I_{g,n}: H^{\otimes n} \longrightarrow H^*(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})$$

satisfying $S_n$-invariance and compatibility with the gluing maps of Deligne–Mumford spaces: pulling back along a separating gluing map inserts the Casimir element $\sum_a T_a \otimes T^a$, and similarly for the non-separating gluing [1].

```haskell
-- Sketch of the CohFT structure carried by Gromov-Witten theory
class CohFT h where
  pairing     :: h -> h -> Rational            -- Poincare pairing eta
  correlators :: Int -> Int -> [h] -> CohomologyClass  -- I_{g,n}
  -- Axioms:
  --   symmetricGroup : correlators invariant under permuting inputs
  --   separatingGlue : pullback along gluing = sum over Casimir insertions
  --   loopGlue       : non-separating gluing contracts with Casimir

data GWTheory = GW { target :: SmoothProjectiveVariety }

instance CohFT GWTheory where
  pairing = poincarePairing
  correlators g n inputs = pushforward virtualClass (evPullbacks inputs)
```

A formal consequence of the CohFT axioms is that the genus-zero sector is equivalent to a Frobenius manifold, and the higher-genus theory is governed by tautological relations on $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}$ [1].

### 4.4 Mirror Symmetry and the Quintic Threefold

The most dramatic application of genus-zero Gromov–Witten theory is the mirror prediction for the quintic threefold $X \subset \mathbb{P}^4$, a Calabi–Yau threefold with $\chi(X) = -200$, $h^{1,1} = 1$, and $h^{2,1} = 101$.

$$\mathcal{L}\, w = 0, \qquad \mathcal{L} = \theta^4 - 5z(5\theta+1)(5\theta+2)(5\theta+3)(5\theta+4),$$

with $\theta = z\frac{d}{dz}$, annihilating the hypergeometric series $w_0(z) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{(5n)!}{(n!)^5} z^n$ [4].

Morrison's formulation of the mirror procedure runs as follows [4]:

1. Compute a basis of solutions $w_0, w_1, w_2, w_3$ of the Picard–Fuchs equation near the large-complex-structure limit.
2. Form the **mirror map** $t = w_1/w_0$ and the canonical coordinate $q = e^t$.
3. The normalized Yukawa coupling $\kappa_{ttt} = \frac{1}{w_0^2}\int_{Y} \Omega \wedge \nabla_t^3 \Omega$ expands as

$$\kappa_{ttt} = 5 + \sum_{d \ge 1} n_d\, d^3 \frac{q^d}{1-q^d},$$

where the integers $n_d$ are the predicted numbers of degree-$d$ rational curves on the quintic.

The first values stunned the enumerative community: $n_1 = 2875$ (the classical count of lines), $n_2 = 609250$, $n_3 = 317206375$ — each subsequently verified by independent algebro-geometric computations [4].

**Givental's mirror theorem** made this rigorous and vastly more general. For a non-negative complete intersection in a symplectic toric manifold, Givental proved that the solutions of the PDE system describing quantum cohomology — the $J$-function encoding all genus-zero descendants — are expressed by explicit hypergeometric series, the $I$-function. The proof uses torus localization on *graph spaces* (moduli of stable maps to $X \times \mathbb{P}^1$) to identify the Lagrangian cone swept out by the $J$-function [5]. This established the mirror prediction for the quintic and for broad classes of Fano complete intersections, and it remains the template for all subsequent mirror theorems.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### The quintic instanton numbers

The mirror computation yields the following genus-zero Gopakumar–Vafa invariants for the quintic threefold [4]:

| Degree $d$ | $n_d$ (rational curves) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 2,875 |
| 2 | 609,250 |
| 3 | 317,206,375 |
| 4 | 242,467,530,000 |
| 5 | 229,305,888,887,625 |

Each entry was an *a priori* prediction: the first three were confirmed by classical intersection theory on Hilbert schemes of curves, and the higher ones by virtual localization computations on the moduli of stable maps [4][5].

### Proof sketch: splitting implies WDVV

We sketch the central deduction, following Kontsevich–Manin [1]:

1. On $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,4} \cong \mathbb{P}^1$, the boundary divisors $D(12|34)$ and $D(13|24)$ are linearly equivalent as points.
2. The forgetful morphism $F: \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}(X,\beta) \to \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,4}$ pulls this relation back to a relation among boundary divisors of the mapping space.
3. Intersecting $[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}(X,\beta)]^{\mathrm{vir}}$ with $F^*D(12|34)$ and applying the **splitting axiom** expresses the intersection as a sum over splittings $\beta = \beta_1 + \beta_2$ and intermediate states $\sum_a \langle T_i, T_j, T_a \rangle \langle T^a, T_k, T_l \rangle$.
4. Doing the same for $D(13|24)$ gives the other side; linear equivalence of the divisors yields the WDVV identity.

The same pattern — *a tautological relation on the Deligne–Mumford space, pulled back and combined with the splitting axiom* — produces all universal equations of Gromov–Witten theory, including the topological recursion relations and Getzler's genus-one relation [1].

### Proof sketch: Givental's mirror theorem

Givental's argument for toric complete intersections proceeds through [5]:

1. **Graph spaces.** Consider $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}(X \times \mathbb{P}^1, (\beta, d))$ with a $\mathbb{C}^*$-action rotating the $\mathbb{P}^1$ factor. Fixed loci are described by maps constant on the parametrized component.
2. **Localization.** The equivariant $J$-function is computed by virtual localization; the contributions factorize over the fixed loci.
3. **Polynomiality.** A delicate analysis shows the $J$-function lies on an explicit Lagrangian cone $\mathcal{L} \subset \mathcal{H}$, Givental's symplectic formalism for genus-zero theory.
4. **Identification.** The hypergeometric $I$-function is shown to lie on the same cone and to share the same asymptotics, forcing $J = I$ after the mirror change of variables.

The consequence is an effective algorithm: quantum products of toric complete intersections are computed by manipulating hypergeometric series, with no moduli spaces in sight [5].

### Families and functoriality

A recent direction studies Gromov–Witten invariants in *families*: Biswas, Das, Oh, and Paul develop the theory over a base, with applications to degeneration formulas that compute invariants of a smooth fiber from those of a singular central fiber.

##6 Limitations

Despite its successes, the theory faces substantial limitations:

- **Higher genus.** Genus $g \ge 2$ invariants remain largely inaccessible computationally. The Virasoro constraints (conjectured by Eguchi–Hori–Xiong, proved for toric targets by Givental and Okounkov–Pandharipande) govern them in principle, but explicit numbers are known only in scattered cases. The BCOV holomorphic anomaly equation offers a physics route that is only partially mathematized.
- **Convergence.** The Frobenius manifold and quantum product are *formal* power series in the Novikov variables; convergence near the large-radius limit is known in important cases but not in general, and analytic continuation past singular loci (conifold points, Landau–Ginzburg phases) requires the full machinery of variation of semi-infinite Hodge structures.
- **Non-convex and non-Kähler targets.** The virtual class handles arbitrary smooth projective targets, but explicit computations beyond the toric and homogeneous settings are rare. For non-Kähler symplectic manifolds, the symplectic construction of the virtual class (Kuranishi atlases, polyfolds) is technically formidable, though recent global-chart constructions have simplified the foundations [3].
- **Open and real invariants.** Extending the theory to maps with Lagrangian boundary conditions (open Gromov–Witten invariants) or to real algebraic curves introduces orientation and compactification problems that are only partially resolved.
- **Enumerativity gap.** Virtual invariants need not count geometric objects: multiple covers, contracted components, and excess contributions mean the numbers are deformation-invariant but not always enumerative. Extracting true curve counts requires the Gopakumar–Vafa or Donaldson–Thomas correspondences, themselves conjectural in general.

##7 Conclusion

Gromov–Witten theory transformed enumerative geometry from a collection of ingenious special cases into a systematic, axiomatic discipline. The Kontsevich–Manin axioms distill the formal behavior of curve counts into a cohomological field theory [1]; the Behrend–Fantechi virtual fundamental class gives those axioms a home on arbitrarily singular moduli spaces [2][6]; and the resulting quantum cohomology ring, governed by the WDVV equations, turns enumerative problems into problems in differential equations and Hodge theory. Mirror symmetry then solves those differential equations by entirely different means — period integrals on a mirror family — producing predictions, like the quintic instanton numbers, that no direct geometric method could reach [4][5].

The frontier has moved outward: to generalized cohomology theories where the formal group law deforms the quantum product [3], to families and degenerations that make the invariants functorial [7], and to the still-mysterious higher-genus theory. But the core lesson endures: *the right compactification, equipped with the right virtual cycle, makes the infinite enumerative content of a variety computable*.
