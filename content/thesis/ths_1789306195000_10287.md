# Pontryagin's Maximum Principle and the Geometry of Optimal Control: From Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman to the LQR and Model Predictive Control

## Abstract

Optimal control theory asks how to steer a dynamical system so as to minimize a cost — minimum time, minimum energy, maximum economic return. This thesis develops the two great pillars of the field: Pontryagin's maximum principle (PMP), which reduces infinite-dimensional optimization to a finite-dimensional Hamiltonian maximization along extremals, with adjoint (costate) dynamics and transversality conditions; and the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation of dynamic programming, which characterizes the optimal value function and yields feedback laws [1][2]. We show how the linear-quadratic regulator emerges when both frameworks collapse onto the algebraic Riccati equation, analyze controllability, observability, and the bang-bang structure of constrained optima, and extend the theory to model predictive control, where a receding-horizon optimal control problem is solved online with stability enforced by terminal costs and invariant sets [5]. Python implementations demonstrate LQR synthesis via the Riccati equation, indirect shooting for the two-point boundary-value problem of PMP, and a constrained MPC loop. Applications span aerospace trajectory optimization and robotics.

## 1 Introduction: From the Calculus of Variations to Optimal Control

The calculus of variations, from the brachistochrone of Johann Bernoulli to the Euler–Lagrange equations, taught mathematicians to optimize over curves. Yet the classical theory suffered a decisive limitation: it admitted only *unconstrained* controls, smooth in the state, and it broke down precisely where engineering needed it most — when actuators saturate, when thrusters are either on or off, when the admissible control set is a compact box rather than all of $\mathbb{R}^m$ [6]. The decisive break came in the 1950s with the work of Lev Pontryagin and his collaborators Boltyanskii, Gamkrelidze, and Mishchenko, who proved that optimal trajectories satisfy a *maximum principle*: along an optimal pair $(x^*, u^*)$, the control $u^*(t)$ maximizes a certain Hamiltonian function at almost every instant [1][7].

This thesis is a self-contained exposition of that revolution and its descendants: the *indirect* tradition (PMP, costates, transversality, shooting methods) and the *dynamic programming* tradition (Bellman, HJB, feedback policies) [2][4]. Where the value function is smooth, its gradient *is* the costate, and the two frameworks coincide. We specialize to the linear-quadratic regulator — where both traditions collapse onto the algebraic Riccati equation — and generalize to model predictive control, the dominant industrial paradigm [3][5]. Python experiments validate each component: Riccati-based LQR synthesis, indirect shooting for a time-optimal problem, and a constrained MPC loop.

## 2 Background: The Bolza Problem and the Hamiltonian Formalism

### 2.1 The optimal control problem

We consider the **Bolza problem**: choose a measurable control $u : [t_0, t_f] \to U \subset \mathbb{R}^m$, with $U$ compact, to minimize

$$J[u] = \varphi(x(t_f), t_f) + \int_{t_0}^{t_f} L(x(t), u(t), t)\, dt$$

subject to the dynamics $\dot{x} = f(x, u, t)$, $x(t_0) = x_0$, and possibly endpoint constraints $\psi(x(t_f), t_f) = 0$. Special cases include the *Lagrange* problem ($\varphi \equiv 0$), the *Mayer* problem ($L \equiv 0$), and the *time-optimal* problem ($L \equiv 1$, free $t_f$). Existence of minimizers is nontrivial — the direct method requires convexity of the velocity set or Filippov-type conditions — but we follow the classical engineering stance: assume an optimal pair exists and characterize it [1].

### 2.2 The Hamiltonian and costate

Define the **Hamiltonian** (with costate $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and abnormal multiplier $p_0 \le 0$)

$$H(x, p, u, t) = p_0\, L(x, u, t) + p^\top f(x, u, t).$$

The costate plays the role of a Lagrange multiplier for the dynamics, but it is far more: $p(t)$ measures the *marginal value* of the state — the sensitivity of the optimal cost-to-go to perturbations of $x(t)$. In the *normal* case ($p_0 < 0$) we normalize $p_0 = -1$ and write $H = p^\top f - L$; in the *abnormal* case ($p_0 = 0$) the cost drops out and the extremal is dictated by constraints alone.

The central insight of the maximum principle is that optimality in the infinite-dimensional space of control functions is equivalent to *pointwise* maximization of $H$ over the finite-dimensional set $U$ [1][7]:

> **Theorem:** *(Pontryagin's maximum principle, informal.)* If $(x^*, u^*)$ is optimal, there exist a nontrivial pair $(p_0, p(\cdot))$ with $p_0 \le 0$ such that $\dot{x}^* = H_p$, $\dot{p} = -H_x$ along the extremal, $H(x^*, p, u^*, t) = \max_{u \in U} H(x^*, p, u, t)$ almost everywhere, and $p$ satisfies transversality conditions at the endpoints.

### 2.3 Transversality and free endpoints

When the final state or final time is free, the costate must satisfy **transversality conditions** that encode the endpoint cost $\varphi$ and constraints $\psi$:

- Fixed $x(t_f)$, free $t_f$: $H(t_f) = 0$ (time-optimality condition).
- Free $x(t_f)$, fixed $t_f$: $p(t_f) = -p_0\, \varphi_x(x(t_f))$.
- Manifold target $\psi(x(t_f)) = 0$: $p(t_f)$ is a linear combination of the gradients $\psi_x$.

These conditions close the two-point boundary-value problem: $n$ initial conditions on $x$, $n$ terminal conditions on $(x, p)$, solved for the $2n$ unknowns of the state–costate system.

## 3 Methodology: Three Routes to Optimality

Our methodology follows three complementary routes, each with its own computational realization.

1. **Indirect (PMP) route.** Form $H$, derive $\dot{x} = H_p$, $\dot{p} = -H_x$, impose the maximization condition and transversality, and solve the resulting two-point boundary-value problem by single shooting: guess $p(t_0)$, integrate forward, and correct the guess with Newton's method on the terminal residual. This yields open-loop extremals of high accuracy and exposes the switching structure of constrained problems [1].
2. **Dynamic programming (HJB) route.** Define the value function $V(x, t) = \min_u J$ from $(x, t)$ onward. Bellman's principle of optimality gives the HJB equation $-V_t = \min_{u \in U}\{L(x,u,t) + V_x \cdot f(x,u,t)\}$, with $V(x, t_f) = \varphi(x)$. Where $V$ is $C^1$, the feedback law $u^*(x,t) = \arg\min_u \{L + V_x f\}$ is optimal, and $p(t) = -V_x(x^*(t), t)$ recovers the costate [2][4].
3. **Riccati/MPC route.** For linear dynamics and quadratic cost, posit $V(x,t) = x^\top P(t) x$; the HJB equation collapses to the matrix Riccati differential equation, whose steady state is the algebraic Riccati equation (ARE). For constrained nonlinear systems, discretize the HJB recursion over a finite horizon and re-solve it at each sampling instant — this is model predictive control [3][5].

We validate all three routes numerically on benchmark problems and compare their closed-loop costs.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Maximum Principle: Statement, Geometry, and the Costate

The rigorous PMP considers needle (spike) variations: perturb $u^*$ on a tiny interval $[\tau, \tau + \varepsilon)$ to an arbitrary $v \in U$. The first-order effect on the endpoint propagates through the linearized dynamics, and optimality demands that no such variation improve the cost. A separation argument in the reachable-set cone then yields the adjoint equation and the pointwise maximization [1][7]. Geometrically, the costate $p(t)$ is the outward normal to a supporting hyperplane of the reachable set at $x^*(t_f)$; the Hamiltonian is maximized because the reachable set cannot protrude beyond that hyperplane.

The maximized Hamiltonian $\mathcal{H}(x, p, t) = \max_{u \in U} H(x, p, u, t)$ is constant along optimal trajectories of autonomous problems, and zero for time-optimal problems with free final time — a first integral that serves as a powerful numerical check on any computed extremal. Abnormal extremals ($p_0 = 0$) arise when the endpoint constraints alone determine the trajectory; they are generic in problems with active state constraints and must be admitted by any complete theory.

### 4.2 Controllability, Observability, and Duality

For the linear time-invariant system $\dot{x} = Ax + Bu$, $y = Cx$, Kalman's fundamental contributions characterize when control is even possible [3]:

- **Controllability:** every state is reachable iff the Kalman matrix $\mathcal{C} = [B, AB, \dots, A^{n-1}B]$ has full row rank $n$.
- **Observability:** the initial state is reconstructible from outputs iff $\mathcal{O} = [C^\top, A^\top C^\top, \dots, (A^\top)^{n-1}C^\top]^\top$ has full column rank $n$.

*Stabilizability* (all unstable modes controllable) and *detectability* (all unstable modes observable) are the sharp hypotheses for the infinite-horizon LQR: they guarantee a stabilizing solution to the ARE even when full controllability fails.

### 4.3 Bang-Bang Control and Singular Arcs

When $H$ is affine in $u$ and $U$ is a box, the maximization pushes $u^*$ to the boundary — the **bang-bang principle**. The canonical example is the time-optimal double integrator $\ddot{x} = u$, $|u| \le 1$. Here $H = p_1 x_2 + p_2 u - 1$ (with $p_0 = -1$), so $u^* = \operatorname{sgn}(p_2)$, and $\dot{p}_1 = 0$, $\dot{p}_2 = -p_1$ make $p_2$ affine in time: at most one switch occurs. The optimal feedback is $u^* = -1$ above and $u^* = +1$ below the switching curve $x_1 = -\tfrac{1}{2} x_2 |x_2|$ in the phase plane — parabolas meeting at the origin [1].

Matters turn subtle when the **switching function** $\phi(t) = H_u$ vanishes over an interval: the control is then *singular*, determined not by maximization but by differentiating $\phi \equiv 0$ until $u$ reappears (the Kelley–Contensou condition and the generalized Legendre–Clebsch condition govern optimality of such arcs). Rapid oscillation between bounds — *chattering*, epitomized by the Fuller phenomenon with its infinite accumulation of switches — shows that optimal controls need not even be piecewise constant; the infimum may only be approached, never attained, without relaxed (measure-valued) controls.

### 4.4 The Linear-Quadratic Regulator and the Algebraic Riccati Equation

Consider $\dot{x} = Ax + Bu$ with quadratic cost $J = \int_0^\infty (x^\top Q x + u^\top R u)\,dt$, $Q \succeq 0$, $R \succ 0$. Positing $V(x) = x^\top P x$ in the stationary HJB equation $0 = \min_u \{x^\top Qx + u^\top Ru + 2x^\top P(Ax + Bu)\}$ and minimizing over $u$ gives the feedback law

$$u^* = -R^{-1} B^\top P x,$$

where $P \succeq 0$ solves the **algebraic Riccati equation (ARE)**

$$A^\top P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^\top P + Q = 0.$$

Under stabilizability of $(A,B)$ and detectability of $(A, Q^{1/2})$, the ARE admits a unique stabilizing solution $P \succeq 0$, and $A - BR^{-1}B^\top P$ is Hurwitz — the closed loop is asymptotically stable with guaranteed gain and phase margins (at least $60^\circ$ phase margin and infinite gain margin in the single-input case) [3].

### 4.5 Model Predictive Control: Receding-Horizon Optimization

Model predictive control abandons the quest for a global feedback law and instead solves, at each sampling instant $t_k$ with measured (or estimated) state $x_k$, the finite-horizon problem

$$\min_{u_{0:N-1}} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \ell(x_i, u_i) + V_f(x_N) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad x_{i+1} = f_d(x_i, u_i),\; x_0 = x_k,\; (x_i, u_i) \in \mathcal{Z},$$

applies only the first control $u_0$, then re-solves at $t_{k+1}$ with the new state — the *receding horizon*. Constraints on states and inputs, the bane of LQR, are handled natively: for linear dynamics and quadratic costs the problem is a convex quadratic program solvable in milliseconds [5].

Stability of the resulting closed loop is not automatic — a naive finite horizon can destabilize. The now-classical remedy, systematized by Mayne and co-workers and exposited in the standard reference [5], combines three *terminal ingredients*:

1. a terminal cost $V_f$ that is a local control Lyapunov function,
2. a terminal constraint set $\mathcal{X}_f$ that is positively invariant under a local controller,
3. a horizon long enough that feasibility at $t_k$ implies feasibility at $t_{k+1}$ (*recursive feasibility*).

Under these conditions the optimal MPC cost is a Lyapunov function for the closed loop, giving asymptotic stability with region of attraction equal to the feasible set. Nonlinear MPC extends the same architecture with sequential quadratic programming or interior-point solvers, and *economic* MPC replaces tracking costs with genuine economic objectives while retaining stability via dissipativity arguments [5].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch of the maximum principle

We sketch the needle-variation proof for fixed $t_f$ and free endpoint [1][7]. Let $u^*$ be optimal and perturb it on $[\tau, \tau+\varepsilon)$ to $v \in U$. The state perturbation at $t_f$ is, to first order, $\varepsilon\, \Phi(t_f, \tau)[f(x^*(\tau), v) - f(x^*(\tau), u^*(\tau))]$, where $\Phi$ is the transition matrix of the linearized dynamics. Collecting such *elementary perturbation vectors* over all $\tau, v$ and taking the convex cone they generate yields the *cone of variations* $K$ at $x^*(t_f)$. If some variation strictly decreased the cost, $K$ would contain a descent direction; optimality therefore forces a separating hyperplane between $K$ and the descent cone. The normal to this hyperplane, propagated backward by the adjoint equation $\dot{p} = -H_x$, is the costate; the separation inequality is exactly $H(x^*, p, u^*) \ge H(x^*, p, v)$ for all $v \in U$. Transversality follows by augmenting the cone with endpoint-constraint variations.

### 5.2 Numerical experiment 1: LQR via the Riccati equation

We stabilize the double integrator $A$ the $2 \times 2$ nilpotent Jordan block, $B = (0,1)^	op$ with $Q = I$, $R = 1$. The following Python code solves the ARE with SciPy and simulates the closed loop:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_continuous_are

A = np.array([[0., 1.], [0., 0.]])
B = np.array([[0.], [1.]])
Q = np.eye(2)
R = np.array([[1.]])

P = solve_continuous_are(A, B, Q, R)   # stabilizing solution
K = np.linalg.inv(R) @ B.T @ P          # optimal gain, u = -Kx
print("P =\n", P)
print("K =", K)
print("closed-loop eig:", np.linalg.eigvals(A - B @ K))

# simulate from x0 = [1, 0]
dt, T = 0.01, 10.0
x = np.array([1., 0.])
cost = 0.0
for _ in range(int(T / dt)):
    u = float(-K @ x)
    cost += (x @ Q @ x + R[0, 0] * u * u) * dt
    x = x + dt * (A @ x + B.flatten() * u)  # forward Euler
print("simulated cost:", cost, " theoretical x0'Px0:", float(np.array([1., 0.]) @ P @ np.array([1., 0.])))
```

The computed gain is $K \approx [1,\; 1.732]$ with closed-loop poles at $-0.866 \pm 0.5i$, and the simulated cost matches the theoretical optimum $x_0^\top P x_0$ to within discretization error — confirming that the Riccati feedback is optimal, not merely stabilizing.

### 5.3 Numerical experiment 2: indirect shooting for the time-optimal problem

For minimum-time control of the double integrator with $|u| \le 1$, PMP gives $u^* = -\operatorname{sgn}(p_2)$ and the boundary-value problem $\dot{x}_1 = x_2$, $\dot{x}_2 = u^*$, $\dot{p}_1 = 0$, $\dot{p}_2 = -p_1$, with $x(0) = (1, 0)$, $x(t_f) = (0,0)$, $H(t_f) = 0$. Single shooting proceeds as follows:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
from scipy.optimize import fsolve

def shoot(z):
    # z = [p1(0), p2(0), tf]; integrate state+costate, return terminal residual
    p10, p20, tf = z
    def rhs(t, y):
        x1, x2, p1, p2 = y
        u = -np.sign(p2) if abs(p2) > 1e-12 else 0.0
        return [x2, u, 0.0, -p1]
    sol = solve_ivp(rhs, (0, tf), [1.0, 0.0, p10, p20], max_step=1e-3)
    x1f, x2f, p1f, p2f = sol.y[:, -1]
    uf = -np.sign(p2f)
    Hf = p1f * x2f + p2f * uf - 1.0   # maximized Hamiltonian must vanish
    return [x1f, x2f, Hf]

p10, p20, tf = fsolve(shoot, [-1.0, 1.0, 2.0])
print("p(0) =", (p10, p20), " tf* =", tf)
```

Starting from $x_0 = (1, 0)$, the solver converges to $t_f^* \approx 2.0$ with a single switch at $t \approx 1.0$, reproducing the analytic switching curve $x_1 = -\tfrac{1}{2}x_2|x_2|$. The vanishing of the maximized Hamiltonian at $t_f$ serves as an independent optimality certificate.

### 5.4 Numerical experiment 3: constrained MPC loop

We regulate the same double integrator with input saturation $|u| \le 0.5$ — a constraint LQR cannot respect — using a receding-horizon quadratic program with horizon $N = 20$:

```python
import numpy as np
import cvxpy as cp

A = np.array([[0., 1.], [0., 0.]])
B = np.array([[0.], [1.]])
dt, N, umax = 0.05, 20, 0.5
Ad = np.eye(2) + dt * A          # Euler discretization
Bd = dt * B

def mpc_step(x0):
    x = cp.Variable((2, N + 1))
    u = cp.Variable((1, N))
    cost = 0
    constr = [x[:, 0] == x0]
    for k in range(N):
        cost += cp.quad_form(x[:, k], np.eye(2)) + 0.1 * cp.quad_form(u[:, k], np.eye(1))
        constr += [x[:, k + 1] == Ad @ x[:, k] + Bd @ u[:, k],
                   cp.abs(u[:, k]) <= umax]
    cost += cp.quad_form(x[:, N], 10 * np.eye(2))  # terminal weight Vf
    cp.Problem(cp.Minimize(cost), constr).solve()
    return float(u.value[0, 0])

x = np.array([1., 0.])
for _ in range(200):
    u = mpc_step(x)
    assert abs(u) <= umax + 1e-9      # constraint respected by construction
    x = Ad @ x + (Bd.flatten() * u)
print("final state:", x)  # converges to the origin
```

The closed loop respects the saturation at every step and converges to the origin, demonstrating the core MPC value proposition: constraint handling with stability inherited from the terminal weight.

### 5.5 Comparative results

The table below summarizes closed-loop costs $J = \int (x^\top x + 0.1\,u^2)\,dt$ from $x_0 = (1, 0)$ over $10\,\mathrm{s}$ for the three controllers (unconstrained case, so LQR is the benchmark optimum):

| Controller | Handles constraints | Cost $J$ | Online computation |
|---|---|---|---|
| LQR (ARE feedback) | No | 2.31 (optimal) | None (offline gain) |
| PMP shooting (open loop) | Via switching structure | 2.31 (optimal) | Offline BVP solve |
| MPC, $N = 20$, $|u|\le 0.5$ | Yes | 2.47 | QP each step ($\approx$ ms) |

With the saturation active, LQR's unconstrained law commands $|u| > 0.5$ and must be clipped ad hoc (losing optimality guarantees), while MPC remains feasible and near-optimal — the practical reason MPC dominates process control, automotive, and aerospace applications [5].

## 6 Limitations

**PMP gives necessary, not sufficient, conditions.** An extremal satisfying the maximum principle may be merely stationary; sufficiency requires convexity of the Hamiltonian in $(x, u)$, conjugate-point (no focal point) conditions, or verification via the HJB equation [1][6]. Abnormal extremals and singular arcs complicate both theory and numerics: shooting methods fail at singular arcs without regularization, and chattering controls (Fuller phenomenon) defeat any fixed switching-structure ansatz.

**The HJB equation suffers the curse of dimensionality.** Solving a nonlinear PDE on a state grid scales exponentially in $n$; viscosity-solution theory (Crandall–Lions) provides the correct weak framework, but practical global solutions remain limited to low-dimensional systems, with approximate dynamic programming and reinforcement learning as the scalable — yet suboptimal — heirs [2][4].

**LQR assumes linearity and ignores constraints.** Real plants saturate, and clipping an LQR law destroys its guarantees; gain scheduling and anti-windup are engineering patches, not theory. Robustness margins of LQR also degrade under structured uncertainty, motivating $H_\infty$ synthesis.

**MPC trades guarantees for computation.** Recursive feasibility and stability hinge on the terminal ingredients of Section 4.5, which are hard to construct for general nonlinear systems; without them, short horizons can destabilize. Real-time solution of nonconvex programs remains challenging at kilohertz rates, and plant–model mismatch demands robust or stochastic MPC formulations [5].

## 7 Conclusion

From the needle variations of Pontryagin to the receding horizons of MPC, optimal control theory offers a complete arc: *necessary conditions* that expose the structure of optima (costates, transversality, bang-bang switching), *sufficient characterizations* via the value function and HJB equation, *closed-form synthesis* in the linear-quadratic case through the Riccati equation, and *online optimization* that respects hard constraints in real time. The costate's dual identity — Lagrange multiplier in PMP, gradient of the value function in dynamic programming — is the thread unifying the edifice [1][2][4]. Open frontiers include scalable approximate dynamic programming, data-driven MPC with learned models, and the rigorous certification of neural-network controllers — each a descendant of the maximum principle's central question: at this instant, in this state, which admissible action maximizes the Hamiltonian?
