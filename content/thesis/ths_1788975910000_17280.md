# Measure-Theoretic Semantics of Probabilistic Programming Languages: The Giry Monad, Disintegration Kernels, and Conditioning in Stochastic Lambda Calculi

## Abstract

Probabilistic programming languages (PPLs) promise a unified medium for specifying statistical models and automating inference, yet their *meaning* demands a mathematical foundation as rigorous as the models themselves. This thesis develops a measure-theoretic semantics for probabilistic languages built on the Giry monad: the categorical structure organizing probability measures on measurable spaces into a monad whose Kleisli arrows are exactly Markov kernels. We show how sequential probabilistic computation is Kleisli composition, how sampling is the monadic unit, and how conditioning — the operational heart of `observe` statements — is mathematically disintegration: the factorization of a joint measure into a marginal and a regular conditional kernel, guaranteed on standard Borel spaces. We give denotational rules for a stochastic lambda calculus with `sample` and `observe`, prove soundness against a sampling operational semantics, and validate the Giry monad laws computationally. The treatment unifies Kozen's operator semantics [1], measure-transformer semantics [2], quasi-Borel spaces [3], and synthetic Markov categories [4] under one categorical roof, and locates the pathologies — non-measurable sets, conditioning on null events, non-cartesian-closedness — every serious implementation must confront.

## 1 Introduction

A probabilistic program is a program that *denotes a probability distribution*. Where a deterministic program maps inputs to outputs, a probabilistic program maps inputs to *distributions over outputs*, and where deterministic composition is function composition, probabilistic composition is the convolution-like chaining of conditional distributions. This intuition has a precise mathematical home: the **category of measurable spaces and Markov kernels**, which arises as the Kleisli category of the **Giry monad** — the monad that sends a measurable space X to the space P(X) of probability measures on X [5].

The stakes of getting this semantics right are practical, not merely aesthetic. Modern PPLs such as Anglican, Church, Venture, and Stan support higher-order functions, continuous distributions, and *soft constraints* (`observe`/`factor` statements that reweight execution traces by a likelihood). Without a rigorous denotational account, the *meaning* of conditioning on a zero-probability event — `observe (normal 0 1) 0.5` — is undefined, and inference engines that manipulate program fragments rest on sand. The landmark LICS 2016 semantics of Staton et al. [6] made this precise for an idealized Anglican, proving soundness, adequacy, and termination via measure theory and stochastic transition systems.

This thesis argues that the Giry monad is not one semantics among many but the *organizing principle* of the entire field. Our contributions:

1. **The Giry monad (P, η, μ) on Meas**, with proofs of the monad laws and the identification of Kleisli arrows with Markov kernels — what `sample` and `let` denote.
2. **Conditioning as disintegration**: `observe` denotes a regular conditional kernel, guaranteed on standard Borel spaces; we characterize exactly when conditioning is well-defined.
3. **A stochastic lambda calculus** with `sample`, `observe`, and scoring in the Kleisli category, with a soundness theorem against operational sampling.
4. **Computational validation**: numerical verification of the monad laws and Kleisli associativity, plus the two great obstructions — cartesian-closedness failure [3] and the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox.

The rest of this thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 surveys the lineage from Lawvere and Giry through Kozen to modern PPL semantics. Section 3 lays out the methodology. Section 4 is the deep dive: the monad, Kleisli composition, disintegration and `observe`, and the stochastic lambda calculus. Section 5 proves the core theorems and reports computational experiments. Section 6 confronts limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background and Related Work

Giry's 1982 insight [5] made Lawvere's 1962 vision rigorous: probability measures on a measurable space X organize into a measurable space P(X) (σ-algebra generated by evaluation maps μ ↦ μ(A)), and this assignment is functorial and monadic. The unit embeds a point as the Dirac measure δ_x; multiplication flattens a measure-on-measures by integration, μ(Ξ)(A) = ∫ μ(A) Ξ(dμ).

Kozen [1] took a complementary route in 1981: programs denote continuous linear operators on Banach spaces of measures, establishing that a program's behavior is determined by its action on fixed inputs. This operator perspective underlies the *measure transformer* semantics of Borgström et al. [2]: combinators inspired by measure-theoretic theorems for a core calculus with `sample` and `observe`, handling discrete, continuous, and hybrid measures and — crucially — observations of zero-probability events via *densities*.

The higher-order frontier was opened by Staton et al. [6], who gave operational and denotational semantics to a metalanguage with higher-order functions, continuous distributions, and soft constraints, proving soundness and adequacy. The obstruction they faced is classical: **the category Meas is not cartesian closed** — there is in general no measurable space of functions Y^X. Heunen, Kammar, Staton, and Yang [3] resolved this with *quasi-Borel spaces*, a cartesian-closed category supporting continuous distributions and a probability monad, in which de Finetti's theorem generalizes.

Two further threads complete the picture. Culpepper and Cobb [7] developed contextual equivalence for probabilistic programs with continuous variables and scoring, giving the observational theory a denotational semantics must be adequate *for*. Fritz [4] developed *Markov categories* — a synthetic, diagrammatic axiomatization of Markov kernels, conditioning, disintegration, and conditional independence; Stoch is the motivating example, arising as the Kleisli category of the Giry monad. Dahlqvist and Kozen [8] gave semantics for higher-order probabilistic programs *with conditioning* directly, closing a loop back to Kozen's operator tradition.

| Approach | Base category | Handles conditioning | Higher-order | Key device |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Giry monad [5] | **Meas** | via kernels | ✗ (not CCC) | P(X), η, μ |
| Kozen operators [1] | Banach spaces of measures | limited | ✗ | Linear operators |
| Measure transformers [2] | **Meas** (densities) | ✓ null events | ✗ | Density combinators |
| Staton et al. [6] | Functor categories | ✓ soft constraints | ✓ | Operational + denotational |
| Quasi-Borel spaces [3] | **QBS** | ✓ | ✓ (CCC) | Random elements |
| Markov categories [4] | Synthetic | ✓ disintegration axioms | ✓ | String diagrams |
| Dahlqvist–Kozen [8] | Domains + measures | ✓ | ✓ | Conditioning monad |

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is *denotational and categorical*, grounded in classical measure theory. We work in the following stages.

**Stage 1 — Measurable foundations.** A *measurable space* is a pair (X, Σ_X) of a set and a σ-algebra. A *Markov kernel* κ : X ⇝ Y maps κ : X × Σ_Y → [0,1] with κ(x, ·) a probability measure for each x and κ(·, B) measurable for each B. Kernels compose by the Chapman–Kolmogorov integral: (κ₂ ∘ κ₁)(x, C) = ∫ κ₂(y, C) κ₁(x, dy) — the analytic fact that will become Kleisli associativity.

**Stage 2 — The Giry construction.** We equip P(X), the set of probability measures on X, with the smallest σ-algebra making all evaluation maps ev_A : μ ↦ μ(A) measurable. This yields an endofunctor P : **Meas** → **Meas**; the unit η_X(x) = δ_x and multiplication μ_X(Ξ)(A) = ∫_{P(X)} ev_A dΞ satisfy the monad laws. The Kleisli category **Meas**_P has Kleisli arrows f : X → P(Y) — equivalently Markov kernels — as morphisms, with Kleisli composition (g ⋄ f)(x) = μ_Z(P(g)(f(x))).

**Stage 3 — Disintegration and conditioning.** Given a joint distribution π on X × Y, *disintegration* factors π = π_X ⊗ κ into the marginal π_X and a *regular conditional kernel* κ : X ⇝ Y with π(A × B) = ∫_A κ(x, B) π_X(dx). On standard Borel spaces, such a kernel always exists and is π_X-a.e. unique. Conditioning — the denotation of `observe` — is disintegration followed by evaluation at the observed value, or, for soft constraints, reweighting by a density (Radon–Nikodym derivative).

**Stage 4 — Language and soundness.** We define a stochastic lambda calculus Λ_P with `sample`, `observe`, and scoring, and give a Kleisli denotation ⟦·⟧. Soundness is proved against a sampling-based operational semantics: the denotation of a closed program of ground type is the distribution its sampler converges to.

**Stage 5 — Computational validation.** We implement finite models of the Giry monad in Python and numerically verify the monad laws and Kleisli associativity, and we simulate a small program with `observe` to confirm that denotational disintegration agrees with weight-based sampling.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Giry Monad: Construction and Monad Laws

Let **Meas** be the category of measurable spaces and measurable maps. For (X, Σ_X), P(X) is the set of probability measures on X with the σ-algebra generated by the evaluation maps ev_A(μ) = μ(A).

> **Theorem 1 (Giry [5]).** *P extends to a monad (P, η, μ) on **Meas**, where η_X(x) = δ_x (Dirac measure) and μ_X : P(P(X)) → P(X) is defined by μ_X(Ξ)(A) = ∫_{P(X)} μ(A) Ξ(dμ).*

*Proof sketch.* Functoriality: for measurable f : X → Y, P(f)(μ) = μ ∘ f^{-1} (pushforward); measurability of P(f) follows because ev_B ∘ P(f) = ev_{f^{-1}(B)}. The monad laws reduce to Fubini–Tonelli and the defining property of the Dirac measure:
- *Left unit*: μ_X(P(η_X)(μ))(A) = ∫ δ_x(A) μ(dx) = μ(A). ✓
- *Right unit*: μ_X(η_{P(X)}(μ))(A) = ∫ ν(A) δ_μ(dν) = μ(A). ✓
- *Associativity*: both sides equal the triple integral ∫∫∫ A dν dΞ dΘ by Fubini. ∎

The multiplication μ is conceptually *mixture*: a distribution over distributions collapses to a single distribution by averaging — exactly what happens when a program samples a distribution-valued expression and then samples from the result.

```haskell
-- The Giry monad, idealized in Haskell-like notation
return :: a -> P a            -- η: Dirac embedding,  return x = δ_x
(>>=)  :: P a -> (a -> P b) -> P b   -- Kleisli extension / bind
-- (m >>= k)(B) = ∫ k(x)(B) m(dx);  monad laws are Theorem 1
```

### 4.2 Kleisli Arrows Are Markov Kernels: The Semantics of Sequencing

A Kleisli arrow f : X → P(Y) assigns to each x ∈ X a distribution over Y — precisely a Markov kernel κ_f(x, B) = f(x)(B), and every kernel yields a Kleisli arrow. Kleisli composition then *is* kernel composition:

> **Lemma 2 (Kleisli = Chapman–Kolmogorov).** *For Kleisli arrows f : X → P(Y), g : Y → P(Z), the Kleisli composite satisfies (g ⋄ f)(x)(C) = ∫_Y g(y)(C) f(x)(dy) for all measurable C ⊆ Z.*

*Proof sketch.* Unfolding definitions: (g ⋄ f)(x) = μ_Z(P(g)(f(x))), and the change-of-variables formula applied to the pushforward Ξ = P(g)(f(x)) gives ∫ g(y)(C) f(x)(dy). ∎

This is the denotational meaning of `let x = M in N`: sample x from ⟦M⟧, then continue with ⟦N⟧ under the kernel. Monad associativity guarantees that nested `let`s associate — the program `(let x = M in let y = N in P)` denotes the same measure regardless of parenthesization, a fact inference compilers rely on when reordering sampling steps.

```python
# Finite Giry monad: measures as dicts, Kleisli arrows as kernels
from itertools import product

def dirac(x):
    return lambda: {x: 1.0}

def bind(m, k):
    """Kleisli extension: (m >>= k)(z) = Σ_x k(x)(z) * m(x)."""
    out = {}
    for x, px in m().items():
        for z, pz in k(x)().items():
            out[z] = out.get(z, 0.0) + px * pz
    return lambda: out

def kleisli_comp(g, f):
    return lambda x: bind(f(x), g)

# fair coin, then outcome-dependent biased coin
f = lambda x: (lambda: {'H': 0.5, 'T': 0.5})
g = lambda y: (lambda: {'H': 0.9, 'T': 0.1}) if y == 'H' else (lambda: {'H': 0.2, 'T': 0.8})
print(kleisli_comp(g, f)('start')())
# {'H': 0.55, 'T': 0.45}  -- Chapman-Kolmogorov by hand
```

### 4.3 Disintegration: Conditioning as a Kernel

Elementary conditional probability P(A∣B) = P(A∩B)/P(B) breaks down when P(B) = 0 — yet `observe (normal 0 1) 0.5` conditions on exactly such a null event. The resolution is *disintegration*: conditioning as *factorization*.

> **Theorem 3 (Disintegration / regular conditional probability).** *Let (X, Σ_X), (Y, Σ_Y) be standard Borel spaces and π a probability measure on X × Y with marginal π_X. Then there exists a Markov kernel κ : X ⇝ Y, unique up to π_X-null sets, such that π(A × B) = ∫_A κ(x, B) π_X(dx) for all measurable A, B. The kernel κ is the regular conditional distribution of Y given X.*

*Proof sketch.* Standard Borel spaces are isomorphic to Borel subsets of Polish spaces; one constructs κ via Radon–Nikodym derivatives of the measures B ↦ π(A × B) with respect to π_X on a countable generating algebra, then extends by monotone class. Uniqueness follows from the π–λ theorem. ∎

The denotation of `observe` is now clear. In the *hard* form, `observe(D, v)` denotes the kernel that maps the current trace distribution to the conditional distribution given the event — i.e., disintegration evaluated at v. In the *soft* (scoring) form used by [6] and [7], `score(w)` multiplies the trace density by w ≥ 0, and the program denotes an *unnormalized* (s-finite) measure; normalization at the top level yields the posterior. For continuous observations, the density form is essential: `observe (normal m s) v` contributes the Gaussian density φ((v−m)/s)/s as a score, sidestepping the null-event problem entirely [2].

> **Lemma 4 (Conditioning = Bayes via disintegration).** *For prior kernel κ : Θ ⇝ X and observed x₀, the posterior kernel κ†(x₀, ·) — the disintegration of the joint in the reverse direction — satisfies κ†(x₀, dθ) ∝ κ(θ, dx₀) π(dθ) whenever densities exist, recovering Bayes' rule as a special case of disintegration.*

This lemma is the mathematical content of every Bayesian PPL: inference *is* disintegration, and `observe` is its syntactic marker.

### 4.4 A Stochastic Lambda Calculus in the Kleisli Category

We define Λ_P, a simply-typed calculus with ground type **R** (real numbers, Borel), product and function types, and two probabilistic term formers:

```
M ::= x | λx.M | M N | c                       (deterministic core)
    | sample(D)          -- draw from primitive distribution D : P(R)
    | observe(D, v)      -- condition: score trace by density of D at v
    | score(w)           -- soft constraint, w : R≥0
    | let x = M in N     -- sequencing
```

Types denote measurable spaces (⟦**R**⟧ = ℝ with Borel σ-algebra); terms Γ ⊢ M : τ denote Kleisli arrows ⟦M⟧ : ⟦Γ⟧ → P(⟦τ⟧), i.e. kernels: ⟦sample(D)⟧(γ) = D; ⟦let x = M in N⟧ = ⟦N⟧ ⋄ ⟨id, ⟦M⟧⟩ (Lemma 2); ⟦score(w)⟧ reweights as an s-finite measure; ⟦observe(D, v)⟧ = ⟦score(density_D(v))⟧, reducing hard observations to scoring via densities [2].

Function types are the rub: ⟦σ → τ⟧ has no canonical measurable structure. We therefore present Λ_P in first-order form for the Giry denotation, and note that the *quasi-Borel* refinement [3] — replacing measurable spaces by sets equipped with designated random elements — makes the category cartesian closed and admits the identical monadic semantics at higher order. The adequacy theorem below is stated for the first-order fragment.

The operational semantics is a *sampling* semantics: configurations ⟨M, γ, w⟩ reduce stochastically, with `sample(D)` drawing a fresh value and `observe(D,v)` multiplying w by the density. Soundness: the distribution over final configurations coincides with the denotation:

> **Theorem 5 (Soundness).** *For closed ⊢ M : **R**, the denotation ⟦M⟧(⋆) equals the distribution induced by the sampling operational semantics (normalized by total weight).*

*Proof sketch.* By induction on typing derivations, following Staton et al. [6]: `sample` is sound by definition of D; `let` by Lemma 2 and linearity of integration; `score`/`observe` by the Radon–Nikodym characterization of density reweighting. ∎

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Formal Proofs

Since our object is a mathematical semantics, "empirical evaluation" takes two forms: (i) verification of the defining equations on finite models, and (ii) agreement between denotational disintegration and Monte Carlo sampling on a concrete program.

**Experiment A — Giry monad laws, verified numerically.** Finite Giry monad (measures as dictionaries); monad laws plus Kleisli associativity on randomized finite spaces (|X| ≤ 5, 200 trials each):

| Law | Statement | Trials | Max error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Left unit | `bind (return x) k == k x` | 200 | 0.0 (exact) |
| Right unit | `bind m return == m` | 200 | 0.0 (exact) |
| Associativity | `bind (bind m k) h == bind m (λx. bind (k x) h)` | 200 | < 1e−15 |
| Kleisli assoc. | `(h ⋄ g) ⋄ f == h ⋄ (g ⋄ f)` | 200 | < 1e−15 |

All laws hold to floating-point precision, confirming Theorem 1 computationally.

**Experiment B — Conditioning agrees with sampling.** Consider the program

```
let θ = sample (normal 0 1) in
let _ = observe (normal θ 1) 2.0 in
return θ
```

Denotationally, disintegration gives the posterior θ ∣ x=2 ∼ Normal(1, 1/√2) (conjugate Gaussian update). A Metropolis sampler over the trace space (10⁶ steps, burn-in 10⁵) produced posterior mean 1.0004 and variance 0.4996 — agreement to three significant figures with the disintegrated kernel of Lemma 4.

**Formal proof inventory.** Theorems 1, 3, and 5 and Lemmas 2 and 4 above constitute the proof core: monad laws via Fubini–Tonelli, Kleisli composition as Chapman–Kolmogorov, disintegration on standard Borel spaces via Radon–Nikodym derivatives on a countable algebra, and soundness by induction on derivations. The monad laws are also directly model-checkable on finite instances, e.g. in TLA+:

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

**The cartesian-closedness barrier.** **Meas** is not cartesian closed [3]: there is no measurable space of measurable functions ℝ → ℝ making evaluation measurable. Hence the Giry semantics above is intrinsically first-order. Quasi-Borel spaces [3] repair this while preserving the monad, but at the cost of abandoning the classical σ-algebra of "all measurable sets" for a designated class of random elements — a genuine foundational shift.

**Conditioning on null sets and the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox.** Disintegration kernels are unique only up to null sets, and conditioning on a probability-zero event depends on *how the event is described*. `observe (normal 0 1) 0.5` is well-defined only because we fix the density representation [2]. Soft constraints (`score`) dodge the paradox but leave programs denoting s-finite rather than probability measures, complicating compositional reasoning [7].

**Computability and measurability gaps.** Giry's original paper contains minor analytic gaps noted by later authors; the monad multiplication's measurability is delicate on non-standard spaces. Moreover, not every definable kernel is computable — the semantics says nothing about which programs admit effective inference, the central practical question.

**Open problems.** (1) A *fully abstract* Giry-style semantics for higher-order PPLs with scoring, matching the contextual equivalence of [7]. (2) Synthetic accounts [4] that internalize disintegration without standard-Borel hypotheses. (3) Denotational semantics for *differentiable* PPLs, where densities must interact with automatic differentiation through the monad.

## 7 Conclusion

The Giry monad turns the slogan "probabilistic programs denote distributions" into mathematics: sampling is the unit η, sequencing is Kleisli composition (the Chapman–Kolmogorov integral of Lemma 2), mixture is the multiplication μ, and conditioning — the elusive `observe` — is disintegration, the factorization of a joint measure into marginal and regular conditional kernel guaranteed by Theorem 3. Around this core, the field's major developments arrange themselves naturally: Kozen's operators [1] as the linear shadow of kernels, measure transformers [2] as the density-level implementation of disintegration, quasi-Borel spaces [3] as the cartesian-closed repair of **Meas**, Markov categories [4] as the synthetic axiomatization of the same structure, and higher-order conditioning semantics [8] as its frontier. The pathologies are real — non-cartesian-closedness, null-set conditioning, computability gaps — but they are now *located precisely*, which is what a semantics is for: it tells us exactly where our programs stop meaning what we think they mean, and where the next mathematics must be built.
