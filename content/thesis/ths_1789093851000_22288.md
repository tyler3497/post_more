# MEV and Proposer–Builder Separation: Auction Theory Meets Consensus Incentives

## Abstract

Maximal Extractable Value (MEV) — the profit obtainable by selecting, excluding, inserting, or reordering transactions within a block — has become the dominant economic force shaping Ethereum's consensus and application layers. This thesis unifies MEV theory with the Proposer–Builder Separation (PBS) architecture built to contain it. We review the taxonomy formalized by Daian et al. in "Flash Boys 2.0" (IEEE S&P 2020): priority gas auctions, arbitrage, liquidations, and sandwich attacks [1][2]. We formalize the PBS pipeline — searchers, builders, relays, proposers — as a two-sided market centered on a sealed-bid auction, and apply classical auction theory to first-price builder bidding, late-bidding timing games, order-flow auctions, and the MEV-Share redistribution protocol [6][7]. Drawing on empirical studies of the builder oligopoly, we show how exclusive order flow drives centralization and censorship risk [5], and we evaluate enshrined PBS (ePBS) proposals against game-theoretic stability criteria [4].

## 1 Introduction

MEV denotes the maximum value a block producer can extract beyond standard rewards by manipulating block contents and ordering [1][3]. Because DeFi transactions carry publicly observable, state-dependent semantics — a pending swap on an automated market maker is an explicit statement that a price is about to move — the public mempool functions as a broadcast channel of exploitable information. Rational *searchers* monitor this channel and compete to capture the value it reveals. Flashbots' quantification program documented hundreds of millions of dollars of extracted MEV, concentrated among a small set of bot operators and the producers who include their transactions [3].

Crucially, Daian et al. showed MEV is a *consensus-layer security* concern: when ordering rents exceed block rewards, the incentive-compatibility arguments of Nakamoto-style consensus break down, and rational producers may fork or undercut each other to capture MEV [1]. Post-Merge, the analogues are *proposer* extractable value, timing games, and ex-ante reorgs.

Proposer–Builder Separation is the architectural response. Specialized *builders* compete in an open auction to construct the highest-value block; the *proposer* commits to the winning block header without seeing its contents, collecting the winning bid [4][6]. The separation converts an opaque, expertise-driven rent into a transparent market: any validator can propose the most valuable block without knowing how to build it.

This thesis treats PBS as an object of mechanism design. What auction format governs builder competition? Does it maximize proposer revenue, minimize user harm, or resist centralization? How does private order flow reshape the game? What would it take to *enshrine* PBS in consensus, eliminating the trusted-relay bottleneck [4]? Our contributions: (i) a formal MEV taxonomy grounded in the empirical literature [1][2][3]; (ii) an auction-theoretic model of builder competition and order-flow auctions [7]; (iii) an assessment of builder-oligopoly centralization and censorship [5]; (iv) a critical evaluation of ePBS designs and their stability properties.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The MEV Taxonomy

Daian et al. formalized MEV through *priority gas auctions* (PGAs): continuous-time, partial-information auctions in which bots observe a profitable opportunity and bid up gas prices for priority ordering, sometimes in bursts of hundreds of milliseconds [1]. The Heimbach–Wattenhofer systemization of knowledge organizes the resulting strategies [2]:

| Strategy | Mechanism | Victim / Counterparty | Information channel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Atomic arbitrage** | Backrun DEX pools to exploit price discrepancies in one transaction | Inefficient pricing (no direct victim) | Public mempool |
| **Sandwich attack** | Frontrun a victim swap to inflate price, backrun to unwind | The victim trader (worse execution) | Public mempool |
| **Liquidations** | Repay undercollateralized positions for the liquidation bonus | The liquidated borrower | Public mempool (oracles) |
| **Priority gas auction** | Escalate gas bids to win ordering priority | Competing searchers; congested users | Public mempool |
| **Time-bandit / reorg MEV** | Rewrite history to recapture settled MEV | Finality of prior blocks | Consensus layer |
| **Cross-domain MEV** | Coordinate extraction across L1, L2s, CEX venues | Cross-venue price staleness | Multi-domain observation |
| **Private order flow** | Extract from exclusive flow sold by wallets/RPCs | Users whose flow is auctioned | Bilateral channels |

Most strategies are *competitive*: multiple searchers detect the same opportunity, and competition transfers surplus to the block producer via gas bidding or coinbase transfers [1][3]. They split into *victimless* strategies (arbitrage, arguably improving price discovery) and *predatory* ones (sandwiches, a direct tax on users) — a distinction motivating order-flow auctions that let users sell their own flow rather than be farmed by it [7].

### 2.2 Consensus Instability

The most consequential claim of "Flash Boys 2.0" concerns consensus: if block MEV exceeds the block reward, a rational producer may fork the chain to steal it (time-bandit attack) or undercut rivals, destabilizing fee markets [1]. In proof of stake, the pathologies include *ex-ante reorgs* — withholding a block to build an MEV-enriched private fork — and *timing games*, in which builders and proposers delay production to the last legal millisecond [5]. PBS neutralizes the first class: if the proposer commits to a builder's block *before* seeing its contents, it cannot steal the builder's MEV or profitably deviate from the highest-bid block.

### 2.3 The PBS Pipeline

The production implementation is **MEV-Boost**, middleware between consensus clients and a relay network [4][6]:

| Actor | Function | Revenue source |
|---|---|---|
| **User / searcher** | Submits transactions or bundles | Extracted MEV, trading profit |
| **Builder** | Assembles blocks from mempool + private bundles; bids for inclusion | Block MEV minus winning bid |
| **Relay** | Verifies builder blocks, runs the auction, forwards winning header + bid | Trusted intermediary (reputation) |
| **Proposer (validator)** | Signs the winning header blindly; publishes the full block after commitment | Winning bid + consensus rewards |

Per slot: builders submit `(block_header, bid_value, signature)` to relays; the relay validates the block and attests the bid is backed by real value; the proposer queries the highest bid and signs the header — a *proposer commitment* — without learning the body; only then does the relay release the full block [6].

> **Theorem (Blind commitment):** *If a proposer commits to a block header before observing the body, and the relay guarantees the bid is payable, then no unilateral proposer deviation can capture the builder's MEV without forfeiting the bid.* The assumptions — relay honesty, bid enforceability, timely release — are the pressure points analyzed in Sections 4 and 6.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis combines three methods:

1. **Literature synthesis.** The foundational MEV literature [1][2], Flashbots' measurement program [3], the Ethereum PBS roadmap [4], MEV-Boost/MEV-Share documentation [6][7], and empirical work on builder centralization [5]. All quantitative claims are sourced; no figures are invented.
2. **Auction-theoretic modeling.** Builder competition as a repeated sealed-bid auction with asymmetric information (builders differ in private order-flow access), covering equilibrium bidding under first-price rules, late-bidding timing games, and order-flow auction revenue properties.
3. **Mechanism evaluation.** PBS variants — trusted-relay MEV-Boost, MEV-Share/OFA redistribution, enshrined PBS — assessed on proposer revenue, user welfare, censorship resistance, and builder-set decentralization, grounded in published measurements.

Scope is limited to Ethereum mainnet PBS; other ecosystems are referenced only comparatively.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Builder Auction as a First-Price Sealed-Bid Game

Each slot, builders bid to the relay and the proposer accepts the maximum — structurally a **first-price sealed-bid auction** for the right to be proposed, with continuous bidding over the slot and "sealedness" enforced only by relay policy.

With `n` builders holding private valuations `v_i` drawn i.i.d. from `F` on `[0, v̄]`, the symmetric Bayesian Nash equilibrium bid is

$$b^*(v) = v - \frac{\int_0^v F(s)^{n-1}\,ds}{F(v)^{n-1}},$$

with shading decreasing in competition `n`. Two real-market features break this model. **Asymmetric information and common values:** builders share the public mempool (common-value component) but differ in *exclusive order flow* — private bundles from searchers, wallets, and RPCs. The informed builder bids less aggressively per unit of value, capturing information rents. **Repeated play:** slots repeat every 12 seconds; bids are public, so monitoring is excellent and the folk theorem admits collusive equilibria — a theoretical foundation for the oligopoly concern in Section 4.3.

Empirically, bids escalate sharply in the slot's final seconds as builders observe rivals' bids — the sealed-bid auction degrades into an *ascending* auction with a hard close, plus a timing game over when the proposer signs [5]. The proposer faces an optimal-stopping problem: waiting raises the expected winning bid but risks missing the attestation deadline.

```python
# Stylized builder auction: asymmetric order flow -> information rents
import numpy as np

def equilibrium_bid(v, n):
    s = np.linspace(0, v, 1000)
    return v - np.trapz(s ** (n - 1), s) / (v ** (n - 1) + 1e-12)

def slot_auction(builder_values):
    """builder_values: list of (value, exclusivity_bonus)."""
    bids = [equilibrium_bid(min(v + b, 1.0), len(builder_values)) + 0.5 * b
            for v, b in builder_values]
    w = max(range(len(bids)), key=lambda i: bids[i])
    return w, bids[w]  # first-price: proposer revenue = winning bid
```

Exclusive flow raises a builder's effective valuation *and* permits more aggressive shading, so surplus accrues to the informed builder rather than the proposer — the micro-foundation of builder centralization.

### 4.2 Order-Flow Auctions and MEV-Share: Redistributing the Surplus

If the builder auction determines *who* captures MEV, **order-flow auctions (OFAs)** determine *whose* MEV it is. A user's transaction is itself a valuable asset — it creates the sandwich and backrun opportunities searchers pay for. Rather than leaking that value to the mempool, the user (or wallet/RPC) can auction the *right to backrun* the transaction.

**MEV-Share**, Flashbots' OFA implementation, operationalizes this [7]. Users submit transactions with programmable privacy — specifying which fields searchers may see (protocol and pair, but not amount or sender) — and a refund condition. Searchers compete by submitting backrun bundles with a *kickback* returned to the user; the matcher selects the best backrun and refunds the user a share of the surplus:

1. User submits protected `tx` with hints `h` and minimum refund `r`.
2. Searchers observe `h`, submit `(backrun_i, kickback_i)`.
3. Matcher selects `argmax_i (payment_i)` subject to `kickback_i ≥ r`.
4. Winner's bundle goes to builders; user receives `kickback_winner`.

> **Proposition (OFA revenue dominance):** *Holding builder competition fixed, a competitive OFA weakly increases user welfare versus public-mempool submission, since the public mempool (kickback = 0) is always the outside option.* The binding constraint is searcher competition: with one searcher, the kickback collapses to the reserve `r`.

The deeper question is whether OFAs *reduce* MEV or merely *redistribute* it. A sandwich still executes; the victim's price impact is unchanged — but surplus flows partly to the victim as rebate. Worse, rebates may incentivize *originating* toxic flow: if wallets are paid per unit of exploited flow, they may optimize for flow toxicity — the *order-flow auction paradox*. The asset being auctioned (the right to exploit the user) is one the user would prefer not to create at all.

### 4.3 The Builder Oligopoly: Centralization Dynamics

The most empirically grounded critique of PBS is builder centralization. "From Competition to Centralization" documents an oligopoly in which a few builders win most slots, driven by economies of scale in *exclusive order flow* and latency infrastructure [5]. The feedback loop: more exclusive flow → more slots won → more attractive to searchers → *more* exclusive flow → better infrastructure and larger bids. A two-sided market with strong network effects, tipping toward oligopoly. Section 4.1's auction theory explains the loop's stability: the informed builder's rents grow with its flow advantage, and no uninformed entrant can profitably outbid it.

Centralization matters for three reasons. **Censorship:** dominant builders can delay or exclude transactions — several major relays and builders have filtered sanctioned addresses, so the capability is exercised, not theoretical. **Robustness:** a handful of relays are single points of failure; outages have caused missed slots. **Value leakage:** oligopolistic winning bids fall short of competitive levels, so proposers and stakers capture less MEV — the surplus is retained as builder profit.

Proposed mitigations include *inclusion lists* (proposer-forced transaction inclusion), *encrypted mempools* (denying builders the plaintext needed for selective censorship), and reputation markets punishing censoring builders via flow withdrawal [4]. None is deployed at scale.

### 4.4 Enshrined PBS: Removing the Trusted Relay

Current PBS trusts relays to validate blocks, hold bids honestly, and release bodies promptly. **Enshrined PBS (ePBS)** moves the auction into consensus itself, making builder bids slashable protocol messages [4]. The sketch: the beacon block carries an *execution payload bid* — a builder's commitment to deliver a payload for a stated payment — with the payload revealed later; non-reveal is punished by burning or slashing bonded stake. Key challenges: **bid enforcement** without trusted execution (fraud proofs or bond-and-slash with dispute windows); **timing and fork choice** (the beacon block must be valid and attestable before the payload arrives, so withholding cannot stall the chain); **builder staking and Sybil resistance** (bonds raise the capital barrier to entry, in tension with decentralization); and **MEV burn** variants that smooth MEV across stakers but weaken the proposer's incentive to wait for the best bid.

> **Design principle:** *Enshrinement trades a trust assumption (honest relays) for a complexity assumption (correct auction logic in consensus code).* The research community has not converged on whether that trade is favorable — which is why ePBS remains on the roadmap rather than in the protocol [4].

### 4.5 Game-Theoretic Stability of the Full Stack

Is the full system — users, searchers, builders, relays, proposers — in equilibrium, i.e., does any actor have a profitable unilateral deviation?

- **Proposer.** Best response: sign the highest relay bid before the attestation deadline. Deviations: local ("vanilla") building — profitable only for a competitive builder, which disciplines relay bids from below; delaying signature (timing game, bounded by attestation penalties); colluding with a builder off-protocol (requires trust, since the builder can defect post-commitment).
- **Builder.** Best response: bid near expected block value, invest in exclusive flow. Deviations: oligopolistic bid shading (stable per the folk theorem, since bids are public and monitoring is excellent); stealing searcher bundles (disciplined by flow withdrawal); censoring (profitable if bribes or regulatory pressure exceed lost flow).
- **Searcher.** Best response: route flow to the highest-win-rate builder. Deviation: vertical integration into building — observed in practice, collapsing the separation the architecture was meant to create.
- **User.** Best response: use OFA/MEV-protection RPCs. But collective action problems keep most flow in the public mempool, which is why toxic MEV persists.

The uncomfortable conclusion: PBS is *locally* incentive-compatible — each actor's myopic best response sustains the pipeline — but the sustained equilibrium is oligopolistic with vertical integration. The auction works; the market structure it produces is the problem.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

This section states the key quantitative findings of the cited literature and sketches the formal arguments behind our central claims.

**Scale of MEV.** Flashbots' MEV-Explore measured extracted MEV in the hundreds of millions of dollars, decomposed into arbitrage, liquidations, and sandwich attacks, documenting the searcher/producer surplus split [3]. Post-Merge, MEV-Boost relays publish per-slot winning bids, making proposer-side MEV publicly auditable — a transparency gain over the PGA era's opaque gas bidding [1][6].

**Builder concentration.** Measurement studies find a handful of builders produce most blocks, with concentration rising over time — consistent with the exclusive-flow feedback loop of Section 4.1 [5]. Winning bids arrive disproportionately late in the slot, confirming the timing-game prediction.

**Censorship evidence.** Builder and relay behavior around sanctioned addresses shows non-trivial censorship rates among the largest builders and relays — the Section 4.3 concern is realized, not hypothetical [5][4].

**Proof sketch — blind commitment (Section 2.3).** The proposer's information set at its decision node contains only `(header, bid, relay_attestation)`, not the block body. Any strategy conditioning extraction on the body's contents is infeasible there; signing the max bid dominates whenever `max bid > R' − R` (local-proposal payoff gap), which holds since builders bid positively. The proof breaks if the relay leaks the body early or bids are unenforceable — hence the trust assumptions.

**Equilibrium shading with exclusive flow (Section 4.1).** When builder 1's value distribution first-order stochastically dominates builder 2's, Maskin–Riley results give that the strong bidder bids less aggressively pointwise yet wins more often and earns higher surplus — while the auctioneer's (proposer's) expected revenue *falls* relative to the symmetric benchmark. Asymmetry taxes the proposer: the formal core of the claim that order-flow concentration diverts surplus to builders.

## 6 Limitations

The empirical picture is Ethereum-mainnet-specific and time-varying: builder shares, relay policies, and OFA adoption shift quarterly, and 2023–2024 measurement windows may not describe today's market [5]. Our auction model abstracts from the common-value component of the public mempool, multi-slot builder strategies, and cross-domain MEV spanning chains and CEXs — the independent-private-values analysis is a simplified benchmark. Censorship measurement is inherently lower-bound: we observe *exclusion*, not *intent*, and cannot separate OFAC compliance from profit-driven filtering. ePBS analysis is speculative — no enshrined design is deployed, and variants differ materially in game theory [4]. We treat searcher–builder vertical integration descriptively rather than modeling the integrated firm's joint optimization. Finally, we do not address L2 sequencer or non-Ethereum MEV, where centralized sequencing simplifies extraction but removes any credible neutrality commitment.

## 7 Conclusion

MEV began as an empirical observation — bots frontrunning DEX trades in priority gas auctions — and matured into the central problem of blockchain mechanism design [1][2]. Proposer–Builder Separation is the most ambitious attempt to solve it: replace the proposer's extraction privilege with a competitive auction, and let markets rather than expertise allocate ordering rights [4][6]. Auction theory clarifies both achievement and shortfall. The achievement is real: blind commitments neutralize the crudest proposer attacks, MEV-Boost bids are publicly auditable, and order-flow auctions let users reclaim part of the surplus extracted from their transactions [3][7]. The shortfall is structural: exclusive order flow turns the builder auction into an asymmetric game whose equilibrium is oligopoly; vertical integration collapses the separation; dominant-builder censorship is empirically observed [5]. Enshrined PBS removes the trusted relay but cannot fix the underlying information asymmetry — whoever controls exclusive flow wins the auction, in-protocol or out.

The frontier lies not in better auctions for a fixed information structure, but in changing the information structure: encrypted mempools, inclusion lists, and sequencing designs that minimize the extractable surface. Until the mempool stops broadcasting exploitable intent in plaintext, MEV remains what Daian et al. diagnosed — a tax on transparency, auctioned to the highest bidder [1].
