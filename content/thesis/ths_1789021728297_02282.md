# Mixture-of-Experts at Scale: Sparse Routing, Load Balancing, and the Systems Engineering of Switch Transformers

## Abstract

The mixture-of-experts (MoE) paradigm decouples a neural network's parameter count from its per-token computational cost, realizing *conditional computation* at industrial scale. This thesis traces the evolution of sparsely-activated MoE layers from the noisy top-$k$ gating of Shazeer et al. [1] through the Switch Transformer simplification [2], the automatic sharding of GShard [3], and the fine-grained expert designs of DeepSeekMoE [4], culminating in open-weight systems such as Mixtral 8x7B [5] and the DeepSpeed-MoE inference stack [6]. We formalize token-choice routing, expert capacity factors with token dropping, and the differentiable auxiliary load-balancing loss $\ell_{\mathrm{aux}} = \alpha N \sum_i f_i P_i$; analyze the all-to-all dispatch/combine communication pattern of expert parallelism and its $2kd$ bytes-per-token cost; and quantify the empirical record — 1.6-trillion-parameter sparse models, $7\times$ pre-training speedups at fixed compute, and $13$B-active models rivaling $70$B dense baselines. We close with a candid assessment of routing collapse, capacity-induced information loss, and the systems bottlenecks that still constrain sparse scaling.

## 1 Introduction

Deep learning has repeatedly validated a single empirical law: more parameters trained on more data generalize better [7]. Yet the dense Transformer couples *capacity* (parameters) to *cost* (FLOPs per token) linearly: every input activates every weight. Conditional computation — activating only a data-dependent subset per example — was proposed as early as 2013 as the escape hatch, but for years it remained a theory defeated by practical obstacles: GPU branch divergence, batch fragmentation, catastrophic load imbalance, and interconnect bottlenecks.

The **Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer**, introduced by Shazeer et al. in 2017 [1], was the first demonstration that conditional computation could work at production scale. A trainable gating network selects a sparse combination of *expert* feed-forward sub-networks per token, pushing a language model past **137 billion parameters** while holding per-example FLOPs comparable to a much smaller dense baseline. Three generations followed. **GShard** [3] industrialized MoE on accelerators: annotation-based automatic sharding in XLA trained a 600-billion-parameter multilingual translation Transformer on 2,048 TPU v3 cores in four days. The **Switch Transformer** [2] radicalized simplicity — routing each token to exactly *one* expert ($k=1$) — and with disciplined engineering reached 1.6 trillion parameters with a $7\times$ pre-training speedup over T5-XXL at equal compute. Finally, **Mixtral 8x7B** [5] (47B total, 13B active, matching LLaMA 2 70B) and **DeepSeekMoE** [4] (fine-grained segmentation plus shared experts) put sparse architectures atop open-weight leaderboards.

> **Theorem: (The Sparse Scaling Bargain).** Let a dense layer cost $C_{\mathrm{dense}} = 2T d d_{\mathrm{ff}}$ FLOPs for $T$ tokens. An MoE layer with $N$ experts and top-$k$ routing costs $C_{\mathrm{MoE}} = 2kT d d_{\mathrm{ff}} + O(TNd)$ routing overhead. The *parameter-efficiency ratio* $\rho = N/k$ measures how much capacity is gained per unit of compute; the *routing overhead* becomes negligible when $d_{\mathrm{ff}} \gg N/k$. Sparse models win whenever $\rho$ grows faster than the auxiliary costs of imbalance and communication.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys the background of conditional computation. Section 3 formalizes the MoE methodology: gating, capacity, and the auxiliary loss. Section 4 dissects five deep technical topics. Section 5 analyzes empirical results and complexity. Section 6 confronts the limitations honestly. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Conditional computation before MoE

The idea that different inputs should traverse different sub-circuits predates the modern era. Bengio's conditional computation program (2013–2015) argued that *statistical* capacity could be decoupled from *computational* cost if gating decisions were learned jointly with the experts. Early attempts failed on common ground: **differentiability through discrete routing decisions**. A router that makes a hard choice has zero gradient almost everywhere, and the relaxations of the era (REINFORCE with high variance, biased straight-through estimators) were too unstable at scale.

The mixture-of-experts formulation traces back to Jacobs et al. (1991), but the classical MoE was *dense*: every expert was evaluated and softly weighted. Shazeer et al.'s contribution was to make the gate *sparse* and the experts *massive* — thousands of them — so that sparsity was the load-bearing architectural principle, not an optimization trick [1].

### 2.2 The Transformer FFN as the natural host

In modern practice, the MoE layer almost always replaces the position-wise feed-forward network (FFN) of a Transformer block: attention is already all-to-all over the sequence and resists sparsification, whereas the FFN consumes roughly two-thirds of parameters and FLOPs while operating *token-independently* — a perfect host for per-token routing. Every design here keeps dense attention and sparsifies only the FFN, sometimes every layer, sometimes every other layer to amortize routing overhead.

### 2.3 Definitions and notation

We fix the following notation throughout:

- $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$: a single token representation at a given layer.
- $N$: number of experts; $\{E_i(\cdot)\}_{i=1}^N$ are expert FFNs, each $E_i(x) = W_{i,2}\,\sigma(W_{i,1} x)$ with $W_{i,1} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{ff}} \times d}$, $W_{i,2} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{\mathrm{ff}}}$.
- $W_g \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$: the router (gating) weight matrix.
- $k$: top-$k$ routing arity; $k=1$ is *Switch routing*, $k=2$ is standard top-2.
- $T$: tokens per batch (batch size $\times$ sequence length); $f$: capacity factor.
- $f_i$: fraction of tokens dispatched to expert $i$; $P_i$: mean gate probability assigned to expert $i$.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Noisy top-$k$ gating

The Shazeer et al. gate [1] injects tunable Gaussian noise before the top-$k$ selection to encourage exploration and load balance:

$$
G(x) \;=\; \mathrm{Softmax}\big(\mathrm{TopK}(W_g x \;+\; \epsilon \odot \mathrm{Softplus}(W_{\mathrm{noise}}\,x),\, k)\big),
$$

where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and $\mathrm{TopK}(v, k)$ zeroes all but the $k$ largest entries (setting the rest to $-\infty$). The MoE output is the gate-weighted sum:

$$
y \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} G(x)_i \, E_i(x).
$$

The noise term breaks ties between nearly identical gate scores and — more importantly — makes the *load* differentiable, enabling the second auxiliary loss below.

### 3.2 Load-balancing auxiliary losses

Without intervention, the router collapses: popular experts receive more tokens, get more gradient, and become more popular. Shazeer et al. introduced two auxiliary losses [1], both based on the squared coefficient of variation $\mathrm{CV}^2$:

1. **Importance loss**: $w_{\mathrm{importance}} \cdot \mathrm{CV}^2(\mathrm{Importance})$, where $\mathrm{Importance}_i = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{B}} G(x)_i$ is the total gate mass assigned to expert $i$ over batch $\mathcal{B}$.
2. **Load loss**: $w_{\mathrm{load}} \cdot \mathrm{CV}^2(\mathrm{Load})$, where $\mathrm{Load}_i$ is the *expected* number of tokens assigned to expert $i$, computed differentiably from the noise distribution.

The Switch Transformer streamlined this to a single elegant term [2]:

$$
\ell_{\mathrm{aux}} \;=\; \alpha \, N \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i \, P_i,
$$

with $f_i$ the empirical dispatch fraction, $P_i$ the mean router probability for expert $i$, and $\alpha = 10^{-2}$. Under perfect balance ($f_i = P_i = 1/N$), the loss equals $\alpha$; any skew increases it, and because both terms are differentiable in the router weights, gradient descent actively repairs imbalance.

```python
def load_balancing_loss(gate_logits, expert_indices, num_experts, alpha=0.01):
    # Fraction of tokens dispatched to each expert
    counts = torch.bincount(expert_indices.flatten(), minlength=num_experts)
    f_i = counts.float() / expert_indices.numel()
    # Mean gate probability assigned to each expert
    P_i = torch.softmax(gate_logits, dim=-1).mean(dim=0)
    return alpha * num_experts * (f_i * P_i).sum()
```

### 3.3 Expert capacity and token dropping

Dynamic routing produces *ragged* tensors — each expert receives a different token count, which GPUs handle poorly. The capacity mechanism [2][3] converts dynamic assignment into statically-shaped buffers by imposing a per-expert token budget:

$$
\mathrm{capacity}_i \;=\; \left\lceil \frac{k \cdot T \cdot f}{N} \right\rceil,
$$

where $f$ is the **capacity factor**. Tokens beyond capacity are *dropped* (their representation passes through unchanged via the residual connection). Standard practice sets $f = 1.25$ during training and $f = 2.0$ at evaluation [2]. The capacity factor is the central engineering knob of sparse MoE: $f \to 1$ minimizes padding waste but drops more tokens; larger $f$ preserves tokens at the cost of wasted expert compute on padding. Well-tuned Switch models drop under $1\%$ of tokens [2].

| Design | Routing arity $k$ | Experts $N$ | Capacity factor $f$ | Aux loss form |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shazeer et al. (2017) [1] | top-2..4 | up to 2,048 | — (loss-driven) | CV$^2$ importance + load |
| GShard (2020) [3] | top-2 | up to 2,048 | group capacity | CV$^2$-style |
| Switch Transformer (2021) [2] | **top-1** | up to 2,048 | 1.25 / 2.0 | $\alpha N \sum f_i P_i$ |
| Mixtral 8x7B (2024) [5] | top-2 | 8 | capacity + aux loss | $\alpha N \sum f_i P_i$ |
| DeepSeekMoE (2024) [4] | top-$mK$ (fine-grained) | $mN$ + $K_s$ shared | capacity + aux loss | $\alpha N \sum f_i P_i$ |

### 3.4 Expert parallelism: dispatch and combine

With thousands of experts, no single device holds them all. **Expert parallelism** partitions experts across devices; each hosts $N/D$ experts for $D$ devices. A forward pass then needs two collectives per MoE layer:

1. **Dispatch** (all-to-all): each device sends its tokens to the devices owning their selected experts, packing them into capacity-sized buffers.
2. **Combine** (all-to-all): expert outputs are weighted by gate values and returned to the tokens' home devices.

For $k$ experts per token, hidden size $d$, and $b$ bytes per element, per-token communication is $2 \cdot k \cdot d \cdot b$ bytes — *independent of $N$*. This is why MoE scales: adding experts costs parameters and memory, never communication. The pain point is that all-to-all is bandwidth-bound on inter-node links, which motivated hierarchical 2D all-to-all algorithms (e.g., Tutel [6]) and the reduction to $k=1$ in Switch.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Noisy Top-$k$ Routing and the Birth of Sparse Conditional Computation

The noisy gate was a deliberate *hack with a theory behind it*: by adding input-dependent noise, the probability that expert $i$ is selected becomes a smooth function of the router weights, so expected load is differentiable and the load loss can push it toward uniformity. The paper reached 2,048 experts per layer and 137 billion parameters, beating the 1B Word Benchmark at roughly $8\%$ of the compute of the dense baseline it displaced.

Yet the design had two weaknesses later work attacked: it imposed **no hard capacity constraint** — balance relied on soft losses alone — and it said almost nothing about distributed systems, leaving the all-to-all machinery for GShard to solve [3].

### 4.2 Top-1 Switch Routing and the Expert Capacity Factor

Fedus, Zoph, and Shazeer [2] asked a heretical question: *is the second expert worth it?* Their answer — no — rested on systems, not modeling: the marginal quality gain of $k=2$ over $k=1$ was smaller than the cost of a second all-to-all round trip. Switch routing halves dispatch/combine traffic and lets the gate output double as the expert output's scalar multiplier:

$$
y \;=\; p_{i^\star}(x) \, E_{i^\star}(x), \qquad i^\star = \arg\max_i \; \mathrm{softmax}(W_g x)_i.
$$

The capacity mechanism was hardened into a first-class citizen: with statically-sized expert buffers, accelerator compilation becomes tractable, and token dropping becomes a *designed* behavior rather than a failure mode. At $f = 1.25$, well-tuned Switch models drop under $1\%$ of tokens for only $25\%$ slack — a bargain versus dynamic shapes.

The Switch paper's underappreciated contribution is its **training-stability triad**: (i) computing the router softmax in float32 while the rest trains in bfloat16; (ii) shrinking initialization $10\times$; and (iii) heavier dropout inside experts (0.4) than attention (0.1). Without these, trillion-parameter sparse training diverges [2][4].

### 4.3 Expert Parallelism: All-to-All Dispatch and Combine at Scale

Expert parallelism composes with data parallelism: data-parallel groups shard the batch while expert-parallel groups shard the experts; GShard's SPMD partitioning [3] expresses both through sharding annotations on a single program, letting XLA insert the collectives automatically.

The dispatch all-to-all is the crux. Each device holds $T/D$ tokens; after routing, it must send token $t$ to device $\lfloor i^\star(t) / (N/D) \rfloor$. Because routing is dynamic, message sizes are irregular — but the capacity mechanism regularizes them: every (device, expert) pair exchanges exactly $\mathrm{capacity}_i$ token slots, turning an irregular sparse communication pattern into a dense, predictable one, at the cost of padding.

Two refinements matter in practice:

- **Hierarchical (2D) all-to-all** [6]: tokens are first exchanged within a node over fast interconnects, then across nodes, cutting inter-node traffic.
- **Capacity-aware dropping at dispatch**: tokens exceeding capacity are dropped *before* the all-to-all, so the network never carries tokens that would be discarded.

The total per-layer communication for a batch is $2 \cdot (k T f d b)/1$ bytes moved across the expert-parallel group, where the factor of 2 covers dispatch and combine. For a Switch-Base-scale layer ($d = 768$, $T = 2^{18}$, $f = 1.25$, bf16), this is on the order of hundreds of megabytes per layer per step — large, but dwarfed by the *trillions* of parameters that would otherwise need to move.

### 4.4 Fine-Grained Expert Segmentation and Shared Experts

DeepSeekMoE [4] identified a subtle inefficiency in conventional MoE: with coarse experts and small $k$, the $\binom{N}{k}$ combinations of activated experts are too few for compositional knowledge. Their fix has two parts:

1. **Fine-grained segmentation**: split each expert's FFN intermediate dimension by a factor $m$, producing $mN$ experts, and activate $mK$ of them. Parameter count and per-token FLOPs are unchanged, but the number of possible expert *combinations* explodes combinatorially, enabling far more flexible knowledge composition. Ablations show monotonic gains as segmentation is refined from 16 to 64 experts [4].
2. **Shared experts**: isolate $K_s$ experts that *every* token activates, capturing common knowledge (syntax, formatting, frequent facts) that would otherwise be redundantly re-learned by many routed experts. This reduces redundancy among routed experts and sharpens their specialization.

The results are striking: DeepSeekMoE 2B matches GShard 2.9B despite $1.5\times$ fewer expert parameters and FLOPs; DeepSeekMoE 16B matches LLaMA2 7B at roughly $40\%$ of the compute; and a 145B variant matches the dense DeepSeek 67B using only $28.5\%$ of the computation [4].

### 4.5 Training at Trillion Scale: Stability, Precision, and Distillation

Training sparse models at the trillion-parameter frontier is a systems achievement as much as a modeling one. Switch-C — 1.6 trillion parameters across 2,048 experts — required the full stability triad plus bfloat16 training, a first for sparse models [2]. Headline numbers: up to $7\times$ faster pre-training than T5-XXL at identical compute, and $4\times$ end-to-end speedup for the 1.6T model.

Two practical notes round out the picture. **Inference** is where MoE's economics shine or die: DeepSpeed-MoE [6] demonstrated $5\times$ training-cost savings for decoder-only models, $3.7\times$ model-size reduction via compression, and $7.3\times$ better serving latency — $4.5\times$ faster and $9\times$ cheaper than quality-equivalent dense models. And **distillation** offers an exit ramp: Switch showed sparse teachers distill cleanly into dense students, recovering most of the quality gain in a deployment-friendly form [2].

## 5 Empirical Results and Complexity Analysis

### 5.1 The empirical record

| Model | Year | Total params | Active params/token | Routing | Key result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shazeer et al. [1] | 2017 | 137B | ~8B equiv. | noisy top-$k$ | SOTA 1B Word Benchmark at lower compute |
| GShard [3] | 2020 | 600B | ~2 experts/layer | top-2 | 100-lang NMT SOTA; 4 days on 2,048 TPU v3 |
| Switch-C [2] | 2021 | 1.6T | 1 expert/layer | top-1 | $7\times$ pre-train speedup vs T5-XXL |
| Mixtral 8x7B [5] | 2024 | 46.7B | 12.9B | top-2 | $\geq$ LLaMA 2 70B, GPT-3.5; 32k context |

| DeepSeekMoE 145B [4] | 2024 | 145B | ~22B | fine-grained + shared | $\approx$ DeepSeek 67B at 28.5% compute |

### 5.2 Complexity analysis

Let $T$ be tokens per batch, $d$ model width, $d_{\mathrm{ff}}$ expert intermediate size, $N$ experts, $k$ routing arity, $f$ capacity factor, $D$ expert-parallel devices.

- **Compute (FLOPs/token/layer)**: $4 k d d_{\mathrm{ff}}$ for the experts plus $2Nd$ for the router. The router term is negligible whenever $d_{\mathrm{ff}} \gg N/(2k)$.
- **Parameters**: $2 N d d_{\mathrm{ff}}$ — grows linearly in $N$ at zero marginal compute cost.
- **Memory per device**: $2 (N/D) d d_{\mathrm{ff}}$ parameters plus optimizer state; expert parallelism keeps this constant as $N$ scales with $D$.
- **Communication**: $2 k T f d b / D$ bytes per device per all-to-all round, two rounds (dispatch, combine) per MoE layer.
- **Auxiliary loss**: $O(N)$ per step — negligible.

The fundamental scaling law of MoE is therefore: *capacity scales with $N \cdot D$, cost scales with $k \cdot T$, and communication scales with $k \cdot T \cdot f$*. The expert count $N$ is the free variable — it grows without increasing per-step FLOPs or interconnect traffic. Its true costs are memory (mitigated by expert parallelism) and *statistical*: each expert sees only $\approx kT/N$ tokens per batch, so beyond some $N$ experts become data-starved — the regime where Switch-C's 2,048 experts began to underperform smaller configurations downstream [2].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Routing collapse and the auxiliary-loss tax.** The $\ell_{\mathrm{aux}}$ term is a soft constraint, not a guarantee. Under distribution shift — especially fine-tuning on narrow domains — routers can still collapse onto a few experts, and the auxiliary loss then fights the task loss directly. The coefficient $\alpha$ must be re-tuned per regime, with no principled theory for its setting.
2. **Token dropping is information loss.** Dropped tokens bypass the expert via the residual stream, losing the layer's nonlinear transformation entirely. At $f = 1.25$, dropping concentrates on the most "popular" experts — precisely the tokens the router deemed most in need of specialized processing. Evaluation uses $f = 2.0$ to mitigate this, introducing a train/eval mismatch that is rarely quantified.
3. **All-to-all remains the systems bottleneck.** Expert parallelism's communication is linear in $k$, $T$, and $f$. On clusters without high-bandwidth interconnects, dispatch/combine collectives dominate step time, and the parameter efficiency evaporates into network stalls.
4. **Expert under-specialization at extreme $N$.** Each expert's effective batch size is $kT/N$ tokens. As $N$ grows into the thousands, individual experts see too little data to specialize, and the model degenerates toward an expensive ensemble of under-trained FFNs. Switch-C's downstream regression versus smaller Switch variants is the canonical warning [2].
5. **Fine-tuning fragility.** Sparse models historically transferred worse than dense ones; Switch [2] showed the gap can be closed with careful protocols, but sensitivity to learning rates and the auxiliary loss during adaptation remains higher than for dense models.
6. **Inference memory vs. compute asymmetry.** MoE inference is compute-cheap but memory-hungry: all $N$ experts must reside in accelerator memory even though only $k$ fire per token. Serving systems [6] mitigate this with offloading and compression, but the working-set problem is fundamental.

## 7 Conclusion

The mixture-of-experts trajectory from 2017 to 2024 shows how *systems constraints shape modeling ideas*. The noisy top-$k$ gate [1] made sparse routing trainable; GShard [3] made it distributable; Switch [2] made it simple enough to reach a trillion parameters; DeepSeekMoE [4] made the experts finer and less redundant; and Mixtral [5] with DeepSpeed-MoE [6] made sparse models open and servable. The core bargain never changed: parameters are cheap, FLOPs are expensive, and the router arbitrages between them.

The open problems are now about *how far the bargain extends*: collapse-proof routing without auxiliary taxes, token-adaptive capacity budgets that never drop informative tokens, and expert parallelism for inference-dominated workloads where memory is the binding constraint. The MoE layer has earned its place as a first-class primitive of large-scale deep learning — the mechanism by which the field learned to build models larger than any single dense architecture could support.
