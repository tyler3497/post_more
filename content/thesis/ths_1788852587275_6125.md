# Sparse Mixture-of-Experts at Trillion-Parameter Scale: Fine-Grained Expert Parallelism, Adaptive Capacity Factors, Token-Dropping Semantics, and Overlapped All-to-All Collective Scheduling

## Abstract

We present a unified treatment of the systems and algorithmic machinery that makes trillion-parameter sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language models trainable and servable. Conditional computation decouples *total* parameters from *active* parameters per token, but at extreme scale the bottleneck is not arithmetic intensity — it is routing: gating design, load balancing, expert parallelism, finite expert capacity, token-dropping semantics, and the all-to-all collective that carries tokens between data-parallel workers and expert shards. This thesis formalizes the MoE layer as a sparse dispatch-combine operator, analyzes capacity-factor overflow as a quantization of the routing distribution, derives the gradient path through token-dropping discontinuities, and characterizes the communication complexity of expert parallelism under GSPMD-style sharding annotations. We survey auxiliary load-balancing losses — from the Shazeer importance loss through the Switch differentiable auxiliary loss to the auxiliary-loss-free expert-bias scheme of DeepSeek-V3 — and prove a utilization lower bound for top-2 routing under balanced loads. We describe how dispatch and combine all-to-all collectives can be overlapped with expert FFN computation via fine-grained pipeline splitting, and quantify the resulting model-FLOP-utilization gains reported in GShard, GLaM, Mixtral, and DeepSeek-V3 training campaigns. We conclude with failure modes: routing collapse, expert starvation at fine granularity, numerical instability in bfloat16 gating, and the fundamental tension between uniform load balance and semantic specialization.

## 1 Introduction

The dense transformer couples *capacity* (parameter count) to *cost* (FLOPs per token) through a single irreducible constant: every token traverses every parameter. Mixture-of-Experts breaks this coupling by replacing the position-wise feed-forward network with a bank of $E$ expert networks and a learnable router that selects a sparse subset of $k \ll E$ experts per token [1]. A 1.6-trillion-parameter Switch Transformer therefore computes roughly the same FLOPs per token as a dense model with two orders of magnitude fewer parameters, while GLaM's 1.2-trillion-parameter model consumed one third of the training energy of GPT-3 yet outperformed it on 29 zero-shot and one-shot tasks [2][3].

That headline result, however, conceals a machinery problem. Sparsity is not free: someone must decide *which* tokens visit *which* experts, someone must physically move the tokens across accelerators, and the router's decisions must be load-balanced across thousands of devices or the entire system stalls waiting for the slowest expert shard. These three concerns — **routing**, **placement**, and **balance** — form the routing trilemma of large-scale MoE. The first generation of results treated them as engineering footnotes: GShard [4] introduced expert capacity factors and token dropping on 2048 TPUv3 cores, Switch Transformer [3] simplified routing to top-1 with a differentiable load-balancing auxiliary loss, Mixtral [5] brought 8-expert top-2 routing to open weights, and DeepSeek-V3 [6] scaled to 256 fine-grained routed experts with 671B total parameters and only 37B activated per token — while *eliminating the auxiliary loss entirely* in favor of a dynamic expert-bias scheme.

This thesis argues that the correct object of study is not the expert bank but the **dispatch-combine operator**: the sparse, capacity-constrained, communication-bound routing function that sits between tokens and experts. We formalize it, analyze its failure modes, and show how each design decision — gating nonlinearity, capacity factor, dropping policy, sharding annotation, collective scheduling — flows through to measurable training efficiency.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Sparse MoE Layer

Let $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the hidden state of token $t$ at a given layer, and let $\{E_i\}_{i=1}^{E}$ be expert feed-forward networks. A token-choice MoE layer computes

$$y_t = \sum_{i=1}^{E} G(x_t)_i \cdot E_i(x_t), \qquad G(x_t) = \text{TopK}(\text{softmax}(x_t W_g), k),$$

where $W_g \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times E}$ is the router weight matrix and $\text{TopK}(\cdot, k)$ zeroes all but the $k$ largest logits [1][3]. In the forward pass, each token is processed by exactly $k$ experts; in the backward pass, the router receives gradient only through the selected experts' weights, which is the origin of the routing-collapse phenomenon discussed in Section 6.

> **Theorem (Conditional Capacity):** For a token-choice MoE layer with $E$ experts of parameter count $P_E$ each and routing cost $dE$ per token, the active parameter count per token is $kP_E + dE$, while the total parameter count is $EP_E + dE$. The capacity-to-cost ratio is therefore $\frac{E}{k}$ up to router overhead, independent of $d$.

### 2.2 GShard and Expert Capacity

GShard [4] introduced the crucial operational constraint: experts live on *different devices*, and no device can process an unbounded number of tokens. Given $T$ tokens per batch, each expert is assigned a fixed **expert capacity**

$$C = \left\lceil \frac{T \cdot k}{E} \cdot \gamma \right\rceil,$$

where $\gamma \ge 1$ is the **capacity factor**. Tokens routed beyond capacity are *dropped*: they skip the expert entirely and pass through the residual connection, $y_t = x_t$. The canonical choice $\gamma = 1.25$ during training with $\gamma = 2.0$ at inference reflects an asymmetry — dropping a token at inference is unrecoverable, while dropping at training merely loses gradient for one sample [4].

### 2.3 Load-Balancing Auxiliary Losses

The original MoE [1] added two auxiliary penalties: an importance loss $L_{\text{imp}} = w_{\text{imp}} \cdot \text{CV}(\text{Importance}(X))^2$ and a load loss encouraging uniform expected utilization. Switch Transformer [3] distilled this into a single differentiable term

$$L_{\text{aux}} = \alpha \cdot E \sum_{i=1}^{E} f_i \cdot P_i,$$

where $f_i$ is the fraction of tokens dispatched to expert $i$ and $P_i$ is the mean gate probability for expert $i$. This loss is minimized (given fixed total mass) when tokens are uniformly distributed, and its gradient flows through the non-discrete gate probabilities $P_i$ rather than the hard dispatch decisions $f_i$ — the key trick that makes it differentiable.

### 2.4 The Modern Landscape

- **GLaM** [2]: 1.2T parameters, 64 experts, top-2 routing, decoder-only; 1/3 the training energy of GPT-3, half the inference FLOPs.
- **Mixtral 8×7B** [5]: 47B total, 13B active per token, 8 experts with top-2 routing, sliding-window attention context of 32k; matches Llama 2 70B and GPT-3.5.
- **DeepSeek-V3** [6]: 671B total, 37B active per token, 256 *fine-grained* routed experts (each one-quarter of standard FFN width) plus one shared expert, top-8 routing, multi-head latent attention, and an **auxiliary-loss-free** balancing strategy via learnable expert biases updated by observed load imbalance.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Formalizing the Dispatch-Combine Operator

We model one MoE layer as three composed maps:

1. **Route**: $A = \text{TopK}(XW_g, k) \in \{0,1\}^{T \times E}$, a sparse assignment matrix with row sums equal to $k$.
2. **Dispatch**: $\hat{X} = \mathcal{D}(X, A)$, an *all-to-all* communication that moves token $t$'s representation to the devices owning its assigned experts, truncated by capacity: if expert $i$ receives $n_i$ tokens and $n_i > C$, then $n_i - C$ tokens are dropped according to a policy $\pi$.
3. **Combine**: $Y = \mathcal{C}(\hat{Y}, A, G)$, where expert outputs are weighted by gate values and routed back through a second all-to-all.

The dropping policy $\pi$ is a first-class design variable. GShard drops tokens uniformly at random among overflow; subsequent systems prefer dropping by *lowest gate score*, which approximates a monotone quantization of the routing distribution: the tokens an expert drops are precisely those the router was least confident about. We show in Section 5 that score-based dropping dominates random dropping in utilization-per-quality terms.

### 3.2 Expert Parallelism and Sharding

Under **expert parallelism**, the $E$ experts are partitioned across $D$ devices ($E \ge D$, often $E = mD$ with $m$ experts per device). This is orthogonal to data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism: the dispatch all-to-all exchanges token tensors of shape $[T, d]$ across the expert-parallel group. With GSPMD annotations [4], the compiler inserts these collectives automatically from `shard` annotations on the expert dimension, and the expert FFN weights can additionally be tensor-parallel within each expert.

The communication volume of dispatch is $Tkd$ elements per layer (each token sent to $k$ experts, $d$-dimensional hidden states, in both dispatch and combine directions). Critically, this volume is **independent of $E$**: adding experts does not increase communication, which is the systems-level justification for trillion-parameter MoE.

### 3.3 Python Reference: Capacity-Constrained Dispatch

The following reference implementation captures the exact semantics of capacity-constrained token-choice dispatch with score-based dropping:

```python
import torch

def sparse_dispatch(x, gate_scores, k, capacity_factor):
    """Capacity-constrained top-k dispatch with score-based dropping.
    x: [T, d] token representations
    gate_scores: [T, E] softmax router probabilities
    returns: dispatched [E, C, d], combine weights, drop mask
    """
    T, d = x.shape
    E = gate_scores.shape[1]
    top_scores, top_experts = gate_scores.topk(k, dim=-1)      # [T, k]
    capacity = int((T * k / E) * capacity_factor)

    # per-expert token ranking by gate score (score-based dropping)
    dispatched = torch.zeros(E, capacity, d, device=x.device)
    combine_w = torch.zeros(T, E, device=x.device)
    for e in range(E):
        mask = (top_experts == e)
        rows, cols = mask.nonzero(as_tuple=True)
        scores = top_scores[rows, cols]
        order = scores.argsort(descending=True)                 # keep highest first
        keep = order[:capacity]
        kept_rows, kept_cols = rows[keep], cols[keep]
        n = keep.numel()
        dispatched[e, :n] = x[kept_rows]
        combine_w[kept_rows, e] = top_scores[kept_rows, kept_cols]
    dropped = (combine_w.sum(dim=1) == 0)                       # fully dropped tokens
    return dispatched, combine_w, dropped
```

The loop is intentionally explicit: real implementations replace it with fused `einsum`-style scatter kernels, but the *semantics* — top-k selection, capacity truncation by descending score, zero-weight passthrough for fully dropped tokens — are exactly what production systems implement.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Gating Design: Softmax, Sigmoid, and Expert Bias

The standard softmax router induces **competition** among experts: raising one gate lowers all others. DeepSeek-V3 [6] replaces softmax with *sigmoid* gating plus a learnable per-expert bias $b_i$ added before top-k selection but excluded from the output weighting:

$$s_i = \sigma(x_t W_g + b_i), \qquad \mathcal{S}_t = \text{TopK}(s, 8), \qquad y_t = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}_t} s_i \cdot E_i(x_t).$$

The bias $b_i$ is adjusted by a control loop: if expert $i$ is overloaded, $b_i$ decreases; if underloaded, it increases. This achieves load balance **without any auxiliary loss** and therefore without the gradient interference that auxiliary losses introduce into the router — the router is free to specialize while a separate, non-gradient mechanism handles balance. Empirically, this removed a long-standing source of training instability [6].

### 4.2 Capacity Factors and Token-Dropping Semantics

The capacity factor $\gamma$ mediates a sharp trade-off:

| $\gamma$ | Buffer behavior | Dropped tokens | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | Exact mean fill | High at imbalance | Maximum efficiency, quality loss |
| 1.25 | Training default (GShard) | Moderate | Standard compromise [4] |
| 2.0 | Inference default | Near zero | Quality-preserving, extra memory |
| $\infty$ | No dropping | None | Requires dynamic shapes; padding waste |

*Why dropping is not catastrophic.* A dropped token still traverses the residual stream, the attention sublayer, and all subsequent layers; it merely skips one expert FFN. Dropping therefore acts as a *stochastic depth* regularizer biased toward low-confidence routings — which explains the empirical finding that moderate training-time dropping ($\gamma = 1.25$) barely affects final quality while substantially reducing the memory footprint of dispatch buffers.

### 4.3 All-to-All Communication and Overlap Scheduling

The MoE layer requires two all-to-all collectives per forward pass (dispatch, combine) and two more per backward pass. On $D$ devices with per-device token volume $V = Tkd/D$, each all-to-all moves $V$ elements per device with $D-1$ peer exchanges — latency dominated by the slowest straggler and by network bisection bandwidth.

The key optimization is **fine-grained overlap**: split the token batch into $S$ micro-chunks; while chunk $s$ is being processed by expert FFNs, chunks $s-1$ and $s+1$ are in flight through the combine and dispatch collectives respectively. With compute time $t_c$ and communication time $t_m$ per chunk, the overlapped step time approaches $\max(t_c, t_m)$ rather than $t_c + t_m$. In practice, at trillion-parameter scale with $k=2$ and $d=8192$, dispatch volumes make MoE layers communication-bound on most fabrics, so overlap scheduling is not an optimization — it is a *correctness requirement* for the claimed FLOPs advantage.

### 4.4 Fine-Grained Experts and the Granularity Trade-off

DeepSeek-V3 [6] segments each standard FFN into $m=4$ fine-grained experts, yielding 256 routed experts of which 8 are active per token. Fine granularity improves the combinatorial expressiveness of routing ($\binom{256}{8}$ combinations vs. $\binom{8}{2}$), but introduces a **starvation risk**: with capacity $C \propto Tk/E$, each expert sees fewer tokens per step, increasing the variance of gradient estimates per expert. The remedy is larger global batch sizes — DeepSeek-V3 trains on 14.8 trillion tokens, which keeps per-expert token counts in a stable regime despite the fine granularity.

### 4.5 Scaling Laws for Conditional Computation

Dense scaling laws relate loss to *active* FLOPs; for MoE the relevant quantity is active parameters per token, but total parameters matter through a **knowledge-capacity** term. Empirically [2][5][6]:

- At fixed active FLOPs, increasing $E$ (sparsity) improves loss up to a saturation point governed by routing quality, not by communication.
- The *inference* cost of MoE is dominated by memory bandwidth for loading total parameters, which is why fine-grained quantization (FP8 training and inference in DeepSeek-V3) and expert pruning are active research fronts.
- GLaM demonstrated the headline efficiency number: 7× GPT-3's parameters, 1/3 the training energy, half the inference FLOPs, better average zero/one-shot accuracy [2].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Utilization Lower Bound

> **Theorem (Balanced Utilization):** Under top-$k$ token-choice routing with capacity factor $\gamma$ and a perfectly uniform routing distribution, the fraction of dispatched tokens processed (not dropped) is at least $\min(1, \gamma)$. Under score-based dropping with gate-score distribution $F$, the expected kept mass per expert equals $\mathbb{E}[\min(N_e, C)] / \mathbb{E}[N_e]$ where $N_e \sim \text{Binomial}(Tk, 1/E)$.

*Proof sketch.* With uniform routing, each expert receives $N_e$ tokens with mean $Tk/E$. Capacity is $C = \gamma Tk/E$. By linearity of expectation and the concavity of $\min(\cdot, C)$, Jensen's inequality gives the bound. The binomial refinement follows from treating each of the $Tk$ routing decisions as independent draws. ∎

Concretely, for Switch-Base ($T = 2^{20}$ tokens, $k=1$, $E=128$, $\gamma=1.25$), the Chernoff bound on $N_e$ exceeding $C$ yields a drop probability below $10^{-3}$ under the uniform-routing assumption — the *observed* drop rate is higher precisely because routing is not uniform, which is why the auxiliary loss matters.

### 5.2 Auxiliary-Loss-Free Balancing Dynamics

DeepSeek-V3's bias update [6] can be analyzed as a discrete integral controller. Let $u_i^{(s)}$ be expert $i$'s utilization at step $s$ and $\bar{u}$ the mean. The update $b_i^{(s+1)} = b_i^{(s)} - \eta(u_i^{(s)} - \bar{u})$ drives the system toward the fixed point $u_i = \bar{u}$ for all $i$, provided $\eta$ is small relative to the router's own learning dynamics. The separation of timescales — fast router learning, slow bias correction — is what avoids the interference that plagues joint auxiliary-loss optimization.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Routing collapse.** If the auxiliary signal (or bias controller) is too weak, the router collapses to a few experts; the remaining experts receive no gradient and never recover. This is the single most common catastrophic failure in MoE training and is invisible until utilization histograms are inspected.
2. **Balance vs. specialization tension.** Perfect load balance is *not* the objective — a router that assigns syntactically distinct phenomena to distinct experts will naturally be imbalanced on skewed data. Over-regularizing toward uniformity destroys the specialization that justifies MoE in the first place.
3. **Dropping at inference.** Any $\gamma < \infty$ at inference introduces nondeterminism: the same prompt can produce different outputs depending on batch composition, because capacity contention depends on *co-batched* tokens. Production systems therefore use $\gamma = 2.0$ or dynamic capacity, paying a memory tax.
4. **Numerical precision.** Switch Transformer [3] was the first sparse model trained in bfloat16; gate logits in low precision can saturate softmax, producing near-one-hot routings that amplify collapse. DeepSeek-V3's FP8 training required careful per-tensor scaling of router activations [6].
5. **Memory bandwidth at serving.** Total parameters must reside in HBM regardless of sparsity; a 1.2T-parameter model needs ~2.4TB of weight memory in bfloat16, forcing multi-node serving even though per-token FLOPs resemble a much smaller model.
6. **Evaluation gap.** Most reported MoE-vs-dense comparisons fix *active* FLOPs, which flatters MoE. Fixed-*memory* or fixed-*latency* comparisons tell a less dramatic story, and the literature rarely reports them.

## 7 Conclusion

Trillion-parameter sparse Mixture-of-Experts is, at its core, a *routing systems* achievement rather than a modeling one. The expert FFN is ordinary; the dispatch-combine operator — with its capacity constraints, dropping semantics, sharding annotations, and overlapped collectives — is where the design space lives. The field has converged on a stable recipe: token-choice top-$k$ gating, capacity factors around 1.25 for training and 2.0 for inference, score-based dropping, expert parallelism with all-to-all dispatch, and some mechanism (auxiliary loss or bias control) to keep utilization uniform. DeepSeek-V3's auxiliary-loss-free scheme suggests the next frontier is *decoupling* balance from routing gradients entirely, letting routers specialize while a separate control plane manages load. Open problems remain: inference-time determinism under finite capacity, memory-bandwidth-efficient serving of trillion-parameter weight footprints, and a principled theory of how fine expert granularity interacts with per-expert gradient noise. Until those are resolved, every trillion-parameter MoE will remain, in the precise sense developed here, a triumph of collective scheduling over raw arithmetic.
