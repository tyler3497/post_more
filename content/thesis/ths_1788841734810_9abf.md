# Statistical Foundations of Large Language Model Evaluation: Benchmark Design, Contamination Analysis, Preference Aggregation, and Inference Rigor

## Abstract

Evaluating large language models (LLMs) has become a measurement discipline in its own right, combining psychometrics, experimental statistics, and crowdsourced preference learning. This thesis unifies the four pillars of modern LLM evaluation: *benchmark design*, from MMLU's 57-subject multiple-choice battery [1] and HumanEval's functional code tests [2] to Big-Bench's collaborative task suite [5] and HELM's multi-metric transparency framework [7]; *contamination analysis*, quantifying train–test leakage and its distorting effect on reported capability [8]; *LLM-as-judge protocols*, characterizing position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases and their mitigation [3]; and *preference aggregation*, deriving Elo and Bradley–Terry rankings from pairwise human votes at scale [4]. We formalize the statistical estimators underlying each pillar — pass@k, unbiased Elo updates, confidence intervals for small-sample leaderboards, and decontamination-adjusted scoring — and prove conditions under which leaderboard orderings are identifiable. Empirical guidance is given for power analysis, stratification, and judge calibration so that published comparisons carry defensible uncertainty. We conclude that evaluation rigor, not raw scale, is now the binding constraint on credible claims about model progress.

## 1. Introduction

The release cadence of large language models has outpaced the science of measuring them. A benchmark that once discriminated cleanly between GPT-3 and its successors — MMLU at 25% versus 86% — now sits within a few points of saturation at the frontier, and reported deltas of 1–2 points routinely decide which model a laboratory promotes [1]. Yet these deltas are frequently smaller than the *measurement noise*: prompt-template variance, few-shot exemplar selection, contamination of test items in training corpora, and the sampling error of finite human-preference votes. As the field has matured, evaluation has therefore shifted from a convenience — "run the harness, report the number" — toward a discipline with its own statistical foundations.

This thesis develops those foundations. We treat LLM evaluation as a measurement problem with four interacting components. **Benchmark design** specifies *what* is measured: the construct (knowledge, reasoning, code, truthfulness, alignment) and its operationalization into items and scoring rules. **Contamination analysis** asks whether the measurement is *valid*: test items memorized during pretraining inflate scores without reflecting generalization. **LLM-as-judge protocols** address *scalability*: strong models grading open-ended outputs approximate human preference at a fraction of the cost, but carry systematic biases [3]. **Preference aggregation** converts *pairwise comparisons* — votes, judge verdicts — into global rankings with quantified uncertainty [4]. Finally, **statistical rigor** binds them: confidence intervals, power analysis, and stratified reporting determine whether a claimed improvement is real.

Our contributions are: (i) a unified formalism for item-based and preference-based evaluation; (ii) derivations of the estimators (pass@k, Elo/Bradley–Terry MLE, contamination-adjusted accuracy) with their variance properties; (iii) a catalog of judge biases with debiasing procedures; and (iv) practical protocols for defensible leaderboard reporting.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From perplexity to capabilities

Early language-model evaluation relied on perplexity over held-out text — a smooth, cheap, but capability-opaque metric. The shift to *task-based* evaluation came with benchmarks like GLUE and SuperGLUE, and accelerated with the scale era. MMLU [1] (Hendrycks et al., 2021) operationalized "massive multitask understanding" as 57 subjects × ~250 four-way multiple-choice questions, evaluated few-shot with exact-match scoring on the answer letter. HumanEval [2] (Chen et al., 2021) replaced multiple choice with 164 hand-written programming problems, each with hidden unit tests, scoring via functional correctness rather than token match. Big-Bench [5] (Srivastava et al., 2023) crowdsourced 204 tasks targeting capabilities "beyond the reach of current models," deliberately including social-bias and calibration probes. HELM [7] (Liang et al., 2023) argued that single-number leaderboards mislead, and instead evaluated models across scenarios × metrics (accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, efficiency, toxicity) to expose trade-offs.

### 2.2 The measurement crisis

Three developments precipitated the current rigor turn. First, **saturation**: frontier models cluster within noise on MMLU (86–92%), motivating harder successors (MMLU-Pro, GPQA). Second, **contamination**: with pretraining corpora spanning the public web, test items — including entire benchmark releases — appear in training data, inflating scores by memorization rather than reasoning [8]. Third, **preference subjectivity**: as models moved from question-answering to open-ended assistance, "correctness" became a human preference, requiring pairwise voting infrastructure (Chatbot Arena [4]) and statistical ranking machinery borrowed from chess.

## 3. Methodology

We formalize evaluation as estimation of latent capability parameters from observed responses.

### 3.1 Item-based model

Let model *m* have latent ability *θₘ* and benchmark *B* = {(*xᵢ*, *yᵢ*)}ⁱ⁼¹..ⁿ with scoring function *s(m(xᵢ), yᵢ) ∈ {0,1}*. The reported score is the sample mean *S̄ = (1/n)Σᵢ sᵢ*, an unbiased estimator of the population accuracy *S = E[s]* with variance *S(1−S)/n* under i.i.d. sampling. The standard error *SE = √(S̄(1−S̄)/n)* gives the 95% Wald interval *S̄ ± 1.96·SE*. For HumanEval-style generation, each problem yields *k* samples of which *c* pass; the unbiased **pass@k** estimator [2] is

> **Theorem (Unbiased pass@k):** *pass@k = 1 − C(n−c, k) / C(n, k)*, where *n* samples are drawn and *c* are correct, is the uniformly minimum-variance unbiased estimator of the probability that at least one of *k* independent samples solves the problem.

Naive estimation via *1 − (1 − c/n)ᵏ* is biased upward for finite *n*; the combinatorial form corrects it.

### 3.2 Preference-based model

In pairwise evaluation, a judge (human or LLM) observes prompt *x* and responses *(a, b)* from models *m₁, m₂*, emitting *y ∈ {1 ≻ 2, 2 ≻ 1, tie}*. The **Bradley–Terry** model posits latent strengths *πᵢ > 0* with

*P(i beats j) = πᵢ / (πᵢ + πⱼ)*.

The Elo system [4] is an online approximation: after a match with outcome *Sₐ ∈ {1, ½, 0}*, ratings update as *R′ₐ = Rₐ + K(Sₐ − Eₐ)* where *Eₐ = 1/(1 + 10^((R_b − Rₐ)/400))*. We prove in §5 that batch MLE of Bradley–Terry strengths and converged online Elo coincide up to the logistic/logit parameterization, justifying Arena-style leaderboard computation.

### 3.3 Contamination formalism

Let *C ⊂ B* be the contaminated subset (items occurring in pretraining). Observed accuracy decomposes as

*S̄ = α·S̄_C + (1−α)·S̄_U*,

with *α = |C|/n*. Under memorization, *S̄_C ≈ 1* regardless of *θₘ*, so reported gains may reflect leakage share *α* rather than capability. **Decontamination-adjusted scoring** reports *S̄_U* on the clean subset, with variance inflated by the smaller effective *n(1−α)*.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Benchmark design: constructs, items, and scoring rules

Good benchmarks begin from a *construct* — the latent capability of interest — and defend the inference from items to construct. MMLU [1] targets broad factual and reasoning knowledge; its construct validity rests on 57 exam-derived subjects spanning elementary to professional difficulty, with 5-shot prompting standardizing the interface. Critiques center on *saturation* and *format artifacts*: multiple-choice letter prediction is sensitive to option ordering and to the model's prior over letters, motivating calibration (e.g., scoring by normalized log-probability of the full answer text) [7].

HumanEval [2] shifted the construct to *functional code synthesis*: 164 problems with hidden tests, docstring prompts, and pass@k scoring. Its design insight is that execution is an objective oracle — no judge needed. Limitations include the small *n* (wide confidence intervals: ±7 points at 95% for typical scores) and Python-only coverage, later extended by HumanEval-X and MultiPL-E.

Big-Bench [5] took the opposite approach — 204 heterogeneous tasks from 442 authors, deliberately adversarial, with canary strings to detect training-data inclusion. Its lesson: collaborative, living benchmarks resist saturation longer but complicate aggregation, since tasks use incompatible metrics (exact match, ROUGE, multiple-choice grade, human rating).

HELM [7] made the meta-point explicit: no single number suffices. Evaluating 30+ models across 16 scenarios × 7 metrics revealed Pareto structure — e.g., instruction-tuned models gain accuracy but lose calibration — that scalar leaderboards hide. The methodological prescription is **stratified reporting**: slice scores by capability, metric, and subgroup, and report uncertainty per slice.

| Benchmark | Construct | *n* (items) | Scoring | Key threat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MMLU [1] | Broad knowledge | 14,079 test | 5-shot MC accuracy | Saturation, contamination |
| HumanEval [2] | Code synthesis | 164 problems | pass@k (exec) | Small *n*, language scope |
| Big-Bench [5] | Frontier capabilities | 204 tasks | Heterogeneous | Aggregation validity |
| TruthfulQA [6] | Truthfulness | 817 questions | MC + generation | Adversarial falsehoods |
| HELM [7] | Multi-metric profile | 16 scenarios | Accuracy, calibration, … | Cost, complexity |
| MT-Bench [3] | Open-ended quality | 80 questions | LLM judge 1–10 | Judge bias |

### 4.2 Contamination and decontamination

Training on the test set is the cardinal sin of machine learning, and web-scale pretraining commits it at scale. Contamination arises through benchmark mirrors on GitHub, paper PDFs containing examples, and forum discussions of test items. Detection methods include:

- **Canary strings**: unique GUIDs (as in Big-Bench [5]) whose presence in training data is checkable via membership queries.
- **Perplexity gaps**: items with anomalously low perplexity relative to paraphrases suggest memorization; MIN-K% PROB and related membership-inference scores formalize this.
- **Temporal splits**: benchmarks released after a model's knowledge cutoff (e.g., problems from recent competitions) are contamination-free by construction.
- **N-gram overlap audits**: 13-gram or 50-character overlap between test items and pretraining corpora, as used in GPT-3/GPT-4 technical reports.

> **Theorem (Contamination bias bound):** *If a fraction α of test items is memorized (scored 1 regardless of ability), reported accuracy S̄ overstates true generalization accuracy S_U by at most α·(1 − S_U). The bound is tight when memorized items would otherwise be failed.*

Empirically, decontaminated re-evaluations have revised headline numbers downward by 3–10 points on affected benchmarks [8]. The practical protocol: *report S̄_U alongside S̄*, with the clean-subset confidence interval, and pre-register contamination audits in evaluation harnesses.

### 4.3 LLM-as-judge: biases, agreement, and mitigation

Zheng et al. [3] showed GPT-4 judges reach >80% agreement with humans on MT-Bench — the same level as human–human agreement — making LLM judges a scalable proxy for preference. But judges carry systematic biases:

- **Position bias**: preference for the first-presented answer; mitigated by swapping order and requiring consistent verdicts across both orderings (consistency filtering).
- **Verbosity bias**: preference for longer responses independent of quality; mitigated by length-controlled win rates (regressing out length, as in AlpacaEval LC).
- **Self-enhancement bias**: judges preferring their own model family's outputs; mitigated by multi-judge panels and blinded identities.
- **Reasoning limits**: weaker judges fail on math/coding items where verification needs execution; mitigated by tool-augmented judging (executing code, checking proofs).

```python
def judge_with_debias(judge, prompt, a, b, n_swaps=2):
    """Position-debiased pairwise judgment via order swapping."""
    votes = []
    for order in [(a, b), (b, a)]:
        verdict = judge(prompt, order[0], order[1])  # returns 0/1/tie
        votes.append(verdict if order == (a, b) else 1 - verdict)
    if len(set(votes)) > 1:
        return "tie"          # inconsistent -> abstain
    return votes[0]

def length_controlled_winrate(results):
    """AlpacaEval-style LC win rate: logistic regression of win on
    (model_indicator, length_diff); report win prob at length_diff=0."""
    import statsmodels.api as sm
    X = sm.add_constant(results[["is_target", "len_diff"]])
    fit = sm.Logit(results["win"], X).fit(disp=0)
    return 1 / (1 + __import__("math").exp(-fit.params["const"]
                                           - fit.params["is_target"]))
```

Agreement should be reported as more than raw accuracy: Cohen's κ for categorical verdicts, and *transitivity* checks (if A≻B and B≻C, then A≻C should hold at above-chance rates) diagnose judge coherence. A judge with 80% pairwise agreement but 55% transitivity is unreliable for ranking even if its headline number looks strong.

### 4.4 Preference aggregation: Elo, Bradley–Terry, and uncertainty

Chatbot Arena [4] collects pairwise human votes and fits Bradley–Terry strengths via MLE, reported on an Elo-like scale (offset 1000, scale 400/log(10)). Key statistical considerations:

1. **Identifiability**: strengths are identified only up to an additive constant (Elo) or multiplicative constant (π); fix one model's rating or the mean.
2. **Uncertainty**: with *nᵢⱼ* matches between *i, j*, the Fisher information gives asymptotic standard errors; bootstrapping votes (resampling with replacement, refitting) yields 95% CIs. Arena reports these — models whose CIs overlap are *not* significantly ranked.
3. **Non-transitivity and style**: the BT model assumes a total order plus noise; systematic intransitivity (rock-paper-scissors between models) or prompt-distribution shift violates it. Stratified BT fits per category (coding, math, writing) detect this.
4. **Vote quality**: adversarial voting (brigading) and identity leakage (model revealing its name) bias outcomes; Arena applies anomaly detection and discards tainted votes.

![Bradley-Terry ranking diagram](/thesis/ths_1788841734810_9abf-2.webp)

*Figure 3: Bradley–Terry preference aggregation — pairwise battle outcomes feed maximum-likelihood strength estimation, with bootstrap confidence intervals determining which leaderboard gaps are statistically significant.*

> **Theorem (Elo–Bradley–Terry correspondence):** *The stationary point of online Elo updates with K → 0 equals the MLE of the Bradley–Terry model under the logistic parameterization Rᵢ = 400·log₁₀ πᵢ. Hence converged Elo ratings and BT strengths encode identical pairwise win probabilities.*

*Proof sketch.* The expected Elo update is *E[ΔRₐ] = K(P̂ₐ − 1/(1+10^((R_b−Rₐ)/400)))*. Setting the expectation to zero for all pairs gives *P̂ₐ = 1/(1+10^((R_b−Rₐ)/400))*, i.e., the BT likelihood's first-order condition with *πᵢ = 10^(Rᵢ/400)*. ∎

### 4.5 Capability versus alignment evaluations

A complete evaluation program separates *capability* (what the model can do: MMLU, HumanEval, GSM8K [8]) from *alignment* (whether it behaves as intended: TruthfulQA [6], bias/toxicity probes, refusal calibration, reward-hacking detection). Conflating them produces misleading claims — a model can be highly capable and poorly aligned, or vice versa. TruthfulQA [6] operationalizes truthfulness adversarially: 817 questions designed so that common misconceptions and imitative falsehoods are the likely wrong answers; the headline finding is that larger models are often *less* truthful (they better imitate human falsehoods), an inverse scaling result invisible to capability benchmarks. Modern alignment evals add: (i) **jailbreak robustness** (adversarial prompt suites), (ii) **sycophancy and deception probes**, (iii) **long-horizon agency evals** (multi-step task completion with side-effect constraints). The methodological rule: report capability and alignment profiles separately, and never let a capability gain license an alignment claim.

## 5. Empirical Results and Statistical Rigor

### 5.1 Power analysis for benchmark comparisons

How many items are needed to detect a true gap *δ* between models at accuracy *p*? For two independent proportions, the required *n* per model at power 1−β is approximately

*n ≈ 2·p(1−p)·(z_{1−α/2} + z_{1−β})² / δ²*.

At *p = 0.7*, *δ = 0.02* (2 points), *α = 0.05*, *β = 0.2*: *n ≈ 8,200* items. MMLU's 14,079 suffice; HumanEval's 164 do not — its ±7-point CIs mean only gaps above ~10 points are significant. The practical consequence: small-benchmark leaderboards should report CIs prominently, and claims like "model A beats B by 1.5% on HumanEval" are statistically vacuous without qualification.

### 5.2 Stratification and the Simpson risk

Aggregate accuracy can reverse within strata (Simpson's paradox): model A may lead overall while model B leads within every subject category, if category sizes differ and accuracies interact with category. HELM-style stratified reporting [7] is the defense: publish per-category scores with CIs, and test heterogeneity (Cochran's Q) before pooling.

### 5.3 Judge calibration protocol

We recommend a five-step protocol for any LLM-judge evaluation: (1) **human agreement audit** on ≥200 sampled items, reporting κ and per-category agreement; (2) **bias battery** — position-swap consistency ≥90%, verbosity correlation check, self-preference test; (3) **transitivity audit** on sampled triples; (4) **length-controlled** or otherwise debiased aggregation; (5) **uncertainty propagation** — bootstrap over both items and judge verdicts so CIs reflect judge noise, not just sampling noise.

### 5.4 Worked example: is a 2-point MMLU gain real?

Suppose model A scores 86.4% and B 84.9% on MMLU test (*n* = 14,079). Difference *δ̂* = 1.5%, SE of difference ≈ √(0.864·0.136/14079 + 0.849·0.151/14079) ≈ 0.42%. The 95% CI for the gap is 1.5% ± 0.82% — significant, *if* items are i.i.d. and uncontaminated. But: prompt-template variance across published MMLU implementations is ~1–2 points, and contamination audits are rarely published. A defensible claim therefore requires: same harness/template for both models, reported CIs, and a contamination statement. Without these, the "gain" is uninterpretable — a pattern that recurs across current leaderboards.

## 6. Limitations

This framework has boundaries. **Construct validity** remains partly philosophical: no benchmark proves it measures "reasoning" rather than sophisticated pattern matching, and Goodhart's law guarantees that optimizing a metric degrades its informativeness over time — benchmark lifetimes are finite. **Human preference** itself is noisy, culturally situated, and prompt-distribution-dependent; Arena rankings reflect the voting population's prompt mix, not a universal quality order. **Contamination detection** is an arms race: paraphrased or translated test items evade n-gram audits, and closed-data trainers cannot be audited externally at all. **LLM judges** inherit the evaluated models' failure modes, creating correlated errors when judge and contestant share a lineage. Finally, our statistical machinery assumes approximate stationarity — model behavior under the eval distribution — while deployment distributions drift. Evaluation is necessary but not sufficient for deployment safety.

---

## 7. Conclusion

LLM evaluation has grown from running a script to a measurement science spanning benchmark design, contamination forensics, judge-bias metrology, and preference-aggregation statistics. The through-line of this thesis is that *every reported number is an estimate with variance, bias, and validity conditions* — and that credible evaluation reports all three. Concretely: design benchmarks from explicit constructs with stratified reporting [1][5][7]; audit and adjust for contamination [8]; characterize judge biases and calibrate against humans [3]; aggregate preferences with Bradley–Terry/Elo machinery *with confidence intervals* [4]; and separate capability claims from alignment claims [6]. The field's binding constraint is no longer model scale but measurement discipline. Laboratories that internalize these protocols will produce rankings that survive scrutiny; those that do not will optimize noise.
