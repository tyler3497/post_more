# The Conformal Bootstrap: Crossing Symmetry, Extremal Functionals, and Numerical Bounds on the 3D Ising Conformal Field Theory

## Abstract

The conformal bootstrap is a nonperturbative program that determines conformal field theories from symmetry, unitarity, and the associativity of the operator product expansion, without reference to any Lagrangian. Expanding four-point correlation functions in conformal blocks and imposing crossing symmetry yields sum rules whose consistency is equivalent to a convex feasibility problem. Extremal linear functionals acting on these sum rules produce rigorous bounds on scaling dimensions and operator product expansion coefficients, and modern arbitrary-precision semidefinite programming, embodied by the SDPB solver, has turned these bounds into precision instruments. Applied to the three-dimensional Ising conformal field theory, the mixed-correlator bootstrap confines the leading scalar dimensions to a microscopic island, with current best values of Δσ = 0.518148806(24) and Δε = 1.41262528(29). This essay explains conformal block technology and OPE convergence, the convex geometry of crossing, the extremal functional method, the SDPB implementation, and the analytic bootstrap at large spin, and closes with the relation to AdS/CFT and open problems including superconformal islands.

## 1 Intro

Quantum field theory has traditionally been studied through the lens of the Lagrangian: one writes down an action, expands in a small coupling, and computes. The *conformal bootstrap* inverts this logic. Instead of postulating microscopic degrees of freedom, it asks what theories are *consistent* with conformal symmetry, unitarity, and the associativity of the operator product expansion (OPE). Any theory satisfying these axioms is a conformal field theory (CFT); the bootstrap seeks to carve out the space of allowed theories and, at distinguished points, to solve them.

The intellectual lineage is long. Polyakov and collaborators formulated the bootstrap philosophy in the 1970s [1], and Belavin, Polyakov, and Zamolodchikov famously used it to exactly solve the two-dimensional minimal models in 1983. For a quarter century the method lay largely dormant in higher dimensions, until Rattazzi, Rychkov, Tonni, and Vichi showed in 2008 that crossing symmetry plus unitarity imply rigorous numerical bounds on operator dimensions in four-dimensional CFTs [1]. That paper ignited the modern numerical bootstrap: a sustained campaign, reviewed comprehensively in [9], that has produced the most precise determinations of critical exponents ever achieved, in any method, for the three-dimensional Ising model.

The 3D Ising CFT is the ideal target. It is the simplest interacting CFT in three dimensions, the continuum limit of the critical Ising model, and it controls the liquid-vapor critical point and the Curie point of real ferromagnets. Its two most important numbers, the scaling dimensions Δσ of the spin field σ and Δε of the energy operator ε, fix the critical exponents through η = 2Δσ − 1 and ν = 1/(3 − Δε). The bootstrap now pins these numbers down to roughly one part in 10^8 [8] — precision that Monte Carlo simulations and the epsilon expansion cannot approach, and that was unthinkable before semidefinite programming entered the subject.

This essay is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews conformal kinematics, conformal blocks, OPE convergence, and the crossing equation. Section 3 explains how crossing becomes a convex feasibility problem attacked with linear functionals. Section 4 dives into the kink, the island, the extremal functional method, SDPB, and the analytic bootstrap at large spin. Section 5 collects the empirical results, Section 6 the limitations, and Section 7 the outlook, including AdS/CFT and open problems.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Conformal kinematics and the OPE

A CFT in d dimensions is invariant under the conformal group SO(d+1,1). Local operators organize into conformal multiplets labeled by a scaling dimension Δ and a spin ℓ (a representation of the rotation group SO(d)), with unitarity imposing

- Δ ≥ (d − 2)/2 for scalars (ℓ = 0),
- Δ ≥ ℓ + d − 2 for spinning operators (ℓ > 0),

saturated by free fields and conserved currents respectively. The dynamical data of a CFT consist of this spectrum {Δ, ℓ} and the OPE coefficients λαβγ appearing in

> **OPE axiom:** φ1(x) φ2(0) = Σ_O λ12O |x|^{Δ_O − Δ1 − Δ2} (descendants of O),

where the sum runs over primary operators O and their descendants, with coefficients fixed by conformal symmetry.

### 2.2 Conformal blocks

Consider the four-point function of identical scalars φ of dimension Δφ. Conformal invariance fixes its form up to a function of two cross ratios,

⟨φ(x1) φ(x2) φ(x3) φ(x4)⟩ = (x12² x34²)^{-Δφ} g(u, v),

with u = x12²x34²/(x13²x24²) and v = x14²x23²/(x13²x24²). Performing the OPE in the s-channel (x1 → x2) gives the *conformal block expansion*

g(u, v) = Σ_{Δ,ℓ} λ_{φφO}² g_{Δ,ℓ}(u, v),

where the conformal blocks g_{Δ,ℓ} are universal kinematical functions — eigenfunctions of the conformal Casimir operators — first systematically studied by Dolan and Osborn. They resum the contribution of an entire conformal multiplet and are now computable to high precision via recursion relations in the radial coordinates.

### 2.3 OPE convergence

A cornerstone result is that the OPE converges with a nonzero radius: the block expansion converges absolutely and exponentially fast for cross ratios inside the unit disk in radial coordinates. Quantitatively, contributions of operators with dimension above Δ* are suppressed roughly like e^{-c Δ*} at fixed cross ratio away from the lightcone.

> **Theorem:** (Exponential OPE convergence.) In a unitary CFT, the s-channel conformal block expansion of a four-point function converges absolutely at any Euclidean configuration with all points distinct, and the truncation error after keeping operators of dimension below Δ* falls off exponentially in Δ*. See the discussion and references in [9].

This convergence is what makes the numerical bootstrap possible at all: crossing can be probed with finitely many derivatives because high-dimension contributions are exponentially suppressed.

### 2.4 The crossing equation

The same four-point function can be expanded in the t-channel (x2 → x3). Equality of the two expansions — associativity of the OPE — gives the crossing equation

g(u, v) = (u/v)^{Δφ} g(v, u),

or, as a sum rule over the spectrum,

Σ_{Δ,ℓ} λ_{φφO}² F_{Δ,ℓ}(u, v) = 0,

where F_{Δ,ℓ}(u, v) = v^{Δφ} g_{Δ,ℓ}(u, v) − u^{Δφ} g_{Δ,ℓ}(v, u) is antisymmetric under u ↔ v. Unitarity (reflection positivity) implies λ_{φφO}² ≥ 0 for the identical-scalar case. The bootstrap problem is therefore: *for which spectra {Δ, ℓ} and positive coefficients does this sum rule admit a solution?* Every consistent CFT provides one; the goal is to rule out everything else.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Linear functionals and convex geometry

The modern numerical method, introduced in [1], attacks crossing with linear functionals. Choose a functional α acting on functions of (u, v), typically a linear combination of derivatives evaluated at the crossing-symmetric point z = z̄ = 1/2 (equivalently u = v = 1/4):

α[F] = Σ_{m+n ≤ Λ} a_{mn} ∂_z^m ∂_z̄^n F(z, z̄)|_{z=z̄=1/2}.

Applying α to the crossing sum rule gives Σ λ² α[F_{Δ,ℓ}] = 0. Now suppose we *assume* a gap — for instance, that all scalars except the identity and ε have dimension above Δ* — and we find coefficients a_{mn} such that α[F_{Δ,ℓ}] > 0 for every (Δ, ℓ) allowed by the assumption. Since every term in the sum is then strictly positive, the sum rule cannot hold: the assumption is ruled out. The boundary of what can be ruled out is a *bound*.

Geometrically, the vectors v_{Δ,ℓ} = (α applied to each basis component) trace out a convex cone as (Δ, ℓ) vary, and crossing asks whether a target vector lies inside it. Finding the functional is therefore a problem in *convex optimization* — linear programming for a single correlator.

### 3.2 From linear to semidefinite programming

Realistic applications use *mixed correlators*: the coupled system {⟨σσσσ⟩, ⟨σσεε⟩, ⟨εεεε⟩} for the Ising case. The OPE coefficients then form positive semidefinite matrices (outer products of vectors of three-point coefficients), and the positivity conditions become matrix inequalities

M_0(x) + Σ_n y_n M_n(x) ⪰ 0 for all x ≥ 0,

where x parametrizes the dimension above the assumed gap and the M_n are polynomial matrices built from derivatives of conformal blocks. This is a *semidefinite program* (SDP): maximize a linear objective over variables y subject to polynomial matrix positivity. It was the generalization of bootstrap numerics to SDP, together with the SDPB solver of [3], that unlocked the precision era.

### 3.3 The computational pipeline

A complete bootstrap computation proceeds in stages:

1. **Block computation.** Conformal blocks and their derivatives at z = z̄ = 1/2 are computed via recursion relations, then approximated by rational functions (pole expansions) so that positivity for all x ≥ 0 becomes a finite SDP.
2. **SDP assembly.** Crossing vectors for each spin channel are converted into polynomial matrix constraints; truncation parameters include the derivative order Λ, the maximum spin, and the pole-expansion order.
3. **Optimization.** SDPB solves the SDP at hundreds of digits of precision, returning either a feasible functional (ruling out the assumed spectrum) or a certificate of infeasibility.
4. **Scanning.** Repeating over a grid of assumptions (e.g., over candidate values of Δσ and Δε) maps the allowed region.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The kink that located the 3D Ising CFT

The first dramatic success came from the single correlator ⟨σσσσ⟩. Plotting the upper bound on Δε as a function of the assumed Δσ, El-Showk, Paulos, Poland, Rychkov, Simmons-Duffin, and Vichi found a sharp *kink* at Δσ ≈ 0.518 [2]: the bound changes slope discontinuously, and the kink sits precisely at the expected Ising values. Two independent optimization criteria — maximizing Δε and minimizing the central charge c (equivalently maximizing the stress-tensor OPE coefficient) — are saturated at the same point on the boundary, a highly nontrivial coincidence strongly suggesting that a unique CFT lives there. The extremal spectrum at the kink contains the stress tensor at exactly Δ = 3, ℓ = 2, as required by Ward identities.

### 4.2 Mixed correlators and the precision island

The kink localizes the theory but leaves a one-dimensional degeneracy. Kos, Poland, Simmons-Duffin, and Vichi broke it with the mixed system {⟨σσσσ⟩, ⟨σσεε⟩, ⟨εεεε⟩} [4]. Now the unknowns include the OPE coefficient ratios λσσε/λ_εεε, and scanning over them while imposing that only one operator sits at each low dimension carves the allowed region down to a tiny *island* in the (Δσ, Δε) plane. Their determination,

Δσ = 0.5181489(10), Δε = 1.412625(10),
λσσε = 1.0518537(41), λ_εεε = 1.532435(19),

remained the world record for nearly a decade [4]. The island shrinks as Λ increases, and its center is stable — the signature of a true isolated CFT rather than a numerical artifact.

### 4.3 The extremal functional method

At the boundary of the excluded region, the optimal functional is essentially unique. The *extremal functional method* exploits this: the zeros of the extremal functional α* as a function of Δ, at each spin, reveal the spectrum of the putative extremal solution to crossing. In favorable cases this reconstructs dozens of operator dimensions — the low-lying spectrum of the 3D Ising CFT — from a *single* optimization. The method also has a rigorous use: it converts bounds into candidate spectra that can be cross-checked against other methods. A caveat is that the extremal spectrum is the spectrum of the boundary solution, which approximates but need not coincide with the true CFT; convergence as Λ → ∞ is the open analytical question.

### 4.4 SDPB: arbitrary-precision semidefinite programming

SDPB, introduced by Simmons-Duffin [3] and scaled to supercomputers by Landry and Simmons-Duffin [5], is purpose-built for bootstrap SDPs. Standard double-precision solvers fail because the polynomial matrices are extremely ill-conditioned; SDPB uses arbitrary-precision arithmetic (typically 1024-bit mantissas) and a primal-dual interior-point algorithm. Version 2.0 added MPI parallelism via the Elemental library, scaling to hundreds of cores and enabling the Λ = 43 computations behind the precision islands. A schematic of the workflow, in the style of the Python wrappers PyCFTBoot and cboot, is:

```python
# Illustrative sketch of the numerical bootstrap loop (pseudocode)
def bootstrap_bound(delta_phi, gap_assumption, Lambda):
    # 1. Conformal blocks -> crossing vectors at z = zbar = 1/2
    F = [crossing_vector(conformal_block(delta_phi, Delta, ell, Lambda))
         for (Delta, ell) in spectrum_grid(gap_assumption)]
    # 2. Assemble the semidefinite program for the functional coefficients
    prob = SDPBProblem(precision_bits=1024)
    a = prob.add_variables((m, n) for m + n in range(Lambda + 1))
    prob.add_constraint(dot(a, unit_vector) == 1)   # normalization
    for vec in F:                                   # positivity on the cone
        prob.add_psd_constraint(dot(a, vec) >= 0)
    # 3. Feasible  =>  gap assumption ruled out (a separating functional exists)
    return prob.solve()

# Scanning Delta_sigma then yields the kink; the mixed-correlator
# version replaces scalar inequalities by matrix PSD constraints.
```

### 4.5 The analytic bootstrap at large spin

Numerics are complemented by analytics in the lightcone limit. When two operators become nearly null-separated, the t-channel is dominated by low-twist operators while the s-channel requires infinite towers of large-spin operators. Komargodski and Zhiboedov showed that crossing in this limit forces universal structure [6]:

> **Theorem:** (Twist additivity and convexity.) In any unitary CFT in d > 2, if twists τ1 and τ2 appear in the spectrum, then operators exist with twists arbitrarily close to τ1 + τ2 at large spin, approached with calculable 1/ℓ corrections; moreover the minimal-twist trajectory at each spin is convex in twist space.

Thus every CFT becomes nearly free at large spin — "liberation at large spin" — with double-twist families [φφ]_{n,ℓ} of twist 2Δφ + 2n + γ(n, ℓ) and anomalous dimensions γ suppressed by powers of 1/ℓ. Simmons-Duffin pushed this into a precision tool for the 3D Ising CFT [7]: solving the lightcone bootstrap to all orders in the asymptotic large-spin expansion, with new resummation techniques for infinite sums of SL(2,R) blocks, he derived analytic approximations for roughly one hundred low-twist operators in terms of just the island data {Δσ, Δε, λσσε, λ_εεε, c_T}, matching numerics to high precision. This *large-spin perturbation theory* is now a general method, systematically improvable via the Lorentzian inversion formula.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The numerical record for the 3D Ising CFT, all obtained by excluding inconsistent spectra rather than by fitting data, is summarized below.

| Study | Δσ | Δε | Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| El-Showk et al. 2012 [2] | ≈ 0.518 | — | Single correlator, kink |
| SDPB debut 2015 [3] | 0.518151(6) | 1.41264(6) | Single correlator, SDP |
| Precision islands 2016 [4] | 0.5181489(10) | 1.412625(10) | Mixed correlators, Λ = 43 |
| Stress tensor 2024 [8] | 0.518148806(24) | 1.41262528(29) | Mixed correlators + T_{μν} |

The 2024 stress-tensor bootstrap of Chang, Dommes, Erramilli, Homrich, Kravchuk, Liu, Mitchell, Poland, and Simmons-Duffin [8] added the stress tensor as an external operator — technically demanding because spinning correlators multiply the crossing system — and improved the precision by nearly two orders of magnitude. The corresponding critical exponents,

- η = 2Δσ − 1 ≈ 0.0362978,
- ν = 1/(3 − Δε) ≈ 0.629971,

are the most precisely known critical exponents in physics, surpassing Monte Carlo and epsilon-expansion determinations. The analytic lightcone results [7] independently reproduce the low-twist spectrum, so the numerical island and the analytic large-spin expansion agree where they overlap — a nontrivial consistency check of the entire framework.

## 6 Limitations

The method has sharp boundaries that ongoing research pushes against:

- **Truncation.** All bounds are computed at finite derivative order Λ, finite maximum spin, and finite pole-expansion order. Bounds are rigorous at fixed truncation but converge to the optimal bound only as Λ → ∞, and the convergence rate is not under analytical control in general.
- **Computational cost.** Island computations at Λ = 43 require supercomputer-scale SDPB runs with MPI parallelism [5]; scanning multi-dimensional assumption spaces multiplies the cost.
- **Conditional statements.** Every exclusion plot assumes gaps above the operators being bounded. The island is rigorous *given* its assumptions, but the assumptions themselves (e.g., that σ and ε are the only relevant scalars) are physical input, not derived.
- **Scope.** Most precision results concern scalar external operators. Spinning correlators, fermions, and non-unitary theories are harder; the 2024 stress-tensor work [8] shows the frontier is moving but the cost is steep.
- **Islands vs. existence.** The bootstrap rules theories out; it does not prove a CFT exists at every surviving point. The Ising island's stability and the kink coincidences are compelling evidence, not a theorem.

## 7 Conclusion

The conformal bootstrap has turned crossing symmetry — once a formal consistency condition — into a computational engine that solves strongly coupled quantum field theories. The 3D Ising CFT serves as the proof of concept: from the 2008 bounds [1] through the 2012 kink [2], the SDPB revolution [3], the precision islands [4], the analytic lightcone solution [7], and the 2024 stress-tensor refinement [8], each generation has sharpened the picture by orders of magnitude.

The implications extend beyond statistical physics. Via AdS/CFT, bounds on CFT data translate into bounds on quantum gravity in anti-de Sitter space — on higher-derivative corrections, on the gap to string states, and on chaos. Open problems abound: narrowing the 3D O(2) island to Ising-like precision, carving out *superconformal* islands in three dimensions, bootstrapping gauge theories, and ultimately understanding analytically why the numerical bounds converge to isolated points. The bootstrap does not need a Lagrangian; it may, in the end, not need much else.
