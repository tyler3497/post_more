# Just-In-Time Specialization: Trace Trees, Polymorphic Inline Caches, Partial Evaluation, and the Economics of Deoptimization

## Abstract

Just-in-time (JIT) compilation is the art of exploiting *run-time* knowledge that no static analysis can obtain cheaply: the actual types flowing through hot code, the call targets dominating a dispatch site, the branch directions a loop takes. This thesis surveys the major architectural answers to one question — how should a virtual machine convert observed behavior into specialized machine code without sacrificing correctness when observations change? We examine trace-based compilation as realized in TraceMonkey [1], meta-tracing as pioneered by PyPy's RPython toolchain [3], polymorphic inline caching and adaptive recompilation from the Self lineage [2][5], partial evaluation of self-optimizing AST interpreters in Truffle and Graal [4][7], and the deoptimization machinery that makes speculation sound [6]. The unifying principle is an economic one: every specialization is a *bet* collateralized by guards, and the VM's profitability is determined by the ratio of guard-elided fast paths to deoptimization bailouts. We formalize guard soundness, compare the four strategies on a common cost model, and report the published empirical results, which range from 2×–20× speedups on traceable JavaScript to language implementations rivaling hand-written optimizing compilers.

## 1 Introduction

Statically typed languages hand their compilers a gift: a proof, checked before execution, of the type of every expression. Dynamically typed languages withhold that gift, and the compiler must pay for its absence on every operation. Adding two numbers in an untyped language is not one machine instruction; it is a decision procedure — are they integers, floats, strings, objects with `valueOf` methods? — followed by one machine instruction. The entire history of JIT compilation is the history of deferring that decision procedure until run time, observing its outcomes, and then compiling as if the outcomes were guaranteed, while retaining a cheap mechanism for retreat when they are not [1][3].

The thesis of this work is that *all* high-performance dynamic-language JITs are instances of one pattern — **speculative specialization under guarded deoptimization** — differing only in the granularity at which they speculate (traces, methods, AST nodes, interpreter loops), the source of their type information (inline caches, trace recording, interpreter rewriting), and the price they pay for being wrong (deoptimization, recompilation, megamorphic fallback). Understanding any one system deeply requires understanding the economic invariant they all share: speculation is profitable exactly when the cost of guards plus the amortized cost of bailouts is less than the cost of the generic code they replace.

We make three contributions. First, we reconstruct the four canonical architectures — tracing, meta-tracing, inline-cache-driven adaptive recompilation, and interpreter partial evaluation — as instantiations of a common formal model with explicit guard and deoptimization semantics. Second, we state and prove a soundness theorem for guarded specialization: observable program behavior is invariant under any deoptimization policy, provided guards are *total* over the specialized state's assumptions. Third, we compare the published empirical evidence and identify the structural limitations each architecture accepts — trace explosion, warmup latency, and the tension between specialization depth and deoptimization cost.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The three execution modes

A language implementation chooses, per unit of code, among three execution modes:

- **Interpretation** dispatches on the program's own structure (bytecodes, AST nodes) at run time. It starts instantly and occupies little memory, but pays a dispatch and decoding tax on every operation — typically 10×–100× slower than native code [8].
- **Ahead-of-time (AOT) compilation** translates the whole program before execution. It is fast to start executing but blind to run-time types, so for dynamic languages it must emit generic, heavily guarded code.
- **Just-in-time compilation** translates during execution, and can therefore observe actual behavior first. It pays compilation pauses and memory, but can emit code specialized to the observed reality.

Production VMs layer these into *tiers*: HotSpot's interpreter feeds the C1 client compiler, which feeds the C2 server compiler [8]; V8's Ignition interpreter feeds Sparkplug and then TurboFan. Tiering exists because compilation is expensive and speculation needs evidence: cheap tiers gather profiling data while expensive tiers invest in optimization [5].

### 2.2 The Futamura projections: compilers from interpreters

A classical result frames the entire field. Let `int` be an interpreter, `p` a program, and `d` its input. The **first Futamura projection** states that specializing the interpreter with respect to a fixed program yields a compiled program:

```haskell
-- Futamura projection 1: specialization of an interpreter is compilation
target      = [[mix]] int p        -- mix: partial evaluator
-- i.e.,  target(d) = int(p, d)   for all d
```

The second projection specializes the partial evaluator with respect to the interpreter to yield a compiler generator; the third specializes it with respect to itself to yield a compiler-compiler. Truffle and Graal are the first industrial-scale realization of the first projection for dynamic languages: the language is defined *solely* by an interpreter, and compiled code is derived automatically by partial evaluation [4]. Meta-tracing JITs such as PyPy's achieve a strikingly similar effect by a different route: instead of partially evaluating the interpreter ahead of time, they *trace* the interpreter as it runs the program, recording the interpreter's own operations on the program's data, then optimize that trace [3].

### 2.3 Guards, speculation, and deoptimization

> **Definition:** A *guard* is a run-time check, emitted in optimized code, that verifies an assumption on which the optimization depends — e.g., that a value is an integer, that a call target's class is unchanged, that a branch takes its observed direction.

> **Definition:** *Deoptimization* is the transfer of execution from optimized code back to a less-optimized tier (or the interpreter) at a guard failure, reconstructing the interpreter-level state — stack frames, locals, operand stacks — that the optimized code had abstracted away [6].

Deoptimization is what makes speculation *sound*. The optimized code need not be correct for all inputs; it must be correct for inputs that pass its guards, and detect all others. This weakens the compiler's proof obligation enormously: instead of proving a property of the program, it proves a property of a *path*, and checks the path's preconditions at run time.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is architectural reconstruction plus formalization. For each of the four JIT families we:

1. **Identify the unit of speculation** — the program fragment the compiler bets on (a loop trace, a method, an AST subtree, an interpreter dispatch loop).
2. **Identify the information channel** — how observed behavior reaches the compiler (guarded trace recording, inline-cache type feedback, AST node rewriting, interpreter profiling).
3. **Identify the retreat mechanism** — what happens when the bet fails (side exits, on-stack replacement, recompilation triggers, megamorphic degradation).
4. **Model the economics** — a cost model in which each specialization's profit is the fast-path savings minus guard overhead minus amortized bailout cost.

We then prove, in Section 5, that any policy satisfying *guard totality* preserves observable semantics, and we tabulate the published performance evidence for each family. Our sources are the primary papers: TraceMonkey [1], the PIC papers [2], the Self adaptive system [5], the deoptimization papers [6], meta-tracing [3], Truffle [7], and the Graal partial-evaluation work [4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Trace Trees and Type Specialization: The TraceMonkey Architecture

Trace-based compilation, introduced for Java by Gal et al. and brought to JavaScript in TraceMonkey, bets on a simple empirical claim: *programs spend most of their time in hot loops, and hot loops are mostly type-stable* — loop counters that start as integers stay integers [1]. Rather than compiling methods, the VM compiles *loop traces*: single paths through a loop, recorded by the interpreter, annotated with a type for every variable (a *typed trace*), and compiled to straight-line machine code with guards at every point where reality could diverge.

The recording protocol is elegant. Execution begins in the bytecode interpreter. When a loop edge becomes hot, the monitor starts recording bytecodes; when control returns to the loop header, recording stops and the trace is compiled. Guards are emitted for every type assumption and every branch outcome. When a guard fails at run time, execution takes a *side exit* back to the interpreter or to another trace. Crucially, hot side exits are themselves recorded and compiled, growing a *trace tree* that incrementally covers the program's actual behavior — nested loops become nested trace trees, with inner traces callable from outer ones [1].

Because traces have no internal control-flow joins, they can be optimized in linear time by a simple compiler — achieving, in effect, interprocedural optimization (inlining across call boundaries falls out of recording) without interprocedural analysis. On traceable JavaScript programs TraceMonkey reported speedups of **2×–20×** over the interpreter [1].

```rust
// Pseudocode: a compiled trace is a guarded straight-line fragment.
fn trace_loop_header(state: &mut Interp) {
    // Guards: verify the type map the trace was compiled under.
    guard_type(state.reg[0], Type::Int);     // else side_exit(1)
    guard_type(state.reg[1], Type::Double);  // else side_exit(2)
    // ... straight-line specialized body, no dispatch, no joins ...
    if !guard_branch_taken() { side_exit(3); }
    jump trace_loop_header;                  // loop back, stay in native code
}
```

The trace-tree model also handles the hard cases gracefully: an untraceable construct (e.g., `eval`, deep recursion patterns) simply aborts recording, and execution continues in the interpreter — the system degrades to mixed-mode execution rather than failing [1].

### 4.2 Meta-Tracing: Specializing the Interpreter Itself

PyPy's meta-tracing JIT inverts the tracing idea. Instead of writing a tracer for the *language*, the implementer writes an ordinary interpreter in RPython (a restricted, translatable subset of Python), and the JIT traces the *interpreter* as it executes the user program [3]. The recorded operations are the interpreter's own — integer adds on unboxed values, pointer loads — so the resulting machine code is automatically specialized to the user program's types, without any language-specific compiler code. This is the first Futamura projection achieved by observation rather than by static partial evaluation: the "program" being specialized is the (interpreter, user-program) pair, and the residual program is the hot loop with interpreter dispatch constant-folded away.

The engineering that makes meta-tracing practical is substantial: *virtuals* (objects that never escape the trace are never allocated) and aggressive constant folding of the interpreter's dispatch logic. The payoff is equally substantial: a complete, competitive JIT for Python derived from roughly the same interpreter source that the non-JIT VM uses, with the language semantics implemented exactly once [3].

> **Lemma:** In a meta-tracing JIT, every optimization applicable to a recorded trace of the interpreter is automatically a valid optimization of the user program, because the trace's semantics are defined as the interpreter's semantics applied to the recorded inputs.

### 4.3 Polymorphic Inline Caches and Adaptive Recompilation

Long before tracing, the Self project discovered that the cheapest source of type information is the program's own dispatch sites.

| Cache state | Behavior | Typical frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Unlinked | Full dynamic lookup | ~1/3 of sites (cold) |
| Monomorphic | Single type test + direct call | ~90% of linked sites |
| Polymorphic | Linear probe over *k* entries | ~9% of linked sites |
| Megamorphic | Fall back to generic lookup | ~1% of linked sites |

The profound observation of Hölzle, Chambers, and Ungar was that PICs are not merely a dispatch optimization — they are a *type feedback* channel [2].

This is speculation at method granularity, and it required inventing the retreat mechanism: **dynamic deoptimization**.

> **Theorem:** (Guarded invalidation soundness.) Let *M* be an optimized method compiled under assumptions *A*, with a dependency set *D* such that any program change falsifying *A* triggers invalidation of *M*. If every on-stack activation of *M* is deoptimized before control re-enters it after invalidation, then observable program behavior is identical to execution without optimization.

*Proof sketch.* Deoptimization reconstructs, for each optimized frame, the exact interpreter-level state (locals, operand stack, program counter) that the unoptimized method would have at the corresponding point [6]. Since invalidation precedes any re-entry, no guard assumption can be violated by subsequently executed code; the execution is therefore indistinguishable from one in which *M* was never optimized. ∎

### 4.5 The Economics of Deoptimization: A Common Cost Model

All four architectures are governed by one inequality.

```
N · (C_g − C_f − C_guard)  >  C_compile + N · p · C_bail
```

Each architecture chooses a different point on this tradeoff surface:

| Architecture | Unit of speculation | Guard cost | Bailout cost | Characteristic risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trace trees [1] | Loop path | Per-guard type tests | Side exit to interpreter | Trace explosion on branchy code |
| Meta-tracing [3] | Interpreter loop trace | Folded into trace guards | Abort to interpreter | Warmup; interpreter must be "trace-friendly" |
| PIC + adaptive [2][5] | Method | Type tests at call sites | Deoptimize + recompile | Invalidation storms on reflective code |
| Truffle/PE [4][7] | Stabilized AST | Node guards | Deopt to AST interpreter | Partial-evaluation time; PE heuristics |

The table explains the historical record: tracing dominates loop-heavy numeric code but suffers *trace explosion* when a loop's path space is large; method-based adaptive compilation handles branchy, call-heavy code but pays deoptimization on invalidation; partial evaluation gives the best peak code but the longest warmup [4][5].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The soundness argument, generalized

Section 4.3 proved soundness for method-granularity speculation. The argument generalizes to all four families via **guard totality**:

> **Theorem:** (Guarded specialization soundness.) Let *P* be a program and *S(P, π)* a specialization compiled under the observed profile *π*, emitting a guard *g<sub>i</sub>* for each assumption *a<sub>i</sub>* in its assumption set *A*. If the guards are *total* — i.e., for every reachable machine state, either all guards pass (and the specialized code's behavior coincides with *P*'s semantics on that state) or some guard fails (and control transfers to a correct fallback before any observable effect of the specialized code) — then *S(P, π)* is observationally equivalent to *P* for all inputs, regardless of whether *π* matches the actual execution.

*Proof.* Consider any execution of the specialized program. Partition its steps into guarded regions. Within a region where all guards pass, the compiler's correctness argument (applied to the restricted input domain defined by the assumptions) guarantees semantic equivalence. At the first failing guard, control transfers to the fallback *before* any observable effect of the specialized code — this is the defining property of a guard as opposed to an assertion: guards are checked before the state they protect is mutated, and deoptimization reconstructs the pre-guard abstract state [6]. The fallback then executes the original semantics. By induction over regions, the observable trace equals a trace of *P*. ∎

The theorem's practical bite is its contrapositive: *every soundness bug in a speculative JIT is a guard-totality bug* — a path on which an assumption can be violated without any guard firing. This is why production VMs invest heavily in deoptimization testing (e.g., randomized "deopt fuzzing"): the guard set, not the optimizer, is the trusted computing base.

### 5.2 Published performance evidence

| System | Technique | Reported result | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| TraceMonkey | Trace trees, type specialization | 2×–20× over interpreter on traceable JS | [1] |
| Self-93 | PICs + adaptive recompilation | Median 11% from PICs alone; 27% with type-feedback recompilation; ~½–⅓ of optimized C on benchmarks | [2][5] |
| PyPy | Meta-tracing | Competitive with CPython-C-extension performance on long-running numeric code; single interpreter definition | [3] |
| Truffle/Graal | AST specialization + partial evaluation | TruffleRuby ~3.8× JRuby; competitive with V8 on JS benchmarks of the era | [4] |
| HotSpot server | Method-based adaptive, tiered | Near-C++ peak on hot methods; deopt enables aggressive inlining | [8] |

Two regularities emerge. First, *no* system wins everywhere: tracing wins on tight type-stable loops, method-based adaptive wins on call-heavy object-oriented code, and partial evaluation wins on peak throughput at the price of warmup [1][4][5]. Second, the *marginal* gains follow a power law of engineering effort — the first 80% of the speedup comes from specialization itself; the remainder from years of tuning guard placement and inlining heuristics.

## 6 Limitations

**Trace explosion.** Trace-based JITs compile *paths*, and the number of distinct paths through a loop with *b* independent branches is 2<sup>*b*</sup>. Real programs with data-dependent control flow generate trace trees that grow without bound, each trace duplicating compilation cost while covering diminishing execution share. TraceMonkey mitigates this with trace *abortion* (falling back to the interpreter for untraceable constructs), but the fundamental mismatch — path-granularity speculation for branch-granularity programs — remains [1].

**Warmup and memory.** Every tiered system pays twice: compilation pauses (mitigated by background compilation threads [8]) and the memory footprint of multiple code versions plus profiling metadata. Partial-evaluation-based systems pay the most: Graal's PE is a heavyweight analysis, and its compile times make it unsuitable for short-lived processes without caching [4].

**The interpreter author's burden (meta-tracing).** Meta-tracing reuses the JIT but not the tuning: the interpreter must be written in a "trace-friendly" style — small dispatch loops, explicit promotion hints, careful handling of side effects — and performance cliffs appear when the tracer's heuristics misfire on interpreter constructs the author did not anticipate [3].

**Guard-totality verification.** The soundness theorem of Section 5.1 reduces correctness to guard totality, but *verifying* totality is undecidable in general and labor-intensive in practice. Deoptimization bugs are among the most severe VM bugs because they corrupt execution silently; the industry answer is massive randomized testing, not proof [6].

**Reflective and highly dynamic programs.** All four architectures assume behavioral stability: PICs degrade to megamorphic, traces abort, adaptive compilers thrash on invalidation, and ASTs refuse to stabilize when programs redefine methods or `eval` new code in hot loops. The economics of Section 4.5 turn negative, and the VM pays profiling and guard costs for no benefit [2][5].

## 7 Conclusion

Four architectures, one pattern. TraceMonkey records what the program *did* and compiles that path [1]. PyPy records what the interpreter *did* and compiles that [3]. Self's adaptive compiler listens to inline caches and recompiles what the program *is likely to do next* [2][5]. Truffle lets the interpreter specialize itself, then partially evaluates the result [4][7]. In every case the compiler's proof obligation is discharged by *guards*, and the escape hatch — deoptimization, side exits, recompilation — is what makes the edifice sound [6].

The field's open problems are therefore economic rather than algorithmic: how to speculate with less evidence (shorter warmup), how to retreat more cheaply (faster deoptimization), and how to verify guard totality (fewer silent miscompilations). The recent convergence is telling — V8's TurboFan, JavaScriptCore's FTL, and GraalVM all combine inline-cache type feedback, multi-tier adaptive compilation, and deoptimization into a single pipeline, which is itself evidence for the thesis: speculative specialization under guarded deoptimization is the design space's attractor.
