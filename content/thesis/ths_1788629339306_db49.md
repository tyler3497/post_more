Modern real-time ray tracing renders global illumination at 60 Hz with exactly one sample per pixel (1 spp), a feat unimaginable a decade ago. This thesis analyzes the four pillars behind it. First, bounding volume hierarchies built with the surface area heuristic (SAH) — derived from Goldsmith and Salmon's geometric-probability cost model — yield near-optimal trees; binned SAH and Morton-code LBVH builders trade quality for millisecond GPU construction. Second, stackless and short-stack traversal, notably the restart-trail generalization to wide BVHs, eliminates per-ray stack memory and keeps traversal resident in on-chip SRAM. Third, the programmable pipelines of DirectX Raytracing and Vulkan KHR ray tracing expose fixed-function traversal through TLAS/BLAS acceleration structures and the shader binding table, cleanly separating traversal from shading. Fourth, spatiotemporal reconstruction — SVGF's variance-guided temporal accumulation and edge-avoiding à-trous wavelet, and the ReSTIR family's reservoir-based importance resampling — converts 1-spp Monte Carlo noise into temporally stable images, with reported speedups up to 65x. We derive each pillar's mathematics, present primary-source empirical results, and characterize the remaining limitations.

## 1 Introduction

The ambition of *real-time ray tracing* — photorealistic global illumination at interactive frame rates — was for decades considered computationally infeasible. A single 1920×1080 frame rendered by a Monte Carlo path tracer at production quality historically required thousands of *samples per pixel* (spp), each spawning a full ray traversal through a scene of potentially tens of millions of triangles. The naive complexity is forbidding: per frame, billions of ray–primitive intersection tests would be required. Yet by 2026, consumer hardware renders ray-traced global illumination at 60 Hz with exactly **one sample per pixel (1 spp)**. This thesis analyzes the four technical pillars that made this possible: (i) *bounding volume hierarchy* (BVH) acceleration structures constructed with the *surface area heuristic* (SAH) for near-optimal tree quality; (ii) *stackless and short-stack traversal* algorithms that make tree descent tractable under the register and memory pressure of massively parallel GPUs; (iii) the *programmable ray tracing pipelines* of DirectX Raytracing (DXR) and Vulkan's `VK_KHR_ray_tracing_pipeline`, which expose acceleration-structure traversal to the shading language; and (iv) *spatiotemporal reconstruction filters* — SVGF and the ReSTIR family — that convert 1-spp Monte Carlo noise into temporally stable images through importance resampling and variance-guided filtering [1][4][5][6][7].

The central intellectual throughline of this thesis is that **every pillar is an exercise in amortization**: SAH construction amortizes traversal cost over rays; short-stack traversal amortizes memory traffic over on-chip registers; the two-level acceleration structure (TLAS/BLAS) amortizes dynamic-scene rebuild cost over instanced geometry; and spatiotemporal reuse amortizes the cost of each ray over a neighborhood of pixels and the frames that precede them. Understanding real-time ray tracing means understanding how modern renderers make one ray do the work of a thousand.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Rendering Equation and the Curse of Variance

Kajiya's rendering equation expresses outgoing radiance \(L_o\) at a surface point \(x\) as the sum of emitted and reflected radiance:

> **Theorem:** *For a scene obeying geometric optics, the equilibrium radiance field satisfies*
> \(L_o(x, \omega_o) = L_e(x, \omega_o) + \int_\Omega f_r(x, \omega_i, \omega_o)\, L_i(x, \omega_i)\, (\omega_i \cdot n)\, d\omega_i\)
> *where \(f_r\) is the bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF) and \(\Omega\) is the hemisphere of incoming directions.*

Monte Carlo estimators of this integral converge at the textbook rate \(O(1/\sqrt{N})\): halving noise requires four times the samples. At 1 spp, the estimator is unbiased but enormously *noisy* — the central problem of modern real-time rendering. All denoising theory in §4.4–4.5 is downstream of this rate.

### 2.2 From Whitted to Hardware: A Condensed History

The lineage relevant to this thesis runs as follows:

1. **1980** — Whitted's recursive ray tracing establishes the algorithmic vocabulary: rays, intersections, recursive shading.
2. **1987** — Goldsmith and Salmon derive the cost model that becomes the *surface area heuristic* (SAH), linking node visit probability to surface area ratios [1].
3. **2006–2007** — Lauterbach et al. demonstrate GPU BVH construction via Morton-code linearization (LBVH), proving that *build* cost need not dominate dynamic scenes.
4. **2010** — Laine and Karras introduce the *restart trail* for stackless binary-BVH traversal, generalized by Vaidyanathan et al. to wide BVHs with a short stack in 2019 [3].
5. **2017** — Schied et al. publish **SVGF**, the first spatiotemporal filter to make 1-spp path tracing visually stable [4].
6. **2018** — NVIDIA ships RTX; Microsoft publishes the **DXR** functional specification, exposing hardware traversal [8].
7. **2020** — Bitterli et al. publish **ReSTIR**, reformulating many-light sampling as *reservoir-based spatiotemporal importance resampling*, achieving up to 65× speedups over prior direct-lighting techniques [5].
8. **2022** — Lin et al. unify the theory as *Generalized Resampled Importance Sampling* (GRIS) [6]; Khronos finalizes the Vulkan ray tracing extensions [7].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *survey-analytic* study: we reconstruct the mathematics of each pillar from primary sources, cross-validate claims against independent implementations (notably the open SVGF reference [4] and the GRIS formalism [6]), and synthesize a coherent performance model. Our methodology has three components:

- **Cost-model derivation.** We derive the SAH cost function from geometric probability (following Goldsmith–Salmon via Wald et al. [1]) and extend it to *binned* SAH, the workhorse of GPU builders.
- **Algorithmic reconstruction.** We re-derive stackless traversal (restart trail [3]) and short-stack wide-BVH traversal, stating invariants precisely enough to implement.
- **Pipeline and filter analysis.** We dissect the DXR/Vulkan execution model (ray generation → traversal → hit/miss → shaders, mediated by the *shader binding table*) [7][8] and the temporal accumulation / à-trous wavelet cascade of SVGF [4], alongside the reservoir resampling mathematics of ReSTIR [5][6].

Where quantitative figures are given (traversal steps, speedups, iteration counts), they are cited from the primary papers' reported measurements, not reproduced experiments.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 BVH Construction and the Surface Area Heuristic

A bounding volume hierarchy organizes scene primitives into a tree of axis-aligned bounding boxes (AABBs). Ray traversal visits nodes in front-to-back order, pruning subtrees whose AABBs miss the ray, reducing intersection cost from \(O(N)\) to \(O(\log N)\) in well-constructed trees. The *quality* of the tree dominates traversal performance — and tree quality is governed by the SAH.

> **Theorem (SAH cost):** *Under the assumption that rays striking the root are uniformly distributed in origin and direction, the expected traversal cost of a BVH is*
> \(C = \sum_{\text{internal } b} C_{\text{trav}} \frac{A(b)}{A(\text{root})} + \sum_{\text{leaf } \ell} C_{\text{isect}}\, |\ell|\, \frac{A(\ell)}{A(\text{root})}\)
> *where \(A(\cdot)\) is surface area, \(C_{\text{trav}}\) the cost of a node visit, and \(C_{\text{isect}}\) the cost of one primitive test.*

The derivation rests on a result from geometric probability: the probability that a random ray hits a convex body's AABB is proportional to its surface area (Santalo's formula), a fact Wald et al. exploit explicitly [1]. Construction then proceeds *top-down and greedily*: at each node, choose the partition minimizing the two-leaf SAH cost

\[C_{\text{split}} = C_{\text{trav}} + \frac{A(S_1)}{A(S)} |S_1| C_{\text{isect}} + \frac{A(S_2)}{A(S)} |S_2| C_{\text{isect}},\]

evaluated over candidate split planes. Exhaustive evaluation is \(O(N^2)\); production builders use **binned SAH**, quantizing each axis into \(B\) bins (typically 32–64) and sweeping split candidates between bins in \(O(B)\) per axis — near-optimal quality at a fraction of the cost [1].

| Builder | Construction | SAH quality | Dynamic-friendly? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full-sweep SAH (CPU) | Slow (minutes for 10M tris) | ~optimal | No (refit only) |
| Binned SAH (CPU/GPU) | Seconds | 95–99% of optimal | Partial |
| LBVH / HLBVH (GPU, Morton) | Milliseconds | 80–90% | **Yes** |
| SBVH (spatial splits) | Very slow | ≥ optimal (tighter bounds) | No |

For *dynamic* scenes, full rebuilds are often unaffordable: the industry standard is a hybrid — refit (update bounds in place, preserving topology) for deformable geometry [1], and LBVH-style Morton-code rebuilds (sort primitives by 30-bit interleaved centroid codes, then build via Karras's single-pass hierarchy algorithm) when topology must change. The GPU's parallelism makes Morton-sort rebuilds of million-triangle scenes achievable in a few milliseconds, at a modest quality penalty that spatial-split refinements (SBVH) can later repair.

```cpp
// Binned SAH split selection (simplified)
float bestCost = FLT_MAX; int bestAxis = 0, bestBin = 0;
for (int axis = 0; axis < 3; ++axis) {
    Bin bins[NBINS] = {};
    for (auto& prim : prims) bins[binOf(prim.centroid[axis])].add(prim);
    // left-to-right and right-to-left sweeps accumulate SA and counts
    for (int b = 1; b < NBINS; ++b) {
        float c = sahCost(prefix[b-1], suffix[b], nodeArea);
        if (c < bestCost) { bestCost = c; bestAxis = axis; bestBin = b; }
    }
}
```

### 4.2 Stackless and Short-Stack Traversal

Classical BVH traversal maintains an explicit *stack* of deferred nodes: when both children of a node are hit, the farther child is pushed and the nearer descended into. On a GPU, a per-thread stack of 32–64 node pointers is a register file catastrophe — thousands of concurrent rays multiply this pressure, and spilling the stack to memory destroys traversal throughput. Two related answers exist.

**Stackless traversal** eliminates the stack entirely. The canonical approach augments each node with *escape/rope* pointers (or computes them implicitly), so that when a subtree is exhausted, traversal continues at a precomputed successor. Karras's 2012 formulation derives neighbor links directly from the Morton-code ordering used in LBVH construction, making stackless traversal a natural complement to GPU builds. The deeper insight is that a total order over leaves (the Morton curve) induces a traversal order requiring no auxiliary storage.

**The restart trail** (Laine–Karras 2010; generalized to wide BVHs by Vaidyanathan et al. 2019 [3]) takes the middle path: keep a *short* stack of the top few entries, and record a *restart trail* — a compact bit-vector encoding, at each level, which child subtree was already visited. When the short stack underflows, the trail allows traversal to resume by descending from the root while skipping visited branches. For a BVH of width \(w\) and depth \(d\), the trail requires only \(d \log_2 w\) bits; Vaidyanathan et al. demonstrate that **five stack entries** achieve traversal-step counts within a few percent of a full stack on wide BVHs, while the entire working set fits in on-chip memory [3]. Their leaf-culling extension additionally discards leaves once a closer hit is known, cutting primitive intersections by up to 14%.

> **Theorem (restart-trail correctness):** *A traversal state consisting of (short stack S, restart trail T, current depth d) is sufficient to visit exactly the unvisited hit nodes of the BVH, because T encodes the visited/unvisited status of every ancestor's sibling subtree, and any pop that exhausts S can be simulated by re-descending along T's unvisited branch.*

The practical consequence is profound: *stack depth is no longer a per-ray memory cost*, which is what allows hardware RT cores and wide-SIMD software traversal to sustain tens of billions of box tests per second.

### 4.3 Programmable Pipelines: DXR and Vulkan Ray Tracing

DXR (2018) and the Vulkan `VK_KHR_ray_tracing_pipeline` / `VK_KHR_acceleration_structure` extensions (finalized 2020) [7][8] converged on a common execution model, because both expose the same hardware. The model has four ingredients:

1. **Two-level acceleration structures.** Geometry lives in *bottom-level* structures (BLAS, one per mesh); *instances* of BLASes with transforms compose the *top-level* structure (TLAS). This mirrors the SAH amortization theme: BLASes are built once (or refit), while the TLAS — small, containing only instance descriptors — is rebuilt per frame, making dynamic scenes affordable.
2. **Dispatch, not draw.** `DispatchRays` (DXR) / `vkCmdTraceRaysKHR` (Vulkan) launches a grid of *ray generation* shader invocations, replacing the raster pipeline's vertex/fragment stages.
3. **The shader binding table (SBT).** Hit groups (intersection + any-hit + closest-hit shader triples), miss shaders, and callable shaders are indexed through the SBT, keyed by ray type, instance, and geometry — the mechanism by which *materials* become programmable under ray tracing.
4. **Payload and recursion control.** Ray payloads carry radiance state through the pipeline; recursion depth is bounded (DXR 1.0 permitted up to 31, practical engines use far less), and modern APIs favor *iterative* multi-bounce path tracing via ray queries (`VK_KHR_ray_query`) in compute shaders over deep recursion.

```hlsl
// DXR ray generation shader (simplified)
[shader("raygeneration")]
void RayGen() {
    RayDesc ray; ray.Origin = camPos; ray.Direction = dir;
    ray.TMin = 0.001; ray.TMax = 1e38;
    RayPayload payload = (RayPayload)0;
    TraceRay(tlas, RAY_FLAG_NONE, 0xFF, 0 /*ray idx*/, 1 /*mul*/, 0 /*miss idx*/,
             ray, payload);
    output[DispatchRaysIndex().xy] = payload.color;
}
```

The API-level lesson is that **traversal is a fixed-function service, shading is programmable**: the hardware walks the BVH and reports ordered hits; everything else — BRDF evaluation, next-event estimation, Russian roulette — is shader code. This division is what allows the same silicon to serve shadow rays, reflections, and full path tracing.

### 4.4 SVGF: Spatiotemporal Variance-Guided Filtering

Schied et al.'s SVGF (2017) [4] answers the 1-spp problem with a three-stage pipeline applied per frame:

1. **Temporal accumulation.** Each pixel reprojects its world-space position into the previous frame using motion vectors; if the reprojected sample is *valid* (depth/normal/mesh-ID consistency checks pass), it is exponentially accumulated: \(\bar{c} \leftarrow \alpha c_{\text{hist}} + (1-\alpha) c_{\text{new}}\). Crucially, SVGF also accumulates **luminance moments** \(\mathbb{E}[l]\) and \(\mathbb{E}[l^2]\), yielding a per-pixel *variance estimate* \(\sigma^2 = \mathbb{E}[l^2] - \mathbb{E}[l]^2\) — the "variance-guided" half of the name. The history length \(n\) adapts the spatial filter that follows.
2. **Variance estimation and spatial prefilter.** A 3×3 neighborhood computes spatial variance where temporal history was invalidated, preventing flicker from bootstrapping with zero history.
3. **Edge-avoiding à-trous wavelet transform.** The color and variance are filtered through 4–5 iterations of the à-trous ("with holes") wavelet: each iteration doubles the sampling stride while keeping a 5×5 kernel, growing the effective footprint exponentially (to ~65×65 px) at constant per-iteration cost. The edge-stopping weights penalize taps whose depth, normal, or luminance differ from the center pixel:

\[w(p,q) = w_z\, w_n\, w_l,\quad w_l = \exp\!\left(-\frac{|l(p)-l(q)|}{\sigma_l \sqrt{\sigma^2(p)} + \varepsilon}\right),\]

so that *the filter width adapts to local confidence*: high variance → wide blur; low variance → edge-preserving tight filter.

The genius of SVGF is that the variance estimate **closes the loop**: the same quantity that measures noise also controls the reconstruction kernel. Its limitations — ghosting on fast motion, over-blurring of fine detail, light leaking through the wide wavelet footprint — motivated the next generation.

### 4.5 ReSTIR: Reservoir Resampling for the Many-Light Problem

If SVGF *reconstructs* noisy samples, ReSTIR [5] *improves the samples themselves*. The many-light problem — scenes with thousands to millions of emissive sources — makes uniform light selection hopelessly inefficient: the probability of randomly choosing the one light that matters through a doorway is negligible. ReSTIR's answer is **resampled importance sampling (RIS)** executed in *reservoirs* and shared *spatiotemporally*.

Each pixel maintains a tiny **reservoir**: a weighted sample (one selected light), its target-function value, and the sum of weights \(w_{\text{sum}}\) over \(M\) candidates. The mathematics, unified by Lin et al. as GRIS [6], rests on two operations:

- **Resampling:** from \(M\) candidates drawn from a cheap source distribution \(p\), select one with probability proportional to the *target* \(\hat{p}\) (unshadowed contribution), correcting with the RIS weight \(W = \frac{1}{M}\sum \hat{p}(x_i)/p(x_i)\).
- **Reservoir merging:** combine the current reservoir with a neighbor's (spatial reuse) or the reprojected prior frame's (temporal reuse) via *weighted reservoir sampling*, updating \(W\) with MIS-like balance heuristics between the contributing domains [6].

> **Theorem (ReSTIR unbiasedness):** *If every merge applies the correct pairwise MIS weight between source domains and visibility is evaluated on the final resampled light, the resulting estimator is unbiased for direct illumination — reuse introduces correlation, not bias [5][6].*

The empirical payoff is staggering: Bitterli et al. report **up to 65× speedup** over prior many-light techniques, rendering millions of dynamic area lights in real time [5]. ReSTIR GI (2021) extends the framework to indirect illumination by resampling *paths* rather than lights, and the 2023 course notes [9] emphasize the subtle failure mode practitioners must respect: *spurious correlation* from naive reuse silently biases results, which is why the GRIS formalism's treatment of shift mappings and Jacobian corrections [6] is not optional theory but production necessity.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We collect the headline quantitative claims of the primary sources, which together constitute the empirical case for 1-spp rendering:

| Result | Source | Figure |
|---|---|---|
| Binned SAH within ~1–5% of full-sweep SAH cost at 10× build speed | Wald et al. 2007 [1] | Build-quality tradeoff |
| 5-entry short stack ≈ full-stack traversal steps on BVH-8 | Vaidyanathan et al. 2019 [3] | Traversal efficiency |
| Leaf culling cuts primitive tests up to 14% | Vaidyanathan et al. 2019 [3] | Intersection reduction |
| SVGF at 1 spp ≈ reference quality; TITAN X (Pascal) real-time | Schied et al. 2017 [4] | Denoiser performance |
| ReSTIR: 65× faster than prior many-light methods | Bitterli et al. 2020 [5] | Sampling efficiency |
| Vulkan RT: hardware traversal via `vkCmdTraceRaysKHR` | Khronos 2020 [7] | API finalization |

A proof sketch of the end-to-end claim — *1 spp suffices* — chains these results: (i) SAH binned construction + short-stack traversal make each ray cheap (memory-bound, not compute-bound); (ii) DXR/Vulkan expose enough ray throughput (~10 G rays/s on flagship GPUs) to path-trace 2 M pixels with 2–3 rays each per frame; (iii) ReSTIR ensures those few rays are *important* (low variance per sample); (iv) SVGF's variance-guided reconstruction converts residual noise into a stable image. The *composition* is the result: no single pillar achieves real-time path tracing alone.

Formally, consider the mean-squared error decomposition \(\text{MSE} = \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance}\). ReSTIR drives *variance per sample* down through importance resampling; SVGF trades a small, controlled *bias* (edge-stopped blur) for a large variance reduction; temporal accumulation drives effective \(N\) up over frames. The system converges because each stage attacks a different term of the decomposition.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires enumerating where the 1-spp edifice strains:

- **SAH's uniform-ray assumption fails** for coherent primary rays and highly directional secondary distributions; learned cost models and *ray-specialized* contraction [2] address this but add build complexity.
- **Dynamic geometry** remains the Achilles' heel: refitting degrades SAH quality over time (the "BVH rot" problem), while full LBVH rebuilds cost milliseconds that compete with the frame budget. Hybrid refit/rebuild schedulers are heuristic, not optimal.
- **Stackless traversal trades memory for computation**: restart-trail re-descents re-test nodes, and escape-pointer layouts inflate node size. The optimum depends on SIMD width and cache hierarchy — there is no universally best traversal.
- **Denoiser failure modes are visible**: SVGF ghosts under fast motion and disocclusion; ReSTIR correlates noise spatiotemporally, producing *structured* artifacts (boiling, smearing) that are perceptually worse than white noise at equal MSE. Fireflies from unclamped specular paths still require heuristic clamping.
- **API fragmentation persists**: DXR, Vulkan RT, and Metal's ray tracing differ in shader-table layout, recursion limits, and ray-query semantics, tripling engine validation cost [7][8].
- **1 spp is a floor, not a ceiling**: glossy interreflections, caustics, and participating media still demand more samples or specialized estimators (e.g., ReSTIR PT [6]).

## 7 Conclusion

Real-time ray tracing at one sample per pixel is not the triumph of a single algorithm but of a *stack of amortizations*: the surface area heuristic amortizes construction effort over rays [1]; Morton-ordered GPU builders amortize rebuild cost over frames; short-stack traversal amortizes memory traffic over registers [3]; the TLAS/BLAS split amortizes scene dynamism over instancing [7][8]; and SVGF and ReSTIR amortize each traced ray over neighborhoods and history [4][5][6]. The rendering equation has not changed since 1986 — what changed is our ability to spend each ray wisely and to reconstruct, from deliberate noise, an image the eye accepts as truth.

The frontier is now clear. *Neural* reconstruction (building on SVGF's variance-guided insight with learned kernels), *shader execution reordering* to tame incoherent divergence, and *unified resampling frameworks* extending GRIS to full path space [6] are converging toward a future where the sample budget ceases to be the binding constraint at all. When that day comes, the pillars analyzed here — SAH trees, stackless descent, programmable pipelines, spatiotemporal reuse — will remain, because they are not artifacts of scarcity. They are the geometry of the problem itself.
