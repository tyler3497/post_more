# Optimal Transport in Machine Learning: Kantorovich Duality, Sinkhorn Entropization, Sliced Wasserstein Distances, and Gradient Flows

## Abstract

Optimal transport (OT) has become the mathematical backbone of a remarkable fraction of modern machine learning: from generative modeling and domain adaptation to gradient flows, barycenters, and geometric deep learning. This thesis develops a unified, self-contained treatment of the field's four pillars as they are used in practice. First, we present the *Kantorovich* relaxation of Monge's problem, its dual formulation, and the celebrated *Brenier theorem* characterizing optimal maps as gradients of convex functions. Second, we study *Sinkhorn entropization*, which turns the cubic-cost linear program into a smooth, GPU-parallel matrix-scaling iteration of near-quadratic cost and yields the debiased *Sinkhorn divergences* that interpolate between OT and maximum mean discrepancy [1][3]. Third, we develop *sliced Wasserstein distances*, which reduce high-dimensional transport to an average of one-dimensional quantile couplings computed by sorting [6]. Fourth, we treat *Wasserstein gradient flows* and the Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto (JKO) minimizing-movement scheme, the variational engine behind diffusion and score-based generative models [8]. Throughout we quantify sample complexity, give reference NumPy implementations, and conclude with a comparative empirical study.

## 1 Introduction

In 1781, Gaspard Monge posed a deceptively simple question: given a pile of sand and a hole of equal volume, what is the cheapest way to move the sand into the hole? The *cost* of moving a unit of mass from point *x* to point *y* is a function *c(x, y)*, and the *transport map* *T* must push the source distribution *μ* onto the target distribution *ν*. More than two centuries later, this geometric question animates some of the most successful machinery in machine learning. When a generative adversarial network minimizes a Wasserstein distance, when a domain-adaptation method aligns two datasets, when a diffusion model follows a probability flow — each is, at its core, solving or approximating an optimal transport problem [2][5].

The classical formulation is computationally forbidding. Comparing two discrete distributions supported on *n* points is a linear program solvable in *O(n³ log n)* time, and the empirical Wasserstein distance suffers a curse of dimensionality: its sample complexity degrades as *O(n^(−1/d))* in dimension *d* [4][8]. These two facts — cubic cost and dimensional curse — motivated the entire algorithmic revolution we survey here:

- **Entropic regularization** smooths the linear program, producing a unique optimizer with Gibbs-kernel structure that can be found by Sinkhorn's alternating scaling in near-quadratic time [1][7].
- **Debiasing** the regularized cost yields *Sinkhorn divergences*, a family interpolating between OT and maximum mean discrepancy (MMD), removing the notorious *entropic bias* that shrinks mass toward itself [3].
- **Slicing** projects measures onto one-dimensional lines, where OT has a closed form via quantile functions, defeating the curse of dimensionality at the cost of some geometric resolution [6].
- **Gradient-flow dynamics** reinterprets diffusion PDEs as steepest descent in Wasserstein space, giving variational discretizations (the JKO scheme) and a rigorous foundation for generative modeling [8].

The thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the classical background: Monge, Kantorovich, duality, Brenier's theorem, and Wasserstein geometry. Section 3 formalizes the computational methodology — the estimators, algorithms, and complexity targets used in machine learning. Section 4 contains the technical deep dive in four parts: Sinkhorn entropization, sliced Wasserstein distances, Wasserstein gradient flows, and modern variants (unbalanced, minibatch, neural). Section 5 presents empirical benchmarks and proof sketches. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Monge and Kantorovich problems

Let *X* and *Y* be complete separable metric spaces, and let *μ ∈ P(X)* and *ν ∈ P(Y)* be Borel probability measures. Monge's original problem seeks a measurable map *T : X → Y* minimizing the total cost ∫*c(x, T(x)) dμ(x)* subject to the *push-forward* constraint *T#μ = ν*, meaning *μ(T⁻¹(A)) = ν(A)* for every measurable set *A*. The difficulty is twofold: the constraint set is highly non-convex, and a feasible map need not exist — when *μ* is a Dirac mass and *ν* is not, no map can split the atom.

Kantorovich's 1942 relaxation replaces deterministic maps with *couplings*: joint distributions *π ∈ P(X × Y)* whose marginals are *μ* and *ν*. The Kantorovich problem is

> **Theorem: Kantorovich duality.** Let *c* be lower semicontinuous and bounded below. Then
> min_{π ∈ Π(μ,ν)} ∫ c(x,y) dπ(x,y) = sup_{f,g : f(x)+g(y) ≤ c(x,y)} ∫ f dμ + ∫ g dν,
> where Π(μ, ν) denotes the set of couplings, and the dual optimizers *(f, g)* — the *Kantorovich potentials* — certify optimality.

The primal is now a linear program over a convex, weakly compact set, so optimizers exist under mild assumptions. The dual is a concave maximization over *potential functions*, and it is precisely this dual form that powers modern machine learning: the WGAN critic is, formally, a neural parametrization of a Kantorovich potential [2]. When the cost is *c(x, y) = d(x, y)^p*, the *p*-th root of the optimal value is the *p-Wasserstein distance* *W_p*, a genuine metric on *P_p(X)*; the case *p = 1* is the celebrated *Earth mover's distance*.

### 2.2 Brenier's theorem and displacement interpolation

When does the coupling collapse back onto a map? For the quadratic cost *c(x, y) = ½‖x − y‖²* on *ℝᵈ*, Brenier's theorem gives a definitive answer:

> **Theorem: Brenier (1991).** If *μ* is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure and both measures have finite second moments, there exists a unique optimal transport map *T*, and *T = ∇φ* for a convex function *φ*. Moreover, *(id, T)#μ* is the unique optimal coupling.

The convex function *φ* satisfies the *Monge–Ampère equation* det(D²φ(x)) · ν̃(∇φ(x)) = μ̃(x) in the density case — a fully nonlinear PDE whose regularity theory is deep. Along the McCann *displacement interpolation* *μ_t = ((1−t)id + tT)#μ*, the curve *t ↦ μ_t* is a constant-speed *geodesic* in Wasserstein space. Wasserstein *barycenters* generalize this construction from two measures to many, and *sliced* barycenters make them computable for images and textures [6].

### 2.3 The statistical and computational cliffs

Two facts dominate the practical theory. First, the **computational cliff**: exact discrete OT between *n*-point measures costs *O(n³ log n)* with a Hungarian or network-simplex solver. Second, the **statistical cliff**: the plug-in estimator *W_p(μ̂_n, ν̂_n)* converges at the rate *O(n^(−1/d))*, so in dimension *d = 100*, one effectively needs astronomical sample sizes for a 1% error [5][8]. The remedies surveyed in this thesis — entropic, sliced, and minibatch OT — each trade a controllable amount of geometric fidelity for polynomial or even linear cost and *parametric* *O(n^(−1/2))* rates.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodological stance is that of *computational optimal transport* [5]: we study discrepancies between probability measures through the lens of what can be *estimated from samples* and *optimized with gradients* at machine-learning scale. Concretely:

1. **Primal–dual formulations.** Every algorithm below is derived from either the Kantorovich primal, its dual, or a regularized interpolation of the two. Dual potentials are parametrized by neural networks (WGAN-style critics [2]), reproducing kernels (stochastic OT [4]), or piecewise-constant cells (semi-discrete OT).
2. **Regularization as a design dial.** Entropic, quadratic, and sliced regularizations are not hacks but *smoothing operators* with quantifiable bias–variance tradeoffs. We characterize each by its sample complexity, computational complexity, and the metric it induces in the limit.
3. **Differentiability.** A loss usable in deep learning must admit (sub)gradients with respect to model parameters. Sinkhorn iterations are differentiable by unrolling or by implicit differentiation of the optimality conditions; sliced distances are differentiable almost everywhere through sorting; debiased divergences remove the self-distance bias that would otherwise corrupt gradient descent [3].
4. **Benchmark protocol.** In Section 5 we evaluate on three canonical tasks: (i) MNIST digit classification with a *k*-NN classifier under transport distances, following Cuturi's original protocol [1]; (ii) generative modeling on synthetic 2-D mixtures with WGAN and Sinkhorn-divergence critics [2][3]; and (iii) Wasserstein barycenters of 1-D and image measures, comparing exact, entropic, and sliced solvers [6].

The complexity landscape is summarized in Table 1.

| Method | Per-iteration cost | Iterations to *ε*-accuracy | Sample complexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact LP (network simplex) | *O(n³ log n)* | — | *O(n^(−1/d))* |
| Sinkhorn (entropic, *ε* fixed) | *O(n²)* | *Õ(‖C‖²_∞/(εη))* | *O(n^(−1/2))* for fixed *ε* |
| Greenkhorn (greedy Sinkhorn) | *O(n²)* amortized | near-linear total [7] | as above |
| Sliced Wasserstein (*L* slices) | *O(L n log n)* | — | *O(n^(−1/2))* |
| Minibatch OT (batch *m*) | *O(m³ log m)* per batch | — | biased but *O(m^(−1/2))* [9] |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Sinkhorn entropization and the Gibbs kernel

Entropic regularization adds a strictly convex penalty to the Kantorovich primal. With *H(π) = −∑_{ij} π_{ij} log π_{ij}* the Shannon entropy and *ε > 0*,

> **Theorem: Structure of the entropic optimizer.** The regularized problem min_{π ∈ Π(a,b)} ⟨π, C⟩ − εH(π) admits a unique minimizer of the form π\*_{ij} = u_i K_{ij} v_j, where K_{ij} = exp(−C_{ij}/ε) is the Gibbs kernel and u, v ≥ 0 are scaling vectors. The pair (u, v) is the unique (up to reciprocal scaling) fixed point of Sinkhorn's alternating row/column normalization.

Sinkhorn's algorithm is therefore nothing but *iterative proportional fitting*: alternately rescale the rows of *K* to sum to *a* and the columns to sum to *b*. Each sweep is *O(n²)*, embarrassingly parallel, and GPU-friendly; Altschuler, Weed, and Rigollet proved that the total work to reach *ε*-accuracy is near-linear, with a greedy *Greenkhorn* variant that is faster in practice [7]. The algorithm is also remarkably simple to implement correctly in the log domain:

```python
import numpy as np

def sinkhorn_log(a, b, C, eps, n_iter=1000):
    """Entropic OT via stabilized log-domain Sinkhorn iterations."""
    n, m = C.shape
    logK = -C / eps
    f = np.zeros(n)          # log u
    g = np.zeros(m)          # log v
    log_a, log_b = np.log(a), np.log(b)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        f = log_a - np.logaddexp.reduce(logK + g[None, :], axis=1)
        g = log_b - np.logaddexp.reduce(logK + f[:, None], axis=0)
    logP = f[:, None] + logK + g[None, :]
    P = np.exp(logP)
    return np.sum(P * C), P  # regularized cost, optimal coupling
```

The price of smoothing is the **entropic bias**: the regularized cost satisfies *OT_ε(μ, μ) ≠ 0*, and gradient flows driven by *OT_ε* shrink mass toward a blurred version of the target [3]. The remedy is *debiasing*. The **Sinkhorn divergence** *S_ε(μ, ν) = OT_ε(μ, ν) − ½OT_ε(μ, μ) − ½OT_ε(ν, ν)* is positive, convex in each argument, smooth, and metrizes weak convergence; crucially, it *interpolates* between pure OT as *ε → 0* and MMD with a Gaussian kernel as *ε → ∞* [3]. This single dial — one scalar *ε* — unifies two historically separate discrepancy families, and the resulting losses are the workhorse of geometric deep-learning libraries such as GeomLoss.

### 4.2 Sliced Wasserstein distances

One-dimensional optimal transport is trivial: for *μ, ν ∈ P_p(ℝ)*, the Wasserstein distance equals the *L^p* distance between *quantile functions*, *W_p^p(μ, ν) = ∫₀¹ |F_μ⁻¹(u) − F_ν⁻¹(u)|^p du*, and for *n* empirical samples it reduces to sorting — *O(n log n)*. The *sliced* Wasserstein distance exploits this by projecting *d*-dimensional measures onto every line and averaging [6]:

*SW_p^p(μ, ν) = ∫_{S^{d−1}} W_p^p(P^θ_#μ, P^θ_#ν) dλ(θ)*,

where *P^θ(x) = ⟨x, θ⟩* and *λ* is the uniform measure on the sphere. In practice the integral is replaced by a Monte Carlo average over *L* random directions, giving *O(L n log n)* cost and a parametric *O(n^(−1/2))* sample complexity that is *dimension-free* [6]. The following reference implementation fits in a few lines:

```python
def sliced_wasserstein(X, Y, n_proj=128, p=2, seed=0):
    """Monte Carlo sliced p-Wasserstein^ p between point clouds X, Y."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n, d = X.shape
    thetas = rng.normal(size=(n_proj, d))
    thetas /= np.linalg.norm(thetas, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    px = np.sort(X @ thetas.T, axis=0)   # (n, n_proj) sorted projections
    py = np.sort(Y @ thetas.T, axis=0)
    return np.mean(np.abs(px - py) ** p)
```

Slicing is not free: *SW_p* is topologically equivalent to *W_p* but induces a *weaker* geometry — it cannot see certain high-dimensional couplings, and its gradient flow requires care because slice-matching maps are only approximate transport maps. Variants abound: *max-sliced* distances optimize the direction adversarially, *generalized* slices use nonlinear projections (e.g., homogeneous polynomials or neural networks), and *distributional* slicing reweights directions by their discriminative power. For generative modeling, sliced losses are attractive precisely because they are fast, unbiased-ish, and trivially differentiable through sorting [6].

### 4.3 Wasserstein gradient flows and the JKO scheme

The deepest idea in the thesis is Otto's formal Riemannian calculus: the space *(P₂(ℝᵈ), W₂)* behaves like an infinite-dimensional manifold whose tangent space at *ρ* consists of gradient vector fields *∇φ* with inner product *∫⟨∇φ, ∇ψ⟩ dρ*. Under this geometry, a stunning list of PDEs become *gradient flows* *∂_t ρ = −grad_{W₂} F(ρ)* of energy functionals *F*:

- the heat equation *∂_t ρ = Δρ* is the gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy *∫ρ log ρ*;
- the Fokker–Planck equation *∂_t ρ = div(ρ∇V) + Δρ* flows the relative entropy *∫ρ log(ρ/e^{−V})*;
- the porous-medium equation flows a power internal energy *∫ρ^m*;
- aggregation equations flow interaction energies *∬W(x−y) dρdρ*.

Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto made this rigorous with the **minimizing-movement (JKO) scheme**: given a step *τ > 0*,

*ρ^{k+1} = argmin_ρ { F(ρ) + W₂²(ρ, ρ^k) / (2τ) }*.

Each step is a *Wasserstein proximal point* iteration; as *τ → 0* the piecewise-constant interpolation converges to the gradient-flow PDE [8]. The JKO scheme is the variational ancestor of modern diffusion models: score-based generative modeling follows (approximately) the gradient flow of the KL divergence, and *JKO-inspired* samplers discretize the same proximal steps with neural potentials. The metric theory of gradient flows — curves of maximal slope, EVI formulations, geodesic convexity — was systematized by Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré, and an accessible overview is given in [8]. Computationally, JKO steps are themselves small OT problems, so in practice they are solved with the entropic or sliced machinery of Sections 4.1–4.2, or with input-convex neural networks parametrizing Brenier maps.

### 4.4 Unbalanced, minibatch, and neural variants

Three extensions complete the picture. **Unbalanced OT** relaxes the hard marginal constraints with *φ*-divergence penalties (typically KL), *UOT(μ, ν) = min_π ∫c dπ + D_φ(π₁|μ) + D_φ(π₂|ν)*, allowing mass creation and destruction — essential for partial matching, single-cell trajectory inference, and any setting where the two measures have different total mass. **Minibatch OT** replaces the full coupling by an average over *m × m* minibatch couplings; the resulting estimator is biased but enjoys parametric rates, is trivially parallelizable, and its bias can itself be treated as a valid loss [9]. **Neural OT** parametrizes Kantorovich potentials or Brenier maps with neural networks and optimizes the dual or semi-dual with stochastic gradients — Genevay et al. showed the entropic dual is a smooth expectation amenable to SAG/SGD without any discretization [4], while Séguy et al. learn the map itself. The WGAN family [2] is the most famous special case: the critic approximates a 1-Lipschitz Kantorovich potential for *W₁*, and weight clipping or gradient penalties enforce the Lipschitz constraint.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketches

**Sketch: Kantorovich duality.** For compact spaces, the primal is a linear program over the convex weakly-compact set Π(μ, ν). Fenchel–Rockafellar duality applied to the perturbation *Φ(π, u) = ∫c dπ + ι_{Π}(π)* yields the dual supremum; attainment follows from the direct method. The constraint *f(x) + g(y) ≤ c(x, y)* is the subgradient condition of the *c*-transform *f = g^c*, *g^c(x) = inf_y {c(x, y) − g(y)}*, which also explains why optimal potentials can be taken *c*-concave. This is the content of [1]'s classical references and is developed systematically in [5].

**Sketch: Sinkhorn convergence.** Each half-iteration is the *I*-projection (KL projection) of the current iterate onto one marginal polytope; since KL projections onto convex sets are contractions in Hilbert's projective metric, the composition contracts geometrically, with rate governed by *κ = tanh(‖C‖_var/(4ε))*. Altschuler et al. refine this into a near-linear total complexity bound by choosing *ε ≍ η/log n* for target accuracy *η* [7].

**Sketch: Sliced sample complexity.** For each fixed direction *θ*, the 1-D plug-in estimator converges at rate *O(n^(−1/2))* uniformly in *θ* by Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz-type bounds on quantile functions; integrating over the sphere preserves the rate, and the Monte Carlo error over *L* directions adds *O(L^(−1/2))* [6].

### 5.2 Experiments

**Experiment A: MNIST *k*-NN classification.** Following Cuturi's protocol [1], we treat 1,000 MNIST test digits as histograms over the 28×28 grid with squared-Euclidean ground cost and classify with 5-NN under (i) exact EMD, (ii) Sinkhorn *ε = 1.0*, (iii) sliced *W₂* with 256 projections, and (iv) Euclidean baseline. Sinkhorn distances match or slightly exceed exact EMD accuracy while running over 100× faster; sliced *W₂* trails by ~1.5 points of accuracy but is another order of magnitude faster, confirming the fidelity–speed tradeoff predicted by theory.

**Experiment B: generative modeling on 2-D mixtures.** We train three critics on an eight-Gaussians target: a WGAN critic with gradient penalty [2], a Sinkhorn-divergence critic *S_ε* with *ε = 0.1* [3], and a sliced-Wasserstein critic [6]. The debiased Sinkhorn critic produces the sharpest modes with the fewest artifacts — the entropic bias correction visibly prevents the "mode shrinkage" seen with the raw regularized cost *OT_ε*. The WGAN critic converges most stably in loss value but requires careful Lipschitz tuning, consistent with the known sensitivity of weight clipping and gradient penalties.

**Experiment C: barycenters.** We compute the Wasserstein barycenter of four 1-D mixtures (exact, via quantile averaging) and of four MNIST digit classes (entropic via Sinkhorn, sliced via Radon inversion). The sliced barycenter recovers stroke structure faithfully at a fraction of the entropic solver's cost, reproducing the texture-mixing phenomena first demonstrated by Rabin et al. [6]. As predicted, increasing *ε* in the entropic barycenter progressively blurs the result — the entropic bias made visible.

## 6 Limitations

The theory, for all its elegance, has sharp edges that practitioners must respect.

1. **The entropic bias is real.** Any use of the raw regularized cost *OT_ε* as a loss — rather than the debiased divergence *S_ε* — introduces shrinkage artifacts and a non-zero self-distance. Debiasing costs two extra *OT_ε(μ, μ)* evaluations, tripling the budget [3].
2. **Numerical stability at small *ε*.** The Gibbs kernel *K = exp(−C/ε)* underflows in floating point for small *ε*; the log-domain stabilization above is mandatory, and even then, very small *ε* demands many iterations. There is no free interpolation all the way to *ε = 0*.
3. **Slicing loses geometry.** *SW_p* metrizes weak convergence but its geodesics and barycenters are coarser than Wasserstein's; adversarial or high-frequency structure aligned orthogonally to typical slices can be invisible. Max-sliced variants recover some power at the cost of a minimax optimization.
4. **Curse of dimensionality persists for *W_p* itself.** Entropic and minibatch estimators achieve parametric rates only at *fixed* regularization; letting *ε → 0* or *m → ∞* to recover unregularized OT reintroduces the *O(n^(−1/d))* curse [4][8][9].
5. **Lipschitz enforcement in WGANs.** The Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual requires a 1-Lipschitz critic, but weight clipping cripples capacity and gradient penalties only enforce the constraint softly along interpolation lines; the resulting "Wasserstein distance" is an approximation whose approximation quality is rarely quantified [2].
6. **JKO steps are OT problems.** The variational elegance of minimizing movements does not remove the computational burden — each proximal step is itself a transport problem, so practical JKO relies on the same entropic/sliced/neural solvers, compounding approximation errors [8].

## 7 Conclusion

Optimal transport in machine learning is best understood as a single story told in four dialects. *Kantorovich duality* provides the language: couplings, potentials, and the *c*-transform, with Brenier's theorem as the bridge from plans back to maps. *Sinkhorn entropization* provides the engine: a strictly convex smoothing that converts a cubic linear program into a parallel matrix-scaling iteration with near-linear guarantees [1][7], and whose debiased form interpolates all the way from OT to MMD [3]. *Sliced Wasserstein distances* provide the escape hatch from dimensionality: one-dimensional quantile couplings, averaged over the sphere, at *O(n log n)* cost and parametric statistical rates [6]. *Wasserstein gradient flows* provide the dynamics: PDEs as steepest descent on the manifold of measures, discretized by the JKO proximal scheme that underlies modern diffusion models [8]. The practitioner's rule of thumb emerging from our benchmarks is simple: reach for *sliced* distances when speed dominates, *debiased Sinkhorn divergences* when geometric fidelity matters, *neural duals* when measures are only accessible through samples [4], and the *JKO lens* whenever the model itself is a flow of probability. Open frontiers include dimension-robust *neural* OT with certificates, sharp non-asymptotic theory for minibatch estimators [9], and a principled account of the approximation gaps in WGAN-style dual optimization [2].
