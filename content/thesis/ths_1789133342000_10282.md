# HotStuff and the Linear-Communication Revolution in Byzantine Fault-Tolerant State Machine Replication

## Abstract

Byzantine fault-tolerant state machine replication (BFT SMR) underpins permissioned blockchains, yet classical protocols such as PBFT pay quadratic communication per view and forfeit responsiveness by pacing every phase on worst-case network delays. This thesis presents HotStuff, the first partially synchronous BFT replication protocol to combine *linear* communication complexity with *optimistic responsiveness*. We dissect its core mechanisms — threshold-signature quorum certificates (QCs), a uniform three-phase voting pipeline (prepare, pre-commit, commit) terminating in a decide phase, the `safeNode` locking predicate, and the Pacemaker view-synchronization module — and prove why each view costs only *O(n)* authenticators after the global stabilization time. We compare HotStuff against PBFT, Tendermint, and SBFT, quantify how signature aggregation sidesteps the Dolev–Reischuk lower bound, and survey its industrial lineage through LibraBFT, DiemBFT/Jolteon, and AptosBFT. We close with the protocol's residual superlinear worst case, trusted-setup costs, and the research frontier of two-chain and asynchronous-fallback variants.

## 1. Introduction

State machine replication (SMR) under Byzantine faults is the bedrock abstraction of every permissioned blockchain: *n* replicas, at most *f* of them arbitrarily malicious, must agree on a single ever-growing sequence of commands and execute them deterministically [2]. For two decades after Castro and Liskov's Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT), the field lived with an apparent law of nature — **quadratic communication**. PBFT's prepare and commit phases are all-to-all broadcasts; each view burns *Θ(n²)* authenticators, and its view-change subprotocol, which must ferry *O(n)* prepared certificates inside *O(n)* view-change messages, costs *Θ(n³)* in the worst case [2]. As deployments grew from LAN clusters of four replicas toward geo-replicated committees of hundreds, this quadratic tax became the binding constraint on scalability [5].

Two desiderata crystallized, and for years they seemed jointly unattainable in partial synchrony. The first is **linearity**: after the global stabilization time (GST), a correct leader — including a *replacement* leader — should need only *O(n)* communication to commit a block. The second is **optimistic responsiveness**: after GST, a correct leader should advance at the speed of the *actual* network delay *δ*, waiting only for the first *n − f* responses rather than for a pessimistic worst-case bound *Δ* [1]. HotStuff, introduced by Yin, Malkhi, Reiter, Gueta, and Abraham at PODC 2019, is the first partially synchronous BFT replication protocol to exhibit both properties simultaneously [1]. Its contribution is as much conceptual as algorithmic: it reframes BFT consensus as the repeated formation of **quorum certificates** — constant-size threshold signatures attesting that *n − f* replicas voted in a view — and shows that PBFT, Tendermint, and Casper are all instances of one generic QC-driven framework. By making every phase share an identical communication pattern (leader broadcast, replica votes, QC aggregation), HotStuff becomes trivially **pipelineable**: the chained variant overlaps the phases of successive blocks the way a CPU pipeline overlaps instruction stages, committing one block per view at steady state.

This thesis reconstructs the linear-communication revolution from first principles. Section 2 reviews the classical landscape and the Dolev–Reischuk lower bound that linearity had to circumvent. Section 3 describes our analytical methodology — authenticator complexity, the generic-QC framework, and mechanized verification. Section 4 dissects the protocol's four load-bearing ideas: threshold-aggregated QCs, the three-phase pipeline and its chained commit rules, the Pacemaker, and responsiveness. Section 5 states the safety and liveness theorems with proof sketches and surveys empirical results, including the Jolteon two-chain optimization. Section 6 confronts the limitations: the residual superlinear worst case, trusted setup, and leader bottlenecks. Section 7 concludes with the protocol's industrial lineage and open frontiers.

## 2. Background

**The model.** We work in the *partially synchronous* model of Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer: message delays are unbounded until an unknown *global stabilization time* GST, after which they are bounded by an unknown *Δ*. With *n = 3f + 1* replicas, at most *f* Byzantine, safety must hold always and liveness must hold after GST. A *quorum* of *n − f = 2f + 1* replicas intersects any other quorum in at least *f + 1* replicas — hence at least one correct replica — which is the combinatorial engine of every quorum-based BFT protocol.

**PBFT and the quadratic barrier.** In PBFT's normal case, the primary broadcasts a pre-prepare; every replica then broadcasts prepare messages to *all* replicas, and later commit messages to *all* replicas [2]. Each of these all-to-all rounds costs *Θ(n²)* messages and authenticators. View change is worse: each replica's view-change message carries its prepared certificates, and the new primary's new-view message bundles *n − f* of them, for *Θ(n³)* worst-case communication. For *n = 4* this is invisible; for *n = 200* it is fatal — precisely the regime SBFT's geo-replicated deployment (209 replicas, *f = 64*) targeted [5].

**The lower bound that had to be dodged.** Dolev and Reischuk proved in 1985 that deterministic Byzantine agreement requires *Ω(nt)* messages — and, in the authenticated model, *Ω(nt)* signatures — in the worst case [3]. HotStuff does not refute this: its linearity is *optimistic* (per view, with a correct leader) while its worst case over *f* consecutive faulty leaders remains superlinear [6]. The escape hatch is a change of *units*. Threshold signatures (e.g., BLS) let *n − f* distinct signature shares be combined into a *single* constant-size signature verifiable under one public key [5]. HotStuff therefore measures **authenticator complexity** — counting each (threshold) signature as one — so that a leader collecting *n − f* votes and rebroadcasting one QC spends *O(n)* authenticators per phase rather than *O(n²)* [1].

**Tendermint and SBFT.** Tendermint structures consensus as repeated heights with gossip-disseminated prevote/precommit rounds at *O(n²)* per round, rotating leaders every round [4]. SBFT was the first system to demonstrate linear BFT at scale: it designates *collectors* that aggregate threshold signatures, adds an optimistic fast path, and reduces client communication — achieving roughly 2× the throughput and 1.5× better latency than an optimized PBFT at 200+ replicas [5]. HotStuff's advance over SBFT is structural: rather than bolting collectors onto PBFT's phase structure, it redesigns the phase structure itself around QCs, gaining responsiveness and pipelinability as corollaries.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis follows the methodology of the HotStuff paper itself, extended with a decade of follow-up literature [1][6][8][9].

**Authenticator complexity as the cost model.** We count the total number of authenticators (partial signatures, threshold signatures, MACs) transmitted, with one threshold signature counting as one unit regardless of how many shares it aggregates [1]. A protocol is *linear* if a correct leader's view costs *O(n)* authenticators. This is the honest accounting for blockchain deployments, where signature verification — not raw byte movement — dominates CPU cost.

**The generic-QC framework.** The paper's central analytical device is to express PBFT, Tendermint, Casper, and HotStuff in one vocabulary: views, leaders, votes, QCs, and a `safeNode` predicate. This lets us isolate exactly which ingredient buys linearity (uniform phases + threshold QCs) and which buys responsiveness (no phase waits on *Δ*).

**Mechanized verification.** HotStuff's safety-critical core — the lock update and commit rules — has been modeled in TLA+ and exhaustively checked with TLC (over 7×10⁹ states generated, no errors found) in an industrial setting [10], giving high confidence that the chained commit rules below are sound.

**Empirical grounding.** We report the paper's own geo-replicated evaluation (100+ replicas, throughput/latency comparable to BFT-SMaRt with linear failover instead of quadratic) [1] and the Jolteon/Ditto experimental comparison (commit latency improved by 200–300 ms across system sizes) [6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Quorum Certificates and Threshold Aggregation

A **quorum certificate** for block *b* in view *v*, written *QC(b, v)*, is a threshold signature over *(b, v)* combining partial signatures from *n − f* distinct replicas. The QC is constant-size (one BLS signature plus a signer bitmap), so a leader can *prove* quorum support to all replicas in a single broadcast:

```rust
/// A quorum certificate: constant-size proof that n-f replicas
/// voted for `block_hash` in `view`. One threshold signature
/// counts as ONE authenticator, however many shares it aggregates.
pub struct QuorumCertificate {
    pub view: u64,
    pub block_hash: [u8; 32],
    pub threshold_sig: BlsSignature,
    pub signer_bitmap: Vec<u64>,
}

impl QuorumCertificate {
    /// Leader-side aggregation: O(n) partial signatures in,
    /// one constant-size certificate out.
    pub fn aggregate(view: u64, block_hash: [u8; 32],
                     shares: &[PartialSig]) -> Option<Self> {
        if shares.len() < QUORUM { return None; } // QUORUM = n - f
        let threshold_sig = BlsSignature::combine(shares)?;
        Some(QuorumCertificate { view, block_hash, threshold_sig,
                                 signer_bitmap: bitmap(shares) })
    }
}
```

Every HotStuff phase has the *same* shape: the leader broadcasts a message carrying a QC; each replica validates it and returns a single vote (one partial signature) to the leader; the leader aggregates *n − f* votes into the next QC. Per phase the leader sends *n* messages and receives *n − f*, all carrying constant-size authenticators: *O(n)* per phase, *O(1)* phases per view, hence *O(n)* per view [1]. Contrast PBFT, where the leader's proposal triggers *n* replicas each broadcasting to *n* replicas — *Θ(n²)* — twice per view [2].

### 4.2 The Three-Phase Pipeline and the Chained Commit Rules

Basic HotStuff proceeds in five stages per view. The new leader first collects `NEW-VIEW` messages, each carrying the sender's highest known prepare-QC, and selects the freshest one (*highQC*). It then drives three *voting* phases plus a finalization phase:

| Phase | Leader action | Replica action | Artifact produced |
|---|---|---|---|
| `NEW-VIEW` | collects *n − f* `NEW-VIEW` msgs | sends highest prepare-QC | *highQC* selected |
| `PREPARE` | broadcasts proposal extending *highQC* | votes iff `safeNode` holds | *prepareQC* |
| `PRE-COMMIT` | broadcasts *prepareQC* | records it, votes | *precommitQC* |
| `COMMIT` | broadcasts *precommitQC* | sets *lockedQC*, votes | *commitQC* |
| `DECIDE` | broadcasts *commitQC* | executes command, replies | finality |

The replica's voting rule is the protocol's safety core:

```python
def safe_node(block, justify_qc, locked_qc) -> bool:
    """A replica votes for `block` iff it extends the locked block,
    or its justifying QC is strictly newer than the lock.
    (HotStuff, Alg. 2: the SafeNode predicate.)"""
    extends_locked = (block.parent_hash == locked_qc.block_hash)
    qc_outranks_lock = (justify_qc.view > locked_qc.view)
    return extends_locked or qc_outranks_lock
```

Why three voting phases? Each phase elevates the *epistemic strength* of the QC. After `PRE-COMMIT`, a prepare-QC exists: *n − f* replicas saw the block. After `COMMIT`, a precommit-QC exists and every voter *locks* on it — a correct replica will never vote against that block's branch unless shown a newer QC. After `DECIDE`, the commit-QC proves that *n − f* replicas locked on the block, so no conflicting block can ever gather a quorum: any future leader's *highQC* must outrank or extend it, by quorum intersection. **Chained HotStuff** pipelines the phases. Each block embeds its parent's QC, so one block per view simultaneously serves as the `PREPARE` of view *v*, the `PRE-COMMIT` evidence of view *v − 1*, and the `COMMIT` evidence of view *v − 2*. Two local rules replace the phase handlers:

- **Two-chain rule (lock):** on observing blocks *b₁ ← b₂* with consecutive views, set `lockedQC ← max(lockedQC, QC(b₁))`.
- **Three-chain rule (commit):** on observing *b₁ ← b₂ ← b₃* with consecutive views, execute *b₁* and its ancestors.

At steady state the pipeline commits one block per view with seven message delays of latency (three two-delay voting rounds plus the one-delay decide); the two-chain Jolteon variant cuts this to five [6].

### 4.3 The Pacemaker: View Synchronization as a Separate Concern

HotStuff factors *liveness* out of consensus into a module called the **Pacemaker**, responsible solely for moving correct replicas into the same view with a correct leader for long enough to decide [1]. The Pacemaker's contract is minimal. Each replica runs a view timer; on expiry it sends `NEW-VIEW` (with its highest prepare-QC) to the next leader and advances its view. Leaders are determined by a rotation function (e.g., round-robin). After GST, if timers exceed *Δ* and grow on consecutive timeouts, correct replicas eventually overlap in a view led by a correct replica long enough for the three-phase pipeline to complete — the standard partial-synchrony liveness argument, now isolated from the safety proof.

```tla+
---- MODULE Pacemaker ----
EXTENDS Naturals
CONSTANTS Replicas, LeaderOf(_)  \* LeaderOf : Nat -> Replicas
VARIABLES view, timerExpired

Timeout(r) ==
    /\ timerExpired[r]
    /\ view' = [view EXCEPT ![r] = view[r] + 1]
    /\ SendNewView(r, LeaderOf(view[r] + 1), HighestPrepareQC(r))
    /\ UNCHANGED <<timerExpired>>

\* Safety of consensus never depends on this module:
\* votes are validated by safeNode, not by clocks.
```

This modularity explains both HotStuff's strength and its residual weakness. Because view synchronization is *quadratic* in the worst case (every replica times out and contacts the next leader), the protocol's celebrated linearity is a *happy-path* property: over *f* consecutive faulty leaders the total cost is *Θ(n²)* [6]. Subsequent work attacked exactly this seam — Fever gives an *optimally responsive* view synchronizer [8], and Ditto replaces view synchronization wholesale with an asynchronous fallback that preserves linear happy-path cost while guaranteeing progress under asynchrony and DDoS [6].

### 4.4 Responsiveness: Consensus at the Speed of the Network

**Optimistic responsiveness** means: after GST, a correct leader waits only for the first *n − f* votes in each phase — i.e., it advances at the *actual* network delay *δ*, never at the pessimistic bound *Δ* [1]. Three design choices make this possible. First, phases have no timeouts in the voting path; only the Pacemaker's view timer can interrupt a view. Second, QCs make progress *self-certifying*: a leader that assembled *n − f* votes holds cryptographic proof of support and needs no further confirmation round. Third, pipelining means the leader never idles waiting for a global round boundary — each view's proposal simultaneously advances three in-flight blocks.

The contrast with predecessors is stark. PBFT paces its view-change timer on *Δ* and, worse, requires replicas to wait out timeouts before suspecting a primary, so a faulty leader can stall the protocol for multiples of *Δ* per view [2]. Tendermint's round structure similarly advances on timeouts [4]. HotStuff's evaluation showed that responsiveness is not merely theoretical: with leaders rotating every block, the protocol sustained throughput and latency comparable to BFT-SMaRt — a highly optimized, non-rotating-leader system — while reducing failover communication from quadratic to linear [1].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Deployment at scale.** The HotStuff paper reports a geo-replicated deployment of *over 100 replicas* achieving throughput and latency comparable to BFT-SMaRt, the production-grade PBFT descendant, while enjoying a *linear* communication footprint during proposer failover versus BFT-SMaRt's quadratic one [1]. **The two-chain dividend.** Jolteon, a hybrid of HotStuff and classical PBFT, keeps HotStuff's structure and happy-path linearity but replaces the three-chain commit rule with a two-chain rule at the cost of a quadratic view change (acceptable because the Pacemaker is quadratic anyway) [6]. Measured block-commit latency drops from 7 rounds to 5 — a ~30% reduction, 200–300 ms across system sizes — and Ditto, which adds an asynchronous fallback to Jolteon, matches Jolteon's fault-free performance while outperforming both Jolteon under attack and VABA (a state-of-the-art asynchronous protocol) in the happy path [6].

**Safety.** The safety argument rests on quorum intersection and lock monotonicity:

> **Theorem (Safety) [1].** If two correct replicas execute commands at the same log position, the commands are identical. Equivalently, no two conflicting blocks are ever committed.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose block *b* is committed: some correct replica observed a three-chain ending in a commit-QC, so *n − f* replicas voted in the `COMMIT` phase and set `lockedQC` to a QC at least as fresh as *QC(b)*. Consider any later view *v′* with a QC for a block *b′* conflicting with *b*. The *n − f* voters of that QC intersect the *n − f* locked replicas in a correct replica *r*, which voted for *b′* only if `safeNode` held — i.e., *b′* extends *r*'s locked block or carries a newer QC. The former contradicts conflict; the latter implies a QC newer than the commit-QC on a conflicting branch, which by induction reduces to the same contradiction at the view where the branch diverged. ∎

**Liveness.** After GST, the Pacemaker's growing timeouts guarantee an interval in which all correct replicas share a view with a correct leader for at least the seven message delays the pipeline needs; the leader's proposal then gathers *n − f* votes per phase by responsiveness, forms all three QCs, and every correct replica commits [1].

**Complexity ledger.** The table below summarizes per-decision costs after GST (authenticator complexity; *δ* = actual delay):

| Protocol | Happy-path / view | View change (faulty leader) | Responsive? | Commit latency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [2] | *Θ(n²)* | *Θ(n³)* | no | 3 delays |
| Tendermint [4] | *Θ(n²)* | *Θ(n²)* | no | *O(n)* gossip rounds |
| SBFT [5] | *Θ(n)* | *Θ(n²)* | no | 2–3 delays (fast path) |
| **HotStuff [1]** | ***Θ(n)*** | *Θ(n)* msgs, *Θ(n²)* worst case [6] | **yes** | 7 delays |
| Jolteon [6] | *Θ(n)* | *Θ(n²)* | yes | 5 delays |

## 6. Limitations

**Linearity is optimistic, not worst-case.** The Dolev–Reischuk bound is not cheated, only sidestepped in the good case: *f* consecutive Byzantine leaders force *f* view changes at *Θ(n)* each, for *Θ(n²)* total, and Civit et al. argue the worst-case communication of partially synchronous protocols in this family is in fact cubic, motivating their quadratic-worst-case SQuad protocol [9]. Any deployment threat model that includes sustained leader-targeted DDoS must budget for this [6].

**Trusted setup for threshold signatures.** BLS threshold schemes need a distributed key generation (or trusted dealer) ceremony; a compromised setup breaks QC unforgeability silently.

**Leader bottleneck and load imbalance.** Star communication concentrates *O(n)* signature verifications on the leader every phase — at *n = 1000* the leader verifies ~2000 signatures per view while followers verify a handful. SBFT's collector rotation and Narwhal-style mempool/data-availability separation (later adopted around HotStuff-family cores) address this, but vanilla HotStuff is leader-bound [5].

**Latency price of the third phase.** Seven message delays per commit is the cost of the lock-then-prove structure; Jolteon's two-chain rule shows one phase was nearly redundant, cutting latency 30% [6]. Fully asynchronous networks are out of scope entirely — Ditto's fallback is the principled fix [6].

## 7. Conclusion

HotStuff's linear-communication revolution was a triumph of *framing*: by reducing every phase to "broadcast a QC, collect votes, form the next QC," Yin et al. made linearity a structural property rather than an optimization, and responsiveness and pipelinability fell out as corollaries [1]. Its industrial lineage is direct: Facebook's LibraBFT instantiated HotStuff for the Libra blockchain [7]; DiemBFT evolved it through the Jolteon two-chain optimization toward production [6]; AptosBFT carries the design forward today. The research frontier it opened remains active: HotStuff-1 collapses speculation to a single phase [11], Fever optimizes the Pacemaker seam [8], Ditto hardens liveness against asynchrony [6], and SQuad probes whether the quadratic worst case is fundamental [9]. Four decades after Dolev and Reischuk drew the quadratic line, HotStuff taught the field to walk it — linearly, and at network speed.
