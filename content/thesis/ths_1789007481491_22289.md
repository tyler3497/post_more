# From Small-Step Semantics to Abstract Machines: The CESK Machine, Defunctionalization, and Deriving Correct Interpreters

## Abstract

This article presents a unified account of how small-step operational semantics give rise to abstract machines, centered on the CESK (Control, Environment, Store, Kontinuation) machine of Felleisen and Friedman [1], John Reynolds's defunctionalization transformation [2], and the functional correspondence of Ager, Danvy, and Midtgaard [3]. We trace the intellectual lineage from Reynolds's 1972 series of definitional interpreters, through the derivation of the CEK machine from an extended interpreter, to the modern program of *rationally deconstructing* abstract machines into interderivable evaluators. We give the CESK transition rules precisely, show how defunctionalizing a continuation-passing evaluator yields a machine whose continuations are first-order frames, and explain refocusing [4] as the bridge between reduction semantics with evaluation contexts and small-step machines. We conclude with a correctness argument sketch, the limits of the correspondence (nondeterminism, effects beyond state, abstract interpretation), and open problems in mechanized derivation.

## 1. Introduction

Programming language semantics has long been divided between *denotational* accounts that map programs to mathematical functions and *operational* accounts that describe execution on idealized machines. Between these poles lies a rich ecosystem of intermediate artifacts: definitional interpreters, continuation-passing evaluators, reduction semantics with evaluation contexts, and abstract machines. For decades, each artifact was studied on its own terms, with correctness proofs constructed by hand between each pair. The central thesis of this article is that these artifacts are not independent inventions but *interderivable views of the same computational content*, connected by a small set of program transformations that are, in Reynolds's original phrase, "informal but constructive" [2].

The story begins in 1972 with Reynolds's "Definitional Interpreters for Higher-Order Programming Languages," a paper that showed how a series of interpreters could be transformed, one into another, ending with a compiler [2]. Buried in that paper was an observation that would prove enormously fertile: continuations — higher-order functions representing "the rest of the computation" — can be replaced by first-order data structures together with a single *apply* function that dispatches on them. This is **defunctionalization**, and it is the hinge on which the entire modern correspondence turns.

Fifteen years later, Felleisen and Friedman used Reynolds's machinery in reverse. Their 1986 paper "Control Operators, the SECD Machine, and the λ-Calculus" introduced the CEK machine — Control, Environment, Kontinuation — derived from Reynolds's extended Interpreter IV [1]. They noted explicitly that "the CEK machine is derived from Reynolds' extended interpreter IV," an admission that abstract machines can be *extracted* from interpreters rather than invented from whole cloth. The CESK machine extends CEK with a store, trading environment-to-value mappings for environment-to-address mappings, which makes mutable state and control operators tractable [5].

The modern synthesis arrived in 2003, when Ager, Danvy, and Midtgaard published "A Functional Correspondence between Evaluators and Abstract Machines" at PPDP [3]. They showed that starting from an ordinary compositional evaluator for the λ-calculus, the pipeline of *closure conversion*, *CPS transformation*, and *defunctionalization of continuations* mechanically yields abstract machines: Krivine's machine for call-by-name, the CEK machine for call-by-value, and — by inverting the pipeline through refunctionalization — evaluators recovered from machines, including Landin's SECD machine. Correctness of the derived machine becomes a corollary of the correctness of the source evaluator and of the transformations themselves.

This article reconstructs that program in detail. Section 2 fixes terminology; Section 3 describes the transformation pipeline as a methodology; Section 4 dives into the CESK machine, defunctionalization, refocusing, and the correspondence; Section 5 sketches the empirical and proof-theoretic evidence; Section 6 states the limits; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Operational semantics in miniature

An operational semantics describes how programs execute on an abstract machine. A **small-step semantics** defines a transition relation between *configurations* — pairs (or tuples) of machine state — written `ς → ς'`, whose transitive closure models complete runs. A **big-step** (natural) semantics instead relates a term directly to its final value, `e ⇓ v`. The two styles coexist uneasily: small-step accounts expose intermediate states and divergence, while big-step accounts are more compositional and easier to relate to denotational models. The correspondence program reconciles them by showing how, under the right transformations, one can be fused into the other [6].

**Reduction semantics** refines small-step semantics by making the *evaluation context* explicit: a term is decomposed into a context `E` with a hole and a redex `r`, written `E[r]`; the redex is contracted to `r'`; the contractum is plugged back into the context, yielding `E[r']`. This decompose–contract–plug cycle is canonical for deterministic languages satisfying the unique-decomposition property [4]. As Danvy observed, a reduction context is exactly the *defunctionalized continuation* of a one-step reduction function [6] — a slogan that does much of the heavy lifting in what follows.

### 2.2 From SECD to CEK to CESK

Peter Landin's SECD machine (Stack, Environment, Control, Dump), presented in the 1960s, was the first widely studied abstract machine for the λ-calculus. The CEK machine simplified it: the dump became a proper continuation, and arguments were placed directly into the environment rather than pushed onto a parameter stack [5]. The state of a CEK machine is a triple `⟨c, e, k⟩` — control term, environment, continuation — and its transitions implement left-to-right call-by-value evaluation.

The **CESK machine** adds a fourth component, the store `σ`, yielding states `⟨c, e, σ, k⟩`. Environments map variables to *addresses* rather than values, and the store maps addresses to values. This indirection is not bureaucratic decoration: it is what lets the machine model mutable state, since assignment updates the store in place while the environment's bindings remain valid. Felleisen and Friedman's original calculus with control operators (the `λv-CS` calculus and its machines) exploited exactly this structure [1], and the CESK machine has since become the canonical substrate for *abstracting abstract machines* — finite abstractions of the store yield computable static analyses of higher-order programs with state.

### 2.3 Evaluators as semantic artifacts

A **compositional evaluator** for the λ-calculus is the familiar recursive interpreter:

```haskell
eval :: Term -> Env -> Value
eval (Var x)   e = lookup x e
eval (Lam x t) e = Clo x t e
eval (App t u) e = apply (eval t e) (eval u e)

apply :: Value -> Value -> Value
apply (Clo x t e) v = eval t (extend x v e)
```

Here values include *closures* `Clo x t e` pairing a λ-abstraction with its defining environment — Landin's invention, made concrete by closure conversion. Such an evaluator is compositional: the meaning of a compound term is built from the meanings of its parts. The functional correspondence shows that abstract machines are what you get when you systematically *de-compositionalize* this evaluator.

## 3. Methodology

The methodology of the correspondence is transformational. Rather than proving a machine correct against a semantics in one heroic step, we *derive* the machine from a trusted evaluator through a chain of meaning-preserving program transformations, each independently validated. The chain is:

1. **Closure conversion.** Replace higher-order function values in the evaluator with explicit closure data structures. The evaluator remains compositional and direct-style, but its values are now first-order.
2. **CPS transformation.** Convert the evaluator to continuation-passing style. Evaluation order — call-by-name or call-by-value, left-to-right or right-to-left — is fixed by the choice of CPS transform. The continuation `k : Value -> Answer` is a higher-order function representing the rest of the computation.
3. **Defunctionalization.** Since the program contains only finitely many continuation abstractions, replace each with a first-order constructor (`arg`, `app`, `halt`, …) and replace every application `k v` with a call to a dispatch function `continue k v`. The result is *first-order*: an abstract machine whose states are tuples of a term, an environment, and a continuation-as-data.
4. **Transition compression / refocusing.** Fuse administrative steps (decompose after plug, or the trampolining between `eval` and `continue`) so the machine takes one transition per "real" step, yielding the familiar compact machine.

Correctness is compositional in a precise sense: if each transformation preserves meaning, the derived machine is correct by construction. This is the sense in which the machines are *derived*, not designed [3]. The inverse direction — refunctionalization, direct-style transformation, closure unconversion — recovers evaluators from machines, closing the loop.

Complementary to this is **refocusing** [4]: the observation that the naive implementation of reduction semantics, which plugs a contractum into a context and then re-decomposes the resulting term, does redundant work linear in the term size at every step. Refocusing fuses `plug` and `decompose` into a single function that navigates directly from the contractum to the next redex *within* the existing context, turning a quadratic evaluator into a linear one. The refocused evaluator *is* a small-step abstract machine whose "continuation" is the evaluation context.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Small-Step Semantics: Decompose, Contract, Plug

Consider the call-by-value λ-calculus with evaluation contexts

```
E ::= [] | E e | v E
```

where `v` ranges over values. Every non-value term `t` decomposes uniquely into `E[r]` for a redex `r` — here, a β-redex `(λx.t) v` — and one-step reduction is `E[(λx.t) v] → E[t[v/x]]`. A naive interpreter implements this literally:

```haskell
step :: Term -> Maybe Term
step t = do (e, r) <- decompose t
            r'      <- contract r
            return (plug e r')

eval t = case step t of
           Nothing -> t
           Just t' -> eval t'
```

Each call to `plug` rebuilds the term from the root and each call to `decompose` walks it again — `O(n)` overhead per step, hence `O(n²)` overall in the worst case [4]. Refocusing eliminates this: after contraction, the next redex must lie either within the contractum or "above" it in the context. Define

```haskell
refocus :: Context -> Term -> (Context, Redex)
refocus e (App t u) = refocus (Arg u e) t
refocus e v         = ...
```

so that `refocus [] t` finds the first redex, and after contracting `r` to `r'` in context `e`, `refocus e r'` finds the *next* redex directly. The equation `decompose (plug e t) = refocus e t` is the heart of the matter [4]: the refocused evaluator never rebuilds the term, and its state — a context plus a focused term — is already an abstract machine state.

### 4.2 The CESK Machine: State, Transitions, Totality

The CESK machine makes the environment and the store explicit. Its transitions for the call-by-value λ-calculus are:

| Rule | From state | To state |
|------|-----------|----------|
| var | `⟨x, ρ, σ, κ⟩` | `⟨v, ρ, σ, κ⟩` where `σ(ρ(x)) = v` |
| lam | `⟨λx.e, ρ, σ, κ⟩` | `⟨clo(λx.e, ρ), ρ, σ, κ⟩` |
| app | `⟨e₀ e₁, ρ, σ, κ⟩` | `⟨e₀, ρ, σ, arg(e₁, ρ, κ)⟩` |
| arg | `⟨v, ρ, σ, arg(e, ρ', κ)⟩` | `⟨e, ρ', σ, fun(v, κ)⟩` |
| fun | `⟨v, ρ, σ, fun(clo(λx.e, ρ'), κ)⟩` | `⟨e, ρ'[x↦a], σ[a↦v], κ⟩`, `a` fresh |

States are quadruples `⟨c, ρ, σ, κ⟩` of control, environment, store, and continuation; continuations are built from frames `arg(e, ρ, κ)`, `fun(v, κ)`, and the empty continuation `halt`. Evaluation of `(λx.e) v` focuses on the operator, pushes an `arg` frame, evaluates the operand, swaps to a `fun` frame, and enters the closure body with the environment extended at a *fresh store address* [1][5]. Several properties deserve emphasis. First, the machine is **total**: every state matches exactly one transition rule (or is a final answer at `halt`) — a property inherited from the unique-decomposition discipline. Third, the machine is extensible: mutable references need only new transitions over the same store, and control operators like `call/cc` need only new continuation frames that capture and restore `κ`. > **Theorem:** For closed terms, the CESK machine started in `⟨e, ∅, ∅, halt⟩` reaches `⟨v, ρ, σ, halt⟩` iff `e` evaluates to `v` under the standard call-by-value reduction semantics, and the number of machine transitions is linear in the number of reduction steps plus the size of the initial term. The proof proceeds by a lockstep simulation: each `decompose; contract; plug` triple corresponds to a bounded number of machine transitions, and refocusing guarantees the context is never rebuilt [4][3]. ### 4.3 Defunctionalization: Reynolds' Bridge

Reynolds's original setting was the interpretation of higher-order languages by lower-order means. His observation: in any given program, only finitely many function abstractions are ever created, so each can be assigned a unique tag, and each application site replaced by a call to a global `apply` that dispatches on the tag [2]. Applied to the *continuations* of a CPS evaluator, defunctionalization turns higher-order control flow into first-order data:

```haskell
eval :: Term -> Env -> (Value -> Answer) -> Answer
eval (App t u) e k = eval t e (\v0 -> eval u e (\v1 -> apply v0 v1 k))

data Kont = Halt | Arg Term Env Kont | Fun Value Kont

continue :: Kont -> Value -> Answer
continue Halt v        = v
continue (Arg u e k) v = eval u e (Fun v k)
continue (Fun v0 k) v1 = apply v0 v1 k

eval :: Term -> Env -> Kont -> Answer
eval (App t u) e k = eval t e (Arg u e k)
```

The `continue` function is the machine's *trampoline*: `eval` descends into terms while pushing frames, `continue` ascends through frames while dispatching on them. Together they are exactly the CEK machine; adding the store's indirection through `ρ` yields the CESK machine [5][3]. The inverse transformation, **refunctionalization**, recovers the CPS evaluator from the machine [3].

### 4.4 The Functional Correspondence and Refocusing

The full pipeline produces a small zoo of machines from a single evaluator by varying the *evaluation strategy* (call-by-name vs. call-by-value, fixed by the CPS transform) and *representation choices* (environments vs. substitution, stores vs. direct bindings). Ager, Danvy, and Midtgaard demonstrated [3]:

- **Call-by-name** CPS + defunctionalization → **Krivine's machine**.
- **Call-by-value** CPS + defunctionalization → the **CEK machine**.
- **Refunctionalization** of the CEK machine → a call-by-value evaluator — a derivation that was new.
- The same pipeline reveals the "denotational content" of **Hannan and Miller's CLS machine** and **Landin's SECD machine**, and a monadic generalization derives machines for languages with computational effects [7], including a CESK variant with state. It was also extended to **call-by-need**: from an evaluator threading a heap of updatable thunks, the pipeline produces a lazy abstract machine in which the heap-update continuation fragment appears naturally as an *update marker* [8].

Refocusing [4] completes the picture on the reduction-semantics side: a refocused reduction semantics is already an abstract machine, with evaluation contexts as defunctionalized continuations. Danvy later systematized these interderivations as a comprehensive map of the semantic artifact space [6].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

The correspondence program makes claims that are, unusually for programming-language theory, *executable*. Each derivation is a concrete program transformation, and the artifacts at each stage can be run and tested against each other. The principal results are:

1. **Machine derivations validated by construction.** The Krivine, CEK, CLS, and SECD derivations in [3] are presented as complete, runnable evaluators and machines; their agreement is tested by evaluation, and the proofs are simulation arguments between adjacent stages rather than monolithic leaps.
2. **Refocusing complexity bound.** Danvy and Nielsen prove that refocusing reduces the per-step overhead of a reduction-semantics interpreter from linear to constant, yielding overall linear time in the number of reduction steps [4]. This is not an asymptotic nicety: it is the difference between a semantics that can be *executed* and one that can only be contemplated.
3. **Effects via monads.** The 2005 extension derives abstract machines from *monadic* evaluators by inlining the monad and re-running the pipeline, producing a CESK machine with error and state from the lifted state monad [7]. The derived machines inherit the equational theory of their monads, giving a principled account of effectful language features.
4. **Mechanization.** Subsequent work has formalized these derivations in proof assistants, verifying the simulation relations between reduction semantics and machines for call-by-value λ-calculi with de Bruijn indices and explicit substitutions.

> **Theorem (Adequacy of the pipeline):** Let `eval₀` be a compositional evaluator and `M` the abstract machine obtained by closure conversion, CPS transformation, defunctionalization, and transition compression. Then `eval₀ e = v` iff `M` started on `e` halts with `v`, provided each transformation is meaning-preserving — which holds for the standard formulations of these transformations on the λ-calculus [3][6].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

The correspondence, powerful as it is, has boundaries worth stating plainly.

**Nondeterminism and concurrency.** The pipeline assumes a deterministic source evaluator: unique decomposition, a single evaluation order, a single answer. Languages with nondeterministic choice, true concurrency, or weak-memory effects do not admit a canonical CPS transform, and defunctionalizing a nondeterministic continuation is ill-defined. Extending the correspondence to concurrent abstract machines remains largely open.

**Effects beyond state.** The monadic extension [7] handles effects expressible as monads — state, exceptions, I/O in the lifted sense — but delimited control, algebraic effect handlers with multi-shot continuations, and linearity strain the first-order frame representation. Refunctionalizing a machine whose continuations can be *copied* (as in `call/cc`) or *resumed multiple times* (as in effect handlers) requires care that the simple datatype-plus-dispatch story does not provide.

**Abstract interpretation.** The CESK machine's real modern glory is as a substrate for static analysis: finitize the store, and the machine becomes a computable abstract interpreter. But the functional correspondence derives *concrete* machines; lifting the derivation to produce *abstract* machines with soundness proofs — the "abstracting abstract machines" program — requires additional machinery (widening, allocation policies, garbage collection of abstract addresses) that does not fall out of CPS and defunctionalization alone.

**Automation and scale.** The derivations in the literature are carried out by hand, albeit systematically. A fully automatic "evaluator-to-machine compiler" that a language designer could run on a new language definition — with machine-checked proofs — exists only in fragments. Scaling the pipeline to realistic languages (modules, exceptions, objects, gradual typing) without the derivation collapsing under its own case analysis is an open engineering challenge.

**Pedagogy vs. practice.** Finally, there is a sociological limitation: working language implementers rarely derive their machines this way. The correspondence is a tool for *understanding* and *certifying* machines, not (yet) for building production compilers. Closing that gap — making derivation the path of least resistance — is the program's unfinished business.

---

## 7. Conclusion

From Reynolds's 1972 interpreters [2] through Felleisen and Friedman's CEK and CESK machines [1][5] to the functional correspondence [3] and refocusing [4], a single idea recurs: the distance between a high-level evaluator and a low-level abstract machine is not a chasm to be leapt by ingenuity but a staircase to be descended by transformation. Closure conversion makes values explicit; CPS transformation makes control flow explicit; defunctionalization makes continuations first-order; refocusing makes reduction efficient. Each step is small, each is meaning-preserving, and their composition turns the art of abstract-machine design into something approaching a science.

The practical moral is correspondingly sharp. When you design a new language feature, write the evaluator first — the compositional, obviously-correct one — and *derive* the machine. When you inherit a machine, refunctionalize it to recover the evaluator it secretly is. The CESK machine is not an alternative to the λ-calculus's semantics; it is the λ-calculus's semantics with its administrative overhead made manifest and then compiled away. That, fifty years after Reynolds, is still the deepest lesson definitional interpreters have to teach.
