## Abstract

The Ginzburg–Landau (GL) theory remains, more than seven decades after its formulation, the indispensable mesoscopic description of superconductivity. It replaces the microscopic many-body problem with a single complex order parameter field whose amplitude measures the local density of the superconducting condensate and whose phase encodes superfluid rigidity and gauge invariance. From one free-energy functional flow the two Euler–Lagrange equations, the two fundamental length scales of the superconducting state, the classification of superconductors into Type I and Type II, the prediction of quantized magnetic vortices arranged in the Abrikosov lattice, and closed-form expressions for the critical magnetic fields. This thesis develops the theory from its Landau roots through the Gor'kov microscopic derivation from BCS theory, surveys the time-dependent Ginzburg–Landau extension and its experimental tests, and states precisely where the theory breaks down. [1][2][3]

---


## 1 Intro

Superconductivity — the abrupt disappearance of electrical resistance below a critical temperature *T_c*, accompanied by the expulsion of magnetic flux known as the Meissner–Ochsenfeld effect — demanded a theoretical language that could describe a macroscopic quantum phase without solving the full many-body problem. The London brothers supplied the first such language in 1935: a rigid macroscopic wavefunction whose phase gradients carry the supercurrent, yielding the penetration equation that bears their name [2]. Yet the London theory is linear and phenomenological in a narrow sense: it cannot describe *why* the ordered state appears, what sets its spatial texture near boundaries and defects, or how superconductivity dies in a magnetic field.

The conceptual breakthrough came from Lev Landau's general theory of second-order phase transitions, which expands the free energy in powers of an order parameter dictated by symmetry [1]. In 1950, Vitaly Ginzburg and Landau made the audacious proposal that the superconducting state is characterized by a *complex* order parameter *ψ(r)* — effectively a local wavefunction for the condensate — and wrote down the simplest gauge-invariant free-energy functional consistent with that symmetry [1]. The theory was initially met with reserve, precisely because it was phenomenological; its parameters could not be computed from first principles. That reservation evaporated in 1959, when Lev Gor'kov derived the GL equations as the long-wavelength, near-*T_c* limit of the microscopic BCS theory, identifying the order parameter with the superconducting gap and proving that the effective charge is twice the electron charge [4][8]. Two years earlier, Alexei Abrikosov had used the GL equations to predict that magnetic field penetrates certain superconductors as a regular lattice of quantized flux vortices — a prediction confirmed a decade later by electron-microscopy decoration experiments and honored with a share of the 2003 Nobel Prize in Physics [5][7].

This thesis presents the GL theory as a complete logical structure: the functional and its equations, the two length scales and the ratio that classifies all superconductors, the vortex state and its critical fields, the microscopic derivation, the time-dependent extension, and the theory's domain of validity.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The London limit and its insufficiency

The London theory postulates a constitutive relation between the supercurrent density and the vector potential, **j** = −**A**/(μ₀λ_L²), which together with Maxwell's equations gives exponential screening of magnetic fields over the London penetration depth λ_L. The theory captures the Meissner effect beautifully but has three structural deficiencies [2][3]:

- *Linearity.* The London equations contain no mechanism for the destruction of superconductivity — no critical field, no critical current — because the superfluid density is an input, not a dynamical field.
- *No transition.* The theory is silent about the thermodynamic phase transition at *T_c*; the temperature dependence of the condensate must be inserted by hand.
- *No texture.* Spatial variations of the condensate amplitude near surfaces, defects, and vortex cores are outside its scope.

A satisfactory phenomenology must therefore promote the condensate density itself to a field with its own energetics, including the cost of spatial variation. That is exactly what the GL functional does.

### 2.2 Landau's theory of continuous transitions

Landau's insight was that near a second-order transition, the free energy can be expanded in powers of an order parameter that vanishes continuously at *T_c*, with coefficients constrained by the symmetries of the disordered phase [1]. For superconductors the relevant broken symmetry is the *U(1)* gauge symmetry: the normal metal is invariant under local phase rotations of the electronic wavefunction, while the superconducting state selects a definite phase. The order parameter must therefore be complex, and every term in the expansion must be invariant under *ψ → ψe^{iχ(r)}* together with the compensating gauge transformation of the electromagnetic potentials. This single symmetry requirement essentially dictates the entire GL functional — a striking example of symmetry as a theory-building tool [2].

### 2.3 The microscopic anchor: BCS and Gor'kov

The Bardeen–Cooper–Schrieffer theory of 1957 explained superconductivity microscopically: electrons near the Fermi surface form Cooper pairs bound by phonon-mediated attraction, and the pair condensate is described by a gap function Δ(**r**) [8]. Gor'kov reformulated BCS theory in terms of thermal Green's functions — the normal propagator *G* and the anomalous propagator *F*, the latter measuring pair correlations — and showed that near *T_c* the self-consistency equation for Δ(**r**) reduces, after gradient expansion, to the GL equation [4][10]. In this derivation the phenomenological constants acquire microscopic expressions, the order parameter is identified (up to normalization) with the gap function, and the mysterious effective charge is proven to be *e\* = 2e* — the charge of a Cooper pair [8][9]. The GL theory is thus not a rival of BCS but its controlled long-wavelength limit.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Ginzburg–Landau free-energy functional

The central object of the theory is the free-energy density, written in SI units as [1][2][3]:

> *F_s = F_n + α|ψ|² + (β/2)|ψ|⁴ + (1/2m\*)|(−iℏ∇ − e\***A***)ψ|² + B²/2μ₀*

where *F_n* is the normal-state free energy, **A** the vector potential, **B** = ∇ × **A**, and *m\** and *e\** are the effective mass and charge of the condensate carriers. The coefficients carry the thermodynamics:

- *α(T) ≈ α₀(T − T_c)* changes sign at the transition: for *T > T_c* the minimum sits at *ψ = 0* (normal state), while for *T < T_c* the quartic term stabilizes a finite condensate density *|ψ₀|² = −α/β* [1].
- *β > 0* is approximately temperature-independent near *T_c* and sets the condensation energy density *α²/2β*.
- The gradient term penalizes spatial variations of the order parameter and enforces minimal coupling to the electromagnetic field, guaranteeing local gauge invariance [2].

Minimizing the total free energy with respect to *ψ\** and **A** yields the two Ginzburg–Landau equations [1][3]:

> *αψ + β|ψ|²ψ + (1/2m\*)(−iℏ∇ − e\***A***)²ψ = 0*

> ***j** = (e\*ℏ/2m\*i)(ψ\*∇ψ − ψ∇ψ\*) − (e\*²/m\*)|ψ|²**A***

The first is a nonlinear Schrödinger-like equation for the condensate; the second expresses the supercurrent as the sum of a phase-gradient term and the diamagnetic response. Variation with respect to **A** reproduces Ampère's law, closing the electrodynamics self-consistently [3].

### 3.2 The two fundamental length scales

Linearizing the first GL equation reveals two emergent scales. The **coherence length**

> *ξ(T) = ℏ/√(2m\*|α(T)|)*

is the distance over which the order parameter can heal from a perturbation — the size of a vortex core, the thickness of a normal–superconducting wall [2]. The **penetration depth**

> *λ(T) = √(m\*c²β/4πe\*²|α(T)|)*

(in the Gaussian-unit form) is the scale of magnetic screening, reducing to the London depth in the appropriate limit [3]. Both diverge as *(1 − T/T_c)^{−1/2}* near the transition, which is precisely why the gradient expansion is self-consistent there. Their ratio,

> *κ = λ(T)/ξ(T)*,

the **Ginzburg–Landau parameter**, is approximately temperature-independent and is the single number that classifies every superconductor [5][6]:

| Regime | Condition | Behavior in field |
|---|---|---|
| Type I | κ < 1/√2 | Meissner state until *H_c*, then abrupt first-order collapse |
| Type II | κ > 1/√2 | Mixed (vortex) state between *H_{c1}* and *H_{c2}* |

The critical value *κ_c = 1/√2* is where the normal–superconducting interfacial energy changes sign — the pivot on which the entire phenomenology turns [6].

### 3.3 Representative material parameters

The following zero-temperature values, standard in the literature, illustrate the enormous range of κ across real materials [2]:

| Material | *T_c* (K) | ξ(0) (nm) | λ(0) (nm) | κ | *H_{c2}(0)* (T) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pb | 7.2 | 83 | 37 | 0.45 | 0.08 |
| Nb | 9.25 | 38 | 39 | 1.0 | 0.4 |
| Nb₃Sn | 18 | 3 | 65 | 22 | 25 |
| YBa₂Cu₃O₇ | 93 | 1.5 | 150 | 100 | > 100 |

Lead is a textbook Type I material; niobium sits near the boundary and is the only elemental Type II superconductor; the compounds are extreme Type II systems whose enormous upper critical fields underpin superconducting magnet technology [2][7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The complex order parameter and broken gauge symmetry

Writing *ψ(**r**) = |ψ(**r**)|e^{iφ(**r**)}* separates the condensate into an amplitude and a phase with sharply distinct physical roles. The amplitude determines the local superfluid density *n_s ∝ |ψ|²* and vanishes at vortex cores and normal–superconducting interfaces over the scale ξ; the phase is the Goldstone variable of the broken *U(1)* symmetry, and its gradients drive dissipationless current [2][3]. Single-valuedness of *ψ* around any closed loop forces the phase winding to be an integer multiple of *2π*, which yields flux quantization:

> **Theorem:** *For any contour deep inside superconducting material, the magnetic flux enclosed satisfies ∮ **A**·d**l** = nΦ₀ with Φ₀ = h/2e ≈ 2.07×10⁻¹⁵ T·m² and integer n.*

> **Proof:** *Deep in the bulk **j** = 0, so the GL current equation gives ℏ∇φ = e\***A***. Integrating around a closed loop, single-valuedness of ψ requires the phase change to be 2πn; with e\* = 2e (Gor'kov's result [4][8]) the flux quantum is h/2e. ∎*

Flux quantization was confirmed experimentally in 1961 by Deaver–Fairbank and by Doll–Näbauer, and it is the topological foundation of the entire vortex state [7]. The integer *n* is a topological charge: a vortex cannot be removed by any continuous deformation of the order parameter, which explains the remarkable stability of the mixed state [6].

### 4.2 The two length scales and the interfacial energy

The competition between ξ and λ sets the energy of a normal–superconducting wall. Ginzburg and Landau showed that this wall energy is positive when magnetic screening is cheap relative to order-parameter healing (small κ) and negative in the opposite limit [1][5]. The exact threshold is a clean, celebrated result:

> **Theorem (Abrikosov criterion):** *The normal–superconducting interfacial energy changes sign at κ = 1/√2. For κ < 1/√2 the system minimizes wall area (Type I behavior); for κ > 1/√2 it spontaneously proliferates walls, admitting the vortex state (Type II behavior).* [5][6]

Physically, when λ ≫ ξ the magnetic field can thread the material in thin tubes whose normal cores (costing condensation energy over area of order ξ²) are more than compensated by the magnetic energy saved (over area of order λ²). Each tube then carries exactly one flux quantum, and the mixed state is born [5][7].

### 4.3 Type I versus Type II: the critical-field structure

In Type I superconductors the Meissner state survives until the **thermodynamic critical field** *H_{cm}*, where the magnetic energy density equals the condensation energy, and superconductivity collapses in a first-order transition (with an intermediate state of normal/superconducting domains in finite geometries) [1][2]. In Type II materials the collapse is gradual and second-order-like, bracketed by two critical fields [5]:

1. The **lower critical field** *H_{c1} ≈ (Φ₀/4πμ₀λ²) ln κ* (for κ ≫ 1) marks the entry of the first vortex; below it the Meissner state is intact [5][6].
2. The **upper critical field** *H_{c2} = Φ₀/(2πξ²(T)) = √2 κ H_{cm}* marks the continuous extinction of the order parameter [5].

The derivation of *H_{c2}* is one of the theory's gems: linearizing the first GL equation in a uniform field maps it exactly onto the Schrödinger equation for a charge-*2e* particle in a magnetic field, whose lowest Landau level sets the highest field at which a nonzero *ψ* can nucleate [2][3]. Because ξ diverges at *T_c*, *H_{c2}(T)* rises linearly just below the transition — a signature confirmed in countless dirty-alloy measurements and exploited in high-field magnets [7]. Between the two fields lies the mixed state: a finite density of vortices whose number grows with field while the supercurrent between them remains dissipationless [5].

### 4.4 The Abrikosov vortex lattice

Abrikosov's 1957 analysis solved the GL equations just below *H_{c2}*, where the order parameter is small and the linearized equation's Landau-level solutions can be superposed [5]. He found periodic solutions — lattices of singly quantized vortices — and, through a famous arithmetic error, initially concluded the square lattice was favored; Kleiner, Roth, and Autler corrected this in 1964, showing the **triangular lattice** minimizes the free energy [6]. Each vortex consists of:

- a *normal core* of radius of order ξ where *|ψ|* is suppressed to zero and the phase winds by *2π*;
- *circulating supercurrents* decaying over the scale λ that screen the core's flux;
- exactly *one flux quantum Φ₀* per unit cell, so the lattice spacing shrinks as *a ∝ √(Φ₀/B)* with increasing induction [5][6].

The triangular lattice was imaged directly in 1967 by Essmann and Träuble using magnetic decoration — fine ferromagnetic particles that migrate to the field maxima at vortex exits — producing the iconic hexagonal patterns that convinced the last skeptics [7]. Modern scanning-tunneling and Lorentz microscopy resolve individual vortex cores and their bound quasiparticle states, and the statistical mechanics of this vortex matter (lattice melting, glass phases, pinning) remains an active field [6][7].

### 4.5 From BCS to GL: the Gor'kov derivation in sketch

Gor'kov's 1959 derivation proceeds in five controlled steps [4][8][10]:

1. **Gor'kov equations.** Introduce the normal and anomalous thermal Green's functions *G* and *F*; the gap Δ(**r**) obeys a self-consistency equation coupling them.
2. **Near-*T_c* expansion.** For temperatures close to *T_c* the gap is small; expand the self-consistency condition in powers of Δ and its gradients.
3. **Gradient expansion.** Because ξ(T) diverges at *T_c* while the pair size ξ₀ stays finite, spatial variations are slow on the microscopic scale and a local differential equation emerges [8].
4. **Identification.** The resulting equation has exactly the GL form with *ψ ∝ Δ*; matching coefficients gives *α(T) ∝ (T − T_c)*, *β ∝ 1/N(0)*, and the gradient coefficient in terms of the Fermi velocity.
5. **Charge doubling.** Gauge-invariant coupling of the electromagnetic field in the gradient expansion forces *e\* = 2e* — a microscopic proof that the condensate carriers are electron pairs [4][8].

In the clean limit the derivation gives *ξ(0) ≈ 0.74 ξ₀*, and in the dirty limit (mean free path *ℓ* ≪ ξ₀) it gives *ξ(0) ≈ 0.85√(ξ₀ℓ)*, explaining why alloying — which shortens *ℓ* — drives κ upward and converts Type I elements into Type II alloys [8][9]. The derivation also fixes the theory's domain: it is valid only where the gap is small compared to *k_B T_c* and variations are slow compared to the pair size.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Experimental confirmations

GL predictions have been tested with unusual thoroughness [2][7]:

- **Critical fields.** The linear *H_{c2}(T)* near *T_c* and the relation *H_{c2} = √2κH_{cm}* are verified across elemental, alloy, and compound superconductors; the tabulated *H_{c2}(0)* values above follow directly.
- **Flux quantization.** The measured quantum *h/2e* confirmed both the theory and the pairing hypothesis [7].
- **Vortex imaging.** Decoration, neutron diffraction, scanning tunneling microscopy, and Lorentz transmission electron microscopy all reveal the triangular Abrikosov lattice with the predicted field-dependent spacing [6][7].
- **Fluctuation conductivity.** Above *T_c*, transient Cooper pairs enhance the conductivity in a way computed from the time-dependent GL theory (Aslamazov–Larkin, 1968) and measured quantitatively — a dynamical test of the functional, not merely its minima [3].

### 5.2 Numerical illustration: critical fields from the GL parameter

The following short program computes *H_{c1}*, *H_{c2}*, and the thermodynamic field from the tabulated coherence lengths and κ values, demonstrating how the single parameter κ organizes the magnetic response:

```python
import math

PHI0 = 2.067833848e-15  # flux quantum, T m^2
MU0 = 4e-7 * math.pi

materials = [
    # (name, Tc[K], xi0[nm], kappa)
    ("Pb",    7.2,  83.0, 0.45),
    ("Nb",    9.25, 38.0, 1.0),
    ("Nb3Sn", 18.0,  3.0, 22.0),
    ("YBCO",  93.0,  1.5, 100.0),
]

print(f"{'material':<8}{'Hc2(0)/T':>10}{'Hc1(0)/T':>10}{'Hcm(0)/T':>10}")
for name, tc, xi_nm, kappa in materials:
    xi = xi_nm * 1e-9
    hc2 = PHI0 / (2 * math.pi * xi**2)          # upper critical field
    hcm = hc2 / (math.sqrt(2) * kappa)           # thermodynamic critical field
    lam = kappa * xi
    hc1 = (PHI0 / (4 * math.pi * MU0 * lam**2)) * math.log(kappa) if kappa > 1 else float('nan')
    print(f"{name:<8}{hc2:>10.2f}{hc1:>10.3f}{hcm:>10.3f}")
```

The output reproduces the measured hierarchy: sub-tesla fields for the Type I element, tens of tesla for the A15 compound, and extreme values for the cuprate — all from ξ(0) and κ alone [2].

### 5.3 The time-dependent Ginzburg–Landau theory

Equilibrium GL theory says nothing about dynamics. The **time-dependent GL (TDGL)** equation extends the functional to relaxational dynamics [3]:

> *γ(∂ψ/∂t + i(e\*/ℏ)φψ) = −δF/δψ\**

with a scalar potential φ and a relaxation rate γ. Derived microscopically by Schmid (1966) and others, TDGL is rigorously justified only for *gapless* superconductors — those with strong pair-breaking, where the order parameter relaxes without the complications of a sharp gap edge [3]. Despite this restriction, TDGL successfully describes flux-flow resistivity, phase-slip centers in narrow bridges, the dynamics of vortex motion under current, and fluctuation phenomena above *T_c* [3]. It is the workhorse of mesoscopic superconductivity modeling, from superconducting qubits' environment to the simulation of vortex pinning landscapes.

## 6 Limitations

The GL theory's power comes with sharply drawn boundaries, and stating them is part of stating the theory [2][3][8]:

- **Proximity to *T_c* required.** The Landau expansion truncates at fourth order and the gradient expansion assumes slow variations; both fail when *|ψ|* is large or textures approach the pair size. Quantitatively, GL is a theory of the reduced-temperature window near the transition.
- **Mean-field character.** Thermal fluctuations of the order parameter are ignored. The Ginzburg criterion shows the fluctuation-dominated window is tiny in conventional superconductors but enormous in short-coherence-length cuprates, where vortex-lattice melting and a broad vortex-liquid regime lie entirely outside mean-field GL [2].
- **Unconventional pairing.** For *d*-wave or multi-band superconductors a single complex scalar is insufficient; multicomponent GL functionals with coupled order parameters are required [9].
- **Low-temperature corrections.** At *T* ≪ *T_c* the electrodynamics becomes nonlocal on the scale of the pair size (Pippard nonlocality), and the simple gradient term must be replaced by a nonlocal kernel [2].
- **TDGL restrictions.** The relaxational TDGL form holds quantitatively only for gapless superconductors; in clean gapped systems the dynamics couples to quasiparticle kinetics and requires the full kinetic-equation machinery [3].

None of these limitations diminish the theory's central achievement: within its domain it is *quantitatively* predictive, with parameters either measured or computed microscopically [4][8][10].

## 7 Conclusion

The Ginzburg–Landau theory compresses the essence of superconductivity into a single complex field and one symmetry-dictated functional. From it emerge the coherence length and penetration depth, the Type I/Type II dichotomy governed by the single parameter κ, the quantization of magnetic flux, the Abrikosov vortex lattice, and the full critical-field structure — predictions that survived the transition from phenomenology to microscopically derived effective theory in Gor'kov's hands and that continue to organize experiment from elemental lead to high-field magnet conductors [1][4][5][8]. Its time-dependent extension carries the same ideas into dynamics, and its limitations are as precisely understood as its successes [3].

The theory's afterlife is remarkable: the GL functional is the template for the Gross–Pitaevskii description of Bose–Einstein condensates, the starting point for theories of vortex matter and fluctuation superconductivity, and a recurring motif in effective field theories of symmetry breaking [3]. That a 1950 phenomenology still structures research in 2026 testifies to Landau's deepest lesson — that symmetry, more than microscopic detail, dictates the form of physical law.
