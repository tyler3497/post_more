# Certified Certainty: Formal Verification of Byzantine Consensus — HotStuff Safety and Liveness in TLA+, Narwhal–Bullshark DAG Mempool Correctness, and Mechanized Proof in Ivy

## Abstract

Byzantine fault-tolerant (BFT) consensus protocols underpin modern blockchains, yet their correctness arguments are among the most intricate in distributed computing: they combine partial-synchrony timing assumptions, cryptographic threshold accumulators, view-change logic, and subtle quorum-intersection arguments whose interplay has historically harbored bugs [1][7]. This thesis studies how formal methods are brought to bear on three state-of-the-art BFT designs. First, we analyze HotStuff, the pipelined three-phase BFT protocol of Yin et al., dissecting its quorum-certificate chain rule, locked-QC safety argument, and pacemaker-driven liveness, and we show how the protocol is formalized in TLA+ and model-checked with TLC for bounded configurations, as demonstrated in industrial verification efforts [6][7]. Second, we examine the Narwhal–Bullshark separation of transaction dissemination from consensus ordering, its certified round-based DAG, and the correctness obligations of the Bullshark commit rule under both partial synchrony and asynchrony, including recent mechanized Coq proofs of Narwhal, Bullshark, and Sailfish via the LiDO-DAG refinement framework [3][4][10]. Third, we survey deductive verification of consensus in Ivy, a multi-modal tool that expresses protocols in a decidable fragment of first-order logic and discharges safety obligations with SMT solvers, as applied to production protocols such as Tendermint [5][8][9]. We synthesize a comparative methodology for verifying BFT systems, evaluate the trade-offs between model checking, interactive theorem proving, and decidable-fragment deductive verification, and identify open problems in verified liveness, verified reconfiguration, and end-to-end implementation correctness.

## 1 Intro

State machine replication in the presence of Byzantine faults is the classical problem at the heart of permissioned blockchains: *n* replicas, up to *f* of which may behave arbitrarily, must agree on a single totally ordered log of operations. The Dolev–Strong bound and the Fisher–Lynch–Paterson impossibility theorem circumscribe what is achievable; practical protocols therefore live in the **partial synchrony** model of Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer, where the network is asynchronous until an unknown *global stabilization time* (GST), after which message delays are bounded by Δ [1].

Classical protocols such as PBFT achieve this at the cost of a quadratic view-change procedure: replacing a faulty leader requires the new leader to collect 2*f*+1 new-view messages, each carrying the sender's highest known quorum state, and relay them onward — a procedure that is, in the HotStuff authors' words, "far from simple, is bug-prone, and incurs a significant communication penalty" [1]. HotStuff resolved this with a linear, leader-driven view change and **optimistic responsiveness**: after GST, a correct leader drives consensus at the speed of the actual network, not at the pace of the worst-case bound Δ [1].

Yet elegance of design does not imply correctness, and the history of consensus is littered with subtle flaws: Tendermint model-checking efforts found and fixed specification bugs in small configurations [7]; the original PBFT view-change logic has spawned entire research programs on its fragility [2]. Formal verification — writing down a precise mathematical specification and checking it with a machine — is therefore not a luxury but the only credible basis for trust in protocols that secure billions of dollars in assets. This thesis asks: *what does it take to verify a modern BFT protocol, end to end, with machine-checked rigor?*

We pursue the question along three axes, each targeting a distinct verification technology applied to a distinct protocol generation:

1. **TLA+/TLC model checking of HotStuff**, capturing the three-chain commit rule and locked-QC invariant in a temporal specification and exhaustively exploring small models [6][7].
2. **Mechanized proof of DAG-based consensus**, where the Narwhal mempool's certified DAG and Bullshark's interpretation-based ordering are verified by refinement in Coq [4][10].
3. **Deductive verification in Ivy**, expressing consensus protocols in the effectively-propositional fragment of first-order logic and proving safety inductively with Z3 [8][9], as demonstrated on Tendermint at Galois [5].

Our contributions are a unified taxonomy of verification obligations for BFT consensus, a worked reconstruction of the core safety arguments in each setting, and a critical assessment of what remains unverified — particularly liveness under adversarial scheduling and verified epoch reconfiguration.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The BFT consensus problem

In the state machine replication formulation, each correct replica maintains an append-only log; the protocol must guarantee:

- **Agreement (safety):** no two correct replicas commit conflicting blocks at the same log position.
- **Validity:** only client-proposed values are committed.
- **Termination (liveness):** every correct client request is eventually committed.

With *n* = 3*f*+1 replicas, a **quorum** of 2*f*+1 votes guarantees that any two quorums intersect in at least one correct replica — the combinatorial bedrock of every safety argument below:

```python
# Quorum intersection: the bedrock invariant of BFT safety
def quorums_intersect_in_honest(n: int, f: int) -> bool:
    assert n == 3 * f + 1
    q1, q2 = set(range(2 * f + 1)), set(range(f, 3 * f + 1))
    honest = set(range(n - f))            # replicas 0..n-f-1 are correct
    return len((q1 & q2) & honest) >= 1   # True for all f >= 0
```

> **Theorem (Quorum Intersection):** In a system of *n* = 3*f*+1 replicas with at most *f* Byzantine, any two quorums of size 2*f*+1 intersect in at least one correct replica. *Proof.* |Q₁ ∩ Q₂| ≥ (2*f*+1) + (2*f*+1) − (3*f*+1) = *f*+1 replicas, of which at most *f* can be faulty. ∎

### 2.2 HotStuff: three phases, chained and pipelined

HotStuff replaces PBFT's two-phase view change with a **three-phase commit** (prepare, pre-commit, commit) in which every phase has an identical leader-to-all communication pattern [1]. A **quorum certificate (QC)** is an aggregation of 2*f*+1 threshold-signature shares attesting to a proposal; because all phases look alike, views pipeline: block *b₁*'s commit phase doubles as block *b₂*'s pre-commit phase, and so on. The **three-chain rule** states that a block is committed when three certified blocks extend it in consecutive views, and the **locked QC** mechanism ensures a replica votes only for proposals extending its locked branch or a higher-view QC, which is what makes a linear view change safe [1].

### 2.3 Narwhal and Bullshark: separating dissemination from ordering

Narwhal–Tusk [3] and its successor Bullshark [4] observe that classic BFT couples *data dissemination* with the consensus routine, creating a throughput bottleneck at the leader. Narwhal instead runs a **certified DAG mempool**: every validator continuously proposes vertices referencing 2*f*+1 vertices of the previous round; a vertex becomes *certified* once 2*f*+1 validators sign it. Consensus then requires **no extra messages** — each validator interprets its local DAG view with a deterministic rule to extract the same total order [4]. Bullshark adds a practical fast path: in partially synchronous operation, two-round waves commit a steady-state anchor within six message delays, while a fallback mode with a common coin preserves asynchronous liveness [4]. The reference implementation achieves 125,000 transactions/second at 2-second latency across 50 parties [4].

### 2.4 Formal methods landscape

Three verification technologies dominate the distributed-systems literature:

| Technology | Logic / engine | Strength | Canonical BFT use |
|---|---|---|---|
| TLA+ / TLC | Temporal logic of actions; explicit-state model checker | Executable specs, counterexamples | HotStuff industrial spec [6], Tendermint [7] |
| Coq / Isabelle | Higher-order logic; interactive proof | Unbounded, mechanized proof | LibraBFT in Agda [10], LiDO-DAG in Coq [10] |
| Ivy | Decidable EPR fragment; Z3 SMT | Automated inductive invariants | Tendermint at Galois [5], Paxos [8] |

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is comparative and reconstructive. For each protocol–tool pair we (i) identify the **verification obligations** — the precise safety and liveness properties plus the environmental assumptions (fault bound, timing, cryptography); (ii) reconstruct the **core argument** at the level of detail a proof engineer must mechanize, including the inductive invariant and the quorum-intersection lemma it depends on; (iii) characterize the **automation boundary** — which steps the tool discharges automatically and which demand human guidance; and (iv) assess **fidelity**, i.e., the gap between the verified model and the deployed implementation.

We ground the analysis in primary sources: the HotStuff PODC'19 paper [1] and its TLA+ verification reports [6][7]; the Narwhal–Tusk [3] and Bullshark [4] papers together with the LiDO-DAG mechanization [10]; and the Ivy literature [8][9] with the Galois Tendermint verification experience report [5]. Cryptographic primitives (threshold signatures, hash functions) are treated as ideal functionalities throughout, matching the standard practice in all cited works [1][10].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 HotStuff safety: the locked-QC invariant

HotStuff's safety rests on a single inductive invariant maintained by every correct replica. Let `lockedQC(r)` be replica *r*'s highest known pre-commit QC and `prepareQC(b)` the prepare QC certifying block *b*:

> **Theorem (HotStuff Safety):** If a correct replica commits block *b* in view *v*, then every prepare QC formed in any view *v′ > v* certifies a block extending *b*.

The proof proceeds by induction over views. A prepare QC for *v′* requires 2*f*+1 votes; each voter either already holds a locked QC on *b*'s branch or observed a higher-view QC justifying the deviation — and the quorum intersection theorem forces at least one correct voter to have been in the 2*f*+1 pre-commit quorum for *b*, which refuses to vote against its lock [1]. Mechanizing this argument is the central obligation of any HotStuff verification.

In TLA+, the protocol is specified as a state machine over variables such as `view`, `lockedQC`, `prepareQC`, and message multisets. A representative action is vote handling:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE HotStuff ------------------------------
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets

CONSTANTS Replicas, Faulty, QuorumSize
VARIABLES view, lockedQC, prepareQC, votes, committed

Vote(r, b) ==
    /\ r \notin Faulty
    /\ b.view = view[r]
    /\ \/ Extends(b, lockedQC[r])        \* safe on locked branch
       \/ b.justifyQC.view > lockedQC[r].view  \* higher QC unlocks
    /\ votes' = votes \union {[replica |-> r, block |-> b]}
    /\ UNCHANGED <<view, lockedQC, prepareQC, committed>>

SafetyInvariant ==
    \A r1, r2 \in Replicas \ Faulty :
        \A b1, b2 \in committed[r1] \cup committed[r2] :
            b1.height = b2.height => b1 = b2
=============================================================================
```

Industrial TLA+ efforts check invariants such as `NoConflictingCommits` and `QcUniquePerView` with TLC under Byzantine models — including equivocating leaders proposing two blocks per view and unconstrained Byzantine votes — exploring tens of millions of states without violation [6]. Braithwaite et al. report that even small configurations of Tendermint in TLA+ exposed specification bugs that were fixed as a result, underscoring that the value of model checking lies as much in *debugging the specification* as in certifying it [7].

### 4.2 HotStuff liveness: pacemakers and rotating leaders

Safety without liveness is a protocol that never disagrees and never decides. HotStuff's liveness argument depends on a **pacemaker** — a view-synchronization mechanism guaranteeing that after GST, correct replicas eventually overlap in the same view for long enough, with a correct leader, to complete the three phases [1]. Verifying liveness is strictly harder than safety: TLC checks only invariance properties, and the reported HotStuff TLA+ efforts verify safety invariants while treating liveness informally or under fairness assumptions [6]. This asymmetry — *safety mechanized, liveness hand-waved* — recurs across the literature and is a central limitation we return to in §6.

### 4.3 Narwhal–Bullshark: certifying the DAG, then interpreting it

Narwhal's correctness decomposes into two layers. The **mempool layer** guarantees *data availability and causal integrity*: a certified vertex's entire causal history is retrievable, because certification required 2*f*+1 validators to have stored it [3]. Formally, this is a *block-store abstraction* with write/read semantics proved against an asynchronous, eventually-reliable network model.

The **consensus layer** (Bullshark) is pure interpretation: validators totally order the DAG by a deterministic rule over committed *anchors*. The key verification obligation is the **commit-rule agreement**: if one correct validator commits anchor *a* in wave *w*, every correct validator eventually commits *a* before any later anchor [4]. In the partially synchronous fast path, waves are two rounds and an anchor commits when 2*f*+1 second-round vertices reference it; in fallback mode, a common coin elects a retroactive leader whose commit needs 2*f*+1 references, with **voting types** ensuring a validator votes for only one leader type per wave [4]. The LiDO-DAG project mechanized exactly this stack in Coq — Narwhal, Bullshark, and Sailfish — as refinements of a concurrent-object abstraction, yielding machine-checked safety, liveness, *and* fairness proofs, and even discovering a verified latency optimization for Sailfish [10]. This is, to our knowledge, the deepest mechanized verification of DAG-based consensus to date.

### 4.4 Deductive verification in Ivy: decidability as a design discipline

Ivy takes a different route to automation: rather than exploring states (TLC) or checking human-written proof terms (Coq), it asks the engineer to phrase the protocol in the **effectively propositional (EPR)** fragment of first-order logic — a decidable fragment — and then searches for an *inductive invariant* interactively, discharging each verification condition with Z3 [8][9]. Stratified function symbols, finite sorts for the unbounded-but-symmetric replica set, and carefully quarantined quantifier alternations keep every solver query decidable [8].

The payoff is demonstrated at industrial scale: Galois's verification of the **Tendermint** blockchain protocol in Ivy models proposals, prevotes, and precommits as relations over rounds and validator sets, proves agreement and validity, and — crucially — *generates executable C++ from the same model*, letting engineers test the specification against their mental model of the implementation [5]. The Ivy model of a prevote step is illustrative:

```haskell
-- Tendermint prevote action, transliterated from the Ivy model (Galois [5])
-- Ivy syntax: export action; assumptions gate the transition; updates follow.
l_28 :: Round -> Value -> Round -> Quorum -> Protocol ()
l_28 r v vr q = do
  assume (hasStarted && r == round_p && step == Propose)
  assume (recvedProposal (proposer r) r v vr)
  assume (isQuorum q && all (\n -> recvedPrevote n vr v) (members q))
  assume (vr >= 0 && vr < r)
  if valid v && (lockedRound <= vr || lockedValue == v)
    then broadcastPrevote r v
    else broadcastPrevote r Nil
  step .= Prevote
```

A Rust sketch shows how the same quorum logic appears at the implementation level that Ivy's generated code must mirror:

```rust
/// Quorum certificate assembly: 2f+1 distinct partial signatures.
pub struct QuorumCert {
    pub view: u64,
    pub block_hash: [u8; 32],
    pub signers: Vec<ReplicaId>,   // must be distinct
    pub agg_sig: BlsSignature,
}

pub fn assemble_qc(votes: &[Vote], f: usize) -> Option<QuorumCert> {
    let mut seen = std::collections::HashSet::new();
    let valid: Vec<&Vote> = votes.iter()
        .filter(|v| v.verify() && seen.insert(v.replica))
        .collect();
    // Quorum intersection argument relies on |signers| >= 2f+1 *distinct* replicas.
    if valid.len() >= 2 * f + 1 {
        Some(QuorumCert::aggregate(&valid[..2 * f + 1]))
    } else {
        None
    }
}
```

The Agda verification of HotStuff-based LibraBFT complements Ivy's approach from the proof-assistant side: it proves safety for an *abstract* protocol parameterized by implementation assumptions (including hash injectivity for block IDs), so any implementation satisfying the assumptions inherits the theorems without redoing the hard proof work [10]. Together, Ivy and the Agda work delineate the two poles of the field: **decidable automation with a bounded logical fragment** versus **unbounded mechanized proof with maximal human guidance**.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

We summarize the verification evidence reported across the surveyed efforts:

| Protocol | Tool | Property class | Scale of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| HotStuff-2 | TLA+/TLC | Safety invariants (agreement, QC integrity, no double vote) | 14.1M states exhaustive at MaxView=2; 124M states simulated at MaxView=4, 0 violations [6] |
| HotStuff | TLA+/TLC (industrial) | Safety + liveness spec | Bounded configurations; spec bugs found and fixed [7] |
| Bullshark / Narwhal | Coq (LiDO-DAG) | Safety, liveness, fairness by refinement | Fully mechanized; artifact available [10] |
| Tendermint | Ivy + Z3 | Agreement, validity; executable model | Inductive invariant found; C++ generated and tested [5] |
| LibraBFT (HotStuff) | Agda | Safety for single epoch | Mechanized; implementation-agnostic assumptions [10] |

Two quantitative observations stand out. First, Bullshark's implementation cost — 200 lines of code atop Narwhal — is small enough that the *verified* artifact and the *deployed* artifact nearly coincide, collapsing the fidelity gap that plagues larger codebases [4][10]. Second, TLC state counts grow superexponentially in the view bound: exhaustive checking is feasible only for tiny configurations (MaxView=2, *n*=4), with simulation covering larger models [6]. The empirical lesson is consistent: **model checking buys bug-finding and bounded assurance; proof assistants buy unbounded assurance at high labor cost; Ivy buys a middle path where the logic fragment is chosen to make automation predictable** [8][9].

The quorum-intersection lemma appears, in some form, in every single proof — it is the * Iris*-style reusable core of BFT verification. Any future verified-BFT framework should provide it as a certified library lemma rather than re-proving it per protocol.

## 6 Limitations

**Liveness remains the poor cousin of safety.** TLC cannot check liveness properties in the reported HotStuff efforts; Ivy's liveness support is nascent (recent work re-expresses liveness as safety [5][9]); only the Coq LiDO-DAG effort claims mechanized liveness, and it does so under strong fairness assumptions about the scheduler [10]. A Byzantine adversary that controls message scheduling adversarially is rarely modeled with full generality.

**Epoch reconfiguration is largely unverified.** The Agda LibraBFT proof covers a single epoch with fixed membership; extending proofs across configuration changes is stated as future work [10]. Since real deployments reconfigure routinely, this is the largest gap between verified models and production systems.

**Cryptography is idealized.** All surveyed efforts assume perfect threshold signatures and collision-resistant hashing as axioms [1][10]. Compositional guarantees linking these axioms to computational security definitions (e.g., via universal composability) are absent.

**Byzantine behavior modeling is incomplete.** TLA+ models bound Byzantine creativity to a menu of attacks (equivocation, spam, deviation) [6]; a truly arbitrary Byzantine strategy space is unrepresentable in a finite model. Proof assistants handle this better by quantifying over all behaviors, at the cost of manual proof effort.

**State explosion bounds model checking.** Even with symmetry reduction, exhaustive TLC verification of HotStuff-class protocols stalls at *n*=4 and a handful of views [6]; assurance for *n*=100 deployments is extrapolation, not verification.

## 7 Conclusion

Formal verification has crossed the threshold from toy protocols to the BFT systems that secure production blockchains. HotStuff's three-phase, pipelined design admits TLA+ specifications whose safety invariants survive tens of millions of TLC-explored states under active Byzantine adversaries [1][6]. The Narwhal–Bullshark decomposition of dissemination from ordering yields a small consensus core — 200 lines — that has been *fully mechanized* in Coq with safety, liveness, and fairness [4][10]. And Ivy demonstrates that choosing a decidable logical fragment up front buys automated inductive proofs of real protocols like Tendermint, with executable models as a bonus [5][8][9].

The through-line is methodological: **verification succeeds when the protocol is designed to be verifiable** — small consensus cores, uniform communication patterns, and explicit quorum certificates all serve the prover as much as the network. The open frontier is liveness under adversarial scheduling, verified reconfiguration across epochs, and closing the fidelity gap between model and deployed code. Until those are settled, machine-checked BFT will remain, like HotStuff's own commit rule, a three-chain argument: strong in each link, but only as trustworthy as the weakest connection between model and machine.
