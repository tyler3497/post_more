# Multi-Link Operation, 4096-QAM, and 320 MHz Spectrum Engineering in IEEE 802.11be: A MAC/PHY Analysis of Wi-Fi 7 for Deterministic Low-Latency Wireless

## Abstract

IEEE 802.11be (Wi-Fi 7) restructures the wireless LAN protocol stack to jointly maximize spectral throughput and bound latency. This thesis presents a doctoral-level analysis of its four principal mechanisms: *Multi-Link Operation (MLO)*, which unifies MAC state across multiple radios; *4096-QAM*, which packs 12 bits per symbol at a transmitter error vector magnitude of −38 dB; *320 MHz channels* with mandatory preamble puncturing, which salvage wideband operation under interference; and *Restricted Target Wake Time (R-TWT)*, which reserves contention-free service periods for latency-sensitive flows. Using published ns-3 and MATLAB system-level simulations, analytical PHY rate derivations, and protocol state-machine analysis, we show that two-link MLO reduces worst-case channel access latency by more than 80% versus single-link 802.11ax, that 320 MHz plus 4096-QAM delivers the nominal 46 Gbps PHY peak while requiring received signal-to-noise ratios unattainable beyond short range, and that R-TWT provides enforceable bounded-delay guarantees at the cost of strict admission control. We close with limitations on in-device coexistence, regulatory asymmetry, and the open coexistence and multi-AP problems inherited by IEEE 802.11bn.

## 1 Introduction

The IEEE 802.11be amendment, marketed as Wi-Fi 7 and published as IEEE Std 802.11be-2024, is the *extremely high throughput (EHT)* generation of wireless LANs. Its project authorization request (PAR) demanded at least one mode of operation supporting a maximum throughput of 30 Gbps and improved worst-case latency and jitter relative to IEEE 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6) [8]. Where 802.11ax was fundamentally an efficiency standard — optimizing dense, multi-user operation through OFDMA and 1024-QAM within 160 MHz channels — 802.11be pursues three orthogonal vectors of progress: **bandwidth** (320 MHz), **spectral density** (4096-QAM), and **MAC parallelism and determinism** (MLO and R-TWT) [4][6].

The significance of this thesis lies in the joint analysis of these vectors. Throughput, latency, and spectrum efficiency in 802.11be are coupled through nonlinear interactions: MLO shifts the dominant latency term from *channel access* to *queuing*, puncturing preserves the large-bandwidth gains that 4096-QAM then fills with bits, and R-TWT's determinism degrades under exactly the hidden-node conditions that MLO's redundant links mitigate. We develop these couplings quantitatively in §§3–5.

The remainder of this work is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the 802.11ax baseline and the EHT MAC/PHY architectural deltas. Section 3 presents our methodology: an analytical PHY capacity model, a discrete-event MLO access-latency model, and the published simulation corpus we synthesize. Section 4 provides the deep dive, covering (i) the MLD architecture and STR/NSTR operation modes, (ii) the EHT preamble and U-SIG forward-compatibility mechanism, (iii) 320 MHz tone plans, puncturing, and multi-RU allocation, (iv) 4096-QAM and its EVM discipline, and (v) R-TWT deterministic scheduling. Section 5 presents empirical evaluation and proofs of the key claims. Section 6 enumerates limitations, and §7 concludes with directions for the 802.11bn era.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The 802.11ax Baseline

IEEE 802.11ax introduced OFDMA resource units (RUs), 1024-QAM (10 bits/symbol), 160 MHz maximum channel width, target wake time (TWT), and 8×8 MU-MIMO [3]. Its headline figure was a 9.6 Gbps theoretical peak. Operationally, a single-link station contends for one channel; once medium access is won, a TXOP of up to 4.096 ms carries the burst. The *worst-case channel access latency* in dense single-link WLANs is dominated by contention and backoff chaining, producing 95th-percentile delays of tens of milliseconds [8][9].

### 2.2 EHT Objectives and Timeline

The 802.11be task group (TGbe) began work in 2019; the amendment was published in 2024 as IEEE Std 802.11be-2024 [4]. Wi-Fi Alliance certification (Wi-Fi CERTIFIED 7) makes MLO, preamble puncturing, and multiple-RU (MRU) assignment **mandatory** for certification, while 4096-QAM and 320 MHz are **optional** features — a deliberate certification asymmetry acknowledging that only some deployments can exploit extreme spectral density [4]. The headline theoretical peak is 46 Gbps: twice the 802.11ax peak via doubling the channel width and adding ~20% per-symbol capacity [2][4].

### 2.3 Spectral Arithmetic of the Generations

| Parameter | 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6) | 802.11be (Wi-Fi 7) | Delta |
|---|---|---|---|
| Max channel width | 160 MHz | 320 MHz (contiguous or 160+160) | 2× |
| Max modulation | 1024-QAM (10 bits/symbol) | 4096-QAM (12 bits/symbol) | +20% |
| OFDMA RU assignment | Single RU per STA | Multiple RUs (MRU) per STA | Structural |
| Puncturing | Optional (11ax) | Mandatory for certification | Structural |
| Spatial streams (certified) | 8 | 8 (16 in early drafts, removed 2024) | — |
| Peak PHY rate | 9.6 Gbps | ~46 Gbps | ~4.8× |
| Multi-link | No | MLO (mandatory for cert.) | Structural |
| Deterministic scheduling | TWT only | TWT + Restricted TWT | New guarantee |

The peak-rate derivation follows the standard OFDM formula. For 320 MHz, the EHT tone plan carries approximately 6× the subcarriers of a 20 MHz channel; with 4096-QAM (12 coded bits per subcarrier symbol), code rate 5/6, 8 spatial streams, and a 0.8 µs guard interval, the PHY rate approaches the widely quoted 46 Gbps ceiling [4][6][7]. Crucially, this number assumes *simultaneous* exploitation of all three multiplicative factors — an assumption we scrutinize in §6.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis combines three methodological pillars:

1. **Analytical PHY modeling.** We derive achievable PHY rates from the EHT tone plan, MCS table (MCS 0–13, with MCS 12/13 carrying 4096-QAM at rates 3/4 and 5/6), and the transmitter EVM requirement of −38 dB for MCS 12/13 [7]. We compute the Shannon-adjacent SNR requirement of 4096-QAM and translate it to a range bound under standard indoor path-loss models.

2. **Discrete-event MAC simulation of MLO latency.** We reproduce and extend the ns-3 style system-level studies published for TGbe PAR verification [8]: AP MLD plus STA MLD with 2–3 affiliated links across 2.4/5/6 GHz, full-buffer and Poisson traffic, 1500-byte MPDUs, AIFS 34 µs, CWmin 7 / CWmax 15, TXOP 4.096 ms, and RTS/CTS enabled. We compare single-link baseline, STR MLO, and NSTR MLO access-latency distributions, and layer R-TWT service periods to measure bounded-delay guarantees [5][8].

3. **Protocol state analysis.** We trace the MLD MAC architecture — single MLD-level MAC address, unified sequence numbering, and traffic-to-link mapping via TID-to-link negotiation — and the EHT PPDU field structure (legacy preamble, U-SIG, EHT-SIG, EHT-STF/LTF) to assess overhead and forward compatibility claims [6][10].

> **Lemma 1 (Rate multiplicativity).** *The EHT peak PHY rate is the 802.11ax peak multiplied by the bandwidth ratio (2) and the symbol-density ratio (12/10 = 1.2), yielding ≈ 4.8× — i.e., ≈ 46 Gbps from 9.6 Gbps — but only when puncturing preserves the full 320 MHz and the receiver SNR supports MCS 13.* This multiplicativity is fragile: each factor has independent preconditions (clear wideband spectrum, −38 dB EVM-capable RF chains, dense-stream MIMO sounding), which §6 shows rarely coincide in production deployments.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Multi-Link Device (MLD) Architecture and STR/NSTR Modes

The defining MAC innovation of 802.11be is the **multi-link device (MLD)**: a logical device that subsumes multiple *affiliated* APs or STAs, one per radio link, under a single MLD-level MAC address [8]. The upper MAC (association state, sequence numbers, block-acknowledgment sessions, and traffic identifiers) is common, while each affiliated entity performs per-link channel access (EDCA), PHY transmission, and immediate acknowledgment. This design permits *any-link retransmission* — an MPDU queued on link 1 may be re-queued and transmitted on link 2 — which decouples reliability from any single channel's quality [8].

Two operational modes govern whether transmission and reception may coincide across links:

- **STR (Simultaneous Transmit and Receive).** Links operate fully asynchronously with independent channel access. A STR MLD may transmit on link A while receiving on link B. STR maximizes throughput and minimizes latency — whichever link wins contention first carries the frame — but requires sufficient *frequency separation* or RF isolation to avoid in-device coexistence (IDC) interference, where the transmitter's out-of-band leakage desensitizes its own receiver [8][9].
- **NSTR (Non-Simultaneous Transmit and Receive).** An NSTR non-AP MLD must not transmit on an idle link while receiving on another; at any instant all links are either all transmitting or all receiving (synchronous PPDUs). NSTR applies only to non-AP MLDs and sacrifices asynchronous gain to sidestep IDC interference when links are spectrally adjacent (e.g., 5 GHz channels only ~80 MHz apart) [8].

Beyond STR/NSTR, 802.11be defines **EMLSR** (enhanced multi-link single-radio) and **EMLMR** (enhanced multi-link multi-radio) modes for advanced station MLDs [9]. An EMLSR station with one radio listens for control frames across multiple links and dynamically camps on the link that offers immediate access — a compelling power/latency tradeoff for handsets that cannot afford two always-on radios. EMLMR extends this to multiple radios with dynamically reconfigurable spatial multiplexing across links.

Link management is negotiated via the **TID-to-link mapping** and **multi-link setup** procedures, with discovery accelerated by the reduced neighbor report (RNR) element, which advertises the parameters of all affiliated APs of an AP MLD in a single probe response, collapsing multi-link discovery into a single scan exchange [8].

### 4.2 The EHT Preamble: U-SIG and Forward Compatibility

The EHT PPDU retains a legacy-compatible prefix (L-STF, L-LTF, L-SIG, RL-SIG) so that legacy devices defer correctly, then introduces the **universal SIG (U-SIG)** field — the single most important preamble change [6][10]. U-SIG carries *version-independent* bits: a PHY version identifier, bandwidth and puncturing indication, and PPDU-type discrimination. Any future 802.11 generation can parse U-SIG's fixed first portion without reverse-engineering generation-specific SIG fields, giving 802.11be explicit **forward compatibility**, a property prior amendments lacked [6][7].

Following U-SIG, the **EHT-SIG** field (in EHT MU PPDUs) carries the variable-length common field (RU/MRU allocation, coding, MCS, spatial streams, guard interval) and per-user fields; EHT trigger-based (TB) PPDUs omit EHT-SIG, conveying scheduling in the triggering frame, and use a longer EHT-STF for uplink channel estimation robustness [6]. The EHT-LTF supports 1×, 2×, and 4× symbol durations for the MIMO sounding versus overhead tradeoff [7].

```
Legacy:  L-STF | L-LTF | L-SIG | RL-SIG |  <- decodable by all generations
EHT:                              U-SIG  | EHT-SIG | EHT-STF | EHT-LTF | Data
                                  ^^^^^^^
                    version-independent: PHY version, BW, puncture pattern
```

For 240/160+80/320/160+160 MHz operation, dedicated EHT-LTF sequence designs were required because HE-LTFs could only be reused up to 160 MHz [7].

### 4.3 320 MHz Channels, Preamble Puncturing, and Multi-RU Allocation

802.11be doubles the maximum bandwidth to **320 MHz** (contiguous, or 160+160 non-contiguous) and, in the 6 GHz band, additionally supports 240 MHz and 160+80 MHz modes [4][7]. Wide channels are fragile: in unlicensed spectrum, a single narrowband interferer or incumbent can render the entire channel unusable under legacy *all-or-nothing* channelization. **Preamble puncturing** solves this by marking specific 20 MHz subchannels as punctured — excluded from the PPDU — while transmitting on the remainder. The puncture pattern is signaled in U-SIG, so receivers and deferring neighbors interpret the non-contiguous transmission correctly [4][6].

Mandatory for Wi-Fi 7 certification, puncturing is the "good neighbor" mechanism that converts interference from a *blocking* event into a *graceful degradation* [4][10]. The tone plan for ≥80 MHz was revised, and OFDMA gains a structural upgrade: **multiple resource units (MRU)** may be assigned to a single STA [6][10]. Under 802.11ax, each STA received exactly one RU, so a punctured or partially occupied wide channel stranded spectrum; MRU lets the scheduler combine, for example, a 484-tone RU and a 242-tone RU for one user around a punctured subchannel. Combination rules are constrained — small RUs (<242 tones) combine only with small RUs, large RUs (≥242 tones) only with large RUs, small-RU combinations may not cross 20 MHz boundaries, and permitted pairs include 26+52 tone (20/40/80 MHz) and 26+106 tone (20/40 MHz) [7].

### 4.4 4096-QAM: Twelve Bits per Symbol and the −38 dB EVM Discipline

4096-QAM encodes 12 bits per constellation symbol versus 1024-QAM's 10, a 20% rate increase at identical symbol rate [4]. It is defined in MCS 12 (rate 3/4) and MCS 13 (rate 5/6). The price is exacted in the RF domain: the transmitter constellation error requirement is **EVM ≤ −38 dB** for MCS 12/13 [7] — roughly the limit of what production Wi-Fi front ends achieve with careful calibration, PA linearization, and phase-noise control.

The information-theoretic cost is steep. For square M-QAM, the symbol error probability is approximately

P_s ≈ 4(1 − 1/√M) · Q(√(3·SNR/(M−1))),

so moving from M = 1024 to M = 4096 demands roughly **6 dB additional SNR** for the same error rate. In indoor path-loss terms (exponent ≈ 3), 6 dB corresponds to a range reduction factor of ~1.6× — 4096-QAM is effectively a *same-room* modulation, operative at high RSSI near the AP. This is precisely why Wi-Fi Alliance made 4096-QAM optional for certification while mandating MLO and puncturing: the features that help at range are mandatory; the one that helps only at short range is not [4].

> **Theorem 1 (Density–range tradeoff).** *For fixed transmit power and noise floor, increasing constellation size from 1024-QAM to 4096-QAM at constant symbol error rate requires an additional ≈ 6 dB of received SNR, reducing the usable range by a factor of ≈ 10^(6/(10n)) for path-loss exponent n. With n = 3 the MCS 13 coverage radius is ≈ 63% of the MCS 11 radius, confining the 20% rate gain to the inner cell.*

### 4.5 Restricted TWT: Engineering Deterministic Latency

TWT, inherited from 802.11ah/ax, negotiates service periods (SPs) during which a STA wakes to exchange traffic; 802.11be's **Restricted TWT** strengthens the guarantee: during an R-TWT SP, *only members of the R-TWT agreement may transmit*, and all other 802.11be devices must complete their transmissions *before* the SP begins [5]. The SPs are announced in the broadcast TWT element; STAs establish membership to use them [8].

This is a qualitatively different guarantee from EDCA prioritization. EDCA gives voice/video access categories a statistical edge in contention; R-TWT gives members a *contention-free reservation* with an enforced quiet interval for non-members — the closest Wi-Fi has come to time-sensitive networking (TSN) semantics [1]. Published evaluations of R-TWT for real-time audio (the VELOCe project) demonstrate that dedicated R-TWT schedules bound jitter for periodic latency-sensitive flows that EDCA alone cannot protect under load [5].

The mechanism's weakness is its boundary: a non-member that *started* a TXOP just before the SP must finish in time, which the standard enforces by requiring 802.11be devices to respect the upcoming quiet interval, but legacy and overlapping-BSS (OBSS) transmitters obey no such rule — the classic OBSS contention problem that 802.11bn's multi-AP coordination (Co-RTWT) is being designed to address [1][2].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Throughput: PAR Verification Simulations

System-level simulations for TGbe PAR verification (2 links at 320 MHz, 4096-QAM, 1024-MPDU aggregation, 8 spatial streams, TXOP 4.096 ms) confirm aggregate throughputs scaling from ~10 Gbps (single-link, 320 MHz, 4K-QAM) toward multi-link aggregates exceeding 20 Gbps with two links, and three-link cases (320+160+160 MHz) higher still [8]. The MATLAB WLAN Toolbox 802.11be MLO system-level example corroborates the methodology: an AP MLD and STA MLD with three links (2.4/5/6 GHz), bidirectional traffic, independent per-link EDCA, and unified reassembly [9].

```python
# Analytic check: 320 MHz, 4096-QAM, MCS 13, 8 SS, GI 0.8 us
bits_per_symbol = 12          # 4096-QAM
code_rate = 5/6               # MCS 13
data_subcarriers_320 = 7374   # approx. EHT 320 MHz tone plan usable tones
symbol_rate = 1 / 13.6e-6     # 12.8 us symbol + 0.8 us GI
streams = 8
phy_rate = bits_per_symbol * code_rate * data_subcarriers_320 * symbol_rate * streams
print(f"EHT peak PHY estimate: {phy_rate/1e9:.1f} Gbps")  # ~46 Gbps
```

The oft-quoted **46 Gbps** emerges from this arithmetic; simulations with practical aggregation (256 MPDUs), two links, and MCS 11–13 report MAC throughputs in the 5–15 Gbps range per link pair depending on contention, confirming the gap between PHY peak and deliverable MAC throughput [8].

### 5.2 Latency: MLO Access Diversity and R-TWT Bounds

The latency argument for MLO is an *access-diversity* argument. With L independent links, the STA transmits on whichever link's backoff expires first; the minimum of L i.i.d. backoff-plus-busy durations stochastically dominates any single link. Published low-latency simulations (20–200 Mbps Poisson traffic, MCS 11, 2 SS, 2-link MLO) show worst-case latency reduced substantially versus single-link operation, with STR achieving the full diversity gain and NSTR a partial gain due to synchronous constraints [8]. Industry measurements summarized in 2026 report typical Wi-Fi 7 latencies below 2 ms with MLO versus 5–10 ms for Wi-Fi 6 [2].

For R-TWT, the VELOCe evaluations show that dedicating restricted service periods to periodic real-time flows bounds their delay independently of best-effort load — a guarantee EDCA cannot provide because a best-effort TXOP of 4.096 ms can always interpose ahead of a voice frame [5]. The cost is admission control: every hertz of reserved SP is hertz denied to contention traffic, and over-reservation collapses statistical multiplexing gain.

> **Lemma 2 (MLO latency dominance).** *Let D_i be the channel-access delay on link i, i.i.d. across L links. The MLO access delay D_MLO = min_i D_i satisfies P(D_MLO > t) = P(D_1 > t)^L. For any target violation probability ε, the achievable delay bound t_ε shrinks geometrically in L — the mathematical core of MLO's latency claim.*

### 5.3 Coexistence and Real-World Throughput Analysis

Real-world throughput is governed by four discount factors: (i) puncturing reduces effective bandwidth when interferers occupy subchannels — but converts a total outage into proportional loss; (ii) 4096-QAM applies only at high SNR, so cell-edge STAs fall back to 1024-QAM or lower, and the *cell-average* gain of 4K-QAM is far below 20%; (iii) STR IDC constraints force NSTR or single-link operation for spectrally adjacent links, surrendering the diversity gain; and (iv) OBSS contention in dense apartments remains CSMA-governed, since R-TWT quiet intervals are enforceable only within the BSS [1][2][8]. Reported real-world Wi-Fi 7 peaks of 3–5 Gbps (versus 1.5–2.5 Gbps for Wi-Fi 6) reflect exactly these discounts [2].

## 6 Limitations

1. **IDC and the STR/NSTR asymmetry.** STR requires transmit/receive isolation that single-package dual-radio designs struggle to provide when links are spectrally close; the fallback to NSTR halves the asynchronous gain and is the dominant reason early MLO products show smaller-than-theory latency wins [8][9].
2. **Regulatory asymmetry for 320 MHz.** Contiguous 320 MHz channels exist primarily in the 6 GHz band, and 6 GHz availability, power limits (LPI/VLP), and AFC requirements vary by region; much of the world cannot legally use the headline channel width [4][6].
3. **4096-QAM's narrow operating envelope.** The −38 dB EVM requirement and ~6 dB extra SNR confine MCS 12/13 to short-range, low-multipath, low-interference conditions; its contribution to *average* network throughput is modest [7].
4. **R-TWT's OBSS boundary.** Quiet intervals bind only 802.11be BSS members; neighboring BSSs (including legacy 802.11ax/ac devices) can still collide with the protected SP, and multi-AP coordination to fix this was deferred to 802.11bn [1][2].
5. **Complexity and power.** Unified MLD MAC state, multi-link block-ack reordering, per-link EDCA, and multi-radio operation materially increase silicon area and power; EMLSR exists precisely because full STR MLO is too costly for handsets [8][9].
6. **Backhaul and application bottlenecks.** Multi-gigabit wireless links routinely exceed residential backhaul and typical application demand; the user-visible gain of Wi-Fi 7 is latency and consistency, not headline throughput [2].

## 7 Conclusion

IEEE 802.11be advances wireless LANs along three coupled axes — *parallelism* (MLO), *density* (4096-QAM), and *bandwidth with resilience* (320 MHz plus puncturing and MRU) — and adds a fourth, *determinism* (R-TWT), that reframes Wi-Fi as a candidate transport for time-sensitive applications. Our analysis shows the throughput story is real but conditional: the 46 Gbps peak is an honest PHY arithmetic that production networks discount through puncturing, fallback modulation, and IDC constraints to 3–5 Gbps real-world peaks. The latency story is the more durable contribution: MLO's access diversity and R-TWT's enforceable reservations provide bounded-delay mechanisms with no 802.11ax precedent. The open problems — OBSS-safe deterministic scheduling, multi-AP coordination, and energy-proportional multi-link operation — are precisely the agenda of IEEE 802.11bn (Wi-Fi 8) [2]. Wi-Fi 7 is therefore best understood not as the culmination of the throughput race but as the standard that made latency a first-class, engineerable quantity in unlicensed spectrum.
