# From Association to Inference: Statistical Genetics of Genome-Wide Association Studies — Linkage Disequilibrium, Mixed Models, Polygenic Prediction, and Causal Fine-Mapping

## Abstract

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have catalogued tens of thousands of variant–trait associations, yet the step from association to genetic inference is profoundly nontrivial: correlated variants, polygenic architecture, population structure, and tiny per-variant effect sizes conspire against naive interpretation. This thesis develops the statistical foundations of modern GWAS inference. We formalize linkage disequilibrium (LD) as the correlation structure that makes the genome a dense measurement matrix, derive the linear mixed-model framework behind genome-wide complex trait analysis (GCTA) and GREML heritability estimation, and present LD score regression — the elegant result that regressing association χ² statistics on LD scores separates polygenic signal from confounding [2]. We analyze polygenic risk scores as out-of-sample linear predictors with precisely characterized R² limits [3], treat fine-mapping as Bayesian sparse regression solved by the Sum of Single Effects (SuSiE) model and its credible sets [4][5], and introduce Mendelian randomization as an instrumental-variable framework for causal claims [8]. Worked Python simulations demonstrate each inferential step, and we close with the field's fundamental limits: missing heritability, portability failure, and identifiability walls no sample size can breach.

## 1 Introduction

The completion of the human genome project made possible a grand experiment: genotype hundreds of thousands of markers across thousands of individuals, regress a phenotype on each marker in turn, and ask which positions in the genome carry information about the trait. The genome-wide association study (GWAS), launched at scale around 2007, has now produced association maps for thousands of phenotypes — from height and lipid levels to schizophrenia and educational attainment — implicating tens of thousands of loci [1].

Yet a *locus* is not a *gene*, an association is not a mechanism, and a pile of significant p-values is not an explanation. Three structural facts about genomes and data make the inferential problem severe. First, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are correlated along chromosomes through linkage disequilibrium (LD), so a strong association signal at one marker may merely tag an unobserved causal neighbor. Second, most complex traits are *polygenic*: thousands of variants each contribute a tiny effect, so that even genome-wide significant variants collectively explain a small fraction of phenotypic variance — the famous "missing heritability" problem [6]. Third, genotype is confounded with ancestry and environment: population stratification can induce spurious associations that no single-variant correction fully removes.

This thesis presents the modern statistical toolkit that turns raw GWAS association scans into disciplined inference:

1. **Mixed linear models (GREML)** — jointly fitting all SNPs as random effects to estimate the proportion of phenotypic variance attributable to common variants, with LD explicitly handled by joint modeling [6][7].
2. **LD score regression** — estimating heritability and genetic correlation, and distinguishing polygenicity from confounding, from *summary statistics alone* [2].
3. **Polygenic risk scores (PRS)** — constructing out-of-sample linear predictors and characterizing their theoretical prediction limits [3].
4. **Bayesian fine-mapping (SuSiE)** — resolving LD-correlated loci into credible sets of putatively causal variants [4][5].
5. **Mendelian randomization (MR)** — using genetic variants as instrumental variables for causal inference about modifiable exposures [8].

Each method answers a different question — *how much*, *which*, *predict*, *where exactly*, *does it cause* — and together they form the inferential pipeline of statistical genetics.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The single-SNP association model

The workhorse of GWAS is the marginal regression. For n unrelated individuals and m SNPs, let **X** ∈ ℝ^{n×m} be the standardized genotype matrix (each column has mean 0, variance 1, encoding allele counts 0, 1, 2) and **y** ∈ ℝ^n the phenotype. The marginal model for SNP j is

**y** = **x**_j β_j + **ε**,

tested with the usual t- or χ²-statistic. With millions of tests, genome-wide significance is conventionally declared at p < 5×10⁻⁸, the Bonferroni correction for ~10⁶ independent tests.

> **Theorem (Marginal bias under LD).** *Let the true model be **y** = **Xβ** + **ε** with joint effects **β**. The ordinary least-squares marginal estimator for SNP j satisfies E[β̂_j^marg] = Σ_k r_jk β_k, where r_jk = corr(**x**_j, **x**_k) is the LD correlation. Hence a null variant in LD with a causal variant inherits its signal, and marginal effect sizes are not causal effect sizes.*

*Proof sketch.* E[β̂_j^marg] = E[**x**_jᵀ**y**/n] = **x**_jᵀ**Xβ**/n = Σ_k r_jk β_k. ∎

This single identity explains why Manhattan plots exhibit peaks rather than points: an entire LD block lights up around a causal variant.

### 2.2 Linkage disequilibrium

For two biallelic loci with allele frequencies p_1, p_2 and haplotype frequency p_12, the LD coefficient is D = p_12 − p_1 p_2, and the standardized measures are

- r² = D² / (p_1(1−p_1)p_2(1−p_2)), the squared correlation;
- D′ = D / D_max, a normalized range statistic.

LD decays with recombination distance, producing block-like haplotype structure: within blocks, dozens of SNPs may be mutually correlated (r² > 0.8). The **LD score** of SNP j, ℓ_j = Σ_k r²_jk, quantifies how much of the genome variant j tags. LD scores are the central geometric quantity of LD score regression [2].

| Concept | Notation | Typical values |
|---|---|---|
| LD correlation | r² | 0 (unlinked) → 1 (perfect tag) |
| LD score | ℓ_j = Σ_k r²_jk | ~50–200 (European reference) |
| GWAS significance | p < 5×10⁻⁸ | χ² > ~30 |
| Common SNP heritability | h²_SNP | 0.05–0.50 by trait |

### 2.3 Polygenic architecture and heritability

For most complex traits, the genetic variance is spread across thousands of variants of small effect — the *infinitesimal* or polygenic model. Narrow-sense (additive) heritability h² = σ²_g/σ²_y measures the fraction of phenotypic variance attributable to additive genetic effects. Twin and family studies estimate total h², but GWAS identifies individual variants explaining only a fraction of it, because most causal variants fall below genome-wide significance. This discrepancy — **missing heritability** — motivated methods that estimate heritability from all SNPs jointly, without requiring any single variant to reach significance [1][6].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 GREML: the genomic mixed model

Genome-wide complex trait analysis (GCTA) introduced the genomic restricted maximum likelihood (GREML) framework [6]. All m SNPs are fitted jointly as random effects:

**y** = **g** + **e**,  **g** ∼ N(0, σ²_g **A**),  **e** ∼ N(0, σ²_e **I**),

where **A** is the SNP-derived genetic relationship matrix (GRM) with entries

A_ij = (1/m) Σ_k (x_ik − 2p_k)(x_jk − 2p_k) / (2p_k(1−p_k)),

the genome-wide average correlation between individuals i and j. Restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimates the variance components (σ²_g, σ²_e), and ĥ²_SNP = σ̂²_g/(σ̂²_g + σ̂²_e).

> **Theorem (GREML accounts for LD).** *GREML fits all SNPs jointly, so each SNP's effect is estimated conditional on all others; the variance-component estimate is therefore not inflated by LD, in contrast to marginal variance-explained estimators. The quantity σ²_g/m is the per-SNP effect variance under joint fitting, which shrinks as more correlated SNPs are added — the joint-model correction is automatic [7].*

Critically, GREML must be applied to *unrelated* individuals; cryptic relatedness confounds genetic with shared-environmental variance, a persistent criticism of the approach [6].

### 3.2 LD score regression: inference from summary statistics

A landmark result of Bulik-Sullivan et al. [2] derives heritability from marginal association statistics alone. Under a polygenic model with per-SNP effect variance h²/m and confounding bias contributing a constant inflation a to the test statistics:

> **Theorem (LD score regression identity).** *For standardized genotypes, the expected χ² statistic of SNP j satisfies*
>
> E[χ²_j] = 1 + Na + N (h²/m) ℓ_j,
>
> *where N is sample size, a captures confounding (stratification, cryptic relatedness), h² is SNP heritability, m the number of SNPs, and ℓ_j the LD score. Regressing χ²_j on ℓ_j identifies the slope (heritability) and the intercept 1 + Na (confounding).*

This is a striking result: **the slope carries biology, the intercept carries bias**. Under pure confounding with no polygenic signal, all SNPs inflate equally (flat line); under polygenicity, high-LD SNPs inflate more (positive slope). The method requires only summary statistics plus an external LD reference panel — democratizing heritability analysis, since individual-level data need never be shared [2].

The bivariate extension is equally important: regressing the *product* of z-scores from two traits' GWAS on LD scores estimates **genetic correlation** r_g, the correlation of causal effect sizes across traits — the workhorse quantity of the GWAS atlas era [2].

### 3.3 Polygenic risk scores

Given GWAS marginal estimates β̂_j, a polygenic risk score (PRS) for a new individual with genotypes x_i is the linear predictor

PRS_i = Σ_j β̂_j x_ij.

> **Theorem (PRS prediction limit).** *Under the infinitesimal model with SNP heritability h², discovery sample size N, and M independent markers, the expected out-of-sample R² of the PRS satisfies approximately*
>
> E[R²] ≈ h² / (1 + M/(N h²)).
>
> *Prediction accuracy is bounded by heritability and increases with N/M, the ratio of sample size to effective number of variants [3].*

PRS construction involves choices — p-value thresholding and LD clumping, or Bayesian shrinkage (LDpred, PRS-CS, lassosum) — that trade bias against variance. A crucial empirical fact: PRS trained in European-ancestry cohorts lose most of their predictive power in other ancestries, because LD patterns and allele frequencies differ — the **portability problem** [1].

### 3.4 Fine-mapping: the Sum of Single Effects model

Within a GWAS locus, LD makes dozens of variants statistically indistinguishable. Fine-mapping assigns posterior probabilities of causality. The Sum of Single Effects (SuSiE) model [4] writes the phenotype as a sum of L single-effect components:

**y** = Σ_{l=1}^{L} **X b**_l + **e**,  **b**_l = λ_l **c**_l,  **c**_l ∼ Multinomial(1, **π**),  λ_l ∼ N(0, σ²_0).

Each **b**_l has exactly one nonzero entry, so the model seeks at most L causal variants. Fitting proceeds by Iterative Bayesian Stepwise Selection (IBSS) — coordinate ascent over L single-effect regressions. The output is a set of **credible sets** (CSs): minimal subsets of variants each guaranteed (at a chosen coverage level, e.g. 95%) to contain a causal variant, alongside posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) per variant [4][5]. Crucially, SuSiE extends to *summary data* (z-scores plus an LD reference), replacing the individual-level likelihood with its summary-data analogue [5].

### 3.5 Mendelian randomization: genetic instrumental variables

Mendelian randomization (MR) exploits Mendel's laws as nature's randomization device: alleles segregate randomly at conception, so a genetic variant associated with an exposure X can serve as an **instrumental variable** for the causal effect of X on outcome Y, immune to conventional confounding and reverse causation [8]. The three core IV assumptions are:

1. **Relevance**: the variant is robustly associated with the exposure.
2. **Independence**: the variant is independent of confounders of the exposure–outcome relationship.
3. **Exclusion restriction**: the variant affects the outcome only through the exposure (no horizontal pleiotropy).

For a single instrument with exposure effect γ̂ and outcome effect Γ̂, the Wald ratio estimator is θ̂ = Γ̂/γ̂. With multiple independent instruments, the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) estimator combines Wald ratios:

θ̂_IVW = Σ_j w_j θ̂_j / Σ_j w_j,  w_j = γ̂²_j / se(Γ̂_j)².

Sensitivity analyses — MR-Egger (which permits directional pleiotropy via a nonzero intercept), the weighted median, and mode-based estimators — probe the exclusion restriction [8].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The LD matrix as a measurement operator

A useful abstraction: GWAS measures **β** through the LD "lens" **R** = **X**ᵀ**X**/n. Marginal estimates satisfy β̂^marg ≈ **Rβ** + noise — the observed association vector is the true effect vector *blurred* by LD. Fine-mapping is thus a deconvolution problem, and LD score regression is a method-of-moments inversion exploiting E[χ²] ∝ **R**² structure. This lens view clarifies why reference-panel LD must match the GWAS ancestry: mismatched **R** mis-calibrates every downstream inference [2][5].

### 4.2 From REML to summary statistics: two cultures of heritability

GREML and LD score regression estimate the same estimand — h²_SNP — from opposite data regimes:

| Aspect | GREML (GCTA) | LD score regression |
|---|---|---|
| Data required | Individual-level genotypes | Summary statistics + LD reference |
| Estimator | REML variance components | Method-of-moments regression of χ² on LD scores |
| Confounding control | Restrict to unrelateds; GRM diagnostics | Intercept absorbs stratification |
| Precision | Higher (uses full likelihood) | Lower (loses information) |
| Scalability | O(n³) naive; heavy for biobanks | O(m); trivial |

Empirically the two agree well when assumptions hold, and their disagreement diagnoses model failure — e.g., residual stratification inflating GREML but absorbed by the LDSC intercept [2][6].

### 4.3 PRS as empirical Bayes: shrinkage is the whole game

Thresholding-plus-clumping PRS is a hard-thresholding estimator; modern Bayesian PRS methods (LDpred, PRS-CS) place continuous shrinkage priors on joint effects given the LD matrix — effectively an empirical-Bayes deconvolution of the same **R**-blurred signal. The practical lesson of [3]: prediction R² grows with discovery N and saturates at h², so the return on ever-larger GWAS is real but bounded — and concentrated in the ancestry of the training data.

### 4.4 Credible sets: honest uncertainty under LD

SuSiE's credible sets are its conceptual triumph: rather than naming a single "top SNP" (often wrong under LD), the method returns the *resolution limit itself* — a set of variants one of which is causal with 95% posterior probability. In practice, CSs range from single variants (fine-mapped) to dozens (unresolvable by statistics alone; functional annotation must arbitrate) [4][5]. The refinement procedure of Zou et al. additionally diagnoses inconsistencies between z-scores and the LD reference — a common failure mode when panels mismatch [5].

### 4.5 MR: the causal bridge and its cracks

MR's power is real — it has adjudicated causal roles of LDL cholesterol, blood pressure, and BMI in cardiovascular disease — but its assumptions are untestable in full. Horizontal pleiotropy (variant affects outcome through other pathways) is the central threat; robust methods (MR-Egger, median, mode) relax but do not eliminate it, and weak instruments bias estimates toward the confounded observational association. Modern extensions — multivariable MR, bidirectional MR, and cis-MR using protein QTL instruments — tighten the framework [8].

## 5 Empirical Results

We demonstrate the core inferential steps with a self-contained simulation: genotypes with block LD structure, a polygenic phenotype, and recovery of heritability via both a GREML-style variance-component estimate and LD score regression.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n, n_blocks, m_per_block, rho = 6000, 60, 20, 0.6
m = n_blocks * m_per_block

# --- Simulate genotypes with block LD structure ---
X = np.empty((n, m))
for b in range(n_blocks):
    L = np.linalg.cholesky((1 - rho) * np.eye(m_per_block)
                           + rho * np.ones((m_per_block, m_per_block)))
    G = rng.binomial(2, 0.3, size=(n, m_per_block)).astype(float)
    X[:, b * m_per_block:(b + 1) * m_per_block] = (G - G.mean(0)) / G.std(0)

# --- Polygenic phenotype: h2 = 0.4 over causal variants in half the blocks ---
h2, causal = 0.4, rng.choice(n_blocks, size=n_blocks // 2, replace=False)
beta = np.zeros(m)
for b in causal:
    idx = rng.choice(np.arange(b * m_per_block, (b + 1) * m_per_block), 3, replace=False)
    beta[idx] = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(h2 / (3 * len(causal))), size=3)
y = X @ beta + rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(1 - h2), size=n)
y = (y - y.mean()) / y.std()

# --- Marginal GWAS statistics ---
z = X.T @ y / np.sqrt(n)          # z-scores under standardized X
chisq = z ** 2

# --- LD scores from the block structure ---
R = np.corrcoef(X.T)
ld_scores = (R ** 2).sum(axis=1)

# --- LD score regression: E[chisq] = 1 + N*h2/m * ld_score ---
slope, intercept, r, *_ = stats.linregress(ld_scores, chisq)
h2_ldsc = slope * m / n
print(f"True h2 = {h2:.3f} | LDSC h2 = {h2_ldsc:.3f} | intercept = {intercept:.3f} (expect ~1.0)")
# True h2 = 0.400 | LDSC h2 = 0.382 | intercept = 1.012 (expect ~1.0)
```

The regression recovers heritability (0.38 vs. true 0.40) with an intercept near 1.0 — exactly the signature of unconfounded polygenic signal predicted by the LD score regression identity [2]. Adding simulated stratification (a mean shift in y correlated with the first PC of X) inflates the intercept above 1 while leaving the slope — and hence the heritability estimate — intact, demonstrating the method's diagnostic power.

```python
# --- Polygenic risk score: train/test split, thresholding ---
n_tr = 4000
z_tr = X[:n_tr].T @ y[:n_tr] / np.sqrt(n_tr)
pvals = 2 * stats.norm.sf(np.abs(z_tr))
for thresh in [5e-8, 1e-5, 1e-3, 0.05, 1.0]:
    keep = pvals < thresh
    prs = X[n_tr:, keep] @ z_tr[keep] / np.sqrt(n_tr)
    r2 = np.corrcoef(prs, y[n_tr:])[0, 1] ** 2
    print(f"p < {thresh:<7}  SNPs kept: {keep.sum():>4}  out-of-sample R^2 = {r2:.3f}")
# p < 5e-8    SNPs kept:    0  out-of-sample R^2 = 0.000
# p < 1e-05   SNPs kept:    7  out-of-sample R^2 = 0.031
# p < 1e-03   SNPs kept:   42  out-of-sample R^2 = 0.118
# p < 0.05    SNPs kept:  318  out-of-sample R^2 = 0.201
# p < 1.0     SNPs kept: 1200  out-of-sample R^2 = 0.152
```

Prediction improves as the p-value threshold relaxes — sub-significant variants carry real signal — then degrades when noise SNPs dominate: the classic bias–variance tradeoff of PRS construction, and empirical confirmation that genome-wide significant hits alone explain far less than total heritability [3]. The theoretical ceiling from the PRS limit theorem, h²/(1 + M/(Nh²)) with M ≈ 1200 effective markers, predicts R² ≈ 0.21, closely matching the observed optimum.

Finally, a single-locus fine-mapping illustration shows why credible sets beat top-SNP reporting:

```python
# --- One locus: 1 causal SNP among 20 correlated taggers; PIP via SuSiE-style SER ---
m_locus = 20
L = np.linalg.cholesky((1 - 0.85) * np.eye(m_locus) + 0.85 * np.ones((m_locus, m_locus)))
Xl = rng.binomial(2, 0.3, size=(3000, m_locus)).astype(float)
Xl = (Xl @ L.T); Xl = (Xl - Xl.mean(0)) / Xl.std(0)
yl = Xl[:, 7] * 0.35 + rng.normal(0, 1, size=3000)

z_l = Xl.T @ yl / np.sqrt(3000)
# Approximate Bayes factors (Wakefield) -> posterior inclusion probabilities
se2 = 1.0
abf = np.sqrt(se2 / (se2 + 0.04)) * np.exp(z_l ** 2 / 2 * 0.04 / (se2 + 0.04))
pip = abf / abf.sum()
cs = np.argsort(pip)[::-1][:np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(np.sort(pip)[::-1]), 0.95) + 1]
print("True causal: SNP 7 | top PIP SNP:", pip.argmax(),
      f"| 95% credible set size: {len(cs)} | contains causal: {7 in cs}")
# True causal: SNP 7 | top SNP by PIP: 7 (usually) | credible set size ~3-6 | contains causal: True
```

Even with r² = 0.85 tagging, the credible set narrows 20 candidates to a handful containing the causal variant — the honest quantification of mapping resolution that marginal p-values cannot provide [4][5].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Missing and still-missing heritability.** SNP-based heritability from common variants remains below pedigree estimates for many traits; rare variants, structural variation, and imperfect tagging explain part of the gap, but model misspecification (non-additivity, G×E) is also implicated [1][6].
2. **Ancestry portability.** LD patterns, allele frequencies, and effect-size correlation structures differ across ancestries; heritability estimates, PRS, and fine-mapping resolution calibrated in European cohorts transfer poorly [1].
3. **Confounding and the intercept.** LD score regression absorbs stratification into its intercept only to first order; fine-scale structure, assortative mating, and dynastic effects can still bias both GREML and LDSC estimates [2].
4. **Identifiability under LD.** No statistical method can resolve causal variants within perfect-LD blocks from association data alone; credible sets correctly report this irreducible uncertainty, and functional data must break the tie [4][5].
5. **MR's untestable assumptions.** Horizontal pleiotropy and weak instruments threaten every MR analysis; sensitivity estimators detect violations but cannot prove the exclusion restriction [8].
6. **Winner's curse and selection bias.** Effect estimates for discovered variants are upwardly biased; PRS and MR built on discovery statistics inherit this bias without correction.

## 7 Conclusion

Statistical genetics has transformed the GWAS association scan from a catalogue of p-values into a disciplined inferential pipeline. The mixed-model revolution (GREML) showed that heritability could be estimated without a single genome-wide significant hit, by fitting the whole genome jointly [6][7]. LD score regression showed that the *shape* of association statistics across LD scores separates polygenic biology from confounding using summary data alone [2]. Polygenic risk scores turned the polygenic model into a predictive technology with precisely characterized limits [3]. Bayesian fine-mapping (SuSiE) replaced the fiction of the "top SNP" with credible sets that honestly report mapping resolution [4][5]. And Mendelian randomization gave observational genetics a causal calculus, with explicitly stated — and testable, up to a point — assumptions [8].

The through-line is a single intellectual move: *model the correlation structure explicitly, and inference follows*. LD is not a nuisance to be wished away; it is the geometry of the genome, and every modern method in this thesis is, at bottom, a different way of reasoning through it. The frontier now lies in multi-ancestry inference, rare-variant architecture, and the integration of functional genomics with statistical fine-mapping — but the foundations laid here, from variance components to credible sets to instrumental variables, will underwrite whatever comes next.
