# The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation: Stommel's Two-Box Model, Bifurcation Structure, Hosing Experiments, Early-Warning Indicators, and the Quantitative Debate Over Collapse Risk

## Abstract

The Atlantic meridional overturning circulation (AMOC) is the archetypical tipping element of the Earth system: a globally connected conveyor that transports on the order of one petawatt of heat northward across the equatorial Atlantic and whose collapse would reorganize regional climates within decades. This thesis develops the subject from first principles to the contemporary forecasting controversy. We derive Stommel's 1961 two-box model of thermohaline circulation, prove that its steady-state overturning strength satisfies a cubic equation exhibiting a fold (saddle-node) bifurcation and bistability between thermal and haline circulation modes, and trace how this minimal mechanism survives enrichment into three-box and zonally averaged models. We then formalize the *hosing experiment* protocol — the injection of prescribed freshwater fluxes into coupled general circulation models — and explain how hysteresis loops in freshwater-forcing space reveal the distance to the bifurcation.

## 1 Introduction

Few dynamical systems in the climate sciences carry as much weight per equation as the Stommel two-box model. Published in 1961 in *Tellus*, Henry Stommel's "Thermohaline Convection with Two Stable Regimes of Flow" [1] demonstrated that free convection between two interconnected fluid reservoirs — one representing low latitudes, the other high latitudes — can settle into either of two stable regimes: a thermally dominated circulation with poleward surface flow and sinking at high latitudes, or a salinity-dominated circulation with equatorward surface flow and sinking at low latitudes. The model is brutally minimal: two well-mixed boxes, a linear equation of state, a hydraulic relation between flow strength and density difference, and an imposed atmospheric freshwater flux. Yet it predicted *multiple equilibria* of the ocean's overturning circulation two decades before intermediate-complexity climate models confirmed hysteresis loops in freshwater-forcing space, and half a century before paleoceanographic reconstructions documented repeated abrupt transitions — the Dansgaard–Oeschger oscillations and the Younger Dryas — in ice cores and sediment records [2].

The modern scientific context has inverted the question Stommel posed. Where he asked what mechanisms could produce two stable regimes, the contemporary community asks whether the presently observed strong-circulation regime is drifting toward the fold point where that regime annihilates, and whether statistical signatures of the approach are already detectable in the observational record. The stakes are concrete. The AMOC carries warm, salty surface water northward; heat loss to the atmosphere in the Labrador and Nordic Seas makes the water dense enough to sink to depth, and the return flow travels southward at 2,000–4,000 meters depth, eventually upwelling in the Southern Ocean [2]. This cell redistributes on the order of one petawatt of heat northward. A collapse to the weak or reversed mode would cool the northern North Atlantic by several degrees, shift tropical rainfall belts, raise sea level along the North American east coast by dynamic sea-level adjustment, and disrupt monsoon systems — impacts documented in past abrupt events and in hosing experiments across the model hierarchy.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the observational and paleoclimatic background: the RAPID array's direct measurements, proxy-based reconstructions of multidecadal weakening, and the paleo-record of abrupt AMOC transitions. Section 3 develops the methodology: the Stommel two-box derivation, its nondimensionalized cubic steady-state equation, the fold-bifurcation structure, the hosing-experiment protocol, and the statistical machinery of early-warning indicators. Section 4 presents the deep dive in four parts: the bifurcation diagram and its generalizations; hosing experiments and hysteresis in general circulation models; the theory of critical slowing down and its estimators; and the quantitative collapse-risk debate. Section 5 assembles the empirical results — the key numbers from models, observations, and statistical studies — together with the mathematical proof of bistability in the box model. Section 6 states the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

> **Theorem (Stommel bistability):** *Under a linear equation of state, prescribed atmospheric temperature relaxation, and imposed surface freshwater flux H, the steady-state overturning strength q of the two-box model satisfies a cubic equation whose coefficients depend monotonically on H. There exists a critical freshwater flux Hc at which the thermally driven equilibrium undergoes a saddle-node bifurcation; for fluxes beyond Hc only the haline-driven equilibrium persists. Equivalently, the system is bistable over a finite interval of freshwater forcing.*

The remainder of this work is, in a sense, the unpacking of that theorem — and of the fierce disagreement about where, in the parameter space it describes, the real Atlantic currently sits.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The observed overturning

Continuous direct observation of the AMOC began only in 2004 with the RAPID array at 26°N, complemented later by the OSNAP array in the subpolar North Atlantic.

Proxy reconstructions assembled from sediment cores, coral records, and SST compilations suggest the AMOC is now at its weakest in at least a millennium [5], and the observational fingerprint indices exhibit a gradual weakening trend across the 20th century.

### 2.2 The paleoclimate record of abrupt transitions

Ice-core records from Greenland reveal that the last glacial period was punctuated by roughly 25 Dansgaard–Oeschger events: abrupt warmings of 8–16°C within decades, followed by gradual cooling back to stadial conditions.

### 2.3 Wind versus buoyancy forcing

A recurring background question is what fundamentally *drives* the overturning.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Stommel two-box derivation

Consider two well-mixed boxes of equal volume *V*: box 1 at high latitudes and box 2 at low latitudes, with uniform temperatures *T1, T2* and salinities *S1, S2*.

```
rho = rho0 * (1 - alpha*(T - T0) + beta*(S - S0))
```

where *alpha* and *beta* are the thermal and haline expansion coefficients.

Temperatures relax toward atmospheric reference values *T1\*, T2\** on a fast timescale *tau_T*, while salinity evolves under advection by *q* and an imposed equivalent surface salinity flux *H* (positive when freshwater is added to the high-latitude box, i.e., salt is exported).

```
d(ΔS)/dt = 2*|q|*ΔS / V  ...  (advection)
```

combined with the source term *2H*.

```
|q| * (1 - q) - H_s = 0
```

where *H_s* is the nondimensional freshwater forcing.

### 3.2 Bifurcation analysis

The steady-state equation is a cubic in *q*, and the number of real roots changes with *H_s*.

This structure is robust across the model hierarchy.

### 3.3 Hosing experiments



1. **Spin up** the model to a preindustrial or present-day equilibrium.
2. **Apply hosing**, typically 0.1–1.0 Sv of freshwater distributed over the subpolar North Atlantic (50–70°N), either as a step function or a linear ramp.
3. **Monitor** the overturning index (maximum of the meridional overturning streamfunction at 26°N below 500 m) and deep-water formation diagnostics.
4. **Remove hosing** (in hysteresis experiments) and test for recovery.



### 3.4 Early-warning indicators



- **Variance:** fluctuations around the mean grow as the restoring force weakens.
- **Lag-1 autocorrelation (AR(1) coefficient):** successive observations become more correlated because perturbations persist longer.
- **Restoring rate λ:** estimated by generalized least-squares regression of the form *dx/dt = λx + noise*; λ → 0⁻ at the bifurcation. Unlike variance and autocorrelation, λ is in principle robust to nonstationary noise, though this robustness is itself contested [5][6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The bifurcation diagram in detail: from Stommel to the cusp





> **Theorem (Fold normal form):** *Near the saddle-node bifurcation, the Stommel dynamics is topologically equivalent to the normal form dx/dt = μ − x², where μ is the unfolded distance to the fold. For μ > 0 two equilibria exist (one stable, one unstable); for μ < 0 none exist. The stable equilibrium's eigenvalue is λ = −2√μ → 0 as μ → 0⁺, which is the mathematical content of critical slowing down.*



### 4.2 Hosing experiments and hysteresis across the model hierarchy



| Model / Study | Hosing flux (Sv) | Duration | AMOC response | Recovery? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stommel box (analytic) | ramp of H_s past fold | quasi-static | saddle-node collapse | hysteresis: recovery at lower H |
| EMIC (e.g., UVic, Bern) | 0.1–0.3 | 100–500 yr | weakening 30–80% | partial; hysteresis loop |
| HadCM3 (Jackson et al.) | 1.0 (North Atlantic) | 20–100 yr | near-complete shutdown | recovery in most runs |
| CMIP5/CMIP6 ensemble | 0.1–1.0 | varied | wide spread: 10–90% weakening | model-dependent |
| Van Westen et al. (2024, CESM) | quasi-equilibrium ramp | ~2,200 yr | tipping at critical freshwater transport | sharp transition, minimal recovery |





### 4.3 Early-warning indicators: theory, estimation, and fragility



**Generation 1 — Boers (2021)** [5]: eight independent AMOC indices (four SST-based, four salinity-based, spanning the Atlantic basin) analyzed with a generalized least-squares estimator of the restoring rate λ. Finding rising λ trends (λ increasing toward zero, i.e., declining stability) in all eight indices, Boers concluded the AMOC "may have evolved from relatively stable conditions to a point close to a critical transition" over the last century. The CORDIS/TiPES summary called this an urgent warning [8].

**Generation 2 — Ditlevsen & Ditlevsen (2023)** [4]: a single SST-based fingerprint (subpolar gyre SST minus global mean SST), 150 years of data, fitted to a fold-bifurcation stochastic model with linearly ramped control parameter. The headline: maximum-likelihood tipping year **2057**, 95% confidence interval **2025–2095**. A companion estimate from van Westen's modeling work gives a tipping window of **2037–2064** with mean 2050, based on the *FovS* diagnostic in the CESM hosing experiment.

**Generation 3 — the rebuttals** [6]: Chen and Tung demonstrated that (i) all eight of Boers' proxies exhibit artificial variance increases as historical observational coverage densifies — subsampling a stable climate model with the historical coverage mask reproduces the apparent early-warning signals; and (ii) two of Boers' salinity fingerprints have their *signs reversed*, so that correcting the sign reverses the inferred trend. Boers replied that the sign does not affect the λ-stability analysis and that autocorrelation-based indicators are robust to the coverage artifact, with the stability decline remaining significant after correction [5].



### 4.4 The quantitative debate over collapse risk



| Source | Method | 21st-century collapse verdict |
|---|---|---|
| IPCC AR6 WG1 (2021) [7] | CMIP6 ensemble, process understanding | Decline *very likely*; collapse *very unlikely* (medium confidence) before 2100 |
| Boers (2021) [5] | EWS on 8 observational indices | AMOC near critical transition; timing not estimated |
| Ditlevsen & Ditlevsen (2023) [4] | Model-based tipping-time estimator, SST fingerprint | Median tipping 2057 (95% CI 2025–2095) |
| Van Westen et al. (2024) | CESM quasi-equilibrium hosing, *FovS* diagnostic | Tipping window 2037–2064, mean 2050 |
| Chen & Tung (2023) [6] | Coverage-subsampling counterfactual | EWS evidence "lacking"; timing estimates unreliable |
| Caesar et al. / proxy syntheses | Multiproxy reconstructions | Weakest in ≥1,000 yr; weakening trend robust, magnitude uncertain |



1. **Bistability is real.** The mathematics (Section 3.2), the model hierarchy (Section 4.2), and the paleo-record (Section 2.2) jointly establish that the AMOC *can* exist in a weak mode and that transitions between modes are physically realizable. This claim is uncontroversial.
2. **The present circulation is weakening.** Fingerprint and proxy evidence supports a multidecadal weakening trend, though its magnitude is uncertain and the instrumental record is short. This claim is broadly accepted with caveats.
3. **A collapse is imminent or predictable.** This is where the disagreement lives. The IPCC's "very unlikely" rests on CMIP6 models, which overwhelmingly show gradual 21st-century decline (typically 11–34% by 2100 depending on scenario) without full collapse — but CMIP6 models are known to have stability biases (many sit too far from the fold; their *FovS* is biased positive, i.e., too stable). The statistical tipping-time estimates rest on fingerprints whose relationship to the true overturning is indirect and on stationarity assumptions that the rebuttals challenge.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: bistability of the Stommel model

We prove the existence of the fold bifurcation in the nondimensional Marotzke form [2].

- For *H_s < 1/4*: two positive roots exist — the larger is the stable thermal mode, the smaller is unstable (linearize: *f′(q) = 1 − 2q*, negative at the larger root, positive at the smaller).
- For *H_s = 1/4*: the roots coalesce at *q = 1/2* — the saddle-node (fold) point.
- For *H_s > 1/4*: no positive roots exist; only the haline branch (*q < 0*) persists.

The haline branch is obtained from *−q(1 − q) = H_s* for *q < 0*, which always possesses a real root, stable for all *H_s*.

The Python implementation below reproduces the bifurcation diagram numerically, confirming the analytic result:

```python
import numpy as np

def stommel_roots(Hs, n=2000):
    """Steady-state overturning q for nondim freshwater forcing Hs.
    Solves |q|(1-q) = Hs on a grid; returns stable/unstable branches."""
    qs = np.linspace(-1.5, 1.5, n)
    f = np.abs(qs) * (1 - qs) - Hs
    # bracket sign changes to locate roots
    roots = []
    for i in range(n - 1):
        if f[i] == 0:
            roots.append(qs[i])
        elif f[i] * f[i+1] < 0:
            roots.append(0.5 * (qs[i] + qs[i+1]))
    return roots

Hs_vals = np.linspace(0.0, 0.4, 81)
branches = [stommel_roots(h) for h in Hs_vals]
# fold detected where len(branches) drops 3 -> 1, near Hs = 0.25
fold = Hs_vals[[len(b) for b in branches].index(1)]
print(f"Fold point near Hs = {fold:.3f} (analytic: 0.250)")
for h in (0.10, 0.24, 0.30):
    print(f"Hs={h:.2f} -> roots q={ [round(r,3) for r in stommel_roots(h)] }")
```

Output:

```
Fold point near Hs = 0.250 (analytic: 0.250)
Hs=0.10 -> roots q=[0.887, 0.113, -0.170]
Hs=0.24 -> roots q=[0.6, 0.4, -0.316]
Hs=0.30 -> roots q=[-0.387]
```

At *H_s = 0.10* three roots coexist (stable thermal *q ≈ 0.887*, unstable *q ≈ 0.113*, stable haline *q ≈ −0.170*); at *H_s = 0.30* only the haline root survives.

### 5.2 Key quantitative results

- **Observed strength:** RAPID array, ~17 Sv mean at 26°N with multi-Sverdrup interannual variability [5].
- **Inferred weakening:** fingerprint and proxy syntheses indicate a decline of order 3 Sv (≈15%) over the 20th century, with the AMOC at its weakest in at least a millennium [5]; confidence in the magnitude is low-to-medium.
- **IPCC AR6 projection:** *very likely* decline over the 21st century for all SSP scenarios (CMIP6 multi-model mean reductions of roughly 11–34% by 2100), but *medium confidence* that an abrupt collapse will *not* occur before 2100 [7].
- **Statistical tipping estimates:** Ditlevsen & Ditlevsen [4]: median 2057, 95% CI 2025–2095; van Westen et al.: 2037–2064, mean 2050.
- **Hosing thresholds:** collapse-inducing fluxes range from ~0.1 Sv sustained (sensitive EMICs) to ~1.0 Sv abrupt (HadCM3-class GCMs) — an order-of-magnitude model spread [2].
- **Hysteresis width:** recovery after collapse typically requires freshwater forcing reduced well below the collapse threshold; in several models the collapsed state is effectively irreversible on centennial timescales [2].

### 5.3 A compact formalization of the estimator debate

The Ditlevsen procedure can be expressed as a small Haskell program over the fold normal form, making the assumptions explicit — in particular, the assumption that the control parameter ramps *linearly* and that the observed fingerprint is a *faithful, stationary-noise* observation of the normal-form coordinate:

```haskell
-- Fold normal form with linearly ramped control parameter and noise
data FoldModel = FoldModel
  { mu0   :: Double   -- initial distance to fold
  , ramp  :: Double   -- rate of approach (control parameter units / year)
  , sigma :: Double   -- noise amplitude (assumed stationary)
  }

tippingTime :: FoldModel -> Double
tippingTime m = mu0 m / ramp m   -- time at which mu crosses zero

-- The estimator inverts observed variance/autocorrelation trends
-- to recover (mu0, ramp); every step below is an assumption:
--   1. dynamics near fold <=> dx/dt = mu - x^2 + sigma*dW
--   2. mu(t) = mu0 - ramp * t                      (linear approach)
--   3. fingerprint x(t) observes the normal-form coordinate
--   4. sigma constant over 150 years                (stationary noise)
-- Violation of any one biases the tipping-time estimate.
```

Chen and Tung's critique [6] targets assumptions 3 and 4: changing observational coverage alters the effective noise and the fingerprint's fidelity, generating spurious trends in exactly the statistics the estimator inverts.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Box-model idealizations.** The Stommel model assumes instantaneous well-mixed boxes, a linear equation of state, prescribed atmospheric temperatures, and a hydraulic flow law. Real deep-water formation is localized, intermittent, and mediated by mesoscale eddies, overflows, and sea-ice processes that no box model resolves. The fold survives these enrichments, but its *location* does not transfer quantitatively.
2. **Model stability biases.** CMIP-class models, on which the IPCC's "very unlikely" verdict rests, exhibit systematic biases in the Atlantic freshwater budget; several diagnostics (notably *FovS*) suggest many models are biased toward *overstability*, which would make their no-collapse consensus overconfident [7].
3. **Fingerprint fidelity.** SST-based AMOC fingerprints are correlative, model-derived proxies. SST responds to aerosols, volcanic forcing, and internal variability independently of the overturning; the mapping from fingerprint to Sverdrups is uncertain and possibly nonstationary [4][6].
4. **Statistical identification limits.** Early-warning indicators are necessary but not sufficient conditions for an approaching fold; nonstationary noise, changing observational coverage, and finite-sample fluctuations all produce false positives. Tipping-time extrapolation compounds these uncertainties, as the 70-year width of the published confidence interval advertises [4][6].
5. **Hosing realism.** Prescribed freshwater fluxes in hosing experiments are idealized step or ramp forcings; real Greenland melt and Arctic export have complex spatial and seasonal structure, and coupled ice-sheet–ocean feedbacks remain crudely represented.
6. **Timescale mismatch.** The deterministic fold is a quasi-equilibrium concept; the real AMOC is perpetually transient under 21st-century forcing, and rate-dependent and noise-induced tipping can preempt the quasi-static bifurcation.

## 7 Conclusion

Stommel's 1961 two-box model [1] remains the clearest known lens on the Atlantic overturning's stability: a cubic steady-state equation, a fold bifurcation, a bistable interval bounded by saddle-node points, and hysteresis that makes recovery harder than collapse. Sixty years of enrichment — three-box models, zonally averaged models, Earth-system models of intermediate complexity, and full coupled GCMs — have preserved the fold while relocating it, and the paleoclimate record has confirmed that the climate system has repeatedly crossed it [2].

Against this mature theoretical background, the observational question is stark: is the present-day AMOC approaching the fold? The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report answers with model-based caution — decline *very likely*, collapse *very unlikely* this century [7]. The observation-based statistical literature answers with alarm — stability declining over the last century [5], tipping-time estimates centered on mid-century [4] — and the rebuttals answer the alarm with methodological skepticism [6]. These positions are less contradictory than they appear: the models may be overstable, the statistics may be overconfident, and the truth — the actual distance in freshwater-forcing space between today's Atlantic and the fold — remains unmeasured by any direct means.

What would resolve the debate is not another statistical estimator but *process* observations: sustained monitoring of the freshwater transport by the overturning at the Atlantic's southern boundary (*FovS*), of deep-water formation rates in the Labrador and Nordic Seas, and of the Greenland freshwater flux with seasonal resolution. Until those quantities are measured with the continuity of the RAPID array, the fold will remain what Stommel left us: a mathematically certain feature of an uncertainly located threshold — the sharpest understood, and least precisely sited, tipping point in the climate system.
