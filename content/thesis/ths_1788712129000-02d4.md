# Precision Genome Editing with Prime Editors: pegRNA Design, Reverse-Transcriptase Templating, PE2/PE3/PE7 Architectures, and Off-Target Profiling

## Abstract

Prime editing is a "search-and-replace" genome editing technology that directly writes new genetic information into a specified genomic locus using a catalytically impaired Cas9 nickase fused to an engineered reverse transcriptase, programmed by a prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA) that both specifies the target site and encodes the desired edit. Unlike homology-directed repair, it requires neither double-strand breaks nor donor DNA templates, and unlike base editors it can install all twelve classes of point mutation as well as targeted insertions, deletions, and combinations thereof. This thesis develops the molecular anatomy of the pegRNA—spacer, primer binding site (PBS), and reverse transcriptase template (RTT)—the reverse-transcriptase templating cycle and 3′ flap resolution chemistry, the architectural lineage from PE1 through PE2, PE3/PE3b, PEmax, and the La(1–194)-fused PE7, and the genome-wide off-target profiling toolkit (nDigenome-seq, TAPE-seq, PE-tag, GUIDE-seq). Synthesizing primary literature and design-tool practice, it argues that prime editing's requirement for three sequential hybridization events confers a specificity advantage that genome-wide sequencing has largely confirmed, while efficiency bottlenecks—pegRNA stability, mismatch repair, and flap equilibration—remain the central engineering frontier.

## 1 Introduction

The programmable genome editing field has long been divided between the precision of what is *possible* and the efficiency of what is *practical*. CRISPR-Cas9 nuclease editing generates double-strand breaks (DSBs) whose repair by non-homologous end joining or homology-directed repair (HDR) produces a statistical mixture of products: the desired allele, insertions and deletions at the cut site, and chromosomal rearrangements. Base editors avoided DSBs but were confined to transition mutations (C→T, A→G) within a narrow activity window, leaving the transversions, insertions, and deletions that underlie the majority of pathogenic human variants out of reach [1].

Prime editing, reported by Anzalone, Randolph, Davis, Sousa, Koblan, Levy, Chen, Wilson, Newby, Raguram, and Liu in *Nature* in 2019 [1], collapsed this dichotomy. The method fuses a *Streptococcus pyogenes* Cas9 H840A nickase to an engineered Moloney murine leukemia virus (M-MLV) reverse transcriptase (RT) and programs the fusion with an extended guide RNA—the pegRNA—that carries both the spacer that finds the target and a 3′ extension encoding the edit itself. In the original demonstration, more than 175 distinct edits were performed in human cells, encompassing all twelve point-mutation classes, targeted insertions, targeted deletions, and combinations, without DSBs or exogenous donor DNA [1]. The authors demonstrated correction of the primary genetic causes of sickle cell disease (an A→T transversion in *HBB*) and Tay–Sachs disease (a 4-bp deletion in *HEXA*), installation of a protective transversion in *PRNP*, and precise insertion of protein tags—and estimated that prime editing could in principle correct up to 89% of known pathogenic human genetic variants [1].

This thesis examines prime editing at four levels: (i) the design grammar of the pegRNA; (ii) the enzymology of reverse-transcriptase templating and flap resolution; (iii) the architectural lineage from PE1 through PE2, PE3/PE3b, PEmax, and the La(1–194)-fused PE7; and (iv) the specificity profile measured by genome-wide off-target assays—addressing not merely how prime editing works, but *why* it works as well as it does, *where* it fails, and *how* practitioners design around those failures.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The double-strand break problem

Conventional Cas9 editing relies on a DSB repaired by endogenous machinery. HDR is restricted to S/G2, competes with error-prone end joining, and requires a long-homology donor whose incorporation is inefficient in therapeutically relevant cells—post-mitotic neurons, quiescent stem cells—yielding product distributions dominated by indels [2].

### 2.2 Base editing: DSB-free but mutation-restricted

Base editors, also pioneered by the Liu laboratory, fused deaminases to nickase Cas9 and achieved DSB-free chemistry, but cytosine and adenine base editors are fundamentally restricted to the four transition mutations (C→T, G→A, A→G, T→C), operate within an activity window of a few nucleotides (risking bystander edits), and cannot create or remove insertions or deletions [1]. Roughly 30% of pathogenic variants are transversions or indels inaccessible to base editing, motivating a more general DSB-free writer.

### 2.3 The reverse transcription insight

The conceptual breakthrough was relocating the "template" from an exogenous DNA molecule into the guide RNA itself. The pegRNA's 3′ extension contains a primer binding site (PBS) that anneals to the nicked DNA strand and a reverse transcriptase template (RTT) encoding the desired edit. The fused RT then *copies the edit from RNA into DNA in situ*, generating a 3′ DNA flap bearing the edit that competes with the original 5′ flap for incorporation—hence "search-and-replace" [1].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis synthesizes the primary prime editing literature, structural and mechanistic studies, pegRNA design-tool documentation, and genome-wide specificity datasets. Design rules are drawn from high-throughput pegRNA evaluation studies and the PrimeDesign framework [3]; architectural comparisons from the original PE papers [1], the PEmax engineering study [4], and the PE7 discovery [5]; and specificity claims from unbiased genome-wide assays including nDigenome-seq, TAPE-seq, and PE-tag [6, 7]. Where quantitative design parameters are stated (PBS/RTT lengths, nick-to-edit distances), they represent consensus ranges from multiple independent studies rather than universal optima, since efficiency is strongly sequence-context dependent.

### The prime editing reaction cycle

Prime editing proceeds through five biochemically distinct steps:

1. **Target search and nicking.** The nCas9(H840A)–RT fusion binds the target specified by the pegRNA spacer and nicks the *edited* (PAM-containing) DNA strand, exposing a 3′ DNA end.
2. **PBS annealing.** The PBS portion of the pegRNA 3′ extension hybridizes to the nicked strand's 3′ end, positioning it as a primer.
3. **Reverse transcription.** The RT domain extends the DNA primer using the RTT as template, synthesizing a 3′ DNA flap containing the edit.
4. **Flap equilibration and excision.** The edited 3′ flap and the original 5′ flap compete for annealing; cellular endonucleases (notably FEN1) preferentially excise the 5′ flap, incorporating the edit into one strand.
5. **Mismatch repair resolution.** The resulting heteroduplex is resolved by endogenous DNA repair, and (in PE3-family systems) a second nick on the unedited strand biases repair toward the edited allele.

> **Theorem:** *Prime editing specificity is the product of three sequential hybridization filters.* An off-target edit requires (i) spacer–DNA hybridization sufficient for Cas9 engagement, (ii) PBS–DNA hybridization at the nicked locus, and (iii) productive reverse transcription from the RTT. Because filters (ii) and (iii) depend on the *extension* sequence rather than the spacer, conventional Cas9 off-target sites need not be prime editing off-target sites—and genome-wide data confirm substantially lower off-target editing than Cas9 nuclease at known Cas9 off-target loci [1, 7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 pegRNA Anatomy: Spacer, PBS, and Reverse Transcriptase Template

The pegRNA is the information carrier of the system and the principal design variable. Its 5′ region is a conventional sgRNA: a ~20-nt spacer specifying the target followed by the Cas9-binding scaffold. Its distinguishing feature is the 3′ extension:

- **Primer binding site (PBS), 8–16 nt (commonly ~13 nt).** The PBS is complementary to the genomic sequence immediately 3′ of the nick on the edited strand. It must be long enough to form a stable primer–template duplex at physiological temperature but short enough to avoid kinetic traps; excessive PBS length increases the probability of annealing to partially complementary off-target nicks.
- **Reverse transcriptase template (RTT), 10–20 nt (commonly 10–16 nt).** The RTT encodes the edit and the homology needed to anchor the flap. The edit should generally be positioned such that sufficient RTT homology exists on both sides, and the RTT should disrupt the PAM or the protospacer seed where possible, since destroying the target site after editing prevents re-engagement and increases product purity [3].

Quantitative studies converge on useful heuristics: a GC-rich PBS improves annealing; for RTTs ≤12 nt, terminating the template with G improves efficiency; 13-nt PBS / 12-nt RTT is a strong default, though sequence context dominates [3, 8]. Self-complementarity between the spacer and the 3′ extension can sequester the pegRNA in an autoinhibitory conformation—documented for the PBS and the first 3 nt of the RTT annealing to the spacer—so predicted secondary structure must be inspected during design [5].

The following Python sketch illustrates the core computational step performed by design tools such as PrimeDesign [3]: enumerating candidate PBS/RTT combinations and ranking them by heuristic scores.

```python
def design_pegrna_extension(target_seq, edit_pos, edit_seq,
                            pbs_lengths=range(8, 17), rtt_lengths=range(10, 21)):
    """Enumerate candidate (PBS, RTT) pairs; rank by heuristic score."""
    candidates = []
    for pbs_len in pbs_lengths:
        for rtt_len in rtt_lengths:
            pbs = reverse_complement(target_seq[edit_pos:edit_pos + pbs_len])
            rtt = reverse_complement(target_seq[edit_pos - rtt_len:edit_pos]) + edit_seq
            score = 2.0 * gc_content(pbs) - 0.05 * rtt_len - homopolymer_penalty(rtt + pbs)
            candidates.append({"pbs": pbs, "rtt": rtt, "score": score})
    return sorted(candidates, key=lambda c: c["score"], reverse=True)
```

> **Theorem:** *The pegRNA extension is a thermodynamic compromise.* Every additional nucleotide in the PBS stabilizes the primer–template duplex (increasing priming efficiency) while simultaneously increasing the probability of productive annealing at near-cognate sites and of intramolecular autoinhibition. Optimal extension design therefore cannot be derived from first principles alone; it must be *screened*, which is why testing 3–5 pegRNAs per target with varied PBS/RTT lengths remains standard practice [1, 3].

### 4.2 Reverse-Transcriptase Templating and 3′ Flap Resolution

The enzymology of prime editing is a hybrid of CRISPR targeting and retroviral reverse transcription. The RT domain of PE1 was wild-type M-MLV RT; **PE2** replaced it with an engineered pentamutant (D200N, L603W, T306K, W313F, H594Q) selected for increased thermostability, processivity, and DNA–RNA substrate affinity, yielding roughly 1.6–5.1-fold higher editing efficiency across tested loci [1]. The RT initiates synthesis from the 3′-OH of the nicked strand annealed to the PBS and polymerizes through the RTT, producing a single-stranded 3′ flap that contains the edit.

Flap resolution is where cellular biology takes over from the editor. The edited 3′ flap and the displaced original 5′ flap exist in a dynamic annealing equilibrium; structure-specific endonucleases, principally FEN1, cleave 5′ flaps with high preference, so the edited 3′ flap is preferentially retained and ligated, generating a heteroduplex in which one strand carries the edit [2]. Endogenous mismatch repair (MMR) then resolves the heteroduplex—restoring the original sequence or installing the edit permanently. This MMR step is a major efficiency determinant: MMR-proficient cells (e.g., HeLa, U2OS) show markedly lower prime editing than MMR-deficient cells, motivating the PE4/PE5 systems that co-express dominant-negative MLH1 (MLH1dn) to transiently suppress MMR [4, 5].

Two failure modes dominate at this stage. First, **scaffold readthrough**: the RT can polymerize past the RTT into the pegRNA scaffold, integrating scaffold sequence at the target—mitigated by inserting a riboabasic spacer between RTT and scaffold in synthetic pegRNAs, which terminates reverse transcription [2]. Second, **indel byproducts**: when flap equilibration is slow, or when PE3's second nick is processed as a DSB, small indels arise—typically low single-digit percentages, far below Cas9 nuclease levels but nonzero [1, 7].

### 4.3 Architectural Evolution: PE1, PE2, PE3/PE3b, PEmax, and PE7

| System | Editor protein | Additional guides | Key innovation |
|---|---|---|---|
| PE1 | nCas9(H840A)–WT M-MLV RT | pegRNA only | Proof of concept: search-and-replace without DSBs [1] |
| PE2 | nCas9(H840A)–engineered RT (5 mutations) | pegRNA only | Thermostable, processive RT; 1.6–5.1× PE1 efficiency [1] |
| PE3 | PE2 protein | pegRNA + nicking sgRNA (non-edited strand) | Second nick biases MMR toward the edit; higher efficiency, more indels [1] |
| PE3b | PE2 protein | pegRNA + nicking sgRNA overlapping the edit | Nicks only *after* edit incorporation; efficiency of PE3 with PE2-like purity [1, 3] |
| PEmax | Codon-optimized PE2 + R221K/N394K Cas9, 34-aa linker, dual NLSs | pegRNA ± nicking sgRNA | Improved expression/nuclear localization; new baseline [4] |
| PE4/PE5 | PEmax + MLH1dn | pegRNA ± nicking sgRNA | Transient MMR suppression; largest gains in MMR-proficient cells [4] |
| PE7 | PEmax + N-terminal La(1–194) fusion | pegRNA ± nicking sgRNA | Endogenous RNA-binding protein stabilizes pegRNA 3′ ends; up to ~21× in U2OS [5] |

**PE1 → PE2** was an RT engineering story: the wild-type M-MLV enzyme is mesophilic and only moderately processive, so Anzalone et al. screened RT variants and installed five substitutions that together substantially improved edit incorporation [1]. **PE2 → PE3** was a DNA-repair manipulation: nicking the non-edited strand directs the cell's repair machinery to use the edited strand as template, typically doubling or tripling efficiency at the cost of elevated indels, because two proximal nicks can be processed as a DSB [1]. **PE3b** refines this by designing the nicking guide to overlap the edit itself: the guide only matches (and hence only nicks) *after* the edited flap has been incorporated, temporally separating the two nicks and recovering PE2-level product purity with PE3-level efficiency [1, 3]. PrimeDesign explicitly annotates candidate nicking guides as *PE3b seed*, *PE3b non-seed*, or *PE3* and ranks them accordingly [3].

**PEmax** (Chen et al., 2021) rebuilt the PE2 coding sequence for mammalian expression: human codon optimization, SpCas9 mutations R221K and N394K that improve nuclease-dead Cas9 function, an extended 34-amino-acid linker containing a bipartite SV40 nuclear localization signal, and an additional C-terminal c-Myc NLS [4]. PEmax became the chassis for subsequent systems. **PE4 and PE5** add co-expression of MLH1dn, a dominant-negative fragment of the MMR protein MLH1; PE4 = PEmax + MLH1dn (PE2-equivalent purity), PE5 = PEmax + MLH1dn + nicking guide (PE3-equivalent efficiency), with the largest relative gains in MMR-proficient cell lines [4].

**PE7** (Yan et al., *Nature*, 2024) emerged from genome-wide CRISPR screens for cellular factors modulating prime editing, which identified the La protein—an endogenous small RNA-binding protein that protects nascent RNA polymerase III transcripts from exonucleolytic degradation—as a potent enhancer [5]. PegRNAs expressed in cells frequently carry 3′ polyuridine tracts (a byproduct of Pol III termination), which La binds and protects. Fusing just the N-terminal RNA-binding domain, La(1–194), to PEmax produced PE7, improving intended editing up to 21.2-fold (median) in U2OS cells with PE2-style pegRNAs; epegRNAs added no further benefit, suggesting a shared 3′-end stabilization mechanism, and mutations disrupting La's polyU binding abolished the improvement [5].

### 4.4 epegRNAs and the 3′-End Stability Problem

The pegRNA's 3′ extension is its Achilles' heel: cellular 3′→5′ exonucleases degrade it, and truncated extensions prime inefficiently or not at all. **Engineered pegRNAs (epegRNAs)**, developed by Nelson et al., append structured RNA motifs—most notably *tevopreQ1*, an engineered variant of the preQ1 riboswitch aptamer—to the extreme 3′ end of the pegRNA [8]. The pseudoknotted structure blocks exonuclease progression, increasing the intracellular half-life of full-length pegRNA and improving editing efficiency 3–4-fold at many loci without increasing off-target editing [8]. Notably, the PE7 result—that epegRNAs add little to PE7's improvement—implies that 3′-end protection is a saturable bottleneck: once the extension is stabilized, further gains must come from elsewhere (repair manipulation, RT processivity, delivery) [5].

## 5 Empirical Evidence

The empirical record of prime editing spans four lines of evidence: on-target efficiency across systems, therapeutic proof-of-concept edits, pegRNA design rules derived from high-throughput screens, and genome-wide specificity measurements.

**Efficiency.** In the original report, PE1 installed a +5 G→T transversion at *RNF2* at ~20% efficiency and a +4 CTT insertion at *HEK3* at ~30%, with indel byproducts of ~1–10% depending on the edit [1]. PE2 improved these figures 1.6–5.1-fold, and PE3 roughly doubled PE2 at most loci [1]. Across the modern literature, PE3/PE5 with epegRNAs routinely reaches 40–70% precise editing in HEK293T cells, with PE5max averaging ~60% at tested loci with ~1.2% indels [7]. Efficiency remains highly locus- and cell-type-dependent, varying over an order of magnitude for the same edit in different chromatin contexts.

**Therapeutic edits.** Beyond the original HBB/HEXA demonstrations [1], prime editing has corrected disease variants in patient-derived cells and animal models, including liver targets via LNP-formulated editors in translational programs, with in vivo hepatocyte efficiencies now reaching therapeutically meaningful thresholds [2]. Its ability to correct transversions and small indels without DSBs makes it uniquely suited to variants inaccessible to base editors.

**Design rules.** High-throughput evaluation of thousands of pegRNAs (Kim et al.) recommends a 13-nt PBS, 12-nt RTT, GC-rich PBS, and PAM disruption via the RTT as defaults [8]. PrimeDesign operationalizes these into a web tool: a single input sequence encoding reference and desired alleles, enumeration of candidate spacers/extensions/ngRNAs within optimized parameter ranges, CFD-based specificity ranking, and pooled-design modes for saturation mutagenesis screens [3].

**Specificity.** Three independent genome-wide nomination methods—nDigenome-seq (nickase-based in vitro digestion), TAPE-seq (tagmentation-based), and PE-tag (in vitro/in vivo tag integration)—converge on one conclusion: prime editor off-target events are rare, typically undetectable above background at Cas9 off-target sites, because productive editing requires spacer, PBS, *and* RTT homology [6, 7]. Whole-genome sequencing of prime-edited rice plants found no increase in pegRNA-independent SNVs or indels versus nickase-only controls, no retrotransposon copy-number changes from ectopic M-MLV RT, and no detectable pegRNA or mRNA insertions—indicating the RT does not promiscuously reverse-transcribe cellular RNAs into the genome [6]. A 2026 systematic comparison of PE2max/PE3max/PE4max/PE5max found no detectable editing at 12 Cas-OFFinder-predicted off-target sites (all <1%, indistinguishable from wild type) [7].

## 6 Limitations

Prime editing's limitations are quantitative rather than conceptual, and each maps to an active engineering front:

1. **Locus-to-locus variability.** Efficiency can range from <1% to >70% for different pegRNAs at different targets, driven by chromatin accessibility, sequence context, and MMR status. A failed pegRNA does not imply a failed target—testing multiple PBS/RTT combinations is mandatory [3].
2. **The PAM constraint.** SpCas9-based editors require an NGG PAM near the edit; edits far from any PAM need PAM-relaxed Cas9 variants (e.g., SpRY) or accept long RTTs with reduced efficiency.
3. **Size limits on the edit.** While small insertions (<50 bp) are efficient, large insertions (>100 bp) suffer declining efficiency as RT processivity and flap annealing become limiting; paired-pegRNA strategies (twinPE, PEDAR) address this at the cost of complexity.
4. **Delivery.** The PE coding sequence (~6.3 kb) exceeds single-AAV capacity, requiring dual-AAV intein-split systems, LNP-mRNA delivery, or RNP formats—each with tissue-specific trade-offs [2].
5. **Residual indels and scaffold integration.** PE3-family systems generate low-percentage indels, and processive RTs can integrate pegRNA scaffold sequence; PE3b design and riboabasic spacers mitigate but do not eliminate these byproducts [1, 2].
6. **MMR dependence.** In MMR-proficient primary cells, heteroduplex correction actively removes edits; MLH1dn co-expression helps but adds a second transgene and transient MMR suppression carries theoretical mutagenic risk [4].
7. **Off-target detection limits.** Current genome-wide assays cannot exclude extremely rare clonal events below WGS detection thresholds; embryo-based lineage-tracing assays (e.g., GOTI) will be needed for definitive preclinical safety assessment [6].

## 7 Conclusion

Prime editing redefined the achievable scope of DSB-free genome editing by fusing target search and edit templating into a single RNA molecule. The intellectual arc from PE1 to PE7 traces a systematic attack on successive bottlenecks: RT processivity (PE2), repair-pathway bias (PE3/PE3b), expression and localization (PEmax), mismatch repair (PE4/PE5), and pegRNA 3′-end stability (epegRNAs, PE7). The PE7 discovery is particularly instructive: an unbiased screen revealed that an endogenous RNA-binding protein was already, inadvertently, participating in prime editing through polyU tracts on expressed pegRNAs—and recruiting it deliberately yielded the largest single efficiency jump in the field's history [5].

The specificity case is now strong. Three hybridization checkpoints, corroborated by nDigenome-seq, TAPE-seq, PE-tag, and whole-genome sequencing across mammalian and plant systems, give prime editors an off-target profile decisively better than Cas9 nuclease and at least comparable to base editors, without base editors' bystander-window constraint [1, 6, 7]. What remains is the translational gap: delivering a large editor–guide payload to therapeutically relevant tissues at efficiencies that reliably convert target alleles. From 2019's proof of concept to 2024's PE7 and LNP-formulated in vivo programs, prime editing is transitioning from research tool to therapeutic platform—its ultimate ceiling set not by reverse-transcription chemistry, but by our ability to deliver and control it *in vivo*.
