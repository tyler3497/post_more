# ARIES Recovery and Write-Ahead Logging: Steal/No-Force Buffer Management, Physiological Logging, and the Three-Pass Recovery Paradigm

## Abstract

The Algorithms for Recovery and Isolation Exploiting Semantics (ARIES) family, introduced by Mohan, Haderle, Lindsay, Pirahesh, and Schwarz in 1992 [1], remains the dominant paradigm for transaction recovery in database engines. ARIES unites write-ahead logging with steal/no-force buffer management, physiological logging, and a rigorously defined log sequence number (LSN) protocol to guarantee atomicity and durability across system crashes and media failures. Its signature contribution is the three-pass restart recovery algorithm — analysis, redo, and undo — which first *repeats history* by re-applying every logged update and only then rolls back uncommitted transactions, using compensation log records (CLRs) to make undo itself idempotent and restartable. This thesis reconstructs ARIES from first principles: the WAL invariant, log record anatomy, the LSN protocol, fuzzy checkpointing, the mechanics of each recovery pass, CLRs and nested top actions, and media recovery over mirrored disks. We survey empirical evidence from three decades of industrial deployment, give proof sketches for redo idempotence and recovery completeness, and examine limitations and open problems in non-volatile-memory and cloud-native storage engines.

## 1. Introduction

Every transaction processing system must answer two questions: *what happens to a transaction's updates when the machine crashes mid-commit*, and *how does the system return the database to a consistent state without losing committed work or leaking uncommitted work*. Before the late 1980s, the dominant answers were *shadow paging* and *force/no-steal* logging disciplines that constrained buffer management so severely that recovery was trivial but performance was not [4]. Both imposed what Mohan later called *unacceptable coupling between recovery and concurrency control*: the recovery method dictated page replacement policy and locking granularity [2].

The ARIES method, published in *ACM Transactions on Database Systems* in March 1992 [1], severed that coupling. Its central insight is the **repeating history paradigm**: restart recovery first re-executes *every* logged update — including those of transactions that will abort — reconstructing the exact pre-crash state, and only afterwards undoes the *loser* transactions. Preceded by an analysis pass reconstructing pre-crash bookkeeping, this discipline is correct under **steal** and **no-force**, supports fine-granularity locking and partial rollbacks, and requires of the log only one property: that redo operations be *idempotent* [5].

The intellectual structure of ARIES rests on four pillars:

1. **The write-ahead logging (WAL) protocol**: the *WAL rule* (log records reach stable storage before the data pages they describe) and the *commit rule* (a commit record must be stable before the transaction is acknowledged) [4].
2. **Physiological logging**: a record identifies the affected page physically but describes the change logically, permitting redo even after the page's structure has evolved [1].
3. **The log sequence number (LSN) protocol**: monotone log numbering coupled with per-page `pageLSN` fields, making redo *conditionally* applicable and therefore idempotent [5].
4. **Compensation log records (CLRs)**: redo-only records describing undo actions, chained by `undoNextLSN` pointers so undo is restartable and correct under repeated crashes [1][6].

ARIES was no laboratory curiosity. Its variants — ARIES/KVL and ARIES/IM for indexes, ARIES/CSA for client–server architectures [3], ARIES/LHS for linear hashing — were adopted by IBM DB2, Informix, Microsoft SQL Server, and Sybase, and its concepts permeate PostgreSQL's WAL, InnoDB's redo log, and cloud-native engines. Mohan's 1999 retrospective traces this impact [2]; the canonical expansion — *Algorithms for Recovery and Isolation Exploiting Semantics* — is summarized in [7].

Section 2 reviews buffer policies and logging disciplines; Section 3 formalizes the LSN protocol, log records, and checkpointing; Section 4 dives into steal/no-force, physiological logging, fuzzy checkpoints, the three-pass algorithm, and CLRs with media recovery; Section 5 gives empirical evidence and proof sketches; Section 6 discusses limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The ACID contract and the failure model

ARIES addresses *atomicity* and *durability* under two failure classes: **system failures** (crashes, power loss), where volatile state is lost but stable storage survives, and **media failures** (disk damage), where portions of stable storage itself are destroyed [1]. Transaction aborts reuse the same undo machinery during normal processing. ARIES assumes *in-place updating* — a page is written back to the location it was read from — which is precisely what makes the WAL rule necessary: unlike shadow paging, there is no atomic page swap to fall back on [4].

### 2.2 Buffer management policies: steal versus force

Two orthogonal policy axes determine the buffer manager's freedom [5]:

| Policy | Meaning | Recovery consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **steal** | A frame holding uncommitted updates may be written to disk (stolen) | Undo information must be logged before the steal; recovery must undo losers' updates |
| **no-steal** | Uncommitted updates never reach disk | No undo needed after crash, but buffer pool pins uncommitted pages, hurting concurrency |
| **force** | All of a transaction's updated pages are forced to disk at commit | No redo needed, but commit latency is dominated by random page writes |
| **no-force** | Updated pages may remain dirty in the buffer pool at commit | Redo information must be logged; recovery must redo winners' updates |

**Steal + no-force** maximizes buffer-manager freedom — and therefore throughput — but demands both undo and redo from recovery. ARIES targets exactly this regime while remaining correct under all four combinations [1]. The Edinburgh lecture notes summarize the consequence crisply: *redo must repeat all actions from an appropriate point in the log, even for transactions that will abort* [6].

### 2.3 Logging disciplines

Log records describe updates at three granularities [1][5]:

- **Physical logging** records byte-level before/after images. Redo is trivially idempotent but log volume is large.
- **Logical logging** records operations (*"insert key k into index I"*). Volume is small, but redo is generally *not* idempotent — consider *"add 5 to attribute A"* [5].
- **Physiological logging**, ARIES's choice, identifies the page physically but describes the change logically (*"on page P, insert record r at slot s"*), permitting redo even after the page's internal structure has evolved [1].

### 2.4 The WAL rules

Two invariants govern write-ahead logging [4]:

> **Theorem (WAL safety).** *Let `flushedLSN` be the greatest LSN durable on stable storage. A dirty page `P` with `pageLSN(P) = L` may be written to its stable location only if `L ≤ flushedLSN`. A transaction `T` may be reported committed only if its commit log record's LSN is `≤ flushedLSN`.*

ARIES never forces the log per record: a volatile log tail is flushed in batches, on commit, when full, or before a page write. The `flushedLSN` bookkeeping — not per-record forcing — is therefore the operative mechanism [4][5].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Log record anatomy

Every ARIES log record carries a header — LSN, transaction id, `prevLSN` chaining the transaction's records, and type — followed by type-specific payload [1][4]:

```rust
struct LogRecordHeader {
    lsn: Lsn,                // unique, monotonically increasing
    trans_id: TxnId,
    prev_lsn: Option<Lsn>,   // previous record of same transaction
    kind: RecordKind,
}

struct UpdateRecord {
    header: LogRecordHeader,
    page_id: PageId,               // physiological identity
    undo_info: Option<BeforeImage>, // None for redo-only
    redo_info: Option<AfterImage>,  // None for undo-only
}

struct CompensationLogRecord {
    header: LogRecordHeader,
    page_id: PageId,
    redo_info: AfterImage,       // the undo action, as redo-only data
    undo_next_lsn: Option<Lsn>,  // next record of this txn to undo
}
```

Update records are classified as **undo-only**, **redo-only**, or **undo-redo** depending on which images they carry [4]. CLRs are *always* redo-only: the undo action they describe is never itself undone — the key to restartable undo.

### 3.2 The LSN protocol

LSNs are the total order on which ARIES's correctness arguments rest [1][5]:

| LSN variable | Stored in | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `LSN(r)` | each log record | unique monotone sequence number of `r` |
| `pageLSN(P)` | page header | LSN of the latest update applied to `P` |
| `flushedLSN` | log manager | greatest LSN durable on stable storage |
| `recLSN(P)` | dirty page table | LSN of the first log record that dirtied `P` since its last disk write |
| `lastLSN(T)` | transaction table | LSN of `T`'s most recent log record |
| `undoNextLSN` | CLR payload | LSN of the next record of `T` to undo after this CLR |

The protocol's core rule is the **redo guard**: a log record `r` affecting page `P` is re-applied during redo only if `LSN(r) > pageLSN(P)` [1]. Because `pageLSN` is set to `LSN(r)` whenever the redo is applied, re-executing the redo pass — after a crash *during* recovery — is harmless. In Haskell-flavored notation, the guard is a simple order constraint:

```haskell
newtype Lsn = Lsn { unLsn :: Word64 } deriving (Eq, Ord, Show)

-- Redo applies r to page p only when the record is newer than the page.
shouldRedo :: Lsn -> Lsn -> Bool
shouldRedo recordLsn pageLsn = recordLsn > pageLsn
```

### 3.3 Fuzzy checkpointing

A checkpoint must capture the transaction table and the dirty page table so recovery need not scan from the log's beginning. ARIES uses **fuzzy checkpoints** that do not quiesce the system [1][4]:

1. Write a `begin_checkpoint` record; remember its LSN, `ckptLSN`.
2. Copy the transaction table and dirty page table into an `end_checkpoint` record — transactions may begin, commit, or dirty pages between the two records.
3. Write the `end_checkpoint` record and flush the log through it.
4. Update the **master log record** (fixed stable location) to point at `ckptLSN`.

Because the snapshot is fuzzy, analysis scans forward from `ckptLSN` and *corrects* the tables with every record it encounters; the checkpoint merely bounds how far back recovery must look [6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Steal/No-Force Buffer Management and the WAL Invariant

Steal/no-force is the *adversarial* regime: uncommitted updates may be on disk while committed updates may not be, so the stable database after a crash is an arbitrary mixture. The WAL invariant makes this mixture recoverable. Consider transaction `T` updating page `P`, generating log record `r` with `LSN(r) = L`:

- **Steal forces the undo path.** If the buffer manager steals `P`'s frame and writes it before `T` commits, the on-disk image contains `T`'s uncommitted update. Recovery must *undo* it — possible only because the WAL rule forced `r`'s undo information to stable storage *before* the stolen write [4].
- **No-force forces the redo path.** If `T` commits while `P` remains dirty in the pool, durability rests entirely on the log. Recovery must *redo* `r` — possible only because the commit rule forced the commit record (and hence all of `T`'s records, by log order) to stable storage before `T` was acknowledged [5].

Together the rules characterize the crash state: *the stable log describes a superset of the stable database's updates, and the commit records describe exactly the durable transactions* [1] — and the three-pass algorithm is a constructive exploitation of this fact.

### 4.2 Physiological Logging and the Log Sequence Number Protocol

Physiological logging is ARIES's answer to a dilemma that defeated earlier WAL schemes: physical logging wastes log bandwidth, while logical logging breaks the idempotence repeating history requires [5]. A physiological record names the page physically (`pageID`) but the operation logically. During redo, the page is fetched by `pageID`, the `pageLSN` guard checked, and the logical operation re-executed against the page's current contents.

During redo, `pageLSN(P)` is the arbiter: records with `LSN ≤ pageLSN(P)` are already reflected and skipped. This single comparison makes the redo pass idempotent under arbitrarily repeated crashes — the *repeating history* property Mohan's retrospective identifies as ARIES's most portable idea [2].

### 4.3 Fuzzy Checkpoints

The mechanics are given in Section 3.3; the rationale is worth stressing. A *sharp* checkpoint — flushing all dirty pages and quiescing transactions — would stall the system, so ARIES snapshots the tables fuzzily while processing continues [4]. The fuzziness is harmless precisely because analysis replays the log from `ckptLSN` forward, correcting every transaction or page state that changed mid-checkpoint [6]. And since redo starts at `min recLSN` rather than at the checkpoint — the `pageLSN` guard filtering already-stable records — the checkpoint need only bound, not pinpoint, the recovery scan.

### 4.4 The Three-Pass Recovery Algorithm: Analysis, Redo, Undo

Restart recovery executes three strictly ordered passes [1][4][6].

**Pass 1 — Analysis.** Scan forward from `ckptLSN` to end of log, maintaining the **transaction table** (`transID → (state, lastLSN)`) and the **dirty page table** (`pageID → recLSN`). Transactions in the table with no commit record at end of log are the **losers**; their `lastLSN` values seed undo. Analysis also computes `redoLSN = min recLSN` — the earliest point redo must scan [1].

**Pass 2 — Redo (repeating history).** Scan forward from `redoLSN`; for each update or CLR record `r` on page `P`: skip if `P ∉` dirty page table or `LSN(r) < recLSN(P)` (already stable); fetch `P` and skip if `pageLSN(P) ≥ LSN(r)` (already applied); otherwise re-apply the physiological operation and set `pageLSN(P) := LSN(r)`.

Redo is *page-oriented* and deliberately re-applies losers' updates too: the goal is the exact pre-crash state, after which undo yields a transaction-consistent state [6].

```python
def redo_pass(log, dirty_page_table, pool):
    redo_lsn = min(dirty_page_table.values(), default=None)
    for r in log.scan_forward(redo_lsn):
        if r.kind not in ("update", "clr"):
            continue
        if r.page_id not in dirty_page_table or r.lsn < dirty_page_table[r.page_id]:
            continue
        page = pool.fetch(r.page_id)
        if page.page_lsn < r.lsn:  # idempotence guard
            page.apply_physiological(r.redo_info)
            page.page_lsn = r.lsn
```

**Pass 3 — Undo.** Roll back each loser *backwards* along its `prevLSN` chain from `lastLSN(T)`, always serving the loser with the maximum remaining LSN so a single backward sweep handles all losers [1]:

- On an **update record** with undo information: apply the inverse operation and write a **CLR** whose `undoNextLSN` is the undone record's `prevLSN`.
- On a **CLR**: do *not* undo it; continue from its `undoNextLSN`.
- On reaching the transaction's first record: write the abort record and remove `T` from the transaction table.

Undo is *logical where it must be*: undoing a B-tree insert after a page split cannot restore a before-image, so ARIES logs structure modifications with logical undo descriptors and uses **nested top actions** — mini-transactions committed as a unit — to undo them atomically [1].

### 4.5 Compensation Log Records, Nested Top Actions, and Media Recovery

If the system crashes *during* the undo pass after undoing half of loser `T`'s updates, the next restart's analysis encounters the CLRs written so far; redo re-applies the already-performed undo actions (idempotent, by the `pageLSN` guard), and undo *skips* them via `undoNextLSN` instead of undoing them twice [1][6].

> **Theorem (Undo idempotence).** *A CLR is never undone. Across any number of crashes during recovery, each loser update is processed at most once, because each processed update is replaced in the undo chain by a CLR pointing past it.*

**Nested top actions** extend the idea to *partial* rollback: a nested top action groups updates (e.g., a B-tree page split's cascade) so rollback to a savepoint *inside* it undoes the whole action as a unit, while rollback to a later savepoint leaves it intact [1]. Its commit marker is an atomic boundary for the undo pass — the ancestor of savepoint machinery in modern engines.

**Media recovery** handles failures destroying stable storage itself. With *mirrored disks* (or an archived copy plus archive log), a bad page is replaced by the good copy and the log replayed forward from the page's archived `pageLSN` — the redo pass restricted to damaged pages [1]. System and media recovery thus share one redo operator.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Industrial evidence

The empirical case for ARIES is the database industry itself. Within a decade of publication, ARIES or its variants were the recovery subsystem of IBM DB2, Informix, Microsoft SQL Server, and Sybase ASE; its physiological logging and LSN protocol were re-implemented in PostgreSQL's WAL and InnoDB's redo log [2]. Mohan's retrospective attributes this adoption to three measurable properties [2][3]:

1. **Bounded recovery time.** Restart work is proportional to log volume since `min recLSN` — controlled by checkpoint frequency — not to database size, and fuzzy checkpoints keep this bound tight without quiescing.
2. **Log efficiency.** Physiological records are far smaller than physical before/after images on index-intensive workloads, while retaining the idempotence logical logging lacks [1].
3. **Concurrency preservation.** Recovery constrains neither buffer replacement nor locking granularity, so the concurrency subsystem — ARIES/KVL fine-granularity locking, the Commit_LSN optimization — evolves independently [3].

ARIES/CSA further showed the three-pass structure survives a buffer pool distributed across clients with unsynchronized clocks, via log records shipped to a single server log [3].

### 5.2 Proof sketches

> **Theorem (Redo idempotence / repeating history).** *Let R be the redo pass over log interval [redoLSN, end]. Executing R any number of times, with crashes between executions, leaves every page P with pageLSN(P) = max{LSN(r) : r updates P, LSN(r) ≤ end} and contents equal to applying those updates in LSN order.*

*Sketch.* The guard applies `r` to `P` iff `LSN(r) > pageLSN(P)`, then sets `pageLSN(P) := LSN(r)`. Records scan in increasing LSN order, so each page's `pageLSN` is non-decreasing and ends at the maximum applicable LSN. Crashes lose only volatile state; re-scanned records are skipped by the guard, so the fixed point is reached by induction on crashes. ∎

> **Theorem (Recovery completeness).** *After analysis, redo, and undo complete without further crashes, the database contains exactly the updates of committed transactions (and no others), and all loser transactions are fully undone.*

*Sketch.* By the WAL characterization, redo reconstructs the exact pre-crash state: every update with `LSN ≤ end` missing from stable storage is re-applied, and the guard prevents double application. Undo then walks each loser `T` backwards from `lastLSN(T)`, inverting every update record exactly once (CLRs chain past already-undone records via `undoNextLSN`) and emitting the abort record at `T`'s first record. Committed transactions are never touched. ∎

The Wisconsin notes emphasize ARIES's single proof obligation for implementers: *redo operations must be idempotent* — discharged by the `pageLSN` guard, which is always checked before re-execution [5].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Logical undo remains the hard part.** Page-level undo is elegant, but *logical* undo of high-concurrency index operations — B-tree splits, rebalancing deletes — needs operation-specific inverse logic and nested top actions whose correctness arguments are notoriously delicate [1][2].

**The LSN as a scalability bottleneck.** A single monotone LSN sequence is a global synchronization point. Multi-core and distributed engines must either partition the log (sacrificing the total order the proofs assume) or accept contention on LSN allocation — an active research area in non-volatile-memory systems.

**Long-running transactions.** One long transaction pins `min recLSN` far in the past, forcing redo to scan large log prefixes. Fuzzy checkpoints bound the *checkpoint-to-crash* interval but not the *recLSN-to-crash* interval; practical systems resort to flush daemons and transaction time limits.

**Disaggregated storage.** ARIES assumes cheap sequential log append and random page reads during redo. When compute is separated from storage, redo becomes network-bound and its random page fetches dominate cost; modern systems keep ARIES's *ideas* (WAL, LSNs, repeating history) while abandoning *page-oriented* redo [2].

**Formal verification gaps.** No machine-checked proof covers the full three-pass algorithm with CLRs, nested top actions, and fuzzy checkpoints. Given the method's well-documented intricacy, closing that gap remains an open, valuable target.

## 7. Conclusion

ARIES endures because it solved the *right* problem at the *right* level of abstraction. Rather than constraining the buffer or lock manager to simplify recovery, it asked what minimal contract the log must provide — *idempotent redo* — and built everything else (the LSN protocol, physiological logging, fuzzy checkpoints, CLRs, nested top actions) to honor that contract under steal/no-force policies. Analysis reconstructs intent, redo repeats history, undo retracts the losers: crash recovery becomes a deterministic function of the stable log, restartable after arbitrarily many crashes mid-recovery. Its ideas have outlived the centralized, disk-resident systems they were designed for — every modern WAL is ARIES's intellectual descendant [2][5]. The open problems — logical undo, LSN scalability, disaggregated redo, verified implementations — define the frontier the next generation of recovery methods must cross. The paradigm itself, *repeating history before rewriting it*, is unlikely to be displaced.
