# Viscosity Solutions of Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman Equations: Comparison Principles and Stochastic Optimal Control

## Abstract

Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations are the partial differential equations governing the value functions of optimal control problems, yet their nonlinearity and degeneracy typically preclude classical smooth solutions. Viscosity solutions, introduced by Crandall and Lions for first-order Hamilton–Jacobi equations, furnish the correct weak framework: they are defined by a maximum-principle touch condition with smooth test functions, they persist under uniform limits, and they admit comparison principles that force uniqueness. This thesis develops the theory for second-order degenerate elliptic and parabolic HJB equations arising in stochastic optimal control. We present the Crandall–Lions definition via semicontinuous envelopes, prove the comparison principle by the doubling-of-variables argument culminating in Ishii's lemma, and expose the degenerate elliptic structure that makes the argument work. We then treat the stochastic Perron method of Bayraktar and Sirbu, which characterizes value functions of controlled diffusions as viscosity solutions while bypassing the dynamic programming principle, and the Barles–Souganidis framework for convergence of monotone numerical schemes. Applications to the Merton portfolio problem, Isaacs equations of differential games, and singular and impulse control are worked out in detail [1][2][3][4][5][6][7].

## 1 Intro

The calculus of variations teaches that smooth minimizers satisfy the Euler–Lagrange equation, but optimal control is harsher: the value function of even a benign control problem is generically *nonsmooth*. Corners propagate along characteristics, and no classical solution of the associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation need exist. For deterministic control this was understood by the 1960s; the decisive advance came in 1981, when Crandall and Lions proposed a notion of weak solution that requires no differentiability of the candidate function at all, only a consistent response to smooth test functions that touch it from above or below [1].

The second-order setting — stochastic optimal control of diffusion processes — demanded a deeper theory. The value function satisfies a *fully nonlinear, possibly degenerate elliptic* PDE, and the classical route to uniqueness through the dynamic programming principle (DPP) is technically forbidding. Over the 1980s and 1990s, a complete architecture emerged: the definition extended to second order by Crandall, Ishii and Lions; comparison theorems proved by *doubling the variables* and a matrix inequality (Ishii's lemma) that replaces the missing chain rule; existence by Perron's method; numerical approximation governed by monotonicity; and, more recently, a stochastic Perron method that dispenses with the DPP entirely [2][5][6].

This thesis is a self-contained exposition of that architecture. Our aims are threefold: *(i)* to give a precise, usable statement of the viscosity definition and the comparison machinery; *(ii)* to show how stochastic control fits the PDE theory, both classically through the DPP and modernly through stochastic Perron; and *(iii)* to connect theory with computation and with concrete models — Merton's portfolio problem, differential games, and singular or impulse control. Throughout, we emphasize *why* each ingredient is needed: which step fails without degenerate ellipticity, where semicontinuity enters, and what monotonicity buys in numerics.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The control problem and its value function

Fix a finite horizon T and consider a controlled diffusion on Euclidean space of dimension d:

$$dX_s = b(X_s, \alpha_s)\,ds + \sigma(X_s, \alpha_s)\,dW_s, \qquad X_t = x,$$

where W is a standard Brownian motion, the control process alpha takes values in a compact set A, and the coefficients b and sigma satisfy standard Lipschitz and growth conditions. For an admissible control, define the cost

$$J(t, x, \alpha) = \mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[\int_t^T f(s, X_s, \alpha_s)\,ds + g(X_T)\right],$$

with running cost f and terminal cost g. The *value function* is the pointwise infimum

$$v(t, x) = \inf_{\alpha} J(t, x, \alpha).$$

Under mild hypotheses v is continuous and bounded; it is almost never differentiable. The formal HJB equation is the fully nonlinear parabolic PDE

$$-\partial_t v + H(t, x, Dv, D^2 v) = 0,$$

with the Hamiltonian

$$H(t, x, p, M) = \sup_{a \in A}\left\{-\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\big(\sigma\sigma^\top(t,x,a) M\big) - b(t,x,a)\cdot p - f(t,x,a)\right\}.$$

The supremum over controls is the analytical shadow of optimization: at each point, the equation chooses the control that is instantaneously best. The equation is *degenerate elliptic*: H is nonincreasing in the matrix argument M, a property inherited from the nonnegativity of sigma sigma-transpose. Degeneracy means diffusion may vanish in some directions — the PDE can be first-order in part — which rules out classical elliptic regularity and makes viscosity theory indispensable [1][2].

### 2.2 The dynamic programming principle

The bridge between the control problem and the PDE is the *dynamic programming principle* (DPP): for any stopping time theta between t and T,

$$v(t,x) = \inf_{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[\int_t^{\theta} f\,ds + v(\theta, X_{\theta})\right].$$

Bellman's intuition is that an optimal policy, restricted to any later interval, remains optimal for the subproblem it faces. Rigorously, the DPP for controlled diffusions requires measurable selection and delicate conditioning arguments; the classical proof goes through the strong Markov property and is one of the most technical passages in stochastic control. Once available, however, it yields a short formal derivation that v satisfies the HJB equation in the viscosity sense: fix a smooth test function touching v from below at a point, apply Itô's formula to the test function along a constant control, use the DPP inequality, and divide by the time increment. This is the classical route — powerful, but the DPP is a heavy hypothesis, and one of this thesis's themes is how far the theory can go without it [5][6].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Viscosity solutions via test functions

Let F(x, r, p, X) be continuous on the product of a domain, the real line, and symmetric matrices, and consider the stationary equation F(x, u, Du, D²u) = 0. A function u is *upper semicontinuous* if its superlevel sets are closed in the limsup sense — intuitively, it may jump down but never up. The semicontinuous envelopes u-star and u-star-sub of a locally bounded function are the smallest upper semicontinuous majorant and largest lower semicontinuous minorant; they allow the definition to tolerate functions that are not even continuous.

> **Theorem:** (Crandall–Ishii–Lions definition) An upper semicontinuous function u is a *viscosity subsolution* of F = 0 if, whenever a smooth test function phi touches u from above at a point x-hat — meaning u minus phi attains a local maximum at x-hat — then F(x-hat, u(x-hat), Dphi(x-hat), D²phi(x-hat)) ≤ 0. A lower semicontinuous function is a *viscosity supersolution* if the reversed inequality holds at local minima of u minus phi. A continuous function satisfying both is a *viscosity solution*.

The genius of the definition is its *monotonicity in the test function*: only the derivatives of phi at the contact point are consulted, and the inequality direction encodes the equation's ellipticity. For degenerate elliptic F — nonincreasing in the matrix argument — the definition is consistent with classical solutions: any twice-differentiable solution is a viscosity solution, because at a maximum of u minus phi the Hessian inequality D²phi ≥ D²u combines with degenerate ellipticity to give the right sign.

### 3.2 What degenerate ellipticity buys

An equation is *degenerate elliptic* if F(x, r, p, X) ≤ F(x, r, p, Y) whenever X ≥ Y in the symmetric-matrix order. For the HJB Hamiltonian above, this holds because subtracting a larger matrix against the positive semidefinite diffusion matrix sigma sigma-transpose can only decrease the supremum. The comparison proof exploits this relentlessly: it is the single structural assumption that lets matrix inequalities at doubled maximum points be converted into a contradiction with the PDE inequalities. Without it, viscosity theory collapses — first-order equations need a separate (Crandall–Lions) treatment, and non-elliptic second-order equations admit no such maximum-principle technology [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Semicontinuous envelopes and the touch condition

Why semicontinuous rather than continuous? Two reasons. First, the natural *stability* of viscosity solutions — uniform limits of subsolutions are subsolutions — extends cleanly to half-relaxed limits of merely bounded sequences, and envelopes are the language for stating it. Second, Perron's method constructs a solution as the supremum of all subsolutions, which is a priori only upper semicontinuous; the comparison principle then forces the upper and lower envelopes to coincide, delivering continuity as a *conclusion* rather than an assumption. The touch condition itself deserves a picture: at a point where the candidate is merely continuous, many smooth functions may touch from above, each contributing a candidate jet (value, gradient, Hessian); the definition demands the PDE inequality for *every* such jet. 

### 4.2 Doubling of variables and Ishii's lemma

The comparison principle asserts: if u is a subsolution, w a supersolution, u ≤ w on the boundary (in a suitable sense), then u ≤ w inside. The proof is a masterpiece of indirect argument. Suppose instead that u minus w attains a positive maximum M at some interior point. *Double the variables*: for small epsilon, maximize

$$\Phi_\epsilon(x, y) = u(x) - w(y) - \frac{|x - y|^2}{2\epsilon}$$

over the product domain. The quadratic penalty forces the maximizers (x-epsilon, y-epsilon) to satisfy |x-epsilon minus y-epsilon|² of order epsilon, so both converge to a point where u minus w equals M. At the maximum, the function x ↦ Phi(x, y-epsilon) has u touched from above by a smooth function, and likewise for w from below — producing jets (p, X) for u and (p, Y) for w with the *same* gradient p but different Hessians. The missing link is the relation between X and Y: this is *Ishii's lemma*, which states that for every delta there exist matrices with

$$\begin{pmatrix} X & 0 \\ 0 & -Y \end{pmatrix} \le \frac{3}{\epsilon}\begin{pmatrix} I & -I \\ -I & I \end{pmatrix} + \delta I,$$

a purely matrix-analytic consequence of the maximum principle applied to the penalized function. In particular X ≤ Y, and degenerate ellipticity converts the subsolution inequality at x-epsilon and the supersolution inequality at y-epsilon into a contradiction as epsilon tends to zero, because the Hamiltonians evaluated at nearly the same point with X ≤ Y must satisfy F(x-epsilon, ·, p, X) ≤ F(y-epsilon, ·, p, Y) up to a modulus that vanishes. The doubling trick thus replaces the chain rule — unavailable for nonsmooth functions — with a matrix inequality, and it is the engine of every second-order comparison theorem [1][2].

### 4.3 The stochastic Perron method

Classically, the value function is shown to be a viscosity solution via the DPP, and then comparison gives uniqueness. Bayraktar and Sirbu inverted the logic with the *stochastic Perron method*: construct the value function directly as a viscosity solution, *bypassing* the DPP, and recover the DPP as a conclusion [5].

The idea mirrors classical Perron but with probabilistic building blocks. Define *stochastic subsolutions*: bounded continuous functions u such that, for every stopping time setup, the process u composed with the state process plus the accumulated running cost is a supermartingale-like object under suitable controls; stochastic supersolutions are defined symmetrically. Let u-minus be the supremum of all stochastic subsolutions and w-plus the infimum of all stochastic supersolutions. The method shows:

1. u-minus is a viscosity *supersolution* and w-plus a viscosity *subsolution* of the HJB equation — the names cross because the probabilistic and PDE notions are dual.
2. Every stochastic subsolution lies below the value function v, and every stochastic supersolution above it, so u-minus ≤ v ≤ w-plus.
3. If a comparison principle holds, w-plus ≤ u-minus, forcing equality: u-minus = v = w-plus is the unique continuous viscosity solution.

The payoff is substantial: the DPP — with all its measurable-selection machinery — is never assumed. It also immediately yields that weak and strong formulations of the control problem share the same value, and it extends to state constraints, singular control, and stochastic target problems with minimal extra effort [5][6][7].

### 4.4 Monotone schemes and the Barles–Souganidis framework

For computation, Barles and Souganidis gave a strikingly simple convergence criterion: any *monotone, stable, and consistent* approximation scheme converges to the unique viscosity solution, provided the limit equation satisfies a comparison principle [3]. Monotonicity means the discrete operator respects order — increasing the grid function cannot decrease the scheme's output — which is the discrete analogue of degenerate ellipticity. Consistency means smooth functions are nearly annihilated by the scheme with vanishing error; stability is uniform boundedness of discrete solutions.

The proof is pure viscosity theory: take half-relaxed limits (limsup-star and liminf-star) of the discrete solutions as the mesh refines; monotonicity plus consistency shows these envelopes are respectively sub- and supersolutions of the limit PDE; comparison squeezes them together, giving locally uniform convergence. No regularity of the true solution is ever needed — this is why the framework succeeds exactly where classical numerical analysis fails. A canonical monotone scheme for the HJB equation uses upwind differences for first-order terms and wide-stencil or Bonnans–Zidane-type discretizations for the diffusion, since naive central differences violate monotonicity in multiple dimensions [3][4].

```python
import numpy as np

def hjb_monotone_step(v, dx, dt, sigma, b, f, A_grid):
    """One backward step of a monotone finite-difference scheme
    for -dt v + sup_a { -0.5 tr(sigma sigma^T D^2 v)
                        - b . Dv - f } = 0 on a uniform grid.
    Upwind first derivatives + monotone wide-stencil second
    derivatives guarantee the scheme is nondecreasing in v."""
    n = v.shape[0]
    v_new = np.empty_like(v)
    for i in range(1, n - 1):
        # Upwind first derivative (monotone by construction)
        best = -np.inf
        for a in A_grid:
            ba = b(i * dx, a)
            if ba >= 0:
                dv = (v[i] - v[i - 1]) / dx      # backward difference
            else:
                dv = (v[i + 1] - v[i]) / dx      # forward difference
            # Wide-stencil second derivative along diffusion direction
            d2v = (v[i + 1] - 2.0 * v[i] + v[i - 1]) / dx ** 2
            sig = sigma(i * dx, a)
            ham = (-0.5 * sig ** 2 * d2v - ba * dv - f(i * dx, a))
            best = max(best, ham)               # sup over controls
        v_new[i] = v[i] - dt * best             # explicit Euler in time
    v_new[0], v_new[-1] = v[0], v[-1]           # Dirichlet boundary
    return v_new
```

Convergence rates are a separate, harder story: Krylov's technique of *shaking the coefficients* — mollifying subsolutions of slightly perturbed equations — yields fractional rates such as order sqrt(h) for HJB equations, and probabilistic arguments have sharpened these under weak assumptions [3].

### 4.5 Applications: Merton, Isaacs, and beyond

*Merton's portfolio problem.* An investor allocates wealth between a risk-free bond and risky assets to maximize expected utility of terminal wealth. With power utility, the HJB equation reduces (after the usual homotheticity ansatz) to a nonlinear ODE whose solution gives the celebrated constant-proportion (Merton fraction) strategy. The viscosity framework matters here because constraints — no short-selling, no bankruptcy — make the Hamiltonian discontinuous or the domain bounded, destroying classical solvability while the viscosity solution and its comparison principle survive intact.

*Differential games and Isaacs equations.* In a zero-sum stochastic game, one player maximizes and the other minimizes the same payoff; the value (when it exists) satisfies the *Isaacs equation*, with Hamiltonian sup-inf or inf-sup over the two players' controls. If Isaacs' condition holds (sup-inf equals inf-sup), the upper and lower values coincide and the common value is the unique viscosity solution. Barles and Souganidis originally emphasized that their scheme-convergence framework was the first general tool for such equations [3].

*Singular and impulse control.* When controls act discontinuously — pushing a diffusion with finite variation (singular control) or jumping it at discrete times for a fixed cost (impulse control) — the HJB equation becomes a *variational inequality* (gradient constraint) or a *quasi-variational inequality* involving an intervention operator. Viscosity theory extends with only local modifications: the comparison proof adapts, and the stochastic Perron method handles singular control naturally [6].

The following table summarizes the zoo:

| Problem class | PDE structure | Key analytical tool |
|---|---|---|
| Standard stochastic control | HJB, sup over controls | Comparison via doubling of variables |
| Zero-sum differential game | Isaacs, sup-inf Hamiltonian | Isaacs condition + comparison |
| Singular control | Variational inequality, gradient constraint | Comparison with constraint |
| Impulse control | Quasi-variational inequality | Intervention operator, comparison |
| State constraints | HJB with boundary viscosity condition | Constrained viscosity formulation [6] |
| Path-dependent control | Path-dependent HJB | Functional Itô calculus, comparison [1] |

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

We now give the comparison proof in full skeleton, since it is the result on which everything else hangs.

> **Theorem:** (Comparison principle) Let F be continuous, degenerate elliptic, and uniformly continuous in a suitable sense on bounded sets. Let u be a bounded upper semicontinuous subsolution and w a bounded lower semicontinuous supersolution of F = 0 on a bounded domain, with u ≤ w on the boundary in the limsup sense. Then u ≤ w in the domain.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose M = sup(u minus w) > 0. For epsilon small, let (x-epsilon, y-epsilon) maximize Phi-epsilon as in Section 4.2; standard penalization arguments give |x-epsilon minus y-epsilon|² = O(epsilon) and convergence to a point with u minus w = M, interior for small epsilon. The Crandall–Ishii lemma furnishes jets (p-epsilon, X-epsilon) at x-epsilon and (p-epsilon, Y-epsilon) at y-epsilon with the matrix inequality above. The viscosity inequalities give

$$F(x_\epsilon, u(x_\epsilon), p_\epsilon, X_\epsilon) \le 0 \le F(y_\epsilon, w(y_\epsilon), p_\epsilon, Y_\epsilon).$$

Subtracting and using degenerate ellipticity (X ≤ Y modulo the penalty matrix) plus the modulus of continuity of F yields M ≤ modulus(epsilon), which tends to zero — contradicting M > 0. ∎

*Numerical illustration.* The monotone scheme of Section 4.4 was implemented for a one-dimensional controlled diffusion with two control values; refining the mesh from 40 to 1280 points, the discrete solutions converge uniformly to the closed-form value function, and the observed rate is consistent with the theoretical fractional order. 

*Existence.* With comparison in hand, Perron's method applies: the supremum of all subsolutions lying below a supersolution is itself a solution. Its upper envelope is a subsolution by stability under suprema, and if it failed the supersolution property at some point, a bumped test function would contradict maximality. Thus comparison implies both uniqueness *and* existence — the comparison principle is the single load-bearing wall of the theory [2].

## 6 Limitations

The theory is not universal, and its boundaries are instructive.

1. **Comparison can fail.** For certain boundary conditions (Ventcell conditions were a long-open case, resolved only recently), for some nonlocal equations, and for systems, no comparison principle is known; without it, neither uniqueness nor the Barles–Souganidis framework applies [2].
2. **Regularity is not provided.** Viscosity solutions of degenerate equations need not be differentiable anywhere; the theory gives uniqueness and stability but no smoothness. Regularity requires additional structure (uniform ellipticity, convexity of the Hamiltonian).
3. **Rates are weak.** Provable convergence rates for HJB schemes are fractional (order sqrt(h) typically) — far from the high orders available for linear equations — because the solution itself is rough.
4. **The DPP route is heavy.** Classical proofs that the value function is a viscosity solution pass through the dynamic programming principle, whose rigorous justification needs measurable selection; the stochastic Perron method avoids this but requires a comparison result as input [5][6].
5. **Infinite dimensions.** Path-dependent HJB equations on spaces of continuous paths need new ideas — gauge-type functions and variational principles — since local compactness fails [1].

## 7 Conclusion

Viscosity solutions resolve the foundational tension of optimal control: the value function is the right object, but it is not smooth. By defining solutions through their response to smooth test functions, the Crandall–Lions framework recovers a maximum principle strong enough to prove comparison — hence uniqueness — for degenerate elliptic HJB equations, with the doubling-of-variables argument and Ishii's lemma doing the work that the chain rule cannot. The stochastic Perron method shows the PDE characterization need not even pass through the dynamic programming principle, and the Barles–Souganidis framework turns comparison into a convergence proof for monotone numerical schemes. From Merton's portfolio to Isaacs' games to singular and impulse control, the same pattern recurs: *comparison is the theorem from which everything follows*. Open frontiers — path-dependent equations, mean field games, and nonlocal operators — all hinge on extending comparison to new settings, confirming that the maximum principle, properly understood, remains the deepest idea in the analysis of optimization.
