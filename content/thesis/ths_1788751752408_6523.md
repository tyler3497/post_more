# ML-DSA and SPHINCS+ Post-Quantum Digital Signatures: Module-Lattice Rejection Sampling, Stateless Hash-Based Hypertrees, and Hardware Acceleration for TLS 1.3 Authentication

## Abstract

The advent of large-scale quantum computing threatens every classical digital signature deployed today: Shor's algorithm collapses the factoring and discrete-logarithm assumptions underpinning RSA, DSA, and ECDSA, leaving authentication the most brittle pillar of the cryptographic ecosystem. This thesis presents a rigorous treatment of the two post-quantum signature standards finalized by NIST — ML-DSA (FIPS 204), a module-lattice construction descended from CRYSTALS-Dilithium, and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205), the stateless hash-based scheme standardized from SPHINCS+ — through the lens of their deployment in TLS 1.3 authentication. We dissect the Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts rejection sampling that makes ML-DSA's zero-knowledge signing simulation possible, the hypertree composition of FORS, WOTS+, and XMSS layers that frees SPHINCS+ from statefulness, and the hardware acceleration strategies — NTT pipelines, vectorized SHAKE, dedicated coprocessors — required to make both schemes viable inside the TLS 1.3 handshake budget. Empirical benchmarks on x86-64 and ARM platforms, a proof sketch of strong existential unforgeability, and a candid assessment of limitations complete the study.

## 1 Introduction

Digital signatures are the connective tissue of modern trust. Every TLS handshake, software update, code-signing certificate, and cryptocurrency transaction leans on the unforgeability of a signature scheme whose security argument has been anchored to the presumed classical intractability of factoring large integers or computing discrete logarithms [1]. Peter Shor's 1994 polynomial-time quantum algorithm for both problems [2] transforms that presumption from an engineering assumption into a countdown: any long-lived authentication infrastructure built on RSA, DSA, ECDSA, or EdDSA is retroactively vulnerable the moment a cryptographically relevant quantum computer materializes — the *harvest-now, forge-later* threat model, applying with equal force to signatures whose verification keys remain public for decades.

NIST answered with its Post-Quantum Cryptography standardization project, finalizing three standards in August 2024: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM for key establishment), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA for signatures), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA for signatures) [3][4]. This thesis focuses on the two signature standards, and specifically on their use in the most latency-critical authentication context on the Internet: the **TLS 1.3 handshake**. In TLS 1.3, the server's identity is established when the client verifies a `CertificateVerify` signature over the handshake transcript — an operation that must complete in milliseconds, survive middlebox ossification, and interoperate across a staggering diversity of hardware [5].

ML-DSA and SLH-DSA could hardly be more different in their foundations. ML-DSA is a *module-lattice* scheme whose security reduces to the Module Learning With Errors (MLWE) and Module Short Integer Solution (MSIS) problems; it signs with small secret vectors, compresses commitments via high/low-bit decomposition, and — critically — employs **rejection sampling** to ensure that published signatures statistically conceal the secret key. SLH-DSA is *stateless and hash-based*: its security rests solely on the second-preimage and pseudorandomness properties of its hash functions, at the price of signatures an order of magnitude larger, built from a **hypertree** of XMSS layers, WOTS+ one-time signatures, and FORS few-time signatures.

Our contributions are organized as follows. Section 2 surveys the mathematical background: module lattices, the Fiat–Shamir paradigm with aborts, and the hash-based signature genealogy from Lamport to SPHINCS+. Section 3 describes our methodology, including a from-scratch Python reference model of both schemes' core loops used to validate the algebraic claims. Section 4 conducts the deep technical dive. Section 5 presents empirical benchmarks and proof sketches. Section 6 confronts limitations honestly. Section 7 concludes with deployment guidance.

> Theorem: Under the Module-LWE and Module-SIS assumptions with the FIPS 204 parameter choices, ML-DSA-65 achieves strong existential unforgeability under chosen-message attack (SUF-CMA) in the quantum random-oracle model, with a tightness loss bounded by the number of signing queries times the rejection-sampling acceptance probability [1].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Module Lattices and Hard Problems

A *lattice* is a discrete additive subgroup of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Module lattices — lattices that are modules over the ring $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^{256}+1)$ — balance key size against assumption strength. The two problems relevant to ML-DSA are:

- **Module-LWE**: distinguish $(A, As_1 + s_2)$ from uniform, where $A \in R_q^{k \times \ell}$ and $s_1, s_2$ are short secrets. This is the *decisional* problem protecting key indistinguishability.
- **Module-SIS**: given $A$, find a short nonzero $z$ with $Az = 0$ (or $Az = w$ for a target $w$). This is the *search* problem protecting unforgeability: a forged signature essentially yields such a short vector.

ML-DSA instantiates three parameter sets — ML-DSA-44, ML-DSA-65, ML-DSA-87 — targeting NIST security categories 2, 3, and 5 respectively, with public keys of 1312/1952/2592 bytes, secret keys of 2560/4032/4896 bytes, and signatures of 2420/3309/4627 bytes [6].

### 2.2 Fiat–Shamir with Aborts

Classical Fiat–Shamir transforms an interactive zero-knowledge proof into a signature by hashing the transcript to produce the challenge. The lattice analogue, pioneered by Lyubashevsky, signs by sampling a masking vector $y$, computing a commitment $w = Ay$, hashing to a sparse challenge $c$, and responding with $z = y + cs_1$. The fatal subtlety: the distribution of $z$ depends on the secret $s_1$. **Rejection sampling** fixes this — the signer repeatedly samples until $z$ falls in a "safe" interval independent of the secret, making the output distribution simulatable without the key [1][7]. FIPS 204 implements two rejection stages: one bounding $\|z\|_\infty$ and the low bits of $w - cs_2$, and a second ensuring the verifier can reconstruct the high bits of $w$ from the hint vector $h$ [1].

### 2.3 The Hash-Based Genealogy: Lamport → WOTS+ → XMSS → SPHINCS+

Hash-based signatures predate public-key cryptography's number-theoretic era: Lamport's 1979 one-time scheme signs each message bit with a preimage. Winternitz (WOTS) compresses this by signing base-$w$ digits via hash chains; WOTS+ adds per-position randomization and a checksum for tight proofs. XMSS organizes $2^h$ WOTS+ keypairs as leaves of a Merkle tree — but the signer must never reuse a leaf, imposing **statefulness**. SPHINCS+ abolishes state by nesting XMSS trees into a *hypertree* of $d$ layers and selecting leaves pseudorandomly, with a FORS (Forest of Random Subsets) few-time signature absorbing the residual collision risk. FIPS 205 standardizes 12 parameter sets (SHAKE- and SHA-2-based, at 128/192/256-bit levels, "small" and "fast" variants) [4][8].

### 2.4 TLS 1.3 Authentication

TLS 1.3 authenticates the server through `CertificateVerify`: a signature over the transcript hash, computed with the private key corresponding to the leaf certificate. The handshake's latency budget is dominated by round trips, so a signature scheme is viable in TLS 1.3 only if signing takes well under a millisecond on server hardware and verification is fast on the weakest client — typically a mobile ARM core [5]. ML-DSA-65's 3309-byte signature and 1952-byte public key fit comfortably in a single flight, while SLH-DSA-128s's 7856-byte signature strains typical record fragmentation but remains deployable.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis follows a reproduce-then-measure discipline. First, we implemented minimal Python reference models of (i) the ML-DSA signing loop with its two-stage rejection sampler over $R_q$, and (ii) the WOTS+ chain and FORS structures of SLH-DSA, cross-checked against the FIPS 204 pseudocode (Algorithms 2, 7) and the FIPS 205 algorithm suite [1][4]. These models are pedagogical, not constant-time, and exist to make every algebraic claim in Section 4 executable.

Second, we surveyed published benchmarks from the Open Quantum Safe project's `liboqs` integration and the `mldsa-native` codebase for cycle counts on x86-64 (AVX2) and AArch64 (Neon) [6][9].

We adopt the following working definitions. A scheme is **SUF-CMA** secure if no efficient adversary can forge a valid signature on any message after adaptive signing queries. The **quantum random-oracle model (QROM)** grants the adversary superposition access to hash functions. **Hedged signing** mixes fresh randomness with key-derived pseudorandomness, defending simultaneously against RNG failure and side-channel leakage of deterministic nonces [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 ML-DSA Signing: Masking, Commitments, and the Two-Stage Rejection Sampler

The ML-DSA signing procedure (FIPS 204, Algorithm 7, `ML-DSA.Sign_internal`) is best understood as a carefully instrumented Fiat–Shamir transcript. The private key contains the short secrets $s_1, s_2$, the public matrix seed $\rho$ (expanding to $A$), and key-derivation material. Signing proceeds as follows [1]:

1. **Message representative.** The signer forms $\mu = H(\text{tr} \parallel M)$, where $\text{tr}$ is a 64-byte hash of the public key. This domain separation delivers beyond-unforgeability (BUFF) properties: the signature commits to the public key, defeating key-substitution attacks [1].
2. **Masking.** Sample $y \in S_{\gamma_1}^\ell$ — a vector of polynomials with coefficients uniform in $[-\gamma_1, \gamma_1]$ — using the hedged randomness $\rho'$.
3. **Commitment.** Compute $w = Ay$, decompose into high and low bits, and keep $w_1 = \text{HighBits}_q(w, 2\gamma_2)$.
4. **Challenge.** Hash $c = H(\mu \parallel w_1)$ into a sparse polynomial with exactly $\tau$ nonzero coefficients in $\{-1, 1\}$.
5. **Response.** Compute $z = y + c s_1$.
6. **Rejection stage 1.** Restart unless $\|z\|_\infty < \gamma_1 - \beta$ and $\|\text{LowBits}_q(w - c s_2, 2\gamma_2)\|_\infty < \gamma_2 - \beta$. This guarantees the signature is independent of the secret and that the low bits do not corrupt verification.
7. **Hint and rejection stage 2.** Compute the hint vector $h = \text{MakeHint}_q(-ct_0, w - cs_2 + ct_0, 2\gamma_2)$ and restart unless $\|ct_0\|_\infty < \gamma_2$ and the Hamming weight of $h$ is at most $\omega$. The hint lets the verifier recover $w_1$ from $Az - ct_1 2^d$ without the signer transmitting the full $w$ — the compression trick that keeps signatures compact.

The expected number of repetitions is small (roughly 4–5 for ML-DSA-65), and because rejection is a function of public quantities and the candidate $z$ alone, a simulator can reproduce the transcript distribution without the secret key — the crux of the SUF-CMA proof [7].

```python
def mldsa_sign_loop(A, s1, s2, t0, mu, gamma1, gamma2, beta, tau, omega):
    """Simplified ML-DSA signing loop with two-stage rejection sampling."""
    attempt = 0
    while True:
        attempt += 1
        y = sample_masking_vector(gamma1)          # y <- S_{gamma1}^l
        w = A @ y                                  # commitment in R_q^k
        w1 = high_bits(w, 2 * gamma2)              # compress: keep high bits
        c = hash_to_sparse_challenge(mu, w1, tau)  # Fiat-Shamir challenge
        z = y + c * s1                             # response
        # --- Rejection stage 1: hide the secret ---
        if norm_inf(z) >= gamma1 - beta:
            continue
        if norm_inf(low_bits(w - c * s2, 2 * gamma2)) >= gamma2 - beta:
            continue
        # --- Hint + rejection stage 2: compressible verification ---
        h = make_hint(-c * t0, w - c * s2 + c * t0, 2 * gamma2)
        if norm_inf(c * t0) >= gamma2:
            continue
        if hamming_weight(h) > omega:
            continue
        return (z, h, c), attempt
```

Verification recomputes $w_1' = \text{UseHint}_q(h, Az - c t_1 2^d, 2\gamma_2)$ and accepts iff $\|z\|_\infty < \gamma_1 - \beta$, the hint weight is bounded, and $c = H(\mu \parallel w_1')$ [1].

### 4.2 The Arithmetic Engine: NTT, Bit-Packing, and Parameter Geometry

Everything in ML-DSA happens in $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_{8380417}[x]/(x^{256}+1)$. The modulus $q = 8380417 = 2^{23} - 2^{13} + 1$ is NTT-friendly: $q \equiv 1 \pmod{512}$, so a full negacyclic NTT of length 256 exists without lifting to a larger ring. Matrix–vector products $Ay$ — the dominant cost — become pointwise multiplications in the NTT domain, giving the scheme its speed: key generation, signing, and verification are each dominated by a handful of NTTs and inverse NTTs [6][9].

The parameter geometry is a three-way tug-of-war:

| Parameter set | $(k, \ell)$ | $\gamma_1$ | $\tau$ | Sig. size (B) | NIST level |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA-44 | (4, 4) | $2^{17}$ | 39 | 2420 | 2 |
| ML-DSA-65 | (6, 5) | $2^{19}$ | 49 | 3309 | 3 |
| ML-DSA-87 | (8, 7) | $2^{19}$ | 60 | 4627 | 5 |

The FIPS choices sit on the Pareto frontier validated by the Dilithium team's cryptanalysis [1][7].


### 4.3 SLH-DSA: FORS, WOTS+, and the Stateless Hypertree

SLH-DSA inverts ML-DSA's design philosophy: instead of structured hardness, it assumes only that SHA-2/SHAKE behave as pseudorandom, second-preimage-resistant functions — assumptions already required of the surrounding TLS stack. The construction is a composition of three primitives [4][8]:

1. **WOTS+** (Winternitz one-time signature, FIPS 205 §5). The private key is $\text{len}$ random $n$-byte strings; the public key is the head of $\text{len}$ hash chains of length $w-1 = 15$. Signing walks each chain forward by the message digit (base 16) plus a checksum. Security: inverting any chain segment breaks second-preimage resistance.
2. **XMSS** (FIPS 205 §6). $2^{h'}$ WOTS+ keypairs become the leaves of a Merkle tree; the root authenticates all of them. Stateful in isolation — the signer must track the leaf index.
3. **Hypertree** (FIPS 205 §7). $d$ layers of XMSS trees; a WOTS+ signature at layer $i$ signs the root of the tree at layer $i-1$. The leaf index is chosen *pseudorandomly* from the message hash, and the FORS layer at the bottom absorbs the birthday-scale leaf-collision probability: FORS signs the message digest with $k$ trees of height $a$, revealing one leaf per tree per signature — a *few-time* signature whose security degrades gracefully with reuse [8].

```python
def wots_chain(x, start, steps, tweak_base):
    """WOTS+ hash chain: apply the tweakable hash `steps` times from `start`."""
    for i in range(start, start + steps):
        adrs = tweak_base.with_chain_position(i)
        x = tweakable_hash(adrs, x)   # F(x) = H(PK.seed, ADRS, x)
    return x

def wots_sign(message_digits, private_key):
    """Sign base-w digits by walking each chain forward."""
    signature, checksum = [], 0
    for digit, sk in zip(message_digits, private_key):
        checksum += (W - 1) - digit
        signature.append(wots_chain(sk, 0, digit, ADRS_WOTS_PRF))
    for digit, sk in zip(to_base_w(checksum), private_key[len(message_digits):]):
        signature.append(wots_chain(sk, 0, digit, ADRS_WOTS_PRF))
    return signature
```

The hypertree converts state management into probability management: with $h = 66$ and $d = 22$ in SLH-DSA-128f, the signer produces $2^{64}$ signatures per key while FORS keeps forgery probability negligible — at the price of 7–50 KB signatures and megahash signing costs [4].

### 4.4 Hardware Acceleration and the TLS 1.3 Handshake Budget

For ML-DSA, acceleration targets three kernels [9]:

- **NTT/INTT pipelines.** The 256-point NTT decomposes into 8 layers of 128 butterflies; hardware implementations unroll these into streaming datapaths achieving one polynomial multiplication per ~256 cycles, with modular reduction by the pseudo-Mersenne $q$ folded into the butterfly. ARMv8 cores with Neon process four coefficients per lane; AVX2 eight.
- **SHAKE-128/256 (Keccak-$f[1600]$).** Matrix expansion, masking-vector sampling, and challenge hashing are all SHAKE-bound. The 24-round Keccak permutation maps well to vectorized lanes and to dedicated instructions (e.g., ARMv8.4 SHA3 extensions), yielding 3–5× speedups over scalar code.
- **Rejection-sampling control.** The variable-iteration signing loop resists naive pipelining; hedged implementations pre-generate candidate $y$ vectors in a batch and filter in parallel, trading memory for constant-time discipline.

For SLH-DSA, the kernel is the **tweakable hash** $T_\ell(\text{PK.seed}, \text{ADRS}, M)$ — invoked hundreds of thousands of times per signature. Hardware wins come from hashing many independent ADRS domains in parallel (the WOTS+ chains and FORS trees are embarrassingly parallel) and from SHA-256/SHAKE instruction extensions [4].

IETF drafts now assign `mldsa44/65/87` code points in `signature_algorithms`, with hybrid X25519+ML-KEM groups for forward secrecy [5]. Measured reality: ML-DSA-65 signs in ~150k cycles (~50 µs at 3 GHz) — *faster* than RSA-2048 — verifies in ~200k cycles, and inflates the handshake by ~5 KB: fine for data-center TLS, marginal for constrained IoT [5][6].

### 4.5 Security Reductions and the QROM Proof Sketch

The ML-DSA security argument proceeds in the now-classical sequence [7]: (i) the rejection sampler makes real signatures statistically indistinguishable from simulated ones, so a forger against the real scheme forges against a keyless simulator; (ii) the simulator's commitments are MLWE-pseudorandom, reducing to Module-LWE; (iii) a successful forgery $(z, h, c)$ on a fresh challenge, combined via *forking* with a second forgery $(z', h', c')$ on the same commitment, yields $(z - z') = (c - c')s_1$ — a short MSIS solution. The loss is quadratic in the number of hash queries (the forking lemma), standard for Fiat–Shamir signatures.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmarks

Cycle counts measured on a 3.0 GHz x86-64 core with AVX2 (via `liboqs`/`mldsa-native`) and on an ARM Cortex-A72 (Neon), rounded to two significant figures [6][9]:

| Scheme | KeyGen | Sign | Verify | Sig. size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA-44 (AVX2) | 120k | 380k | 130k | 2420 B |
| ML-DSA-65 (AVX2) | 180k | 550k | 200k | 3309 B |
| ML-DSA-87 (AVX2) | 290k | 900k | 330k | 4627 B |
| ML-DSA-65 (Neon) | 450k | 1.4M | 520k | 3309 B |
| SLH-DSA-128s (SHAKE, ref) | — | ~120M | ~8M | 7856 B |
| SLH-DSA-128f (SHAKE, AVX2) | — | ~25M | ~1.2M | 17088 B |
| ECDSA P-256 (ref) | 300k | 350k | 700k | 64 B |

Two observations follow. First, ML-DSA-65 *outperforms* ECDSA-P256 at signing and verification on AVX2 — the performance argument against PQC migration is, for signatures, already lost. Second, SLH-DSA verification is genuinely cheap (~1M cycles for the fast variants), suiting verify-heavy workloads like firmware signing; its signing cost and signature size disqualify it from interactive TLS. Hedged signing adds roughly 15% overhead versus deterministic signing, a modest premium for RNG-failure resilience [1].

### 5.2 Proof Sketch: SUF-CMA of ML-DSA

We sketch the reduction establishing the theorem of Section 1, following the Dilithium analysis [7] as adapted in FIPS 204's security rationale.

> Theorem: If Module-LWE$_{k,\ell}$ and Module-SIS$_{k,\ell,\beta}$ are hard, then ML-DSA is SUF-CMA secure in the QROM.

*Proof sketch.* Consider a forger $\mathcal{F}$ making $q_s$ signing queries and $q_h$ hash queries. Replace the real signer with a simulator that samples $z$ uniformly from the acceptance region and programs the random oracle — rejection sampling guarantees the statistical distance is bounded by $2^{-\lambda}$ per query. Next, replace the public key $t = As_1 + s_2$ with uniform: any distinguisher breaks decisional Module-LWE. Now run $\mathcal{F}$; with probability $\epsilon$ it outputs a forgery $(z, c)$ on message $M$ with $c = H(\mu \parallel w_1)$ fresh. Rewind to the hash query and reprogram with fresh $c' \neq c$ (quantum forking lemma, loss $\approx q_h$). The two forgeries combine into a nonzero short MSIS vector — contradiction. ∎

## 6 Limitations

Candor requires enumerating what these standards do *not* solve.

1. **Signature and key sizes remain large.** ML-DSA-65's 3.3 KB signatures are ~50× an ECDSA signature, inflating certificate chains and stressing middleboxes [5].
2. **Side-channel surface.** Rejection sampling's variable iteration counts, NTT access patterns, and Keccak handling all demand constant-time, masked implementations; `mldsa-native` verifies functional correctness, not leakage [9].
3. **SLH-DSA signing latency.** Tens of millions of hash invocations per signature relegate SLH-DSA to offline signing (code signing, PKI roots) for now; hardware SHAKE pipelines narrow but do not close the gap with lattice schemes.
4. **Stateful alternatives excluded.** LMS/XMSS (SP 800-208) offer smaller signatures than SLH-DSA but demand perfect state management — a single VM snapshot rollback is a key-compromise event. We endorse NIST's conservative choice to standardize the stateless variant [4].
5. **Cryptanalytic youth.** Module lattices have a decade of scrutiny versus four for factoring; algebraic attacks exploiting the ring structure remain the principal long-term risk, which is precisely why a hash-based backstop matters.
6. **Migration complexity.** Hybrid deployments double the negotiation surface; downgrade-protection analysis for mixed classical–PQC handshakes is still maturing in the IETF [5].

## 7 Conclusion

ML-DSA and SLH-DSA represent two complementary answers to the same existential question. ML-DSA's module-lattice rejection sampler yields signatures fast enough to *beat* ECDSA in TLS 1.3 handshakes, with a security reduction to well-studied lattice problems (FIPS 204). SLH-DSA's stateless hypertree rests its security on hash-function sanity alone, immune to future lattice cryptanalysis (FIPS 205), at the cost of kilobytes and megacycles.

For TLS 1.3 authentication, the engineering recommendation is clear: **deploy ML-DSA-65 as the primary post-quantum signature**, and **maintain SLH-DSA as the conservative alternative** for root CAs and firmware signing where verification cost dominates. Hybrid classical–PQC handshakes should be the transition vehicle, not the destination. The quantum threat is a matter of *when*, not *if*; with FIPS 204 and FIPS 205, the standards — and the silicon to run them — are ready.
