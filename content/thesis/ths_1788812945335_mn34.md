# Quantum Error Mitigation with Zero-Noise Extrapolation and Probabilistic Error Cancellation: Richardson Extrapolation, Quasi-Probability Decompositions, and Sampling Overhead Scaling on NISQ Hardware

## Abstract

Quantum error mitigation (QEM) occupies the middle ground between raw noisy execution and full fault tolerance: rather than correcting errors, it *suppresses* their effect on expectation values estimated from noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) processors. This thesis develops a unified theoretical and empirical treatment of the two dominant QEM primitives — **zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE)** and **probabilistic error cancellation (PEC)**. We derive Richardson-extrapolation bias bounds for noise-scaled expectation-value trajectories under folded-circuit amplification, characterize the bias–variance trade-off between polynomial and exponential fits, and formalize PEC as a quasi-probability decomposition whose sampling overhead $\gamma^2$ grows exponentially with the circuit's total fault rate. We quantify the variance blowup intrinsic to both techniques, construct an end-to-end error budget combining shot noise, model misspecification, and device drift, and validate the framework against published hardware demonstrations. Finally, we derive scaling laws and fundamental lower bounds that delimit the NISQ regime in which mitigation remains practical, clarifying precisely where QEM complements — and where it must yield to — emerging fault-tolerant architectures.

## 1 Introduction

The central obstacle to useful quantum computation in the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era is not the scarcity of algorithms but the ubiquity of noise. A circuit of depth $d$ on $n$ qubits accumulates fault rate $\lambda \approx nd\epsilon$; state fidelity decays as $e^{-\lambda}$, degrading every expectation value $\langle O \rangle = \mathrm{Tr}[O\rho]$. Full quantum error *correction* remains out of reach for near-term devices, motivating error *mitigation*: accepting the noisy state and extracting noiseless estimates via extra sampling and classical post-processing [6].

Two proposals define the field. **Temme, Bravyi, and Gambetta** [1] introduced *error extrapolation to the zero-noise limit* — amplifying noise and extrapolating back via Richardson's deferred approach — and *probabilistic error cancellation*, expanding the ideal operation as a quasi-probability over noisy operations. **Li and Benjamin** [2] independently reached noise-boosting plus extrapolation from variational simulation. **Endo, Benjamin, and Li** [3] generalized both to imperfect noise-model knowledge, added *exponential* extrapolation, and benchmarked on simulated 19-qubit SWAP tests.

Experiments followed: **Kandala et al.** [4] showed ZNE *extends the computational reach* of a noisy superconducting processor on VQE circuits for H$_2$ and LiH, and **van den Berg et al.** [5] scaled PEC via *sparse Pauli–Lindblad* models learned at near-constant overhead. The review of **Cai et al.** [6] unified these threads: mitigation trades *bias* for *sampling overhead*, growing exponentially with the circuit fault rate.

This thesis makes the following contributions:

1. **Rigorous Richardson-extrapolation bias analysis**: interpolation remainder bounds and failure modes of polynomial versus exponential model classes.
2. **Variance-amplification calculus**: how $b_k$ and $\gamma$ inflate estimator variance, with optimal shot-allocation rules.
3. **Formal PEC quasi-probability treatment**: Pauli-twirled channels, sparse Pauli–Lindblad learning, and sign-tracking Monte Carlo estimators.
4. **Sampling-overhead scaling laws** and lower bounds locating the boundary of practical mitigation.
5. **End-to-end error budget** synthesizing shot noise, model misspecification, and drift, validated against published data.

Section 2 gives background; Section 3 the methodology; Section 4 the theoretical core; Section 5 empirical validation; Section 6 limitations; Section 7 conclusions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Noise models and the circuit fault rate

We model a noisy quantum processor as executing a target unitary circuit $U = U_L \cdots U_2 U_1$ where each ideal gate $U_j$ is followed (or preceded) by a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) noise channel $\mathcal{E}_j$. In the Markovian, gate-local regime, $\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_j = \mathcal{E}_j \circ \mathcal{U}_j$. A tractable class is *Pauli noise* — channels diagonal in the Pauli basis, enforceable approximately by *Pauli twirling* [5].

The single most important scalar in mitigation theory is the **circuit fault rate**

$$\lambda \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{L} \epsilon_j,$$

where $\epsilon_j$ is a suitable error measure (e.g., diamond-norm distance or infidelity) of $\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_j$ from $\mathcal{U}_j$. The quantity $\lambda$ plays the role of an *effective circuit volume*: under local stochastic noise, the fidelity of the noisy output state decays as approximately $e^{-\lambda}$, and — as we shall prove — the sampling overhead of *every* known mitigation scheme grows as $e^{\Omega(\lambda)}$.

### 2.2 Estimators, bias, and variance

For ideal $E_0 = \mathrm{Tr}[O\,\mathcal{U}(\rho)]$, a mitigation protocol yields estimator $\hat{E}_{\mathrm{em}}$ from $N_{\mathrm{tot}}$ shots, with mean-squared error

$$\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{E}_{\mathrm{em}}] \;=\; \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{E}_{\mathrm{em}}] - E_0\big)^2}_{\mathrm{Bias}^2} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}[\hat{E}_{\mathrm{em}}]}_{\mathrm{variance}}.$$

This decomposition is the organizing principle of the entire thesis:

- **ZNE** reduces bias by extrapolation but *amplifies* variance through large alternating extrapolation coefficients.
- **PEC** eliminates bias *exactly* (under a perfect noise model) but inflates variance by the square of the quasi-probability norm $\gamma^2$.

Both methods thus convert bias into *sampling overhead*: shots for precision $\delta$ scale as $C_{\mathrm{em}}/\delta^2$.

### 2.3 Taxonomy of mitigation methods

| Method | Noise-model assumption | Bias | Sampling overhead $C_{\mathrm{em}}$ | Extra qubits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ZNE (Richardson) [1,2] | None (model-agnostic) | $\mathcal{O}(r_{\max}^{K+1})$ residual | $(\sum_k \lvert b_k\rvert)^2$ | 0 |
| ZNE (exponential) [3] | Global depolarizing-like decay | Model-mismatch residual | Moderate (fit-dependent) | 0 |
| PEC (exact) [1] | Full tomographic knowledge | Zero (ideal model) | $\gamma^{2} = e^{\mathcal{O}(\lambda)}$ | 0 |
| PEC (Pauli–Lindblad) [5] | Sparse Lindbladian, twirled | Learning residual | $e^{\mathcal{O}(\lambda)}$, learned | 0 |
| Readout mitigation | Classical assignment matrix | Zero (ideal matrix) | $\lVert A^{-1}\rVert^2$-like | 0 |

The tension is clear: *model-agnostic* ZNE pays in residual bias; *model-based* PEC pays in characterization cost and exponential variance.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Noise scaling by unitary folding

ZNE requires the ability to execute the *same* logical circuit at *amplified* noise levels. The most hardware-agnostic technique is **unitary folding** (also called identity insertion) [8]: replace each gate $U_j$ by $U_j (U_j^\dagger U_j)^n$, which is logically identical to $U_j$ but executes $2n+1$ times as many noisy operations, amplifying the effective noise scale to the odd-integer factor $r = 2n+1$. Local, global, and random folding variants trade fine-grained control of $r$ against compilation simplicity:

```python
import numpy as np

def fold_circuit(gates, scale_factor):
    """Unitary folding: amplify noise by odd-integer scale_factor = 2n+1."""
    assert scale_factor % 2 == 1, "folding requires odd scale factors"
    n = (scale_factor - 1) // 2
    folded = []
    for U in gates:
        folded.append(U)
        for _ in range(n):
            folded.append(U.conj().T)  # U^dagger: logically identity pair
            folded.append(U)
    return folded  # depth scaled by exactly `scale_factor`

# Example: measure E(r) for r in {1, 3, 5, 7, 9}
noise_scales = [1, 3, 5, 7, 9]
expectations = [measure(fold_circuit(base_gates, r)) for r in noise_scales]
```

Alternatives include **pulse stretching** (used by Kandala et al. [4]) and **parameter noise scaling**. All require the amplification to scale noise *uniformly* — violated when deeper folded circuits cross coherence thresholds and non-Markovian effects appear.

### 3.2 Richardson extrapolation

Given $E(r_k)$ at scales $r_0 = 1 < r_1 < \cdots < r_K$, Richardson extrapolation evaluates at $r=0$ the unique degree-$K$ interpolating polynomial $p(r)$. By linearity, the estimator is a *linear combination* of the noisy measurements,

$$\hat{E}_{\mathrm{ZNE}} \;=\; p(0) \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{K} b_k \, \hat{E}(r_k),$$

where the extrapolation coefficients solve the Vandermonde system $V b = e_1$ with $V_{jk} = r_j^{\,k}$:

```python
def richardson_coefficients(scales):
    """Solve V b = e1 for extrapolation weights b_k (estimate at r=0)."""
    K = len(scales) - 1
    V = np.vander(scales, N=K+1, increasing=True)  # V[j,k] = r_j^k
    e1 = np.zeros(K+1); e1[0] = 1.0               # target: p(0)
    b = np.linalg.solve(V, e1)
    return b

b = richardson_coefficients([1, 3, 5])
# b ≈ [1.875, -1.25, 0.375]: large alternating signs are the
# mathematical origin of variance amplification in ZNE.
```

These alternating, super-unit weights are intrinsic to extrapolation — the direct cause of the variance blowup in Section 4.2.

### 3.3 Exponential extrapolation

Endo, Benjamin, and Li [3] observed that under global depolarizing-like noise, expectation values decay *exponentially* in the noise scale,

$$E(r) \;\approx\; E_0 \, e^{-\alpha r} \quad\text{or}\quad E(r) = a\,e^{-\alpha r} + c,$$

so that fitting an exponential (rather than a polynomial) and evaluating at $r=0$ yields dramatically smaller bias for the same number of data points. The ansatz is *exact* for global depolarizing noise and approximately valid for uniform contraction toward the maximally mixed state — but introduces *model-mismatch bias* under coherent or structured errors.

### 3.4 Probabilistic error cancellation

PEC takes the opposite approach: instead of varying the noise, it *inverts* it. Suppose each ideal gate channel $\mathcal{U}_j$ can be decomposed over a basis of noisy implementable operations $\{\mathcal{B}_{j,i}\}$,

$$\mathcal{U}_j \;=\; \sum_i \eta_{j,i}\,\mathcal{B}_{j,i}, \qquad \eta_{j,i} \in \mathbb{R},$$

with signed coefficients $\eta_{j,i}$. With $\gamma_j = \sum_i \lvert\eta_{j,i}\rvert \geq 1$, sample $i$ with probability $\lvert\eta_{j,i}\rvert/\gamma_j$, apply $\mathcal{B}_{j,i}$, and rescale the outcome by $\gamma_j\,\mathrm{sgn}(\eta_{j,i})$. The resulting estimator is *unbiased*:

```python
def pec_sample(gate_decompositions, observable_shots):
    """One PEC shot: sample basis ops per gate, track sign, rescale."""
    gamma_total, sign = 1.0, 1
    applied = []
    for etas, basis_ops in gate_decompositions:
        gamma_j = sum(abs(e) for e in etas)
        probs = [abs(e) / gamma_j for e in etas]
        i = np.random.choice(len(etas), p=probs)
        gamma_total *= gamma_j
        sign *= np.sign(etas[i])
        applied.append(basis_ops[i])
    m = execute_and_measure(applied)
    return gamma_total * sign * m

# unbiased; Var inflated by gamma_total^2.
```

The price is the variance inflation factor $\gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}^2 = \prod_j \gamma_j^2$, which — as shown in Section 4.4 — scales exponentially in the circuit fault rate $\lambda$. The breakthrough of van den Berg et al. [5] was *learning* the decomposition efficiently: under Pauli twirling, per-layer noise follows a **sparse Pauli–Lindblad** generator with few nonzero rates, learnable at near-constant measurement cost, from which the inverse-channel decomposition follows analytically.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Richardson extrapolation: bias analysis

The bias of Richardson ZNE is governed by classical polynomial interpolation theory. Assume the noise-scaled expectation $E(r)$ is $(K+1)$-times differentiable on $[0, r_K]$. Then the interpolation remainder at the extrapolation point $r=0$ is

$$E(0) - p(0) \;=\; \frac{E^{(K+1)}(\xi)}{(K+1)!}\prod_{j=0}^{K}(0 - r_j)$$

for some $\xi \in (0, r_K)$, yielding the bound

> **Theorem (Richardson bias bound).** *Let $E : [0, r_K] \to \mathbb{R}$ be $(K{+}1)$-times differentiable with $\lvert E^{(K+1)}\rvert \le M_{K+1}$. For scale factors $0 < r_0 < \cdots < r_K$, the degree-$K$ Richardson extrapolant satisfies*
> $$\big\lvert \hat{E}_{\mathrm{ZNE}} - E(0)\big\rvert \;\le\; \frac{M_{K+1}}{(K+1)!}\prod_{j=0}^{K} r_j.$$
> *In particular, if $E(r) = E_0 + \sum_{m\ge 1} c_m r^m$ is analytic with radius exceeding $r_K$, the bias is $\mathcal{O}(r_K^{K+1})$.*

Three consequences follow. **First**, bias is controlled by shrinking $r_K$ and raising $K$ — exactly where Section 4.2 shows variance explodes: the central bias–variance trade-off of ZNE. **Second**, the bound degrades when $E(r)$ is non-smooth: threshold effects, leakage, or non-Markovian memory make polynomial extrapolation of non-polynomial data *worse* than the raw value [7]. **Third**, overfitting: high-degree fits to noisy $\hat{E}(r_k)$ oscillate at the extrapolation point (Runge phenomenon), so $K \le 3$ dominates practice.


### 4.2 Variance amplification and optimal shot allocation

Assume independent measurements with $\mathrm{Var}[\hat{E}(r_k)] = \sigma_k^2 / N_k$, where $N_k$ shots are allocated to scale factor $r_k$ ($\sum_k N_k = N_{\mathrm{tot}}$). The extrapolated estimator's variance is

$$\mathrm{Var}[\hat{E}_{\mathrm{ZNE}}] \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{K} b_k^2\,\frac{\sigma_k^2}{N_k}.$$

Minimizing over the shot allocation $\{N_k\}$ gives the optimal rule $N_k \propto \lvert b_k\rvert\,\sigma_k$ and the minimized variance

$$\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{opt}} \;=\; \frac{\big(\sum_k \lvert b_k\rvert\,\sigma_k\big)^2}{N_{\mathrm{tot}}}.$$

For $\sigma_k \approx \sigma$, the **variance amplification factor** is $\Lambda = \big(\sum_k \lvert b_k\rvert\big)^2$:

| Scale factors $\{r_k\}$ | Degree $K$ | Weights $b_k$ | $\sum\lvert b_k\rvert$ | Amplification $\Lambda$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\{1, 3\}$ | 1 | $[1.5, -0.5]$ | $2.0$ | $4.0$ |
| $\{1, 3, 5\}$ | 2 | $[1.875, -1.25, 0.375]$ | $3.5$ | $12.25$ |
| $\{1, 3, 5, 7\}$ | 3 | $[2.1875, -2.1875, 1.3125, -0.3125]$ | $6.0$ | $36.0$ |
| $\{1, 2, 4, 8\}$ | 3 | $[2.33, -2.67, 1.67, -0.33]$ | $7.0$ | $49.0$ |

*Table: Richardson weights grow super-exponentially in degree; each additional order roughly doubles the shot budget required for fixed precision.*

ZNE's cruel arithmetic: each order that halves the bias multiplies sampling cost by $\sim 3$–$4$. Production deployments therefore use *low-order* extrapolation — linear or quadratic — accepting residual bias to keep $\Lambda$ manageable.

### 4.3 Quasi-probability decompositions in PEC

The mathematical heart of PEC is the *quasi-probability representation* of the inverse noise channel. For a Pauli-twirled gate with Pauli error rates $\{p_P\}$, the noisy channel is $\tilde{\mathcal{U}} = \mathcal{E} \circ \mathcal{U}$ with $\mathcal{E}(\rho) = \sum_P p_P\, P\rho P$. Its inverse admits the decomposition

$$\mathcal{E}^{-1} \;=\; \sum_P \eta_P \, \mathcal{P}_P, \qquad \mathcal{P}_P(\rho) = P\rho P,$$

where the quasi-probabilities $\eta_P$ are obtained by inverting the Pauli transfer matrix. The per-gate sampling cost is $\gamma = \sum_P \lvert\eta_P\rvert$. For weak local noise with total error probability $\epsilon \ll 1$, one finds $\gamma = 1 + c\,\epsilon + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)$ with a constant $c$ depending on the noise structure ($c = 2$ for depolarizing-like channels under the diamond-norm accounting of [6]).

> **Theorem (PEC unbiasedness).** *If every ideal gate $\mathcal{U}_j$ admits an exact quasi-probability decomposition over implementable noisy operations, then the PEC estimator satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\hat{E}_{\mathrm{PEC}}] = \mathrm{Tr}[O\,\mathcal{U}(\rho)]$ exactly, i.e., $\mathrm{Bias}[\hat{E}_{\mathrm{PEC}}] = 0$, at the cost of variance inflation $\mathrm{Var}[\hat{E}_{\mathrm{PEC}}] = \gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}^2\,\mathrm{Var}[\hat{m}]$ with $\gamma_{\mathrm{tot}} = \prod_j \gamma_j$.*

The qualifier *"if"* carries the entire practical weight of the method: exact decompositions require exact noise characterization. Gate-set tomography (GST) scales exponentially in qubit count and is infeasible beyond one or two qubits — which is why the **sparse Pauli–Lindblad** learning protocol of van den Berg et al. [5] was transformative. Assuming geometrically local Lindbladian terms, they learned noise models for 10+ qubits at near-constant measurement cost and built PEC decompositions layer-by-layer. Their demonstration — Trotterized 1D spin-chain dynamics with weight-9 and weight-10 Pauli-$Z$ observables showing *vanishing* residuals — is the strongest hardware evidence that learned-model PEC achieves near-zero bias at scale [5].

PEC *requires* invertible noise and a spanning basis: **coherent errors** inflate quasi-probability norms far faster than stochastic noise of equal fidelity, and **leakage** is not invertible within the qubit Pauli basis at all. Pauli twirling converts coherent errors to stochastic ones, at the cost of twirling gates whose noise enters the learned model.

### 4.4 Sampling-overhead scaling laws

Composing the per-gate bound $\gamma_j \approx 1 + c\,\epsilon_j$ over $L$ gates gives

$$\gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}^2 \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^{L}(1 + c\,\epsilon_j)^2 \;\approx\; \exp\!\big(2c\sum_j \epsilon_j\big) \;=\; e^{2c\lambda},$$

so the sampling overhead is *exponential in the circuit fault rate*. For ZNE, $\Lambda$ is $\lambda$-independent at fixed $K$ — but holding bias fixed as $\lambda$ grows demands larger $K$, driving $\Lambda$ up super-exponentially. Both routes hit the same wall:

> **Theorem (Universal sampling-overhead bound, informal [6]).** *For any unbiased mitigation protocol on circuits with fault rate $\lambda$, $C_{\mathrm{em}} = e^{\Omega(\lambda)}$ up to polynomial factors: no scheme suppresses errors at $\lambda \gg 1$ for sub-exponential cost.*

This bound — established rigorously via quantum estimation theory — proves mitigation cannot *asymptotically* substitute for error correction, while leaving a large *constant-factor* regime ($\lambda \lesssim 1$–$10$) of genuine utility:

| Regime | ZNE (Richardson) | ZNE (exponential) | PEC (learned model) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small $\lambda \lesssim 1$ | Excellent: low $K$ suffices | Excellent: near-exact | Excellent: $\gamma^2 \approx 1$ |
| Moderate $\lambda \sim 3$–$5$ | Costly: $K=2$–$3$, $\Lambda \sim 10$–$50$ | Good if decay is exponential | Viable: $\gamma^2 \sim 10^2$–$10^4$ |
| Large $\lambda \gtrsim 10$ | Infeasible: bias or variance explodes | Model-mismatch dominated | Infeasible: $\gamma^2 \gtrsim e^{20}$ |
| Model knowledge needed | None | Decay-shape ansatz | Sparse Lindbladian fit |
| Dominant failure mode | Variance blowup / overfitting | Ansatz mismatch | Learning residual, drift |

In short, mitigation extends NISQ reach by a *multiplicative* factor in circuit volume — roughly one order of magnitude in $\lambda$ — not to arbitrary depth.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Numerical validation: the SWAP-test benchmark

Endo, Benjamin, and Li [3] benchmarked a SWAP test on up to 19 qubits under Pauli and leakage noise, finding:

1. **Exponential extrapolation dominates.** The exponential ansatz landed within a few percent of ideal where linear extrapolation deviated by tens of percent — e.g., $0.5111$ vs.\ $0.3415$ against an ideal $0.5$ [3].
2. **Quasi-probability decomposition eliminates bias at fixed variance cost.** The PEC-equivalent protocol recovered the ideal value *exactly* in expectation, at the largest per-shot variance of all methods — the empirical $\gamma^2$ signature.
3. **Projected overheads are feasible.** For a 51-qubit SWAP test at ion-trap fidelities, projected overheads were only **8.7×** (Pauli) and **18.8×** (leakage) [3].


### 5.2 Hardware demonstration I: ZNE extends computational reach

Kandala et al. [4] provided the first definitive hardware proof of mitigation utility. On a superconducting processor they ran VQE for H$_2$ and LiH, amplifying noise by *stretching* control pulses and extrapolating first- and second-order Richardson fits to zero noise. Mitigated ground-state energies recovered the exact dissociation profile with errors reduced by roughly an **order of magnitude** at sampling overheads of a few × — mitigation *extends computational reach* (the largest circuit volume at target accuracy) rather than merely polishing small circuits.

### 5.3 Hardware demonstration II: learned-model PEC at scale

Van den Berg et al. [5] demonstrated the complementary extreme: near-zero-bias PEC on utility-scale circuits. Their protocol (i) Pauli-twirls each circuit layer to enforce a Pauli noise model, (ii) learns a *sparse Pauli–Lindblad* generator with measurement cost scaling nearly independently of qubit number, and (iii) constructs the inverse-channel quasi-probability decomposition analytically from the learned rates. On Trotterized 1D-lattice dynamics (ten qubits, seven steps), mitigated weight-9 and weight-10 Pauli-$Z$ observables — exponentially noise-sensitive — showed **vanishing residuals** versus exact simulation [5]: the gold standard for model-based mitigation, limited only by learning accuracy and drift.

### 5.4 Consolidated experimental record

Together these validate Section 4: order-of-magnitude gains for $\lambda \lesssim 5$ at affordable cost, with the exponential wall visible where added circuit volume stops responding to larger shot budgets.

## 6 Limitations

Despite its successes, error mitigation faces fundamental and practical limitations that any serious deployment must confront.

1. **The exponential wall is real.** The bound $C_{\mathrm{em}} = e^{\Omega(\lambda)}$ [6] is information-theoretic: mitigation multiplies reachable circuit volume by a constant factor without changing the scaling. "Scalable" claims must always name their $\lambda$ regime.
2. **Model misspecification.** PEC's zero-bias guarantee evaporates when the learned model is wrong: non-Markovian effects, leakage, and *drift* between characterization and execution inject bias no sampling removes. The sparse Pauli–Lindblad prior [5] tames learning cost but may fail on crosstalk-heavy devices.
3. **ZNE's agnosticism cuts both ways.** Model-free and robust to misspecification — yet polynomial extrapolation of non-polynomial trajectories (threshold crossings, leakage turn-on, coherent oscillations in $r$) can do *worse* than raw data [7]; exponential fits repair the common case at the price of ansatz bias.
4. **Coherent and leakage errors.** PEC norms grow far faster for coherent errors than stochastic noise of equal fidelity, and leakage is not invertible in the qubit Pauli basis. Twirling and leakage-rejection gadgets help at their own overhead.
5. **Characterization cost and drift.** Learning-based PEC needs characterization experiments whose cost, though tamed by sparsity [5], is nonzero — and learned models *expire* as devices drift, forcing recalibration that consumes device time.
6. **Algorithmic error is out of scope.** Standard QEM addresses *physical* noise; Trotter and ansatz errors are invisible to it, except via specialized algorithmic-parameter extrapolation that compounds variance [6].

| Limitation | Affects ZNE | Affects PEC | Mitigation of the limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exponential sampling wall | Yes ($\Lambda$ growth) | Yes ($\gamma^2 = e^{2c\lambda}$) | Stay in $\lambda \lesssim 5$; combine with lighter circuits |
| Model misspecification | No (model-free) | Yes (bias returns) | Frequent recalibration; model validation |
| Non-Markovian / drift noise | Partially | Severely | Interleaved characterization |
| Coherent errors | Oscillatory trajectories | Large $\gamma$ | Pauli twirling |
| Leakage | Threshold non-analyticity | Non-invertible | Leakage detection / post-selection |
| Algorithmic error | No (separate extrapolation) | No | Algorithmic ZNE [6] |

## 7 Conclusion

This thesis unified zero-noise extrapolation and probabilistic error cancellation from foundations to hardware demonstrations. Three central results: *first*, Richardson bias obeys the interpolation remainder bound $\mathcal{O}(r_K^{K+1})$, but variance amplification $\Lambda = (\sum_k \lvert b_k\rvert)^2$ grows super-exponentially in order — a hard bias–variance trade-off resolved in practice via low-order or exponential fits [1,2,3,7]. *Second*, PEC's quasi-probability decomposition achieves exactly zero bias under a perfect noise model, at sampling overhead $\gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}^2 \approx e^{2c\lambda}$ — made practical at moderate scale by sparse Pauli–Lindblad learning [5]. *Third*, the universal bound $C_{\mathrm{em}} = e^{\Omega(\lambda)}$ proves mitigation extends NISQ reach by a multiplicative factor in circuit volume, not to arbitrary depth [6].

Empirically: order-of-magnitude gains at single-digit overheads for $\lambda \lesssim 5$ [3,4], and vanishing PEC residuals on utility-scale Trotter dynamics [5]. The honest summary: **error mitigation is the most cost-effective accuracy improvement available on NISQ hardware today, within a well-characterized envelope — complementary to, not competitive with, fault-tolerant quantum computation.** Future work: adaptive cross-method shot allocation, drift-resilient online noise learning, and hybrid mitigation plus early fault-tolerant gadgets.
