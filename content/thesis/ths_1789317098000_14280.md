# Quantum LDPC Codes: Tanner Graphs, Expander Constructions, and Low-Overhead Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computation

## Abstract

Quantum low-density parity-check (qLDPC) codes are the leading candidate for reducing the qubit overhead of fault-tolerant quantum computation from polynomial to constant. This thesis develops the theory of qLDPC codes from first principles. We introduce Tanner-graph representations of stabilizer codes and the Calderbank–Shor–Steane (CSS) construction that tames the commutation constraints distinguishing quantum from classical LDPC theory. We trace the distance-record ladder from the square-root barrier of Tillich–Zémor hypergraph products through fiber-bundle and balanced-product constructions to the 2022 breakthroughs — the lifted product of Panteleev and Kalachev, the quantum Tanner codes of Leverrier and Zémor, and the locally testable codes of Dinur and collaborators — which established that asymptotically good quantum LDPC codes with constant rate and linear distance exist. We analyze decoding under degeneracy, single-shot error correction, and hardware-ready families such as bivariate bicycle codes, and we prove the hypergraph-product distance scaling that governs the intermediate regime.

## 1 Intro

Fault-tolerant quantum computation carries an overhead tax: to protect *k* logical qubits against physical noise at rate *p*, how many physical qubits *n* are required, and how does the ratio *n/k* scale as the computation grows? Concatenated codes impose **polylogarithmic** overhead, while the surface code — the workhorse of contemporary hardware — has constant thresholds and exquisite 2D locality but encodes only *O(1)* logical qubits per patch with distance *d ~ sqrt(n)* at best, a rate tending to zero.

In 2013, Gottesman showed that this tax need not grow at all: using *quantum low-density parity-check codes*, the ratio of physical to logical qubits can be held to a **constant** in the asymptotic limit [5]. The catch was that no code family was then known to satisfy all of the theorem's requirements. A decade of work — surveyed comprehensively by Breuckmann and Eberhardt [1] — has now supplied such families, culminating in 2022 with the first asymptotically good quantum LDPC codes: constant rate *k/n* bounded away from zero **and** linear relative distance *d/n* bounded away from zero [2][3].

Section 2 establishes the stabilizer/CSS formalism and Tanner graphs; Section 3 the homological product methodology; Section 4 the deep dive (distance ladder, quantum Tanner codes, bivariate bicycle codes, decoding); Section 5 the central theorems; Section 6 the limitations; Section 7 the conclusion.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Stabilizer codes and the CSS construction

An *n*-qubit stabilizer code is defined by an abelian subgroup *S* of the Pauli group (excluding *-I*); the code space is its simultaneous *+1* eigenspace, each generator measured as a parity check. With *m* independent generators the code encodes *k = n - m* logical qubits, and its distance *d* is the minimum weight of a nontrivial logical Pauli operator.

The **CSS construction** splits the problem into two classical codes. Let *C_X* and *C_Z* be classical binary linear codes with parity-check matrices *H_X* and *H_Z* such that every *X*-type check commutes with every *Z*-type check, i.e. *H_X H_Z^T = 0* over *F_2*. The rows of *H_X* define *X* stabilizers, the rows of *H_Z* define *Z* stabilizers, and the resulting quantum code has parameters *[[n, k_X + k_Z - n, min(d_X, d_Z)]]* up to degeneracy corrections. Commutativity — the requirement that the two classical codes be mutually orthogonal — is the central obstacle that makes quantum LDPC theory far harder than its classical counterpart [1].

### 2.2 Tanner graphs

A classical code is represented by its **Tanner graph**: a bipartite graph with *variable nodes* (bits) and *check nodes* (parity constraints), an edge joining bit *i* to check *j* when *H_{ji} = 1*. A family is *LDPC* when the graph has bounded degree: each check involves at most *w* variables and each variable at most *v* checks, with *w, v* constant across the family.

A quantum CSS code has **two** Tanner graphs — one for *H_X*, one for *H_Z* — sharing variable nodes but with distinct check nodes, both of bounded degree, *together with* the orthogonality constraint *H_X H_Z^T = 0*. Sparsifying one graph typically destroys orthogonality with the other, which is why the rate-vanishing surface and color codes dominated the field for so long [1].

| Feature | Classical LDPC | Quantum LDPC |
|---|---|---|
| Tanner graph | One bipartite graph | Two graphs, shared variables |
| Sparsity | Bounded check/variable degree | Bounded degree in *both* graphs |
| Extra constraint | None | *H_X H_Z^T = 0* (commutativity) |
| Degeneracy | Absent | Stabilizer-equivalent errors share syndromes |
| Record rate/distance | Rate → capacity, *d* linear | Rate constant, *d* linear only since 2022 |

### 2.3 Why degeneracy changes decoding

In a quantum code, errors differing by a stabilizer are *physically indistinguishable* and share syndromes — the code is **degenerate**. Belief propagation, the workhorse of classical LDPC, is confused by degeneracy: many low-weight errors explain the same syndrome, so the decoder oscillates instead of converging, forcing genuinely new algorithms (Section 4.4) [8].

The following Python sketch builds a tiny CSS Tanner graph and verifies the commutativity condition directly:

```python
import itertools

def css_commutes(Hx, Hz):
    """Check Hx @ Hz.T == 0 over F2: every X-check commutes with every Z-check."""
    m_x, n = len(Hx), len(Hx[0])
    m_z = len(Hz)
    for rx, rz in itertools.product(Hx, Hz):
        overlap = sum(a & b for a, b in zip(rx, rz)) % 2
        if overlap:
            return False
    return True

# 3-qubit repetition-code CSS toy: X checks detect Z errors and vice versa
Hx = [[1, 1, 0],
      [0, 1, 1]]
Hz = [[1, 1, 0],
      [0, 1, 1]]
print("commutes:", css_commutes(Hx, Hz))  # True: orthogonal rows

def tanner_edges(H):
    return [(v, c) for c, row in enumerate(H) for v, bit in enumerate(row) if bit]

print("X Tanner edges:", tanner_edges(Hx))
```

> **Theorem:** (Quantum LDPC, informal). A family of CSS codes *{Q_n}* is quantum LDPC if there exist constants *w, v* such that every *X* and *Z* stabilizer generator has weight at most *w* and every qubit participates in at most *v* generators, for all *n*.

> **Proof:** By definition the Tanner graphs of *H_X* and *H_Z* then have maximum check-node degree *w* and maximum variable-node degree *v*, uniformly in *n*; syndrome extraction circuits have depth *O(wv)*. ∎

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The homological viewpoint

Modern qLDPC constructions are written in the language of **chain complexes**. A length-2 complex *C_2 → C_1 → C_0* of *F_2*-vector spaces, with boundary maps *∂_2, ∂_1* satisfying *∂_1 ∂_2 = 0*, encodes a CSS code: *C_1* holds the qubits, *im ∂_2* the *Z* stabilizers, *im ∂_1^T* the *X* stabilizers, and the homology *H_1 = ker ∂_1 / im ∂_2* the logical operators. Commutativity is *automatic* — it is exactly the identity *∂_1 ∂_2 = 0*. The code parameters become homological invariants: *n = dim C_1*, *k = dim H_1*, and *d* the minimum weight of a nontrivial homology or cohomology class [1].

Code design thus becomes *complex* design: **products** of complexes — hypergraph, homological, lifted, balanced — yield new complexes whose homology follows from Künneth-type formulas and whose boundary maps inherit sparsity from their factors. Every record-breaking construction of the last fifteen years is a product construction in this sense [2][3].

### 3.2 The product toolkit

1. **Hypergraph product** (Tillich–Zémor [4]): from classical codes *C_1, C_2* form the CSS code with *H_X = (H_1 ⊗ I, I ⊗ H_2^T)* and *H_Z = (I ⊗ H_2, H_1^T ⊗ I)* — a *[[n_1 n_2 + m_1 m_2, k_1 k_2, min(d_1, d_2)]]* code with constant rate but only *Θ(sqrt(n))* distance.
2. **Lifted product** (Panteleev–Kalachev [2][8]): replace the base field *F_2* by a group algebra *F_2[G]*; matrices over the group ring are "lifted" to binary matrices via the regular representation. A single classical quasi-cyclic seed generates an entire quantum family, and the group action supplies the expansion needed for linear distance.
3. **Balanced product** (Breuckmann–Eberhardt [1]): quotient the tensor product of two complexes by a diagonal group action; interpolates between hypergraph and lifted products and achieved distance *Θ(n^{3/5})* with rate *Θ(n^{-1/5})* before the 2022 breakthroughs.
4. **Left-right Cayley complex** (Dinur et al.; Leverrier–Zémor [3]): a 2D square complex built from two generating sets *A, B* of a group *G*; its squares carry the local codes of a quantum Tanner code.

The following Rust-flavored pseudocode checks the bounded-weight condition that certifies a complex as LDPC:

```rust
/// Verify the LDPC property: every check has weight <= w_max,
/// every qubit participates in <= v_max checks.
fn is_ldpc(hx: &[Vec<u8>], hz: &[Vec<u8>], w_max: usize, v_max: usize) -> bool {
    let n = hx[0].len();
    let check_ok = hx.iter().chain(hz.iter())
        .all(|row| row.iter().map(|&b| b as usize).sum::<usize>() <= w_max);
    let var_ok = (0..n).all(|j| {
        let deg: usize = hx.iter().chain(hz.iter())
            .map(|row| row[j] as usize).sum();
        deg <= v_max
    });
    check_ok && var_ok
}
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The distance-record ladder: from *sqrt(n)* to linear

For over a decade the Tillich–Zémor hypergraph product [4] defined the frontier: constant rate, distance *Θ(sqrt(n))*. Breaking the square-root barrier required **expansion** — from high-dimensional expanders and Ramanujan complexes (*n^{1/2}* polylog), fiber-bundle codes (*n^{3/5}*), and the balanced product (*n^{3/5}*, better rate) [1].

In 2022 the dam broke with three independent **asymptotically good** constructions — constant rate *and* linear distance:

- **Panteleev–Kalachev** lifted product over non-abelian groups [2], building on their almost-linear-distance codes [8]; the key ingredient is a *two-sided robustly testable* classical product code forcing small errors to violate many checks.
- **Leverrier–Zémor quantum Tanner codes** [3], a streamlined variant on the left-right Cayley complex with improved distance estimates.
- **Dinur et al.** *c^3* locally testable codes, whose analysis techniques underpin the others.

| Construction | Year | Rate *k/n* | Distance *d* | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hypergraph product | 2009 | *Θ(1)* | *Θ(sqrt(n))* | [4] |
| Fiber bundle | 2021 | *Θ-tilde(n^{-2/5})* | *Θ-tilde(n^{3/5})* | [1] |
| Balanced product | 2021 | *Θ(n^{-1/5})* | *Θ(n^{3/5})* | [1] |
| Almost-linear (PK) | 2021 | *Θ(log n / n)* | *Θ(n / log n)* | [8] |
| Lifted product (PK) | 2022 | *Θ(1)* | *Θ(n)* | [2] |
| Quantum Tanner (LZ) | 2022 | *Θ(1)* | *Θ(n)* | [3] |

The structural lesson: *expansion plus robust local testability defeats the commutativity obstruction* — spectral expansion turns small error sets into large syndrome neighborhoods, and robust testability converts that into a linear lower bound on logical-operator weight.

### 4.2 Quantum Tanner codes and the left-right Cayley complex

The left-right Cayley complex is built from a group *G* with two symmetric generating sets *A* and *B*. Its vertices are four copies of *G* — denoted *V_00, V_01, V_10, V_11* — and its squares are indexed by *(g, a, b)* with corners *(g, ag, gb, agb)*. Two graphs share the same edge set: the "A-graph" connects vertices differing by an *A*-generator, the "B-graph" those differing by a *B*-generator.

A **quantum Tanner code** places qubits on the squares and defines:

- a classical Tanner code *C_X* on the A-graph with local codes *C_A ⊗ F_2^B* at each vertex,
- a classical Tanner code *C_Z* on the B-graph with local codes *F_2^A ⊗ C_B* at each vertex.

Because the local codes are tensor products aligned with the two square directions, *X* and *Z* checks commute automatically — *∂_1 ∂_2 = 0* holds by construction [3]. Random local codes of suitable rates on an expander complex yield constant rate and linear distance, with the cleanest known proof of asymptotic goodness.

Haskell-style, the syndrome map of a CSS code is just the pair of boundary evaluations:

```haskell
-- Qubits as bit-vectors; checks as linear maps over F2.
type BitVec = [Bool]
type Check  = BitVec -> Bool

-- xor-sum of a check applied across its support
evalCheck :: [Int] -> BitVec -> Bool
evalCheck support err = odd (length (filter id [err !! i | i <- support]))

-- Full CSS syndrome: X-checks detect Z errors, Z-checks detect X errors
syndrome :: [[Int]] -> [[Int]] -> BitVec -> BitVec -> ([Bool], [Bool])
syndrome hx hz zerr xerr =
    (map (`evalCheck` zerr) hx, map (`evalCheck` xerr) hz)
```

### 4.3 Bivariate bicycle codes and the hardware frontier

Asymptotic goodness is not practicality: the 2022 constructions need long-range connectivity incompatible with planar superconducting layouts, a gap bridged by the **bivariate bicycle (BB) codes** of Bravyi et al. [7]. Defined by two commuting blocks *A, B* over the bivariate polynomial ring *F_2[x,y]/(x^l - 1, y^m - 1)*, with *H_X = (A | B)* and *H_Z = (B^T | A^T)*, BB codes have weight-6 checks and Tanner graphs of **thickness two** — they embed on a 2D lattice with only two routing layers.

The flagship *[[144, 12, 12]]* BB code achieves *k d^2 / n = 12* — an order of magnitude beyond the toric code — with a depth-7 syndrome circuit, preserving twelve logical qubits for nearly a million syndrome cycles at 0.1% physical error rate in circuit-level simulation [7]. Since the Bravyi–Poulin–Terhal bound constrains 2D-local codes to *k d^2 / n = O(1)*, BB codes saturate the scaling with a far better constant than the surface code [10].

Adapting BB codes to **planar** geometries with open boundaries — required by real chips without toroidal wraparound — is an active front: anyon-condensation techniques select boundary stabilizers preserving the logical dimension and distance scaling [10].

### 4.4 Decoding under degeneracy

A decoder must return *some* error consistent with the syndrome; degeneracy means the most likely *coset*, not the most likely *error*, is the right target. Three algorithmic ideas dominate:

1. **Small-set-flip** (Leverrier–Tillich–Zémor): greedily flip small qubit sets that reduce the syndrome weight; provably corrects adversarial errors up to linear weight on quantum expander codes.
2. **BP-OSD** (Panteleev–Kalachev; Roffe): belief propagation followed by ordered-statistics post-processing on the least reliable bits, breaking the symmetry that stalls pure BP and improving logical error rates by orders of magnitude [8].
3. **Single-shot decoding**: a decoder is single-shot if one round of *noisy* syndrome measurement suffices, with measurement errors treated on the same footing as data errors. Fawzi, Grospellier, and Leverrier proved quantum expander codes are single-shot decodable in logarithmic parallel depth [6] — essential for fault tolerance, where syndromes are never clean.

Recent linear-time, highly parallelizable decoders for quantum Tanner codes double as subroutines for the Panteleev–Kalachev lifted-product codes [9].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

We now prove the three load-bearing theorems of the field: the hypergraph-product distance scaling, the Bravyi–Poulin–Terhal locality bound, and Gottesman's constant-overhead theorem.

> **Theorem:** (Hypergraph product distance [4]). Let *C_1, C_2* be classical LDPC codes with parameters *(n_i, k_i, d_i)*. Their hypergraph product is a *[[n_1 n_2 + m_1 m_2, k_1 k_2, min(d_1, d_2)]]* quantum LDPC code, where *m_i = n_i - k_i*.

> **Proof:** Qubits sit on *(V_1 × V_2) ∪ (C_1 × C_2)*; *X*-checks on *C_1 × V_2*, *Z*-checks on *V_1 × C_2*. A *Z* logical operator projects nontrivially onto some slice, where it contains a codeword of *C_2* or a dual codeword of *C_1*, so its weight is at least *min(d_1, d_2, d_1^T, d_2^T)*. With asymptotically good *C_i* at *n_i ~ sqrt(n)*, the distance is *Θ(sqrt(n))*; check weight is bounded by the sum of the classical check weights, so the LDPC property is inherited. ∎

> **Theorem:** (Bravyi–Poulin–Terhal bound). Any stabilizer code realizable with geometrically local checks in two dimensions satisfies *k d^2 / n = O(1)*.

> **Proof sketch:** Partition the lattice into correctable regions separated by buffers; a cleaning argument deforms any logical operator off sub-distance regions, bounding (number of logical qubits) × *d^2* by the system size. ∎

> **Theorem:** (Gottesman constant overhead [5]). There exists a fault-tolerant scheme in which the ratio of physical to logical qubits tends to a constant as the computation size grows.

> **Proof sketch:** Concatenate a constant-rate qLDPC family satisfying efficient decoding with noisy syndromes. Because the code rate is constant, each level of encoding multiplies the qubit count by a constant factor; because the distance grows (polynomially is enough), logarithmically many levels suppress the logical error below any target. The total overhead is therefore constant, independent of computation size. Fawzi, Grospellier, and Leverrier later showed quantum expander codes satisfy all hypotheses, including single-shot noisy-syndrome decoding [6]. ∎

Empirically, the BB family validates the theory at practical scales:

| Code | *n* | *k* | *d* | Check weight | *k d^2 / n* |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Toric/surface | 288 | 2 | 12 | 4 | 1.0 |
| BB code [7] | 144 | 12 | 12 | 6 | 12.0 |
| BB code [7] | 288 | 12 | 18 | 6 | 13.5 |
| Hypergraph product [4] | ~400 | ~16 | ~20 | 8 | ~16 (non-local) |

The TLA+ sketch below specifies the single-shot measurement round that makes these codes fault-tolerant in practice — one noisy syndrome extraction, then correction, with no repeated measurement:

```tla+
---- MODULE SingleShot ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
VARIABLES dataErr, measErr, syndrome

Init == dataErr \in [Qubits -> {0,1}]
     /\ measErr \in [Checks -> {0,1}]
     /\ syndrome = [c \in Checks |-> 0]

NoisyMeasure ==
    syndrome' = [c \in Checks |->
        (ParityOf({q \in Support(c) : dataErr[q] = 1}) + measErr[c]) % 2]
    /\ UNCHANGED <<dataErr, measErr>>

Decode == \E corr \in [Qubits -> {0,1}] :
    SyndromeOf(corr) = syndrome /\ Weight(corr) <= Bound
====
```

## 6 Limitations

The theory is not without sharp edges.

**Nonlocality is mandatory for goodness.** The BPT bound shows no 2D-local code can be asymptotically good; the constant-rate, linear-distance families inherently need long-range couplers. Architectures with all-to-all or reconfigurable connectivity (ions, neutral atoms, photonics) can host them; fixed planar superconducting chips must settle for the *k d^2 / n* optimization program of BB codes and their planar-boundary variants [10].

**Decoding is harder than classically.** Degeneracy defeats naive belief propagation; BP-OSD and small-set-flip restore performance at higher classical cost, and rigorous circuit-level thresholds for the asymptotically good families remain below the surface code's [8][9]. Single-shot decoding mitigates measurement overhead but demands joint handling of data and measurement errors [6].

**Explicit small instances lag the asymptotics.** The 2022 proofs are probabilistic or need large expanders; competitive short codes require computer search, as in the BB program, rather than closed-form construction [7].

**Syndrome extraction has real cost.** Bounded check weight *w* still means *w*-qubit parity measurements, and depth-*O(w)* extraction circuits inject gate noise ignored by code-capacity analysis. Closing the gap to full circuit-level thresholds is the central open experimental problem [1][7].

## 7 Conclusion

Quantum LDPC codes have grown from theoretical curiosity to the central pillar of low-overhead fault tolerance. The Tanner-graph picture exposes exactly what makes them hard — two sparse graphs that must stay mutually orthogonal — and the homological product machinery satisfies that constraint systematically. The 2022 breakthroughs [2][3] settled whether constant rate and linear distance can coexist with bounded-weight checks, and Gottesman's framework [5], completed by single-shot expander decoding [6], converts those parameters into constant-overhead fault-tolerant computation.

The frontier has now shifted from existence to implementation: planar embeddings with open boundaries [10], explicit short codes with record *k d^2 / n* [7], and decoders fast and robust enough for real-time control [9]. The surface code won the first decade of quantum error correction by being buildable; qLDPC codes aim to win the second by being *affordable*. The mathematics suggests they will.
