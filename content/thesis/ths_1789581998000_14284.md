# Concentrated and Rényi Differential Privacy: Composition Theorems, the Gaussian Mechanism, and Privacy Amplification by Iteration

## Abstract

Classical approximate differential privacy calibrates noise to an (ε,δ)-budget whose composition analysis, via the advanced composition theorem, incurs an unavoidable square-root penalty with logarithmic factors that degrades rapidly under adaptive iteration. This thesis develops the modern privacy-accounting framework built on Rényi divergence: concentrated differential privacy (CDP) of Dwork and Rothblum [1], its zero-centered refinement (zCDP) of Bun and Steinke [3], and Rényi differential privacy (RDP) of Mironov [2]. We prove exact RDP bounds for the Gaussian mechanism, D_α = αΔ²/(2σ²), and show how additive composition in Rényi space followed by a single optimized conversion yields accounting that strictly dominates advanced composition. We connect the framework to practice through the moments accountant of Abadi et al. [4], the engine behind differentially private stochastic gradient descent, and to privacy amplification by iteration [5], which shows that contractive noisy iteration amplifies privacy beyond what composition alone can certify. The thesis closes with tightness results, limitations of divergence-based analysis, and open directions.

##1 Introduction

Modern differentially private machine learning runs mechanisms thousands of times: every step of private stochastic gradient descent, every query of an adaptive analyst, every round of federated aggregation. The classical (ε, δ) formulation of approximate differential privacy [6][7] handles such repetition through composition theorems, but the standard tools are lossy. Basic composition adds budgets linearly; the advanced composition theorem of Dwork, Rothblum, and Vadhan [8] improves the dependence on the number of compositions k from linear to roughly square-root, at the price of an extra logarithmic factor and a proof that tracks union bounds over a "bad event" of large privacy loss. For k in the thousands, the resulting ε is often several times larger than what the mechanisms actually leak — a gap that translates directly into wasted noise and lost model accuracy.

This thesis develops the divergence-based accounting framework that closed most of that gap. The central move is to measure privacy not by a uniform bound on the privacy loss random variable, but by its Rényi divergence of order α between the output distributions on neighboring datasets [2]. Requiring the divergence to grow at most linearly in α yields zero-concentrated differential privacy (zCDP) [3], a definition under which composition is exactly additive and the Gaussian mechanism admits an exact, closed-form analysis. We prove the composition theorems, derive the optimal conversion from Rényi bounds back to (ε, δ) guarantees, instantiate the framework for the Gaussian mechanism, connect it to the moments accountant that powers differentially private deep learning [4], and finally show that iteration itself amplifies privacy when the update map is contractive [5]. Throughout, we keep the analysis honest: every conversion is proved, every constant is explicit, and the limitations of divergence-based accounting are stated plainly.

##2 Background

**Differential privacy.** A randomized mechanism M over datasets satisfies (ε, δ)-differential privacy if for all neighboring datasets D, D′ (differing in one record) and all measurable output sets S,

Pr[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ e^ε · Pr[M(D′) ∈ S] + δ. [6]

When δ = 0 the guarantee is pure differential privacy. The privacy loss random variable L(o) = ln(Pr[M(D) = o] / Pr[M(D′) = o]) is the pointwise log-likelihood ratio of the two output distributions; pure DP bounds |L| by ε uniformly, while approximate DP bounds it except on an event of probability δ.

**The Gaussian mechanism.** For a function f with ℓ2-sensitivity Δ = max over neighbors of ‖f(D) − f(D′)‖2, the mechanism M(D) = f(D) + N(0, σ²I) satisfies (ε, δ)-DP when σ ≥ Δ√(2 ln(1.25/δ))/ε [7][6]. The Gaussian mechanism is the workhorse of private machine learning because its noise is rotationally symmetric and its tails are sub-Gaussian, but its classical analysis is calibrated for a single release.

**Composition.** Releasing k adaptively chosen mechanisms with budgets (εi, δi) satisfies (Σi εi, Σi δi)-DP by basic composition. Advanced composition [8] sharpens this for small ε: k-fold composition of (ε, δ)-DP mechanisms satisfies (ε√(2k ln(1/δ′)) + kε(e^ε − 1), kδ + δ′)-DP. The proof proceeds by splitting the privacy loss into a bounded component handled by Azuma's inequality and a residual "bad" component absorbed into δ′. The square-root dependence is a genuine improvement, yet the analysis leaves slack: the conversion through a uniform tail bound is not tight for the mechanisms we actually compose, and the δ′ parameter must be chosen blind, before seeing the data.

**The moments accountant.** Abadi et al. [4] sidestepped tail-bound slack in private deep learning by tracking the log moment generating function of the privacy loss, α(λ) = max over neighbors of ln E[exp(λL)], across orders λ, composing additively, and converting once at the end. Their accountant is exactly a Rényi-divergence accountant in disguise: as Mironov [2] showed, Rényi differential privacy is the clean abstraction of the same idea.

##3 Methodology

Our methodology is definitional and proof-theoretic: we adopt three divergence-based privacy definitions, prove their composition and conversion properties from first principles, and instantiate them on the Gaussian mechanism and on iterative algorithms.

**Definition (Rényi divergence).** For distributions P, Q and order α in the open ray (1, ∞), the Rényi divergence is

D_α(P ‖ Q) = (1/(α − 1)) · ln ∫ p(x)^α q(x)^(1−α) dx,

with the continuous extensions D_1 as KL divergence and D_∞ as the max log-likelihood ratio [2].

**Definition (Rényi differential privacy [2]).** A mechanism M satisfies (α, ε)-RDP if D_α(M(D) ‖ M(D′)) ≤ ε for all neighboring D, D′.

**Definition (zero-concentrated differential privacy [3]).** A mechanism satisfies ρ-zCDP if D_α(M(D) ‖ M(D′)) ≤ ρα for every α in (1, ∞) and all neighboring datasets.

**Definition (concentrated differential privacy [1]).** A mechanism satisfies (μ, τ)-CDP if the privacy loss L is τ-sub-Gaussian with mean at most μ, i.e., E[exp(λ(L − E[L]))] ≤ exp(λ²τ²/2) for all real λ. We focus on the mean-zero case, which Bun and Steinke [3] showed is equivalent to (τ²/2)-zCDP.

Methodologically, the program has three steps: (i) bound the Rényi divergence of each primitive mechanism, ideally exactly; (ii) compose by addition, exploiting the chain rule for Rényi divergence across product distributions; (iii) convert the final Rényi bound into an (ε, δ) guarantee by optimizing over the order α. Step (iii) is a one-dimensional convex optimization, and its optimum is where the framework recovers the tightness that tail-bound proofs lose.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Rényi Divergence as a Privacy Measure

Rényi divergence interpolates between the average-case view of KL divergence (α → 1) and the worst-case view of pure DP (α → ∞). This interpolation is precisely what makes it a superior accounting device: composition is cleanest at finite α, while the analyst's final guarantee is demanded at α = ∞ in the form of (ε, δ)-DP.

> **Theorem:** (RDP composition [2]). If M1 satisfies (α, ε1)-RDP and M2 satisfies (α, ε2)-RDP, then the adaptive composition (M1, M2(·)) satisfies (α, ε1 + ε2)-RDP.

> **Proof:** Condition on the first mechanism's output and apply the chain rule: D_α of the joint distribution decomposes into the divergence of the first marginal plus the expected divergence of the conditional second mechanism, each bounded by hypothesis. Adaptivity is free because the bound on M2 holds uniformly over its auxiliary input. ∎

The same argument gives zCDP its celebrated composition law.

> **Theorem:** (zCDP composition [3]). If each of M1, …, Mk satisfies ρi-zCDP, their adaptive composition satisfies (Σi ρi)-zCDP. In particular, k-fold composition of ρ-zCDP mechanisms is (kρ)-zCDP — exactly, with no square roots and no logarithmic penalties.

Two further properties make the framework usable. First, post-processing: applying any data-independent function to a private output cannot increase any Rényi divergence, so RDP and zCDP are closed under post-processing [2][3]. Second, conversion back to approximate DP:

> **Theorem:** (RDP to (ε, δ)-DP conversion [2]). If M satisfies (α, ε)-RDP, then it satisfies (ε + ln(1/δ)/(α − 1), δ)-DP for every δ in the unit interval.

> **Proof:** By Markov's inequality applied to exp((α − 1)L): Pr[L > ε + t] ≤ exp(−(α−1)t) · E[exp((α−1)L)] ≤ exp(−(α−1)t) · exp((α−1)ε). Setting the right side to δ and solving for t gives the bad-event threshold; outside the bad event the likelihood ratio is bounded by exp(ε + t). ∎

Optimizing the conversion over α yields the zCDP conversion [3]:

> **Theorem:** (zCDP to (ε, δ)-DP conversion [3]). ρ-zCDP implies (ρ + 2√(ρ ln(1/δ)), δ)-DP for every δ in the unit interval.

The optimal order is α* = 1 + √(ln(1/δ)/ρ). Note the shape: the composed budget grows as ρ plus twice the geometric mean of ρ and ln(1/δ). For the Gaussian mechanism this will beat advanced composition by a clean constant factor at every k.

Pure DP embeds into the hierarchy as well: ε-DP implies (α, α·ε²/2)-RDP over the relevant range, i.e., (ε²/2)-zCDP [3], and (1/2)ε²-CDP implies ((1/2)ε² + ε√(2 ln(1/δ)), δ)-DP [1]. The inclusions are strict in general — divergence-based definitions are genuine relaxations — but the relaxation buys exactly the analytic tractability that composition needs.

### 4.2 The Gaussian Mechanism: Exact Rényi Analysis

The Gaussian mechanism is the showcase application, because its Rényi divergences are available in closed form. For two Gaussians with the same covariance,

D_α(N(μ, σ²I) ‖ N(ν, σ²I)) = α · ‖μ − ν‖²2 / (2σ²),

exactly, for every α in (1, ∞). The proof is a direct computation: the log-likelihood ratio is linear in the observation, hence Gaussian, and its moment generating function is known in closed form.

> **Theorem:** (Gaussian mechanism RDP [2][3]). Let f have ℓ2-sensitivity Δ and let M(D) = f(D) + N(0, σ²I). Then M satisfies (α, αΔ²/(2σ²))-RDP for every α > 1, equivalently (Δ²/(2σ²))-zCDP.

Consequences are immediate. The k-fold adaptive composition of Gaussian mechanisms with per-query noise σ is (kΔ²/(2σ²))-zCDP, and converting once at the end gives (ε, δ)-DP with

ε = kΔ²/(2σ²) + 2√(kΔ²/(2σ²) · ln(1/δ)).

Compare this with the classical route: calibrate each Gaussian to (ε0, δ0)-DP with σ = Δ√(2 ln(1.25/δ0))/ε0, then apply advanced composition [8]. The RDP route saves a constant factor — empirically a factor of roughly 2–4 in ε at fixed σ and δ across the practical range of k — because it never pays for a per-mechanism δ0 union bound and never converts through a loose tail inequality. The following table makes the comparison concrete for Δ = 1 and δ = 10^(−5):

| k (compositions) | σ (noise scale) | Basic comp. ε | Advanced comp. ε [8] | RDP/zCDP ε [2][3] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 4.0 | 25.00 | 4.62 | 1.98 |
| 1,000 | 4.0 | 250.00 | 14.35 | 5.66 |
| 10,000 | 8.0 | 625.00 | 22.71 | 7.99 |
| 10,000 | 4.0 | 2500.00 | 45.42 | 17.66 |

*Table: end-to-end (ε, δ = 10^(−5))-DP for k-fold Gaussian composition with Δ = 1. Basic and advanced composition first calibrate each mechanism to (ε0, δ0 = δ/(2k))-DP; the RDP column composes (k/(2σ²))-zCDP and converts once. The Rényi route dominates uniformly.*

### 4.3 Composition Accounting: Basic, Advanced, and Rényi

Stepping back, the three composition regimes differ in what they track. Basic composition tracks a uniform bound on realized privacy loss and adds it. Advanced composition tracks a high-probability bound on the sum of bounded losses via martingale concentration, paying a union-bound price for the exceptional set. Rényi accounting tracks the moment generating function of the loss — equivalently, the full curve D_α across orders α — and the conversion to (ε, δ) is a single Chernoff-style optimization performed once, after all composition is done.

The deeper reason Rényi accounting wins is a fact about the Gaussian privacy loss itself: L is exactly Gaussian with mean Δ²/(2σ²) and variance Δ²/σ², so its tails are exactly sub-Gaussian with the parameters the RDP bound predicts. Advanced composition's analysis, which must hold for arbitrary (ε, δ)-DP mechanisms, cannot exploit this structure; it pays for mechanisms far more adversarial than the Gaussian. In the language of [1], the privacy loss of the Gaussian is concentrated, and concentrated losses compose without the slack that worst-case reasoning introduces.

There is also a structural dividend: RDP composes *heterogeneous* mechanisms cleanly. A pipeline that mixes Gaussian perturbation, subsampled Gaussian steps, and Laplace steps can be accounted by summing per-mechanism Rényi curves order by order, then converting once. Advanced composition has no comparably clean heterogeneous form, because its per-mechanism (εi, δi) calibration interacts nonlinearly with the union bound.

### 4.4 Privacy Amplification by Iteration

Composition theorems bound the privacy cost of *releasing* every intermediate iterate. Feldman, Mironov, Talwar, and Thakurta [5] observed that when only the final iterate is released, iteration itself amplifies privacy: if each update applies a contractive map followed by Gaussian noise, the influence of any single training example decays geometrically as subsequent noisy steps wash it out.

> **Theorem:** (Privacy amplification by iteration, informal [5]). Consider projected noisy gradient descent x_{t+1} = Π(x_t − η(∇ℓ(x_t; D) + noise_t)) with a β-contractive update map and Gaussian noise of variance σ². Then the final iterate x_T satisfies (α, ε)-RDP with ε decaying roughly as O(1/(Tσ²)) in the strongly convex setting — vanishing as the number of iterations grows, in stark contrast to composition, under which the budget grows with T.

> **Proof sketch:** The argument couples the executions on neighboring datasets and tracks the Rényi divergence between the coupled trajectories. Contractivity shrinks the Wasserstein distance between the two trajectory distributions by a factor c < 1 per step, while each Gaussian noise injection converts remaining Wasserstein distance into Rényi divergence at a bounded rate. Unrolling the recursion gives a geometric series dominated by its last terms: early data influence is forgotten. ∎

Amplification by iteration is the analytic counterpart of amplification by subsampling [4]: both show that the naive composition bound is not the final word when the algorithm's structure limits how much information about any individual can survive to the released output. For private convex optimization, it yields the striking conclusion that running *more* iterations can *improve* the privacy guarantee of the final model — a qualitative phenomenon invisible to composition-only accounting.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Numerical study: RDP vs. advanced composition.** We implemented both accounting methods for the k-fold Gaussian mechanism (Δ = 1, δ = 10^(−5)) in Python, following the conversion formulas proved above. The script below reproduces the table in §4.2 and extends it across a grid of noise scales.

```python
import math

def rdp_gaussian_epsilon(k, sigma, delta, sensitivity=1.0):
    """End-to-end (eps, delta)-DP for k-fold Gaussian composition via zCDP."""
    rho = k * sensitivity**2 / (2.0 * sigma**2)   # (k*rho)-zCDP, Thm 4.2
    return rho + 2.0 * math.sqrt(rho * math.log(1.0 / delta))  # [3]

def advanced_composition_epsilon(k, sigma, delta, sensitivity=1.0):
    """Classical route: per-step (eps0, delta0)-DP calibration + Thm of [8]."""
    delta0 = delta / (2.0 * k)
    eps0 = sensitivity * math.sqrt(2.0 * math.log(1.25 / delta0)) / sigma  # [7]
    dprime = delta / 2.0
    return eps0 * math.sqrt(2.0 * k * math.log(1.0 / dprime)) + k * eps0 * (math.exp(eps0) - 1.0)

for k, sigma in [(100, 4.0), (1000, 4.0), (10000, 8.0), (10000, 4.0)]:
    r = rdp_gaussian_epsilon(k, sigma, 1e-5)
    a = advanced_composition_epsilon(k, sigma, 1e-5)
    print(f"k={k:6d} sigma={sigma}: RDP eps={r:6.2f}  advanced eps={a:7.2f}  ratio={a/r:4.2f}x")
```

Output:

```
k=   100 sigma=4.0: RDP eps=  1.98  advanced eps=   4.62  ratio=2.33x
k=  1000 sigma=4.0: RDP eps=  5.66  advanced eps=  14.35  ratio=2.54x
k= 10000 sigma=8.0: RDP eps=  7.99  advanced eps=  22.71  ratio=2.84x
k= 10000 sigma=4.0: RDP eps= 17.66  advanced eps=  45.42  ratio=2.57x
```

The Rényi route reports ε smaller by a factor of 2.3–2.9 across the grid, consistent with the theoretical prediction that it avoids both the per-mechanism δ0 union bound and the (e^ε0 − 1) linearization penalty of [8].

**Moments accountant equivalence.** The accountant of Abadi et al. [4] tracks α_M(λ) = max over neighbors of ln E[exp(λL)] and, for the subsampled Gaussian mechanism, bounds it by q²λ(λ+1)/((1−q)σ²) plus higher-order terms, where q is the sampling probability. Dividing by (λ − 1) recovers an (α, ε)-RDP bound with α = λ + 1, confirming that the moments accountant *is* RDP accounting with a subsampling-aware per-step bound [2].

**Tightness.** The Gaussian RDP bound D_α = αΔ²/(2σ²) is exactly tight — it is an equality, not an inequality — and the zCDP-to-DP conversion is tight up to the universal constant in the Markov step [3]. The remaining slack in end-to-end accounting therefore comes only from the final conversion, which is a one-dimensional optimization with no structural looseness. By contrast, the advanced composition bound is not tight for any natural mechanism: it is tight only against an adversarially constructed mechanism that saturates the bounded-loss assumption.

##6 Limitations

1. **RDP is not robust to post-hoc δ choices in the same way.** The conversion theorem requires δ to be fixed before optimizing α; accounting systems must therefore commit to a target δ upfront [2].
2. **Infinite orders are out of reach.** RDP at any finite α does not control the essential supremum of the privacy loss; a mechanism can satisfy strong RDP bounds while admitting a catastrophic but low-probability failure mode that (ε, δ)-DP with tiny δ would flag. The δ in the conversion is doing genuine work, and it must be chosen cryptographically small [3].
3. **zCDP's linear-in-α requirement is brittle.** Some mechanisms satisfy good RDP bounds only for α in a bounded range — the subsampled Gaussian being the canonical example, where the bound degrades as α grows [4]. These mechanisms are not zCDP at any finite ρ, and forcing them into the zCDP mold either fails or gives vacuous bounds; the analysis must stay in (α, ε)-RDP with a truncated order range [2].
4. **Amplification by iteration demands contractivity.** The results of [5] require the update map to be contractive (or the analysis to be restricted to settings where it is, such as strongly convex objectives with suitable step sizes). For non-convex deep learning — the setting where private training matters most — contractivity fails globally, and it is open whether any comparable amplification holds.
5. **Adaptive choice of α is subtle.** The optimal conversion order α* depends on the total composed ρ, which is known only after accounting is complete. Interactive systems that must report running privacy budgets need either conservative α grids or the fancier "privacy odometer" machinery, which reintroduces some slack [3].

##7 Conclusion

Concentrated and Rényi differential privacy reframe privacy accounting around the Rényi divergence curve of the privacy loss, and the reframing pays off three times. First, composition becomes addition — exact, adaptive, and heterogeneous — replacing the union-bound machinery of advanced composition with a one-line chain-rule argument [1][2][3]. Second, the Gaussian mechanism, the primitive on which private machine learning is built, admits an exact Rényi analysis, so the entire accounting pipeline from per-step noise to final (ε, δ) guarantee is tight up to a single one-dimensional optimization [2][3]. Third, the divergence lens reveals phenomena composition cannot see: privacy amplification by iteration shows that contractive noisy dynamics actively forget individual records, so that releasing only the final iterate can be far cheaper than releasing the trajectory [5]. The moments accountant of Abadi et al. [4] — the tool that made differentially private deep learning practical — is this framework's first and most consequential deployment.

The research frontier is clear: extend amplification arguments beyond contractive settings, develop accounting for mechanisms whose Rényi curves are only valid on bounded order ranges, and build interactive odometers that preserve the tightness of the offline analysis. The definitions are in place; the sharp analysis of realistic private algorithms is still being written.
