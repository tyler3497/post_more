# Ocean Alkalinity Enhancement for Marine Carbon Dioxide Removal: Carbonate-System Thermodynamics, Verification of Atmospheric CO2 Drawdown, and Ecological Risk Assessment

## Abstract

Ocean alkalinity enhancement (OAE) is an abiotic marine carbon dioxide removal (mCDR) strategy that adds alkalinity — dissolved bases, hydroxides, or crushed silicate minerals — to seawater, shifting carbonate equilibria toward bicarbonate and carbonate and driving a compensating atmospheric CO₂ influx [1][2]. This thesis synthesizes the physical chemistry of OAE — total alkalinity and dissolved inorganic carbon budgets, the nonlinear carbonate equilibrium system, and the efficiency parameter η<sub>CO₂</sub> quantifying CO₂ absorbed per mole of added alkalinity — with the operational realities of verification, monitoring, and ecological risk [3][4]. We compare fast-release (electrochemical NaOH generation, ocean liming) and slow-release (marine enhanced rock weathering with olivine) pathways, derive the thermodynamic and kinetic constraints on removal efficiency, and review mesocosm and field evidence on secondary mineral precipitation, trace-metal release, and plankton community responses [5][6]. OAE is thermodynamically robust but metrologically and ecologically delicate: net atmospheric removal is real yet hard to verify over weeks-to-months air–sea equilibration timescales, demanding rigorous measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) frameworks alongside precautionary ecological governance [2][7].

## 1 Introduction

The surface ocean absorbs roughly one quarter of annual anthropogenic CO₂ emissions, a flux governed by the partial pressure gradient across the air–sea interface and the ocean's carbonate buffer chemistry [2]. That buffer has muted atmospheric warming while driving a ~30% decline in surface-ocean pH since the preindustrial era [2]. Ocean alkalinity enhancement proposes to accelerate the buffer's restoration by deliberate addition of alkalinity, defined as the excess of proton acceptors over proton donors in seawater. Because the ocean holds roughly fifty times the inorganic carbon inventory of the atmosphere, even modest fractional increases in surface alkalinity translate into large absolute storage capacities, with theoretical deployment rates of several gigatonnes of CO₂ per year [8][9].

Two broad families of OAE intervention exist. **Fast-release** approaches introduce alkalinity directly to surface waters as dissolved base — sodium hydroxide from seawater electrolysis, or slaked lime (Ca(OH)₂) from calcined limestone — whereupon CO₂ uptake begins immediately upon air–sea gas equilibration [6][8]. **Slow-release** approaches distribute reactive minerals such as olivine ((Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄) onto coastal seafloor sediments, allowing chemical weathering to generate alkalinity *in situ* over years to decades, using the coastal ocean as a biogeochemical reactor and ocean waves as natural milling energy [6][10]. The distinction matters enormously for verification: fast-release alkalinity is a discrete, measurable perturbation of water chemistry; slow-release alkalinity emerges gradually from a dissolution process whose rate depends on grain size, passivating surface layers, and pore-water saturation states [5][11].

This thesis addresses three questions. First, what does carbonate-system thermodynamics actually permit — how many moles of atmospheric CO₂ can one mole of added alkalinity remove, and under what conditions does the theoretical yield collapse? Second, how can that removal be *verified* — attributed to the intervention rather than to natural variability, given air–sea equilibration timescales and ocean mixing? Third, what ecological risks attend deployment, and how do they constrain acceptable feedstocks and dose rates [4][2]? The answers require joint fluency in aqueous geochemistry, ocean-atmosphere gas exchange, marine ecology, and measurement science.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The marine carbonate system

Seawater carbon chemistry is described by four master variables: total dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC), total alkalinity (TA), pH, and the fugacity of CO₂ (fCO₂). Any two fully determine the rest. The equilibrium system is

CO₂(aq) + H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻  (K₁)
HCO₃⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + CO₃²⁻  (K₂)

with stoichiometric equilibrium constants K₁ and K₂ (total scale) that depend on temperature, salinity, and pressure [3]. Total alkalinity is operationally defined as

> **Theorem (Carbonate contribution to TA):** *In seawater with negligible borate-free non-carbonate contributions, TA ≈ [HCO₃⁻] + 2[CO₃²⁻] + [B(OH)₄⁻] + [OH⁻] − [H⁺]. Adding one mole of strong base increases TA by one mole-equivalent while leaving DIC unchanged; the subsequent re-equilibration with atmospheric CO₂ partitions the alkalinity between bicarbonate and carbonate, setting the CO₂ uptake efficiency.*

The Revelle (buffer) factor, *R* = (ΔpCO₂/pCO₂)/(ΔDIC/DIC), quantifies the ocean's resistance to CO₂ absorption; it rises as carbonate ion concentration declines, which is why the modern acidified ocean absorbs less efficiently per unit emission than the preindustrial ocean [2]. OAE reverses this decline.

### 2.2 Alkalinity addition pathways and stoichiometry

Fast-release OAE via slaked lime follows Ca(OH)₂ + 2CO₂ ⇌ Ca²⁺ + 2HCO₃⁻, removing up to two moles of CO₂ per mole of lime at complete equilibration, though dynamic losses (incomplete equilibration, carbonate precipitation) reduce practical efficiency to roughly 1.4 mol CO₂ per mole of Ca(OH)₂ [8]. Electrochemical generation of NaOH achieves similar chemistry without limestone calcination, at the cost of electricity and acid co-product management [2].

Slow-release OAE via forsterite dissolution follows

Mg₂SiO₄ + 4CO₂ + 4H₂O → 2Mg²⁺ + 4HCO₃⁻ + H₄SiO₄,

generating **4 mol of TA per mol of olivine** dissolved, with a mass-normalized uptake of ~0.28 g C (about 1.0–1.25 g CO₂) per gram of olivine [9][10]. Global assessments suggest OAE could store on the order of 4 Gt CO₂ yr⁻¹ — roughly 12% of annual energy-related emissions — at estimated costs of $55–107 per tonne of CO₂ sequestered [9].

### 2.3 The additionality problem

A central theoretical result, formalized by Bach (2024), is that the CO₂ uptake efficiency η<sub>CO₂</sub> — moles of atmospheric CO₂ absorbed per mole of added alkalinity after air–sea equilibration — is not a universal constant but depends critically on *what kind* of alkalinity is added [3]:

| Alkalinity source | Alkalinity character | Approximate η<sub>CO₂</sub> |
|---|---|---|
| NaOH (electrochemical) | Non-carbonate (OH⁻) | 0.39 in high-Ω perturbation experiments; near 0.85 in open-ocean equilibrium theory |
| Steel slag | Non-carbonate | 0.37–0.86 depending on DIC co-addition |
| Olivine dissolution | Mixed (carbonate-forming) | ~0.70 |
| CaCO₃-derived lime | Carbonate-system-coupled | 1.40 mol CO₂/mol Ca(OH)₂ (dynamic estimate) |

The theoretical maximum for non-carbonate alkalinity in open-ocean surface conditions approaches **0.86 mol CO₂ per mol TA** [3]. Yet efficiency collapses if (a) the added alkalinity triggers secondary CaCO₃ precipitation — each mole of carbonate precipitated *removes* 2 mol of TA and 1 mol of DIC, driving alkalinity *below* the pre-perturbation baseline even while pCO₂ remains depressed [3]; or (b) the alkalinity source co-adds DIC; or (c) baseline alkalinity formation is modified by the intervention [3].

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis combines three methodological strands: (i) analytical carbonate-system computation using the standard stoichiometric equilibrium framework; (ii) synthesis of peer-reviewed experimental evidence from mesocosm, flow-through reactor, and modeling studies; and (iii) formal modeling of verification protocols.

### 3.1 Carbonate-system computation

We compute post-perturbation equilibria by solving the alkalinity mass balance

TA(DIC, pH) = [HCO₃⁻] + 2[CO₃²⁻] + B_T/(1 + [H⁺]/K_B) + K_w/[H⁺] − [H⁺],

with [HCO₃⁻] = DIC·K₁[H⁺]/([H⁺]² + K₁[H⁺] + K₁K₂) and [CO₃²⁻] = DIC·K₁K₂/([H⁺]² + K₁[H⁺] + K₁K₂). The uptake efficiency follows from the differential response at constant fCO₂:

> **Theorem (Efficiency bound):** *For a surface-ocean parcel at equilibrium with fixed atmospheric pCO₂, η<sub>CO₂</sub> = −∂DIC/∂TA|_(pCO₂) is bounded above by the ratio [HCO₃⁻] + [CO₃²⁻]-weighted buffer response and evaluates to 0.75–0.86 under typical surface conditions (T = 15–25 °C, S = 35); it vanishes identically if secondary CaCO₃ precipitation removes TA faster than gas exchange restores equilibrium [3].*

A reference implementation of this solver is:

```python
import numpy as np

def carbonate_species(DIC, TA, K1=1.39e-6, K2=1.07e-9, BT=4.326e-4, KB=2.53e-9, KW=6.06e-14):
    """Solve for [H+] given DIC and TA by Newton iteration on the TA residual."""
    H = 1e-8  # initial guess, mol/kg
    for _ in range(60):
        d = H**2 + K1*H + K1*K2
        HCO3 = DIC * K1*H / d
        CO3  = DIC * K1*K2 / d
        BO4  = BT / (1 + H/KB)
        OH   = KW / H
        R = HCO3 + 2*CO3 + BO4 + OH - H - TA
        # numerical derivative
        eps = H * 1e-6
        d2 = (H+eps)**2 + K1*(H+eps) + K1*K2
        R2 = (DIC*K1*(H+eps)/d2 + 2*DIC*K1*K2/d2
              + BT/(1+(H+eps)/KB) + KW/(H+eps) - (H+eps)) - TA
        dR = (R2 - R) / eps
        H -= R / dR
    return H, DIC * K1*H / (H**2 + K1*H + K1*K2)

def efficiency_eta(TA0, DIC0, dTA, pco2_target=420e-6, K0=3.4e-2):
    """eta_CO2: moles DIC gained per mole TA added at fixed pCO2."""
    H0, _ = carbonate_species(DIC0, TA0)
    DIC_eq = pco2_target / (K0 * (H0**2) / (H0**2 + 1.39e-6*H0 + 1.39e-6*1.07e-9))
    H1, _ = carbonate_species(DIC_eq, TA0 + dTA)
    # re-equilibrate: find DIC with pCO2(H1) == target by iteration
    lo, hi = DIC_eq, DIC_eq + 2*dTA
    for _ in range(80):
        mid = 0.5*(lo+hi)
        Hm, _ = carbonate_species(mid, TA0 + dTA)
        co2 = K0 * mid * Hm**2 / (Hm**2 + 1.39e-6*Hm + 1.39e-6*1.07e-9)
        if co2 > pco2_target: hi = mid
        else: lo = mid
    return (0.5*(lo+hi) - DIC_eq) / dTA
```

### 3.2 Verification protocol modeling

MRV for OAE must attribute an air–sea CO₂ flux to the alkalinity perturbation amid a noisy baseline. We formalize the verification state machine in TLA+ to prove that no deployment may claim net-negative status before the air–sea equilibration window closes:

```tla
--------------------------- MODULE OAE_MRV ---------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, TLC

VARIABLES phase,    \* {"dosed", "equilibrating", "verified", "failed"}
          taAnomaly, \* measured ΔTA relative to baseline (umol/kg)
          pco2Air,  \* atmospheric pCO2 above the parcel
          pco2Sea   \* seawater pCO2 of the parcel

Init == /\ phase = "dosed"
        /\ taAnomaly > 0
        /\ pco2Sea < pco2Air          \* perturbation depresses seawater pCO2

Equilibrate == /\ phase = "equilibrating"
               /\ pco2Sea' \in {x \in Nat : x >= pco2Sea /\ x <= pco2Air}
               /\ UNCHANGED <<phase, taAnomaly, pco2Air>>

Verify == /\ phase = "equilibrating"
          /\ pco2Sea = pco2Air        \* gas equilibrium reached
          /\ taAnomaly > 0            \* alkalinity not lost to precipitation
          /\ phase' = "verified"
          /\ UNCHANGED <<taAnomaly, pco2Air, pco2Sea>>

Fail == /\ taAnomaly <= 0
        /\ phase' = "failed"
        /\ UNCHANGED <<taAnomaly, pco2Air, pco2Sea>>

Next == Equilibrate \/ Verify \/ Fail
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<phase, taAnomaly, pco2Sea>>

NoFalseClaim == [](phase = "verified" => taAnomaly > 0)
=============================================================================
```

The invariant `NoFalseClaim` encodes the core MRV integrity property: verified status is reachable only when the alkalinity anomaly survives to equilibration — secondary precipitation cannot be quietly absorbed into a net-removal claim [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Fast-release alkalinity: electrochemical NaOH and ocean liming

Electrochemical OAE splits seawater to produce NaOH (with HCl or chlorine as co-products requiring management). Its appeal is *controllability*: a known dose, added at a known time and place, produces an immediate, measurable TA anomaly against which subsequent pCO₂ depression and air–sea flux can be compared [2][3]. In perturbation experiments, however, NaOH additions that drive Ω_aragonite sharply upward can nucleate secondary CaCO₃ on suspended particles; in sand-bearing incubations the resulting precipitation removed ~560 µmol kg⁻¹ of TA — *exceeding* the added alkalinity — while η<sub>CO₂</sub> remained positive (~0.39) because seawater pCO₂ stayed below baseline [3]. The intervention can thus degrade ocean chemistry even while carbon accounting remains nominally positive.

Ocean liming via Ca(OH)₂ faces a parallel constraint. Lime calcination (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂) releases one mole of CO₂ per mole of lime; unless captured, the cradle-to-grave ledger must subtract it [8]. With a conservative practical efficiency of 1.4 mol CO₂ per mol Ca(OH)₂, roughly 1.62 tonnes of CaCO₃ are required per tonne of CO₂ removed [8] — a logistics figure that bounds scalability by limestone supply and shipping emissions.

### 4.2 Slow-release alkalinity: olivine dissolution kinetics and the grain-size trap

Olivine's appeal is its natural abundance, fast intrinsic weathering rate among silicates, and avoidance of high-temperature processing [6][9]. But laboratory kinetics translate poorly to the ocean because of a *surface-area–residence-time dilemma*. Dissolution rate laws scale with reactive surface area; for particles to dissolve completely *within the mixed layer* (before sinking below the air–sea equilibration horizon), modeling indicates grain sizes on the order of **1 µm** are required [10]. Grinding dunite to 1 µm is enormously energy-intensive, and lifecycle analyses estimate that grinding energy alone can reduce net carbon sequestration efficiency by up to **30%** when powered by fossil fuels [6][10].

Sediment application sidesteps grinding to some extent by using the surf zone as a natural mill: wave-driven grain collisions abrade passivating secondary layers and continuously refresh reactive surfaces. Flow-through beach simulations show initial alkalinity release rates of ~11.5 mmol per mol olivine per day in turbulent olivine–sand mixtures, settling to a steady ~1.0–1.1 mmol mol⁻¹ d⁻¹ after ~74 hours [11]. Alkalinity released into *pore water* must still exchange with the overlying water column and then the atmosphere — a transport chain with its own weeks-to-months timescale that complicates attribution [11][2].

Secondary mineral formation further erodes the theoretical 4:1 alkalinity-to-olivine ratio. Incomplete dissolution yields alteration products (serpentine, smectites, iddingsite) with reduced or null alkalinity production, while dissolved Mg²⁺ and silicic acid can precipitate carbonates, iron minerals, or clays [6]. The alkalinity production factor *f_AT* — the fraction of the theoretical maximum actually realized — thus ranges from 0 to 1 depending on kinetics, nucleation sites, and water exchange rate [6].

### 4.3 Air–sea equilibration and the verification bottleneck

The CO₂ absorbed by OAE is not the CO₂ hydrated *at the moment of dosing*; it is the cumulative air–sea flux driven by depressed seawater pCO₂ over the gas-equilibration timescale. For a 50 m mixed layer, that timescale is of order weeks to a few months, during which the alkalized parcel advects, mixes, and entrains ambient water that dilutes the TA anomaly [2][3]. Verification therefore requires Lagrangian tracking or well-constrained regional budgets: autonomous sensor networks measuring pH (spectrophotometric), pCO₂ (membrane equilibrator NDIR), and TA/DIC (via paired pH–pCO₂) on moored buoys, gliders, and wave-powered surface vehicles [2].

A minimal telemetry packet for such a buoy can be encoded compactly:

```rust
/// MRV buoy telemetry packet: fixed 32-byte frame, CRC-32 trailing.

impl MrvFrame {
```

Baseline determination is the deepest MRV challenge: the ocean's natural TA and pCO₂ vary on seasonal and interannual timescales, so the counterfactual "flux without dosing" must be constructed from control regions or biogeochemical models, with uncertainty propagated into the credited tonnage [2][3].

### 4.4 Ecological risk assessment: trace metals, silicic acid fertilization, and community shifts

The ecological ledger of OAE is dominated by *co-released substances*, not by alkalinity itself. Olivine-rich dunite contains nickel and chromium at percent-relevant trace levels; flow-through experiments recorded initial Ni spikes of 359 nmol L⁻¹ and Cr of 171 nmol L⁻¹, decaying to background under high dilution but posing acute exposure near dosing points [11]. Nickel is a known toxicant for several marine taxa, and its release scales with the same dissolution rate that generates alkalinity — benefit and hazard are mechanistically coupled [4][11].

Silicic acid is the second co-release: one mole of H₄SiO₄ per mole of olivine dissolved. At global OAE scales of ~10 Gt yr⁻¹ of dunite, this represents a dissolved-silicon source **5–14 times larger than all natural sources combined** [4]. In silicon-limited regions, this fertilizes diatoms, shifting phytoplankton community composition away from calcifiers — a change that alters the biological carbon pump, with both the sign and magnitude of the feedback remaining poorly constrained [4][10]. Mesocosm experiments with olivine and slag additions to a Tasmanian coastal plankton community found that 1 g of slag released 9,626 µmol TA in three weeks versus only 16 µmol TA for olivine of similar grain size — a ~600-fold efficiency gap over short timescales — while both feedstocks measurably altered carbonate chemistry and community dynamics [5].

> **Theorem (Risk–efficiency coupling):** *For mineral-based OAE, the dissolution rate that determines CO₂ removal efficiency simultaneously determines the flux of co-released trace metals and silicic acid; feedstock selection therefore cannot optimize efficiency and ecological safety independently — minimizing the product of (trace-metal content × dissolution rate) is a first-order design constraint [4][11].*

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Quantitative synthesis of efficiency measurements

Compiling experimental η<sub>CO₂</sub> determinations shows a consistent hierarchy: non-carbonate alkalinity (NaOH, slag) achieves 0.37–0.86 depending on DIC co-addition and precipitation losses; olivine-derived alkalinity centers near 0.70; lime is best expressed as ~1.4 mol CO₂ per mol Ca(OH)₂ under dynamic conditions [3][8]. The Bach (2024) perturbation experiments provide the decisive proof that efficiency is *state-dependent*: identical alkalinity doses produced divergent η<sub>CO₂</sub> depending on DIC co-addition and on whether precipitation was nucleated by suspended particles [3].

### 5.2 Dissolution-rate evidence

Three independent experimental lines — sediment incubations, Flipkens grain-collision reactors [12], and surf-zone flow-through systems [11] — converge on the conclusion that olivine dissolves more slowly in real seawater systems than intrinsic rate laws predict, owing to passivating layers and pore-water saturation, with initial rate transients settling to lower steady states.

### 5.3 Community-response evidence

The Tasmanian mesocosm study [5] provides the most direct ecological proof-of-effect at community scale: olivine and slag additions at field-plausible doses restructured the coastal plankton assemblage in three weeks, with slag's rapid dissolution driving larger carbonate-chemistry excursions. Combined with the silicic-acid fertilization analysis [4] and the Köhler ecosystem-model prediction of diatom-vs-calcifier species shifts [10], the evidence establishes that OAE at scale is unavoidably also an ocean-fertilization experiment [2].

## 6 Limitations

First, the thermodynamic efficiencies derived here assume complete air–sea equilibration; in energetic, rapidly diluted coastal deployments, realized η<sub>CO₂</sub> may fall substantially below laboratory values, and no field deployment has yet demonstrated end-to-end atmospheric drawdown at tonne scale with independent verification [2][3]. Second, our carbonate solver uses fixed stoichiometric constants and neglects organic alkalinity, which can bias TA budgets in productive coastal waters by tens of µmol kg⁻¹. Third, secondary precipitation is treated as a binary nucleation event in the TLA+ model, whereas real precipitation is a kinetic, particle-mediated process with hysteresis. Fourth, ecological risk extrapolations from mesocosms (weeks, liters-to-kiloliters) to regional deployments (years, megatonnes) cross many orders of magnitude; chronic low-level trace-metal exposure effects remain essentially unstudied [4][11]. Fifth, cost and lifecycle estimates ($55–107 tCO₂⁻¹) predate recent energy-price volatility and exclude long-term monitoring costs, which for rigorous MRV may rival dosing costs [2][9].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Ocean alkalinity enhancement is thermodynamically sound, kinetically constrained, metrologically demanding, and ecologically non-trivial. The carbonate system guarantees that added alkalinity *can* remove 0.7–0.86 moles of CO₂ per mole of TA — but only if the alkalinity survives secondary precipitation, only if the alkalized water re-equilibrates with the atmosphere before dilution erases the anomaly, and only if verification frameworks can separate the intervention signal from the ocean's natural variability [3][2]. Slow-release olivine pathways trade controllability for lower energy cost, accepting grinding penalties, passivation, trace-metal co-release, and silicic-acid fertilization as the price of scalability [6][10][11][4]. The research strategy articulated by the National Academies — staged, transparent experimentation with a code of conduct preceding any deployment — remains the only credible route from today's mesocosm and reactor studies to a verifiable gigatonne-scale carbon removal technology [2].
