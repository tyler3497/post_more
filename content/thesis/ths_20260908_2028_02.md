# Deterministic Record-Replay Debugging: rr's PTRACE Interception, Chaos Mode Scheduling, and Reverse Execution

## Abstract

Deterministic record-replay debugging captures a failing execution exactly once and replays it with instruction-level fidelity, converting irreproducible *Heisenbugs* into ordinary reproducible failures. This thesis dissects **rr**, Mozilla's user-space record-replay debugger, whose governing design constraint — *deployability on stock Linux with commodity x86 hardware and no kernel modifications* — forced a sequence of unusually elegant mechanisms. We present rr's **ptrace**-based syscall interception, including its injected syscall-buffering library, per-thread scratch-memory redirection of output buffers, and the taxonomy of *blocking* versus *replayed* system calls. We analyze Intel's retired-conditional-branch (**RCB**) counter — the only deterministic progress metric on modern x86 — and show why the pair *(RCB count, full register state)* identifies asynchronous signal-delivery points. We describe **chaos mode**, which perturbs thread scheduling during recording to surface races while keeping every trace exactly replayable, and show how single-core replay, fork-based checkpoints, and replay-from-checkpoint implement `reverse-continue` and `reverse-step`. We contrast rr with **ReVirt**'s virtual-machine logging [3][4], **dthreads**' deterministic multithreading, and **Samsara**'s hardware-assisted chunk replay [5][6], and quantify the overheads that make rr practical: roughly 1.2x recording slowdown on typical workloads [1].

## 1 Introduction

Few problems in software engineering are as demoralizing as the *flaky test*: a failure that manifests once in ten thousand runs and vanishes under observation. The root cause is **nondeterminism** — thread interleavings, signal delivery times, `RDTSC` readings, network arrival orders — that the debugger cannot reproduce. *Record-replay* attacks this directly: record every nondeterministic input during one execution, then replay the identical instruction stream deterministically under full debugger control [1][2].

The literature divides along a fault line. On one side stand systems that instrument *everything*: whole-system VM loggers such as **ReVirt** [3][4], logging below the guest OS, and multiprocessor schemes such as **Samsara** [5][6], tracking read/write-sets to log the total order of memory chunks. On the other side stands **rr**, which made a radical simplifying bet: for *user-space* debugging, intercepting the syscall boundary and constraining thread scheduling suffices to make everything between interceptions deterministic [1]. No hypervisor. No kernel module. Just `ptrace`, `LD_PRELOAD`, and one fortuitous hardware counter.

> **Thesis claim.** Deterministic replay of unmodified multithreaded Linux binaries is achievable in user space with near-native performance, provided that (i) all kernel-mediated nondeterminism is funneled through intercepted system calls, (ii) userspace progress is measured by a counter that is a pure function of the executed instruction sequence, and (iii) thread scheduling is constrained to a single core so that preemptions become the only interleaving events.

Section 2 surveys the lineage from ReVirt through deterministic multithreading to hardware-assisted replay. Section 3 formalizes rr's determinism contract and recording methodology. Section 4 examines ptrace interception and syscall buffering (4.1), the RCB counter (4.2), chaos-mode scheduling (4.3), and checkpoints with reverse execution (4.4). Section 5 states the key theorems and reports empirical overheads; Section 6 enumerates limitations; Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The record-replay problem

An execution is a sequence of *deterministic transitions* (pure functions of prior state) interleaved with *nondeterministic inputs* (syscall results, signals, preemptions). Recording captures a log $L$ of all nondeterministic inputs; replay re-executes while substituting logged values, reproducing the identical state sequence [1]. The difficulty is the *capture*: missing any channel breaks fidelity.

| System | Recording layer | Nondeterminism captured | Typical overhead | Replay granularity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ReVirt** [3][4] | Below the VMM (UMLinux guest) | Interrupts at $(eip, \mathrm{branch\text{-}count})$; syscalls with external data | 0–8% logging; 13–58% virtualization (kernel-heavy) | Whole VM, instruction-by-instruction |
| **dthreads** (Liu et al.) | User-space scheduler (LD_PRELOAD) | Thread interleaving via deterministic tokens | Moderate; serialization of critical sections | Deterministic *by construction*, no log |
| **Samsara** [5][6] | KVM + HAV page tracking | Chunk read/write-sets; chunk total order | ~2.3x recording; log 1/70th of prior schemes | Multiprocessor chunk replay |
| **rr** [1][2] | User space (`ptrace` + preload) | Syscall results, signals, single-core schedule | ~1.2x typical, 1.25–3x worst case | Single process tree, instruction-exact |

### 2.2 ReVirt: logging beneath the machine

ReVirt (Dunlap et al., OSDI 2002) encapsulates the target OS and applications inside a virtual machine and places the logger *beneath* the VMM, so even an intruder who replaces the guest kernel cannot tamper with the log [3]. To replay asynchronous virtual interrupts at exactly the right point, ReVirt records the pair $(\mathit{eip}, \mathit{branch\ count})$ before delivering each interrupt and re-delivers at the same pair during replay [4] — a direct ancestor of rr's $(RCB, \mathit{registers})$ signal-delivery scheme. ReVirt's overheads were modest (0–8% logging), but it required a purpose-built VMM and logged whole-machine state transitions: a heavyweight posture for day-to-day debugging.

### 2.3 Deterministic multithreading: dthreads

An orthogonal tradition eliminates the log by making scheduling itself deterministic. **dthreads** replaces `pthreads` with a deterministic scheduler: threads run as deterministic logical processes synchronized through deterministic tokens, so the same program with the same input always yields the same interleaving. The price is a changed execution model and the inability to replay an *arbitrary historical* execution. rr inverts this tradeoff: it accepts the kernel's wild scheduling during recording, logs the outcome, and reproduces it exactly during replay.

### 2.4 Samsara: hardware-assisted chunk replay for multiprocessors

**Samsara** (Ren et al., ATC 2016) targets replay on commodity *multicore* processors with a chunk-based strategy plus hardware-assisted virtualization (HAV) [5]: each core executes instruction *chunks* and only the chunk total order is logged. HAV page-tracking yields each chunk's read/write-set without instrumenting every access in software, cutting the log to 1/70th of prior schemes and overhead from ~10x to ~2.3x [6]. Samsara shows what hardware assistance buys; rr shows how far one can go *without* it, by pinning execution to a single core.

### 2.5 rr's position

rr's contribution is a *deployability* argument: restricted to single-process-tree user-space execution on one core, every nondeterminism source is observable through `ptrace` at the syscall boundary, and replay reduces to re-executing userspace code with injected recorded values [1][2] — `rr record ./flaky_test` on an unmodified binary on a stock kernel [7][8].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The determinism contract

rr's methodology rests on a single contract:

> **Definition 1 (rr-determinism).** A user-space execution is *rr-deterministic* iff every transition not mediated by the kernel is a pure function of prior user-space state, and every kernel-mediated transition is either (a) recorded during capture and substituted during replay, or (b) provably independent of external state.

Concretely: syscall results, signal delivery points, the thread schedule, and nondeterministic-instruction outcomes (`RDTSC`, `RDRAND`) — everything else replays itself.

### 3.2 Recording architecture

The recorder is a *supervisor process* that launches the target (the *tracee*) and attaches via `ptrace`. Naively, intercepting every syscall costs four context switches per call — prohibitive for syscall-heavy workloads — so rr interposes a **syscall buffering library** (`librrpreload`, injected via `LD_PRELOAD`) that intercepts common `libc` wrappers, executes the real syscall, appends the result to a shared per-thread trace buffer, and returns *without* trapping to the supervisor [1].

The subtlety is output buffers: in `read(fd, buf, size)`, the running tracee thread could race with the kernel's write to `buf`. rr **redirects output buffers to per-thread scratch memory** — a region otherwise unused by the application — via `ptrace` register manipulation at syscall entry. When a *blocked* syscall completes, the supervisor receives a ptrace event, reads the result register, and copies scratch contents to the real destination *while no other tracee thread runs*, eliminating the race [1][2]:

```
tracee thread T                recorder (supervisor)
─────────────                  ─────────────────────
read(fd, buf, size)
  ptrace trap ──────────────► waitpid(T); redirect arg2 → scratch
                               ptrace(T, PTRACE_SYSCALL)
◄───────────── kernel: sys_read writes into scratch
  (blocks)   ──────────────► waitpid(T): syscall-exit event
                               N = result register
                               copy N scratch bytes → trace + buf
                               ptrace(T, PTRACE_SYSCALL)
```

### 3.3 Blocking versus replayed syscalls

rr classifies syscalls four ways:

1. **Buffered** — handled by `librrpreload`; results appended to the trace buffer; the supervisor never wakes.
2. **Descheduled (blocking)** — a blocking syscall triggers a context switch, detected via a perf event; the supervisor runs the scratch-copy protocol and records the result.
3. **Emulated** — syscalls with no externally visible effect implemented in userspace (e.g., `getpid` after recording the pid layout).
4. **Replayed** — during *replay*, syscalls are not executed: the supervisor intercepts at syscall entry, injects the recorded return value and output buffers, and resumes past the syscall instruction. The kernel is never entered.

This classification is the crux of rr's performance: the common case avoids the supervisor entirely, while the rare blocking case pays full ptrace cost only when it must.

### 3.4 Scheduling on a single core

rr binds all tracee threads to a **single CPU core** (configurable via `--num-cores`). With one hardware context, the only interleaving events are kernel preemptions and blocking syscalls — both observable by the supervisor. The supervisor logs the thread-run order during recording and enforces the identical order during replay by keeping all but the scheduled thread stopped.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 PTRACE Interception and the Syscall Buffering Library

The buffering fast path must survive adversarial corners:

- **Interrupted syscalls.** If a signal arrives mid-call, the library must unwind its in-progress trace record so the log stays linearizable. rr makes buffer commits atomic with respect to the signal handler's view: the record length is stored *after* the payload, in a single store.
- **Unbufferable syscalls.** Complex `ioctl`s and `execve` fall back to full ptrace trapping; `execve` is followed and the preload library re-injected into the new image.
- **The `vDSO` problem.** `gettimeofday` via vDSO never enters the kernel and never traps. rr patches or interposes at the libc level so time queries become recorded nondeterministic inputs.

> **Lemma 1 (Buffering transparency).** *For any buffered syscall, the tracee-observable behavior — return value, `errno`, output-buffer contents, signal interactions — is identical to native execution, and the trace record suffices to reproduce all four during replay.*

*Proof sketch.* The genuine syscall runs with identical arguments; outputs are committed to the trace before return; signals are serialized with the commit. ∎

### 4.2 The Retired Conditional Branch Counter as a Deterministic Clock

Signal delivery, preemption points, and reverse-execution targets must all be named by *position in the instruction stream*. A hardware performance counter seems ideal — until one demands **determinism**: re-executing the same instruction sequence must advance the counter identically, independent of caches, page tables, and speculation [1].

Almost no counter satisfies this. "Instructions retired" fails on every known x86 microarchitecture: an instruction interrupted by a page fault is restarted and *counted twice*. But modern Intel Core CPUs provide exactly one deterministic counter: **retired conditional branches (RCB)**. A conditional branch retires exactly once per dynamic execution, so rr programs the PMU to count RCBs in userspace only.

The RCB count alone does not uniquely identify an execution point — two points can share a count — so rr pairs it with the **complete register state**:

> **Theorem 1 (Execution-point identification).** *Let $P$ be a deterministic userspace instruction sequence and $(c, R)$ the RCB count and register file observed at some point. Then, with probability 1 over programs encountered in practice, $(c, R)$ uniquely identifies that execution point.*

*Proof sketch.* $c$ restricts the search to the small set $S_c$ of points with that count; within $S_c$ the full register file (including the instruction pointer) disambiguates, since two distinct points with identical machine state would imply non-termination. ∎

Signal delivery exploits this directly. During recording, rr logs $(c, R)$ when each asynchronous signal arrives. During replay, it breakpoints at the next syscall boundary, programs the PMU to interrupt after $c - c_{\mathrm{now}}$ branches, compares registers to $R$ at each interrupt, and injects the signal on match [1]. VMware's *VM-exit clustering* breaks RCB determinism and must be disabled [1][2].

```python
# GDB automation: reverse-continue to the last write of a poisoned pointer
# (typical rr session, after `rr replay`)
import gdb
gdb.execute("watch *suspicious_ptr")   # hardware watchpoint on the variable
gdb.execute("reverse-continue")          # lands on the last dynamic write
frame = gdb.selected_frame()
print("Last writer:", frame.function(), "at", frame.find_sal().line)
gdb.execute("reverse-step")              # walk further back, one instruction at a time
```

### 4.3 Chaos Mode: Scheduling Determinism by Deliberate Perturbation

A perverse irony of record-replay: the better the recorder, the more faithfully it reproduces the *one* schedule it captured — while thread bugs hide in the schedules not captured. rr's answer is **chaos mode** (`rr record --chaos`), which perturbs scheduling during recording with random descheduling points and timing jitter, so repeated recordings of the same test explore *different* interleavings.

Chaos belongs to **recording**, determinism to **replay**: $k$ chaotic runs yield $k$ distinct, each exactly replayable, traces. A race manifesting in 1 of $k$ runs is captured forever — debuggable with `reverse-continue` as if perfectly reproducible. Mozilla used chaos mode to shake out intermittent Firefox failures that had resisted months of conventional investigation [1][7].

> **Theorem 2 (Chaos coverage).** *Let $\mathcal{B}$ be the interleavings exposing a given race, and $p = \Pr_{S \sim \mathcal{D}}[S \in \mathcal{B}] > 0$ the probability one chaotic recording hits an exposing schedule. Then $k$ independent chaotic recordings capture the race with probability $1 - (1-p)^k$, and every captured execution is exactly replayable.*

Engineers thus *manufacture* failing schedules on demand, then debug them deterministically.

### 4.4 Checkpoints, Single-Core Replay, and Reverse Execution

Reverse execution in rr is **replay from the nearest checkpoint**, in three parts:

1. **Automatic checkpoints.** During replay, rr periodically `fork()`s the tracee process tree, creating copy-on-write snapshots — cheap (page-table copy only), with no kernel support needed, unlike CRIU-based approaches that must detach and reattach `ptrace` around the snapshot [10].
2. **Deterministic forward replay.** Replay is a pure function of the trace, so replaying forward from a restored checkpoint is bit-identical.
3. **Reverse commands as bounded forward search.** `reverse-step` restores the latest checkpoint at or before the target and replays to the preceding event; `reverse-continue` does the same under a breakpoint/watchpoint condition. Checkpoint intervals bound worst-case search latency, trading memory against reverse-step cost.

The canonical idiom [7]: run forward to the crash; `watch` the corrupted variable; `reverse-continue` lands on the *last* dynamic write — even millions of instructions earlier, through an unrelated variable's lifetime bug; then `reverse-step` up the causal chain. Because replay is exact — "the memory layout is always the same, the addresses of objects don't change, register values are identical, syscalls return the same data" [8] — the backward walk is trustworthy in a way heuristic time-travel approximations are not.

```
record:   ──●──────●──────●──────●──✕ (crash)
               ckpt1    ckpt2    ckpt3
reverse-continue to watchpoint W:
          restore ckpt2 ──► replay forward ──► stop at last write to W
```

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Theoretical Results

### 5.1 Theoretical results

rr's theoretical contribution is constructive: user-space nondeterminism sources are *finite, enumerable, and interceptable* at the syscall boundary. Theorems 1–2 give identification and coverage; a third governs overhead:

> **Theorem 3 (Overhead decomposition).** *Let $T_{\mathrm{native}}$ be native time, $n_t$ the number of trapped syscalls, $n_s$ the number of signals, and $c_{\mathrm{trap}}$ the per-trap cost. Then $T_{\mathrm{record}} \approx T_{\mathrm{native}} + n_t \cdot c_{\mathrm{trap}} + n_s \cdot c_{\mathrm{sig}} + \epsilon$. Recording overhead is dominated by the trap rate, not the syscall rate.*

### 5.2 Measured overheads

| Metric | rr [1][8] | ReVirt [3] | Samsara [5][6] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recording slowdown (typical) | ~1.2x | 1.0–1.08x logging | ~2.3x |
| Recording slowdown (worst) | 1.25–3x | 1.13–1.58x (kernel-heavy) | ~10x (prior software schemes) |
| Log growth | ~MB/s trace stream | Months of traffic per disk | 1/70th of prior schemes |
| Memory overhead | Modest (CoW fork checkpoints) | VM snapshot state | Chunk read/write-sets |
| Hardware required | Intel x86 + RCB PMU | Purpose-built VMM | Commodity HAV (KVM) |

rr's memory overhead is "relatively modest and is not an issue in practice" [1]. Recent releases added `perf_event_paranoid=2` compatibility (kernel 6.10+), AArch64 builds, and a `WaitManager` for thousand-thread workloads [8].

### 5.3 Deployability as an empirical claim

The evidence is sociological: rr records unmodified Firefox test runs on stock kernels, composes with sanitizers, speaks the `gdb` remote protocol (plus recent `lldb` replay support [8]), and pairs with fuzzers for reverse-execution debugging [7][9]. Practitioners report root-causing concurrency bugs "in a matter of seconds or minutes, instead of the usual days or weeks" [7].

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **The RCB monoculture.** rr rests on a single Intel counter. AMD support has been partial, AArch64 support arrived only recently [8], and counter-perturbing virtualization features (VMware VM-exit clustering) must be manually disabled [1]. A microarchitecture without a deterministic counter cannot host rr.
2. **Single-core scheduling.** Pinning to one core removes true parallelism. Bugs requiring genuine multicore execution may not reproduce; conversely rr's scheduling can be unfair enough to produce "fake hangs" absent natively [7].
3. **Invisible nondeterminism.** `RDTSC`/`RDRAND` are trapped and emulated, but GPU execution, DMA, unknown `ioctl`s, and unpatched `vDSO` paths break fidelity. Each new kernel interface is a new source to audit.
4. **Atomic-spin races.** Races mediated purely by `std::atomic` busy-loops generate enormous RCB counts with no observable events: faithful but slow ("excessive conditional branches can make `rr record` several orders of magnitude slower" [7]), and `reverse-continue` may miss breakpoints there [7].
5. **Bounded reverse search.** Worst-case `reverse-continue` latency scales with checkpoint interval — a direct tradeoff between snapshot frequency and reverse latency.
6. **The external world.** Network peers and filesystem state are recorded as *data* at the syscall boundary, not reproduced as *causes*; replaying a distributed interaction yields identical local behavior but cannot regenerate the remote peer's timing.

**Open problems.** (i) *Multicore replay without hardware modification*: absorb Samsara-style chunk tracking [5] while staying deployable? (ii) *Counter-free progress metrics* from purely software-observable signals. (iii) *Composable traces* for distributed debugging, reconstructing cross-node causality over exact local replays.

---

## 7 Conclusion

rr's enduring lesson is architectural: **choose the narrowest boundary that still captures all nondeterminism, and engineer ruthlessly at that boundary**. The syscall interface, plus one deterministic hardware counter, plus single-core scheduling, proved to be exactly such a boundary for user-space Linux debugging. ptrace interception with a buffering fast path made recording cheap; scratch-memory redirection made it correct; the (RCB, registers) pair made asynchronous events nameable; chaos mode turned scheduling from an enemy into a test-generation tool; fork-based checkpoints turned deterministic replay into genuine reverse execution.

The lineage is visible in both directions. Backward: ReVirt's $(eip, \mathrm{branch\text{-}count})$ interrupt timestamps [3][4] prefigure rr's signal-delivery scheme. Forward: Samsara [5][6] shows the price of lifting the single-core restriction, and rr's own evolution — AArch64 support, `perf_event_paranoid=2` compatibility, thousand-thread scalability [8] — shows headroom remains. The thesis stands: record once, with chaos if you must; replay forever, backward as well as forward.
