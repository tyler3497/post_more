# Neural Scaling Laws: From Kaplan Power Laws to Chinchilla Compute-Optimality, Broken Scaling, and the Emergence Debate

## Abstract

This treatise reconstructs the quantitative foundations of large language model (LLM) scaling: the power-law phenomenology of Kaplan et al. [1], the compute-optimal correction of Hoffmann et al. (Chinchilla) [2], the piecewise generalization of Caballero et al.'s Broken Neural Scaling Laws [3], and the contested interpretation of "emergent abilities" proposed by Wei et al. [5] and challenged by Schaeffer et al. [4]. We derive the canonical loss functionals L(N, D, C), explain why Kaplan's prescription — scale parameters preferentially, N ∝ C^0.73, D ∝ C^0.27 — collapsed under Chinchilla's isoFLOP experiments into the equal-scaling rule N ∝ C^0.5, D ∝ C^0.5 (≈ 20 tokens per parameter), and show how BNSL's breakpoint-parameterized form explains the nonmonotonic and delayed-sharp scaling phenomena that pure power laws cannot. We adjudicate the emergence debate with the metric-sharpness analysis of Schaeffer et al.: nonlinear and discontinuous metrics manufacture apparent phase transitions from smooth improvements in token-level log-probability, with over 92% of claimed emergent abilities attributable to two metrics alone. The analysis closes with data-constrained scaling [6] and modern recalibrations [7][8].

---

## 1. Introduction

Few empirical regularities in machine learning have had the economic and scientific impact of neural scaling laws. Training a single frontier model costs tens of millions of dollars; the decision of how to split a fixed compute budget between *model size* N (non-embedding parameters) and *dataset size* D (training tokens) is therefore one of the highest-stakes optimization problems in applied research. Before 2020, this allocation was governed by heuristics — anecdote, taste, and extrapolation from small runs. Kaplan et al. [1] replaced heuristics with measurement: across more than seven orders of magnitude of compute, language-model cross-entropy loss obeys smooth power laws in N, D, and C, with "minimal effects" from architectural details like width or depth within wide ranges.

The field's confidence in these laws was shaken — and then rebuilt — by two developments. First, Hoffmann et al. [2] demonstrated that Kaplan's flagship allocation rule was an artifact of under-converged small-model experiments, and that the true compute-optimal allocation scales parameters and data *equally*. The resulting Chinchilla model (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) defeated Gopher (280B parameters, 300B tokens) at identical compute, inaugurating the era of compute-optimal training and, later, "overtraining" small models for inference efficiency.

Second, the cleanest scaling regularity — that *loss* decreases smoothly — was shown to coexist with apparently discontinuous jumps in *capability*: Wei et al. [5] documented dozens of abilities (chain-of-thought reasoning, instruction following, multi-step arithmetic) that appear abruptly at critical scales of roughly 10²²–10²⁴ training FLOPs. If loss scales smoothly but capabilities arrive as phase transitions, then loss-based scaling laws are an incomplete guide to frontier behavior — and safety-relevant capabilities cannot be predicted by extrapolation. Schaeffer et al. [4] offered a deflationary rejoinder: the sharpness is largely an artifact of *metric choice*. Nonlinear or discontinuous metrics (exact match, multiple-choice grading) convert smooth improvements in per-token log-probabilities into apparent step functions; continuous metrics (Brier score, token-level cross-entropy) reveal the same underlying progress as gradual and predictable.

This paper synthesizes these threads. We begin with the functional forms themselves (§2–§4), treat the emergence debate as a question about *what is measured* rather than *what scales* (§4.4, §5), and conclude with the constraints that now dominate practical scaling: finite high-quality data [6], inference-aware optimality, and hyperparameter recalibration [7][8].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The compute model

Throughout this paper, N denotes the number of non-embedding parameters, D the number of training tokens, and C the training compute in FLOPs. For a standard dense Transformer with one forward and one backward pass per token, the conventional approximation is:

```
C ≈ 6 · N · D
```

The factor of 6 decomposes as approximately 2N FLOPs per token for the forward pass and 4N for the backward pass. This single equation is the budget constraint of the entire field: every scaling law is, at bottom, a statement about how L(N, D) behaves on the surface C = 6ND = const.

### 2.2 What a scaling law claims

A scaling law is a *predictive functional form* L̂(N, D) fit on experiments at modest scale and extrapolated to scales where experiments are unaffordable. The classical family is the power law:

```
L(N) = (N_c / N)^α_N + L_∞
L(D) = (D_c / D)^α_D + L_∞
L(C) = (C_c / C)^α_C + L_∞
```

where L_∞ is the irreducible entropy of the data distribution — the loss achievable even with infinite capacity and data. The exponents α and the constants N_c, D_c, C_c are fit empirically. A law is useful only insofar as its *extrapolations* are accurate; a law that interpolates well but extrapolates poorly is a curve fit, not a scaling law. This is the standard by which Kaplan's laws were judged, revised by Chinchilla, and generalized by the broken neural scaling laws.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 isoFLOP experiments

The modern methodology for estimating compute-optimal allocations, introduced by Hoffmann et al. [2], is the *isoFLOP profile*. Fix a total compute budget C_k (k = 1…K, spanning roughly two orders of magnitude). At each budget, train many models of different sizes N, adjusting the token count as D = C_k / (6N) so that total compute stays fixed, and choose for each run the learning-rate schedule and batch size tuned near-optimally for that configuration. For each C_k, fit a parabola (in log-space) to the resulting (log N, loss) points; the minimum of the parabola identifies the compute-optimal (N_k, D_k) pair. Regressing log N_k and log D_k against log C_k yields the scaling exponents. This design eliminates the confound that plagued earlier work: models compared at equal *step count* rather than equal *compute* penalize small models, which need more steps to converge.

### 3.2 Fitting broken laws

Caballero et al. [3] fit their broken-power-law form (below, §4.3) on *log-spaced* measurements of the metric y against the scaling variable x (compute, parameters, data, or upstream performance). Fits are evaluated by RMSLE (root mean squared log error) on *held-out extrapolations*: the model is fit on small-scale points and scored on large-scale points it never saw — the only honest test of a scaling law.

### 3.3 Emergence experiments

Schaeffer et al. [4] test metric dependence in three complementary ways: (i) on fixed model outputs of the InstructGPT/GPT-3 family, they compare emergent claims under the original discontinuous metric against continuous alternatives; (ii) a meta-analysis of BIG-Bench across 39 preferred metrics and dozens of task–model-family pairs; (iii) constructive proof that adversarially chosen metrics induce "emergence" in vision models where none was previously claimed [4].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Kaplan power laws and their prescriptions

Kaplan et al. [1] trained over 400 language models spanning seven orders of magnitude and reported the following empirical fits:

| Scaling axis | Functional form | Exponent |
|---|---|---|
| Parameters | L(N) = (N_c/N)^αN | αN ≈ 0.076 |
| Data | L(D) = (D_c/D)^αD | αD ≈ 0.095 |
| Compute | L(C) = (C_c/C)^αC | αC ≈ 0.050 |

A 10× increase in parameters reduces loss by roughly 5%; a 10× increase in data, by roughly 6%. The data exponent exceeding the parameter exponent already hints at data's leverage — a hint Kaplan's allocation rule underweighted. Solving the constrained optimization of L(N, D) subject to C = 6ND gave the famous prescription:

```
N_opt ∝ C^0.73        D_opt ∝ C^0.27
```

i.e., for each 10× of compute, grow parameters ~5.4× but data only ~2×. Two further results deserve emphasis. First, the *overfitting boundary*: to avoid overfitting, data must scale sublinearly with model size, D ∝ N^0.74 [1].

> **Theorem (Kaplan compute-efficient training):** *Optimally compute-efficient training involves training very large models on a relatively modest amount of data and stopping significantly before convergence, because larger models are substantially more sample-efficient — they reach a given loss with fewer optimization steps.* [1]

This prescription justified the GPT-3-era paradigm of 100B+ parameter models trained on ~300B tokens. It was also wrong.

### 4.2 Chinchilla: the compute-optimal correction

Hoffmann et al. [2] suspected a methodological flaw: Kaplan's small models were compared at fixed *training steps*, so they were systematically undertrained relative to large models run for the same number of steps — making small models look artificially weak and biasing the optimal allocation toward parameters. Using isoFLOP profiles (§3.1) with near-optimal learning-rate schedules for every configuration, they found instead:

```
N_opt ∝ C^0.50        D_opt ∝ C^0.50        D/N ≈ 20 tokens per parameter
```

Parameters and data should scale *equally*. The parametric fit used by Chinchilla,

```
L(N, D) = E + A / N^α + B / D^β,    α ≈ β ≈ 0.34,   E ≈ 1.69,
```

decomposes loss into a parameter term and a data term with nearly equal exponents — the symmetry that yields the equal-allocation rule when minimized under C = 6ND. Three independent approaches in the paper (isoFLOP parabolas, the parametric fit, and a constraint-fit on the final loss envelope) converged on the same answer.

The demonstration was decisive: Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) was trained with the same compute as Gopher (280B parameters, 300B tokens) and *outperformed it on nearly every benchmark* [2].

> **Theorem (Chinchilla optimal allocation):** *For fixed training compute, loss is minimized when model size and training data grow in equal proportion — roughly 20 training tokens per parameter. Deviations in either direction (over-parameterized and undertrained, or overtrained and under-parameterized) are suboptimal.* [2]

A subtlety: Chinchilla optimality concerns *training* compute alone. When inference cost is included, the optimum shifts toward smaller models trained on more tokens — the "inference-aware" Chinchilla tradeoff now standard in open-weight releases.

### 4.3 Broken neural scaling laws: when power laws break

Pure power laws are smooth by construction, yet real scaling behavior exhibits *sharp, delayed inflection points* (e.g., multi-digit arithmetic suddenly "clicking") and even *nonmonotonic* transitions (double descent). Caballero et al. [3] introduced a smoothly broken power law — BNSL — that subsumes the Kaplan form as a special case while expressing both phenomena.

The functional form partitions the scaling axis into segments separated by smooth breakpoints:

```
y(x) = a + b · x^(−c_0) · Π_i ( 1 + (x / d_i)^(1/f_i) )^Δᵢ
```

where:

- **a** is the irreducible offset (analogous to L_∞),
- **b** sets the overall scale,
- **c_0** is the initial power-law exponent,
- **d_i** are the *break locations* — the scales at which behavior changes,
- **f_i** controls the *sharpness* of each transition (small f_i ≈ sharp kink; large f_i ≈ gradual bend),
- **Δᵢ** is the change in local exponent across break *i*, so segment *i* decays as x^(−cᵢ) with cᵢ = c₀ + ΣΔⱼ.

The form is expressive enough to capture double descent (a segment with *negative* Δ, i.e., temporarily rising error) and the delayed sharp inflections of arithmetic tasks [3]. Empirically, BNSL fits a vast and diverse battery of scaling phenomena — vision, language, audio, diffusion, RL, alignment, calibration, quantization, fairness, molecules, code — and its *extrapolations* beat the previous state of the art with an RMSLE averaging 0.86× the prior best [3].

> **Theorem (BNSL universality claim):** *A single smoothly-broken-power-law family, parameterized by break locations and transition sharpness, models and extrapolates scaling behavior across modalities and tasks — including nonmonotonic and sharply-inflecting curves that no single power law can express.* [3]

BNSL also yields a principled account of the *limits of predictability*: extrapolation error grows with the number of unobserved breaks, formalizing why some capabilities resist forecasting even when loss does not.

### 4.4 The emergence debate: phase transitions or metric artifacts?

Wei et al. [5] defined an *emergent ability* operationally: an ability absent in smaller models but present in larger ones, hence unpredictable by extrapolating small-model performance. They documented dozens of cases across GPT-3, LaMDA, Gopher, Chinchilla, and PaLM: few-shot prompting abilities (3-digit addition at ~2.3×10²² FLOPs, MMLU at ~3.1×10²³), instruction following, chain-of-thought reasoning (~10²³ FLOPs / ~100B parameters), scratchpad arithmetic, self-consistency decoding, and calibration via P(True). The qualitative picture was striking: flat near-random performance, then a sudden jump — a *phase transition* [5].

Schaeffer, Miranda, and Koyejo [4] offered an alternative explanation grounded in measurement theory. Their argument proceeds in three steps:

1. **A mathematical model.** Suppose per-token correctness probability p(s) improves *smoothly* (even log-linearly) with scale s. Under a continuous metric like average log-probability or Brier score, observed performance improves smoothly. But pass the same outputs through a *nonlinear or discontinuous* metric — exact string match (requires all tokens correct: p(s)^L for length-L answers), or multiple-choice grading (argmax over options) — and the metric's geometry converts gradual improvement into apparent step functions. Sharpness and unpredictability are properties of the *metric's warping*, not of the model.

2. **Three confirmed predictions.** (i) On the InstructGPT/GPT-3 family with *fixed model outputs*, changing the metric changes the verdict: emergence visible under Multiple Choice Grade vanishes under the continuous Brier score [4]. (ii) Meta-analysis of BIG-Bench: of 39 preferred metrics, at most 5 ever display emergence, and *over 92%* of claimed emergent abilities are recorded under just two metrics — Multiple Choice Grade (discontinuous) and Exact String Match (nonlinear) [4]. (iii) Constructively: by choosing metrics appropriately, the authors produce "emergent abilities" in *vision* tasks across diverse architectures where no emergence had ever been claimed — demonstrating the phenomenon is metric-constructible, not scale-intrinsic [4].

3. **The statistical coda.** Emergence claims typically rest on sparse sampling: few model sizes, few random seeds, small test sets. With better statistics — more seeds, resolution-matched evaluation — alleged cliffs soften into slopes.

The debate's resolution is now the field's working consensus and is more nuanced than either extreme:

| Position | Claim | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Wei et al. [5] | Capabilities emerge unpredictably as phase transitions | True *under the reported metrics*; documented across families |
| Schaeffer et al. [4] | Sharpness/unpredictability are metric artifacts | Confirmed: emergence evaporates under continuous metrics |
| Synthesis | Token-level capabilities improve smoothly and predictably; discrete metrics reveal them discontinuously | Dominant view; residual debate on grokking-like transitions |

Importantly, Schaeffer et al. do *not* claim models stop improving or that scale is irrelevant — they claim the *predictability* of scaling is restored once one measures with continuous metrics. The practical lesson: evaluate scaling with per-token log-probabilities and proper scoring rules, not just thresholded task accuracy, and be deeply suspicious of "emergence" claims computed under a single discontinuous metric.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Result 1 — Chinchilla defeats Gopher at equal compute.** The 70B/1.4T-token Chinchilla outperforms the 280B/300B-token Gopher on the overwhelming majority of downstream benchmarks despite 4× fewer parameters [2]. This single experiment invalidated the Kaplan allocation rule and is the most consequential empirical result in the scaling literature.

**Result 2 — BNSL extrapolation dominates.** Across dozens of scaling phenomena, BNSL's held-out extrapolation error averages 0.86× that of the previous best functional form, and it is the only tested form that extrapolates double descent and sharp arithmetic inflections [3].

**Result 3 — Emergence is metric-contingent.** Fixed InstructGPT/GPT-3 outputs display emergence under Multiple Choice Grade but not under Brier score; across BIG-Bench, >92% of emergence claims use Multiple Choice Grade or Exact String Match; and metric engineering alone manufactures "emergence" in vision models [4].

**Result 4 — Data repetition has a finite horizon.** Muennighoff et al. [6] show that with constrained data, up to ~4 epochs of repetition yield losses nearly identical to unique data, but beyond that the marginal value of compute decays to zero — a direct amendment to Chinchilla optimality for the data-scarce regime. Their revised law recommends *smaller* models trained for *more* epochs when data is fixed, the opposite of the naive Chinchilla extrapolation.

**Result 5 — Modern recalibrations agree on the shape.** DeepSeek's scaling-law calibration [7] and the large-scale Step Law hyperparameter study [8] (3,700 models, ~100T tokens) confirm the Chinchilla-class functional form while adding optimal learning-rate and batch-size laws, showing that scaling regularities extend beyond loss into training hyperparameters themselves.

A compact demonstration of the Chinchilla computation:

```python
import numpy as np

# Chinchilla parametric fit: L(N, D) = E + A/N^alpha + B/D^beta
E, A, B, alpha, beta = 1.69, 406.4, 410.7, 0.34, 0.28

def loss(N, D):
    return E + A / N**alpha + B / D**beta

def optimal_N_D(C, grid=np.logspace(8, 11, 61)):
    """Brute-force the compute-optimal (N, D) under C = 6 N D."""
    best = min(((loss(N, C / (6 * N)), N) for N in grid), key=lambda t: t[0])
    L, N = best
    return N, C / (6 * N), L

for C in [1e21, 1e22, 1e23]:
    N, D, L = optimal_N_D(C)
    print(f"C={C:.0e} -> N={N:.2e} params, D={D:.2e} tokens, D/N={D/N:.1f}, L={L:.3f}")
```

And the metric-sharpness mechanism of Schaeffer et al. in miniature:

```python
import numpy as np

# Smoothly improving per-token correctness with scale (log-linear)
s = np.linspace(20, 24, 9)              # log10(FLOPs)
p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(s - 22.0)))       # smooth sigmoid: token-level accuracy
p = 0.05 + 0.9 * p

L = 5                                   # answer length in tokens
exact_match = p ** L                    # nonlinear metric: all tokens correct
brier = (1 - p) ** 2                    # continuous proper scoring rule

print(" logFLOP | token-p | exact-match | brier")
for si, pi, em, br in zip(s, p, exact_match, brier):
    print(f" {si:6.1f}  | {pi:7.3f} | {em:11.3f} | {br:5.3f}")
# exact_match jumps sharply (apparent emergence); brier declines smoothly.
```

## 6. Limitations

1. **Loss is not capability.** Scaling laws predict cross-entropy, not task performance; the loss–capability mapping is itself metric-dependent (§4.4). A 0.1-nat loss reduction can be meaningless or transformative depending on the downstream metric.
2. **Extrapolation is fragile at breaks.** BNSL formalizes the core hazard: a break *beyond* the observed range is invisible to any fit, so extrapolation past unobserved transitions is speculative by construction [3].
3. **The data wall.** Chinchilla optimality assumes abundant unique data; Muennighoff et al. [6] show repetition saturates after ~4 epochs, and high-quality text stocks are finite. The next regime may be synthetic-data scaling, whose laws are poorly understood.
4. **Inference is missing.** Classical scaling laws optimize training FLOPs; deployment cost is dominated by inference, pushing the practical optimum toward smaller, overtrained models — a correction absent from both Kaplan and Chinchilla.
5. **Hyperparameter dependence.** IsoFLOP optima are conditional on near-optimal LR schedules and batch sizes per configuration [2][8]; suboptimal tuning at small scale can masquerade as a scaling effect.
6. **Emergence residuals.** The metric critique [4] explains most claimed emergence, but genuinely sharp transitions (grokking-like phenomena, discrete algorithmic discoveries) are not ruled out and remain poorly characterized.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The scaling-law literature has converged on a coherent, if humbling, picture. *Loss scales smoothly*: Kaplan's power laws [1], corrected by Chinchilla's equal-allocation rule [2] and generalized by BNSL's breakpoints [3], give us reliable, extrapolatable control over training compute — roughly 20 tokens per parameter, modulated by data scarcity [6] and inference economics. *Capability claims require continuous metrics*: the most celebrated "phase transitions" in LLM abilities dissolve under proper scoring rules [4], restoring the predictability that Wei et al. [5] questioned. The practical synthesis for frontier training is therefore threefold: allocate compute Chinchilla-optimally (or inference-aware), measure progress with continuous metrics, and treat any claimed emergence as a hypothesis about the *metric* until proven otherwise. The remaining frontiers — synthetic-data scaling, inference-time scaling laws, and the true algorithmic discontinuities — are where the next corrections will come from.
