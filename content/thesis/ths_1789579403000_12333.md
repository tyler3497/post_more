# Post-Quantum Zero-Knowledge Arguments: Transparent SNARKs from IOPs, MPC-in-the-Head, and Hash-Based Commitments

## Abstract

The dominant succinct proof systems of the past decade — pairing-based zk-SNARKs — rest on the discrete-logarithm problem and on trusted setup ceremonies, both of which fail under plausible futures: Shor's algorithm dissolves the first, and catastrophic compromise poisons the second. This essay surveys the escape routes built exclusively on symmetric primitives and hash functions. We develop the interactive oracle proof (IOP) formalism and the Fast Reed–Solomon IOPP (FRI) that powers STARKs, giving linear-time proving and logarithmic verification with no trusted party; the MPC-in-the-head paradigm, which converts passive-secure multiparty protocols into non-interactive proofs of knowledge whose security needs nothing beyond pseudorandomness, with the ZKBoo, ZKB++, Ligero, and Katz–Kolesnikov–Wang lineage culminating in genuinely post-quantum signatures; and the DARK compiler, which turns algebraic-linear IOPs into doubly-efficient transparent SNARKs over groups of unknown order. We compare the three families on soundness, proof size, and concrete prover cost, and isolate the open problems that stand between theory and deployment.

## 1 Introduction

An *argument system* is a protocol by which a computationally bounded prover convinces a verifier that some statement is true. When the proof is *succinct* — shorter than the computation it attests — and *zero-knowledge* — revealing nothing beyond validity — the result is a cryptographic primitive of startling power: verifiable outsourced computation, private transactions, and anonymous credentials. The first generation of practical succinct systems, the zk-SNARK, achieved proofs of a few hundred bytes by composing pairing-based polynomial commitments with knowledge-of-exponent assumptions [2]. This elegance carried two hidden costs. First, every known pairing instantiation is broken by a quantum computer running Shor's algorithm; the entire edifice inherits the vulnerability of elliptic-curve discrete logarithms. Second, the common reference string demands a *trusted setup*: whoever samples the toxic waste can forge proofs undetectably, and no participant can verify that the waste was destroyed. Ceremonies with hundreds of participants reduce but cannot eliminate this trust assumption.

The field has responded along three largely independent axes, each sacrificing one desideratum to rescue the others:

1. **Hash-based IOP arguments (STARKs).** Security rests on collision-resistant hashes and the random oracle model. Proofs grow to hundreds of kilobytes, but the setup is transparent, the verifier is polylogarithmic, and nothing in the construction is known to fall to quantum attacks [3].
2. **MPC-in-the-head NIZKs.** Security rests on pseudorandomness alone — the weakest assumption in the zoo. Proofs are longer and provers are slower, but the machinery yields *post-quantum signatures* from symmetric primitives with no number-theoretic assumptions at all [1][2].
3. **DARK-compiled transparent SNARKs.** Security rests on the strong RSA and adaptive root assumptions over groups of unknown order — transparent but *not* post-quantum. They recover near-SNARK succinctness (logarithmic proofs) without a trusted setup [5].

This essay traces the mathematics behind all three, compares them rigorously, and argues that transparent, hash-based succinctness is the most credible foundation for the decades-long lifetime that verifiable computation demands.

## 2 Background

We fix a binary relation $R$ and write $L_R$ for its language. An *interactive proof* is a protocol between prover $P$ and verifier $V$ satisfying **completeness** (honest parties accept true statements) and **soundness** (no prover strategy convinces $V$ of a false statement beyond negligible probability). A proof of knowledge further demands an *extractor*: any prover that convinces $V$ with non-negligible probability must *know* a witness, in the sense that an efficient extractor with oracle access to the prover can output one. Zero knowledge is formalized through *simulation*: whatever a malicious verifier sees could have been produced by a simulator with no access to the witness. A *succinct* argument requires verification time and communication sublinear in the computation size — ideally polylogarithmic.

> **Theorem (Fiat–Shamir heuristic).** Any public-coin interactive argument secure in the random oracle model yields a non-interactive argument in the standard model, with security loss bounded by the number of oracle queries, provided the interactive protocol has suitable round-by-round soundness.

Two classical results set the stage. First, Shor's algorithm (1994) computes discrete logarithms and integer factorization in polynomial time on a quantum computer, invalidating every pairing-based commitment scheme in use. Second, the PCP theorem (1992) showed that every NP statement admits a proof checkable by reading only a constant number of bits — but the original PCP constructions had enormous constants and superlinear provers, making them unusable.

The *interactive oracle proof* (IOP) model of Ben-Sasson, Chiesa, and Spooner refines the PCP: the prover sends a sequence of oracles, the verifier interacts with them, and only at the end queries a few positions [4]. This flexibility is what makes concretely efficient systems possible. A *proximity* variant (IOPP) tests membership in a code rather than exact computation; the Fast Reed–Solomon IOPP, FRI, is the workhorse of this essay [4].

Notation: $F$ denotes a finite field, $RS[F, L, \rho]$ the Reed–Solomon code of rate $\rho$ evaluated on domain $L \subset F$, and $\lambda$ the security parameter. "PPT" means probabilistic polynomial time.

## 3 Methodology

Our comparison rests on three pillars.

**Threat model.** For hash-based constructions we work in the random oracle model (ROM) and demand *quantum* resilience: the hash is assumed collision- and preimage-resistant against quantum adversaries with Grover-like speedup, which is handled by doubling the digest length. For DARK-based systems we assume classical PPT adversaries only, since the strong RSA assumption has no plausible quantum-safe instantiation. For MPC-in-the-head we assume only the existence of one-way functions — the minimal standard assumption.

**Comparison axes.** Each family is evaluated on: (i) prover complexity as a function of circuit size $s$; (ii) proof size; (iii) verifier complexity; (iv) setup requirements; (v) hardness assumptions. Where the literature provides concrete benchmarks we report them verbatim rather than asymptotically.

**Scope.** We trace the 2016–2023 lineage: IOP formalization, FRI, the STARK paper [3][4]; the MPC-in-the-head transform and its optimizations through ZKBoo, ZKB++, Ligero, and the preprocessing-model NIZK of Katz, Kolesnikov, and Wang [1][2], extended recently to black-box compilers from homomorphic primitives [7]; and the DARK compiler with its Supersonic and RedShift instantiations [5][6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The FRI Engine: Proximity Testing for Reed–Solomon Codes

Every STARK-family proof ultimately reduces to one question: *is this committed function close to a low-degree polynomial?* FRI answers it with a folding protocol that resembles the Fast Fourier Transform [4].

Given $f_0: L_0 \to F$ purportedly in $RS[F, L_0, \rho]$, the prover and verifier iterate. In round $i$, the verifier sends a random folding challenge $\alpha_i \in F$; the prover responds with oracle access to

$$\mathrm{Fold}[f_{i-1}, \alpha_i](x^2) := \tfrac{f_{i-1}(x) + f_{i-1}(-x)}{2} + \alpha_i \tfrac{f_{i-1}(x) - f_{i-1}(-x)}{2x},$$

defined on the halved domain $L_i = (x \mapsto x^2)(L_0)$. If $f_{i-1}$ was a degree-$d$ polynomial, so is the fold, with degree halved; if it was far from the code, the fold is far with high probability over $\alpha_i$. After $\log d$ rounds the function is constant and the verifier accepts iff it matches. The query phase then spot-checks consistency: for random $x \in L_0$, the verifier recomputes each fold from queries to $f_{i-1}$ at $x$ and $-x$, exploiting the *locality* of folding [4].

The complexity figures are the reason FRI conquered practice: for blocklength $N$, the interactive prover's arithmetic cost is below $6N$ — strictly linear, better than any FFT-based scheme — while the verifier needs at most $21 \log N$ operations and $2 \log N$ queries, with soundness error bounded by $\min(\delta (1 - o(1)), \delta_0)$ for words $\delta$-far from the code [4].

### 4.2 From IOPs to STARKs: Arithmetization and the Transparent Pipeline

An IOP is not yet a proof of a computation; the computation must first be *arithmetized*. The STARK framework uses Algebraic Intermediate Representation (AIR): the execution trace is a table whose rows are machine states over time and whose transition function is expressed as low-degree polynomial constraints [3]. The trace is interpolated, extended via the Reed–Solomon code, and committed with Merkle trees; the verifier checks that the constraints vanish on the trace domain, which — after a DEEP-style algebraic transformation — becomes exactly the low-degree question FRI answers.

> **Theorem (ZK-STARK, informal [3]).** For a computation of $T$ steps and security parameter $\lambda$, there exists a transparent zero-knowledge argument with prover time $O(T \log^2 T)$, proof size $O(\lambda^2 \log^2 T)$, and verifier time $O(\lambda^2 \log^2 T)$ — exponentially faster than executing the computation — whose security reduces to the collision resistance of the hash function in the random oracle model.

Concretely, the 2018 implementation demonstrated verification of sequential computations with exponential speedup over re-execution, on proofs measured in the low hundreds of kilobytes [3]. The price is visible: a pairing-based SNARK proves the same statement in under a kilobyte. The STARK buys, for that factor, the elimination of the trusted setup *and* of all quantum-vulnerable assumptions simultaneously — a combination no prior system had achieved in code [3].

### 4.3 MPC-in-the-Head: Zero Knowledge from Symmetric Primitives Alone

The paradigm of Ishai, Kushilevitz, Ostrovsky, and Sahai is disarmingly simple [1]. The prover holds a witness $w$ for a circuit $C$ with $C(w) = 1$. It *emulates in its head* an $n$-party MPC protocol that securely evaluates $C$ on shares of $w$, recording each party's full view. It commits to all $n$ views. The verifier challenges it to open a random subset of $t$ views; the prover complies. The verifier checks that the opened views are pairwise consistent and that the protocol output was $1$.

Soundness follows from a combinatorial fact: a cheating prover must corrupt at least one emulated party's messages, and the random challenge catches the inconsistency with probability governed by the cut-and-choose parameters. Zero knowledge follows from the MPC privacy guarantee: the opened views reveal nothing about the unopened shares, which carry the witness [1]. The original construction achieved communication $O(s) + \mathrm{poly}(k)$ for circuit size $s$ — constant *rate* — improving on the $O(ks)$ of prior protocols [1].

The lineage of optimizations is a case study in turning theory into artifacts:

- **ZKBoo / ZKB++** instantiate the paradigm with 3-party and 5-party MPC over Boolean circuits, yielding the first practical signatures from symmetric primitives.
- **Ligero** recasts the protocol as an IOP with sublinear communication and no trusted setup.
- **Katz–Kolesnikov–Wang** move the underlying MPC to the *preprocessing model*, producing NIZK proofs of knowledge shorter than all prior work for circuits between roughly 300 and 100,000 AND gates, with comparable computation — and, crucially, *witness-independent preprocessing*, so the prover's heavy work shifts offline before the witness is known [2]. Their signature scheme, built on symmetric primitives alone and hence post-quantum, beats ZKB++ signatures and competes with hash-based signatures [2].

The paradigm keeps expanding: recent work shows black-box compilers from any game-based homomorphic primitive — fully-homomorphic encryption, homomorphic secret sharing, randomized encodings — to short zero-knowledge proofs, and even tight compilers from interactive oracle proofs assuming only one-way functions [7]. MPC-in-the-head has become less a single protocol than a design methodology.

### 4.4 DARK Compilers and List Polynomial Commitments

The third family asks: can we keep the tiny proofs of SNARKs while deleting the trusted setup, if we give up post-quantum security? The DARK compiler of Bünz, Fisch, and Szepieniec answers yes [5]. Its polynomial commitment encodes the polynomial's coefficients as *integers* and commits in a group of unknown order — a class group, which needs no trusted setup. Evaluation proofs fold logarithmically, giving a commitment scheme with logarithmic proof size and verification time in the number of coefficients, secure under the strong RSA and adaptive root assumptions [5].

Compiling any *polynomial IOP* — a restricted class of algebraic-linear IOPs that includes the constraint system of PLONK — yields a doubly-efficient public-coin argument: quasi-linear preprocessing, quasi-linear online prover, logarithmic communication, logarithmic online verification [5]. Fiat–Shamir makes it non-interactive: *Supersonic*.

A parallel route, RedShift, replaces the DARK commitment with a *list polynomial commitment* and proves a generic transformation from preprocessing zk-SNARKs to transparent counterparts, applicable over prime and binary fields [6]. The benchmarks are striking: at the 80-bit security level, a circuit of one million gates proves in about a minute with proofs around 515 KB — competitive with the non-transparent state of the art, with no ceremony [6].

The honest caveat: groups of unknown order and the strong RSA assumption are *not* post-quantum. DARK systems are transparent SNARKs, not post-quantum SNARKs — they solve the setup problem while leaving the quantum problem open, the mirror image of lattice-based approaches.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proof Sketches

The table below consolidates the reported concrete figures. Proof sizes are approximate and depend on the security parameter; the qualitative ordering is robust.

| System | Prover | Proof size | Verifier | Setup | Assumption |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| STARK (FRI-based) [3] | $O(T \log^2 T)$ | ~100–500 KB | polylog($T$) | transparent | CR hash (PQ) |
| FRI IOPP alone [4] | $< 6N$ ops | — | $\le 21\log N$ ops | transparent | CR hash (PQ) |
| MPCitH / KKW [2] | quasilinear | beats ZKB++ on 300–100k AND gates | linear-ish | transparent | OWF (PQ) |
| Supersonic (DARK) [5] | quasilinear | $O(\log s)$ | $O(\log s)$ | transparent | strong RSA (classical) |
| RedShift [6] | ~1 min at 1M gates | ~515 KB at 80-bit | sublinear | transparent | list-PC (classical) |

Two proof sketches illustrate the soundness accounting. First, the FRI query phase: each query round catches a $\delta$-far word with probability at least $\delta(1 - o(1))$, so $q$ independent queries drive the error below $(1 - \delta)^q$; the *commit* phase contributes an additional term from the folding challenges, and the two are balanced so neither dominates [4]. Second, the MPC-in-the-head soundness: with $n$ parties and $t$ opened, a cheating prover that corrupts views must hope the challenge misses every corrupted pair; repeating the base protocol $\tau$ times (or enlarging $n$) pushes the error to $2^{-\lambda}$. The KKW preprocessing model reduces the per-repetition cost, which is what makes their parameter regime concretely attractive [2].

A minimal Python sketch of the FRI soundness estimator used in parameter selection:

```python
import math

def fri_soundness_error(num_queries, distance, folding_rounds, field_bits):
    # query-phase error: independent spot checks on a delta-far word
    query_err = (1.0 - distance) ** num_queries
    # commit-phase error: folding challenge hits the bad locus
    commit_err = folding_rounds * (2.0 ** -field_bits)
    # union bound over the two phases
    return query_err + commit_err

def queries_for_lambda(target_bits, distance, folding_rounds, field_bits):
    q = 1
    while -math.log2(fri_soundness_error(q, distance, folding_rounds, field_bits)) < target_bits:
        q += 1
    return q

print(queries_for_lambda(128, 0.25, 64, 64))  # typical STARK parameter regime
```

The script encodes the standard practice: pick the query count so the query-phase term dominates at the target bit level, then verify the commit-phase term (folding challenges over a 64-bit field, 64 rounds) sits well below it.

## 6 Limitations

**Proof size remains the tax.** Even the best transparent post-quantum systems produce proofs two to three orders of magnitude larger than pairing-based SNARKs. For on-chain verification, where every byte has a price, this is often decisive — though recursive composition of STARKs (proving the verifier itself) compresses the on-chain footprint to a single proof at the cost of prover overhead.

**Prover memory and constants.** The asymptotic $6N$ prover of FRI hides significant constants: Merkle-tree hashing of the full extended trace dominates wall-clock time, and memory consumption scales with the trace. MPC-in-the-head provers emulate $n$ parties per repetition; the KKW preprocessing shifts work offline but does not eliminate it [2].

**Assumption asymmetry.** The DARK family is transparent but not post-quantum; class groups are poorly battle-tested at cryptographic scale, and the adaptive root assumption is young [5]. Conversely, hash-based systems are plausibly quantum-safe but pay in proof size. No known construction is simultaneously transparent, post-quantum, *and* logarithmically succinct — the central open problem of the area.

**Arithmetization friction.** STARKs demand AIR arithmetization, and MPC-in-the-head demands Boolean circuits; translating real programs into either is labor-intensive and error-prone. Arithmetization-friendly hashes (Poseidon, Rescue) ease the former but import their own cryptanalytic risk.

**Soundness analysis gaps.** The concrete soundness of FRI with grinding attacks, and of Fiat–Shamir in the quantum random oracle model, remain active research areas; deployed parameters carry heuristic margins rather than proven bounds in the QROM.

## 7 Conclusion

Three distinct mathematical ideas now offer a way out of the trusted-setup, quantum-vulnerable SNARK monoculture. Interactive oracle proofs, through FRI and STARKs, deliver the only systems that are *simultaneously* transparent and plausibly post-quantum, at the cost of proof size [3][4]. MPC-in-the-head delivers zero knowledge from the weakest possible assumption — one-way functions — and turns it into real post-quantum signatures, a striking demonstration that minimal assumptions need not mean impractical ones [1][2][7]. DARK compilers recover logarithmic succinctness without any setup ceremony, honestly priced in classical assumptions [5][6].

The thesis of this essay is that the hash-based transparent family deserves the default position in long-lived infrastructure: assumptions about symmetric primitives have survived half a century of cryptanalysis, while number-theoretic assumptions have fallen before — and will fall again to — algorithmic and quantum advances. The open problems are concrete and tractable: tighter QROM soundness for FRI, better AIR toolchains, class-group cryptography at scale, and optimal cut-and-choose parameters for the preprocessing model. Transparent post-quantum succinctness is no longer a research curiosity; it is an engineering program.
