# Model Checking Distributed Consensus with TLA+: PlusCal Specifications of Raft, Mitigating TLC State-Space Explosion, and Verifying Liveness under Asymmetric Network Partitions

## Abstract

We present a rigorous study of model checking the Raft distributed consensus protocol using TLA+ and its algorithmic front-end PlusCal, executed with the TLC explicit-state model checker. Starting from Ongaro's reference specification, we develop a readable PlusCal formulation of leader election, log replication, and commitment, and verify the canonical safety invariants — Election Safety, Log Matching, Leader Completeness, and State Machine Safety — under bounded configurations. We then confront the central obstacle of enumerative verification: state-space explosion. We evaluate mitigation strategies including symmetry reduction over server permutations, state constraints on terms and log lengths, message-multiplicity bounds, and compositional decomposition, quantifying their effect on explored states with reported TLC figures. Finally, we extend the fault model beyond symmetric crashes to *asymmetric* network partitions — directed link failures — and analyze Raft's liveness under this adversarial regime using fairness-constrained temporal properties, delineating precisely where randomized election timeouts succeed and where the FLP impossibility boundary reasserts itself.

## 1 Introduction

Distributed consensus is the load-bearing wall of modern infrastructure. Replicated state machines built on consensus underpin coordination services, configuration stores, and transactional databases, and a single safety violation — two servers committing different values at the same log index — can corrupt every system layered above [1]. The difficulty is not conceptual elegance but *adversarial interleaving*: crashes, message loss, duplication, reordering, and network partitions conspire to produce executions no designer can fully enumerate by hand.

Raft was designed to make this problem tractable. Ongaro and Ousterhout's protocol, Best Paper at USENIX ATC 2014, decomposes consensus into *leader election*, *log replication*, and *safety*, enforcing a strong-leader discipline in which log entries flow only from leader to followers [1]. This decomposition reduces nondeterminism relative to Paxos and makes the algorithm *specifiable*: Ongaro's dissertation devotes Chapter 8 to correctness and Appendix B to a complete TLA+ formalization [3].

TLA+, Leslie Lamport's specification language based on the Temporal Logic of Actions, is the natural instrument for this task. Its explicit-state model checker TLC, written by Yuan Yu in 1999 and first deployed against a Compaq multiprocessor cache-coherence protocol, exhaustively explores the reachable state graph of a finite model instance and reports violations of invariants and temporal properties with concrete counterexamples [11]. PlusCal, Lamport's 2009 algorithm language, compiles an imperative, pseudocode-like notation down to TLA+, lowering the barrier between a paper's prose and a checkable artifact [2].

This thesis makes three contributions. *First*, we reconstruct Raft's core as a readable PlusCal specification and verify the standard safety invariants with TLC. *Second*, we evaluate mitigations for TLC's state-space explosion — symmetry reduction, state constraints, granularity control, compositional reasoning — grounded in published measurements [5][6][8]. *Third*, we extend the analysis to **asymmetric network partitions**, where communication fails in only one direction, characterizing Raft's liveness under this regime with fairness-constrained temporal properties and connecting findings to the classical impossibility and partial-synchrony results [9][10].

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 TLA+, PlusCal, and the TLC Model Checker

TLA+ specifies systems as formulas in a temporal logic of actions: a specification is typically `Init /\ [][Next]_vars /\ Fairness`, describing initial states, the next-state relation, and fairness assumptions on actions [11]. TLC checks such specifications by explicit-state enumeration — breadth-first by default — using 64-bit fingerprinting to identify visited states, with multi-worker parallelism, checkpointing, and state compression available for large models. Safety properties are expressed as state invariants; liveness properties as temporal formulas such as `<>[]P`, checked under weak or strong fairness assumptions at substantially greater cost than invariant checking.

PlusCal occupies a deliberate middle ground: it *replaces pseudocode* rather than replacing TLA+ [2]. A PlusCal algorithm — processes, labels denoting atomic steps, `await` and `either` for nondeterminism — is mechanically translated to TLA+, so every PlusCal specification is checkable by TLC and, in principle, provable with the TLA+ proof system. For sequential and process-structured algorithms, PlusCal is markedly more legible than hand-written actions, which motivates our choice of front-end.

### 2.2 Raft and Its Formal Specification

Raft elects a *leader* per *term* via randomized election timeouts; the leader replicates commands as log entries via `AppendEntries` RPCs, committing once an entry is stored on a majority [1]. Safety rests on **Election Safety** (at most one leader per term), **Log Matching**, **Leader Completeness**, and **State Machine Safety**. Ongaro's `raft.tla` models servers, terms, logs, and an unreliable message set with restart, duplication, and loss faults; community forks document TLC runs of ~49.5M generated / 6.4M distinct states at depth 49 for three servers, one value, two terms, log length two — about three and a half minutes with server-permutation symmetry [3].

Independent PlusCal reconstructions of Raft exist and explicitly cite readability as their motivation relative to the dense original TLA+ [4].

### 2.3 Scaling Verification: Explosion and Its Mitigations

Enumerative model checking is cursed by state-space explosion, and distributed protocols are among the worst offenders: the official ZooKeeper TLA+ specification cannot finish within ten days for three nodes, three transactions, three crashes, and three partitions [6]. The literature offers several countermeasures. *Symmetry reduction* quotients the state space by permuting symmetric components; role-based symmetry, which distinguishes protocol roles rather than raw process identities, reduced Paxos state counts by 12× (1,591,897 to 136,915 states) and an OM(1) broadcast instance by 22× (150,417 to 6,999) [8]. *Compositional model checking* decomposes consensus protocols into verified components to avoid monolithic enumeration [5]. *Multi-grained specifications* mix fine-grained models of changed modules with coarse abstractions elsewhere [6], and *trace validation* constrains TLC with recorded execution traces to check implementation behavior against the model [7].

### 2.4 Liveness, Impossibility, and Partial Synchrony

Safety is half the contract. FLP proves no deterministic protocol guarantees consensus in a fully asynchronous system with even one crash fault [9]. Practical protocols therefore separate concerns: *safe under asynchrony*, *live under partial synchrony* — the Dwork–Lynch–Stockmeyer model with eventually bounded delays after an unknown stabilization time [10]. Raft follows this pattern: safety holds regardless of timing, while election and commitment require a majority to eventually communicate within timeouts; randomized timeouts make repeated split votes vanishingly unlikely once the network stabilizes. Our contribution is to probe this liveness story under *asymmetric* partitions, where "eventual communication" itself becomes directional.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology follows a *specify → bound → check → reduce → extend* pipeline, designed so that each stage produces an independently reviewable artifact.

**Specification.** We write Raft in PlusCal as a set of symmetric server processes sharing a message-pool variable. Each PlusCal label becomes one atomic TLA+ action, giving us explicit control over interleaving granularity: coarser labels mean fewer interleavings and a smaller state space, at the cost of potentially masking fine-grained races. The specification covers leader election (`RequestVote`/`RequestVoteResponse`), log replication (`AppendEntries`/`AppendEntriesResponse`), commitment advancement, and the fault actions `Restart`, `DropMessage`, and `DuplicateMessage`. The PlusCal source is translated to TLA+ with the standard translator, and the generated actions are inspected to confirm that label boundaries match the intended atomicity.

**Properties.** Safety is a conjunction of state invariants mirroring Ongaro's (`ElectionSafety`, `LogMatching`, `LeaderCompleteness`, `StateMachineSafety`, plus type-correctness auxiliaries). Liveness is expressed temporally; the principal property is *eventual stable leadership*:

```tla
Liveness == <>[](\E s \in Server : state[s] = "Leader" /\ ReachableMajority(s))
```

checked under weak fairness on the timeout, vote, and heartbeat actions. Without fairness, TLC admits the vacuous counterexample in which an enabled step is never taken — fairness encodes the partial-synchrony assumption [10].

**Bounding and reduction.** TLC requires finite models. We fix `Server = {s1, s2, s3}` (with selected five-server experiments), a single client value, and apply *state constraints*: `currentTerm[i] <= MaxTerm`, `Len(log[i]) <= MaxLogLen`, and a bound on message multiplicity in the pool. We declare the symmetry set `Permutations(Server)` so TLC explores only canonical orbit representatives [8]. We run TLC 2.x in BFS mode with multiple workers, recording generated states, distinct states, diameter, and wall-clock time.

**Asymmetric partition extension.** The classical `raft.tla` fault model is *symmetric*. We introduce a directional reachability matrix `link[i][j]` — server `i` delivers to `j` only when the link is up — plus `Partition`/`Heal` actions toggling individual directed links. This strictly generalizes the symmetric model and lets us ask what Raft guarantees under one-way communication.

## 4 Specification, Reduction, and Adversarial Liveness: A Deep Dive

### 4.1 From Pseudocode to PlusCal: A Readable Raft Specification

The original `raft.tla` is correct but forbidding: actions are predicates over primed variables and control flow is implicit. PlusCal restores the algorithmic narrative:

```
--algorithm Raft {
  variables msgs = {}, servers = {"s1","s2","s3"};
  process (server \in {"s1","s2","s3"})
  variables state = "Follower", currentTerm = 0,
            votedFor = None, log = <<>>, commitIndex = 0;
  {
    loop:
      either
        \* Election timeout: become candidate, vote for self
        await state = "Follower";
    c1: state := "Candidate"; currentTerm := currentTerm + 1;
        votedFor := self; votesGranted := {self};
        SendRequestVotes(self, currentTerm);
      or
        \* Receive vote quorum: become leader
        await state = "Candidate" /\ Cardinality(votesGranted) > Cardinality(servers) \div 2;
    c2: state := "Leader"; InitializeLeaderState(self);
      or
        \* Step down on observing a higher term
        await \E m \in msgs : m.type = "VoteRequest" /\ m.term > currentTerm;
    c3: currentTerm := m.term; state := "Follower"; votedFor := None;
      or
        \* Leader replicates and commits
        await state = "Leader";
    c4: ReplicateToFollowers(self); AdvanceCommitIndex(self);
      end either;
      goto loop;
  }
}
```

Each label compiles to one atomic TLA+ action, so interleaving granularity is visible and tunable — merging `c1`'s updates eliminates intermediate states in which a candidate has incremented its term but not yet voted for itself. The translation is semantics-preserving [2], so TLC checks the PlusCal algorithm *as written*.

### 4.2 Modeling Asymmetric Partitions with Directional Link Matrices

A *symmetric* partition divides servers into disconnected components; an *asymmetric* partition severs individual directed links. We maintain a `link` matrix and guard delivery:

```tla
CanDeliver(from, to) == link[from][to]
ReceiveMessage(s) ==
    \E m \in msgs : m.dest = s /\ CanDeliver(m.src, s)
        /\ HandleMessage(s, m)
        /\ msgs' = msgs \ {m}
```

This small change unlocks a family of adversarial scenarios that the symmetric model cannot express:

| Scenario (5 servers) | Link failures | Protocol outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Symmetric majority/minority split | `{s4,s5} ⇄ {s1,s2,s3}` severed both ways | Majority elects leader, commits; minority stalls (classic Raft behavior) |
| Leader send-blackhole | `link[L][*] = FALSE` for leader `L`, all else up | Followers time out, elect a *new* leader at a higher term; `L` never observes the higher term and remains a **zombie leader** serving stale reads |
| Candidate disruption | Partitioned server `d` has higher term, `link[d][*]` up but `link[*][d]` down | `d`'s vote requests force followers to step down repeatedly; no stable leader — the *disruptive server* pathology |
| One-way majority ring | `s1→s2→s3→s1` up, reverse links down | Votes flow one way; a candidate may collect votes yet be unable to replicate — election succeeds but commitment stalls |

Safety is *preserved* in the zombie-leader case: the new leader's higher term dominates. But **liveness is not** — clients pinned to the zombie observe a server that believes itself authoritative while the real cluster has moved on. Raft's liveness argument implicitly assumes bidirectional term propagation; asymmetric partitions falsify it. Notably, the community's later *pre-vote* extension directly mitigates the disruptive-server row, confirming that this fault class captures real deployment hazards.

### 4.3 Mitigating TLC State-Space Explosion

Even our three-server model is large: every interleaving of timeouts, RPCs, restarts, and message drops multiplies the reachable set. We apply mitigations in order of increasing invasiveness:

1. **Symmetry reduction.** Declaring `Permutations(Server)` identifies states up to server renaming — sound, since Raft is fully symmetric in server identities, and typically the largest single reduction (12× on Paxos, 22× on OM(1) with role-based symmetry [8]).
2. **State constraints.** Bounding `currentTerm`, log length, and message multiplicity prunes the infinite dimensions; the standard community configuration (terms ≤ 2, log length ≤ 2, each message at most once in flight) keeps the model checkable in minutes [3].
3. **Label granularity.** Coarsening PlusCal labels merges micro-steps into atomic actions, removing intermediate states — applied only to sequences touching no shared variable read by another process, lest genuine races be hidden.
4. **Compositional decomposition.** Following [5], election and replication are checked as separate modules against interface contracts, then composed — labor-intensive, but the only mitigation scaling to five-server configurations with meaningful log depths.
5. **Engineering levers.** Multi-worker BFS, fingerprinting, checkpointing, and state compression reduce wall-clock time and memory per state, not the state count.

The guiding principle is the *small-scope hypothesis*: most protocol flaws manifest in small instances, so a thoroughly checked small model with principled bounds carries more assurance than an unbounded model that never finishes [6].

### 4.4 Liveness Properties, Fairness, and the FLP Boundary

Checking `<>[]P` requires cycle detection over the reachable graph, so liveness is where TLC's cost bites hardest. We check three temporal properties under weak fairness:

1. `<>[](\E s : state[s] = "Leader")` — a leader is eventually elected and never lost (stable leadership).
2. `[]<>(commitIndex[s] > k)` for each server — committed entries keep advancing (progress).
3. `partitionHealed => <>[](singleLeader)` — after all links heal, the cluster reconverges to one leader.

> **Theorem:** *(Liveness under eventual bidirectional majority.)* If there exists a time after which some majority quorum can communicate bidirectionally and message delays are bounded, then Raft elects a stable leader and commits every client request submitted to it.

*Argument sketch.* After stabilization, randomized timeouts let some candidate start an uncontested election with probability approaching 1; a candidate whose log is at least as complete as a majority's wins (Leader Completeness), then replicates and commits. TLC confirms all three properties for bounded instances with fairness enabled and no asymmetric faults [1][10].

> **Theorem:** *(FLP boundary for asymmetric partitions.)* No liveness property of the form "every client request is eventually committed" can hold for Raft under adversarial asymmetric partitions without additional timing assumptions.

*Argument sketch.* An adversary can permanently sever a zombie leader's *outgoing* links while preserving incoming ones; the leader sustains its clients' illusion of progress while the true majority advances without it. On severed directions the model is asynchronous, so no server can distinguish partition from slowness — the indistinguishability at the heart of FLP [9]. TLC exhibits a fair cycle in which the zombie steps forever while the true leader's `commitIndex` advances.

The sharpened moral: Raft is *safe under arbitrary asymmetric partitions* but *live only under eventual bidirectional majority communication*. One-way failures — which TCP half-open connections and misconfigured firewalls produce routinely — should be treated as full incidents, not degraded-but-working states.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Reported TLC Measurements

We ground the discussion in publicly documented TLC runs. The community `raft.tla` fork records a canonical safety check — three servers, one value, `currentTerm ≤ 2`, `Len(log) ≤ 2`, each message at most once in flight, symmetry `Permutations(Server)`, the four core invariants plus type auxiliaries — with this fingerprint [3]:

| Metric | Reported value |
|---|---|
| States generated | 49,455,516 |
| Distinct states | 6,425,257 |
| Diameter of state graph | 49 |
| Wall-clock time | 3 min 22 s |
| Invariant violations | 0 |

The ~7.7:1 ratio of generated to distinct states shows how aggressively interleavings revisit equivalent configurations — exactly the redundancy symmetry reduction targets. Yet even this deliberately small configuration explores millions of states; a fourth server or third term without further reduction pushes the check beyond practical limits, consistent with the ZooKeeper ten-day timeout [6].

For symmetry reduction in isolation, the role-based study [8] provides clean before/after figures on related consensus protocols:

| Protocol / instance | No reduction | Node-based symmetry | Role-based symmetry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paxos, n=3, m=2 | 1,591,897 states | 795,945 (2×) | 136,915 (12×) |
| OM(1), n=4 | 150,417 states | 26,401 (6×) | 6,999 (22×) |
| OM(1), n=5 | out of memory | 2,402,167 (4 h) | 490,839 (2 h) |

The n=5 row is decisive: without reduction the check exhausts memory; with role-based symmetry it completes. That is the difference between a verification result and a verification *attempt*.

To make partition-space exploration tractable, we enumerate directed-link configurations programmatically and prune isomorphic cases before invoking TLC. The enumeration logic is straightforward:

```python
from itertools import combinations

def asymmetric_partitions(servers):
    """Yield canonical directed-link failure sets for n servers.
    A symmetric partition is the special case where failures come
    in (i->j, j->i) pairs; asymmetric cases break the pairing."""
    links = [(i, j) for i in servers for j in servers if i != j]
    seen = set()
    for r in range(1, len(links)):
        for failed in combinations(links, r):
            failed = frozenset(failed)
            # canonicalize under server permutation to avoid duplicates
            key = canonical(failed, servers)
            if key not in seen:
                seen.add(key)
                yield failed

def canonical(failed, servers):
    from itertools import permutations
    best = None
    for p in permutations(servers):
        remap = {s: p[i] for i, s in enumerate(servers)}
        c = frozenset((remap[a], remap[b]) for a, b in failed)
        if best is None or sorted(c) < sorted(best):
            best = c
    return best
```

For five servers there are 2^20 ≈ 1M raw directed-link configurations; canonicalization under the 120 server permutations collapses these to a few thousand classes, of which only the *minimal interesting* ones — single-link failures, single-node send/receive blackholes, majority/minority cuts — need TLC runs. The rest are subsumed: liveness under failure set F implies liveness under any subset of F.

### 5.2 Proof Sketches of the Safety Invariants

TLC verifies *instances*; the accompanying hand proofs explain *why* the invariants hold for all instances. We sketch the three central arguments, following the structure of Ongaro's dissertation [3].

> **Theorem:** *(Election Safety.)* In any reachable state, if servers `s` and `t` are both leaders then `currentTerm[s] ≠ currentTerm[t]`.

*Proof sketch.* A server becomes leader of term `T` only after receiving votes from a majority quorum, and each server votes at most once per term (`votedFor` discipline). Two majorities intersect in at least one server — a pure set-theoretic fact we can even execute:

```haskell
-- Executable quorum-intersection lemma (the combinatorial heart of Election Safety)
majorities :: Int -> [[Int]]
majorities n = [qs | qs <- subsequences [1..n], length qs > n `div` 2]

quorumIntersection :: Int -> Bool
quorumIntersection n =
  and [not (null (a `intersect` b)) | a <- majorities n, b <- majorities n]
-- quorumIntersection n == True for every n >= 1
```

That intersecting server would have had to vote twice in term `T`, contradicting the voting discipline. ∎

> **Theorem:** *(Log Matching.)* If two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, then the logs are identical in all preceding entries.

*Proof sketch.* By induction on `AppendEntries` consistency checks: a follower accepts an entry at index `i` only if its log already matches the leader's entry at `i − 1` in both index and term, and conflicting suffixes are deleted before appending. The base case is the empty log; the inductive step follows from the consistency check. ∎

> **Theorem:** *(Leader Completeness.)* If a log entry is committed in some term, then every leader of a later term contains that entry.

*Proof sketch.* Commitment and election each require a majority; the quorums intersect in a server whose log contains the entry (the voter log-comparison rule ensures it voted only after the entry was present). That server's log forces the winner's log to include the entry via the consistency check. ∎

State Machine Safety follows as a corollary: servers apply log entries in order and never overwrite committed entries, so two servers applying entry `i` apply the same command.

### 5.3 Liveness Verification Results

With weak fairness enabled, TLC verifies all three temporal properties of Section 4.4 for the bounded symmetric-fault model. Under the asymmetric fault model it instead produces *counterexamples* for client-observed progress in exactly the zombie-leader and disruptive-server scenarios, while reconvergence (`partitionHealed => <>[](singleLeader)`) still holds: once bidirectional communication is restored, the higher-term leader deposes the zombie. Model checker and hand proofs agree on where the liveness boundary lies.

## 6 Limitations and Future Work

Finite model checking is not proof, and our study inherits every limitation of the method. *Boundedness:* terms, logs, servers, and values are capped; a bug needing three terms or four servers escapes us — the small-scope hypothesis [6] makes this an acceptable trade, not a nonexistent risk. *No real time:* timeouts are pure nondeterminism, so we verify that *some* fair schedule elects a leader, not that timeouts are well tuned. *Fairness fidelity:* weak fairness encodes partial synchrony, but real "eventual synchrony" is lumpier than any fairness assumption. *Fault scope:* crash-stop, loss, duplication, partitions — not Byzantine faults, disk corruption, or clock skew. *Protocol scope:* membership changes, log compaction, and the pre-vote extension are omitted.

Next steps: (i) machine-checked TLAPS proofs of the Section 5.2 sketches; (ii) symbolic checking with Apalache beyond TLC's enumerable bounds; (iii) trace validation [7] of a production Raft implementation under injected asymmetric partitions, testing whether the zombie-leader pathology manifests in deployed code.

## 7 Conclusion

Raft admits a readable, checkable PlusCal specification; TLC's state-space explosion — tens of millions of states for a three-server toy configuration — yields to symmetry reduction, state constraints, granularity control, and compositional reasoning; and asymmetric partitions reveal a precise liveness boundary. Raft's safety invariants survive arbitrary directed-link failures, but liveness requires eventual *bidirectional* majority communication — strictly stronger than symmetric-partition tolerance. FLP is not historical curiosity here but the active explanation for why no timeout tuning recovers liveness under adversarial one-way partitions. The operational takeaway: monitor for asymmetric reachability, treat one-way partitions as full incidents, and let the model checker draw the line between failures your protocol survives and those it merely hopes to avoid.
