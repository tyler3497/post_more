# Beyond Hindley-Milner: Bidirectional Type Inference, Higher-Rank Polymorphism, GADTs, and Type Families via OutsideIn(X)

## Abstract

Hindley–Milner inference, mechanised by Damas and Milner's Algorithm W, remains the most successful union of expressiveness and decidability in language design: full inference, no annotations, guaranteed principal types [1][2]. Yet its predicative, rank-1 ceiling excludes first-class polymorphic arguments, local refinements from pattern matching, and type-level computation. This thesis unifies the four landmark extensions defining modern production inference: (i) higher-rank polymorphism via the bidirectional algorithm of Peyton Jones et al. [3], with its ρ/σ-type stratification and "propagate types inward" checking discipline; (ii) the ordered-context system of Dunfield and Krishnaswami [4] with greedy instantiation, and its mechanically verified worklist refinement [5]; (iii) GADTs, wobbly types, and local equalities [6], culminating in OutsideIn(X) [7] — constraint generation decoupled from solving, implication constraints, and the Given/Wanted discipline; and (iv) type families, open [8] and injective [9], elaborated to System FC. We state declarative and algorithmic specifications, soundness/completeness and principality results, the GHC implementation lineage including Quick Look impredicativity [10], and the persistent challenge of error-message quality.

## 1. Introduction

In 1969 Hindley proved an algorithm for the simply typed λ-calculus always infers the most general type [2]; in 1978 Milner independently discovered Algorithm W for ML; and in 1982 Damas and Milner proved W sound and complete for a predicative polymorphic calculus, establishing *principal types* [1]. The Hindley–Milner (HM) bargain is extraordinary: complete inference with zero annotations. It rests on two restrictions:

1. **Predicativity.** Quantifiers appear only at the outermost position of a *type scheme*; instantiation substitutes monotypes only. Polymorphic types are second-class: `id :: ∀a. a → a` cannot be passed as an argument at its polymorphic type.
2. **Rank 1.** `let`-generalisation is the sole source of polymorphism, and no `∀` may appear left of an arrow. `(∀a. a → a) → Int` is inexpressible.

The practical costs are concrete. Rank-2 types like `runST :: (∀s. ST s a) → a` — the standard safe encapsulation of state threads — lie outside HM. Pattern matching over generalised algebraic data types introduces *local* equalities: matching a scrutinee of type `Expr a` against `Num :: Int → Expr Int` refines `a` to `Int` *inside that branch only*, a phenomenon foreign to HM's global unification. Type families generate wanted equalities discharged against an open axiom environment. This thesis argues four complementary ideas constitute the modern answer: **bidirectionality** (checking `Γ ⊢ e ⇐ A` against synthesis `Γ ⊢ e ⇒ A` [4], propagating expected types inward); **ordered contexts with greedy instantiation** (decidable higher-rank inference without a constraint store [4][5]); **constraint-based inference with local assumptions** — OutsideIn(X) [7], with implication constraints `Q ⊃ C` and a Given/Wanted solver discipline; and **type families as disciplined type-level functions** elaborated to System FC, with injectivity restoring backward reasoning [8][9].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Hindley–Milner core

HM distinguishes monotypes τ (no quantifiers) from prenex type schemes σ:

```haskell
τ ::= α | τ → τ | T τ₁ … τₙ
σ ::= τ | ∀α. σ
```

Four declarative rules — variable, application, abstraction, and `let` — suffice; only `let` introduces polymorphism, via *generalisation* of variables free in the inferred type but not the environment [11]. Algorithm W returns a substitution-type pair `(S, τ)`, and closing `Sτ` yields the principal scheme.

> **Theorem:** *(Principal types, Damas–Milner 1982 [1])*. For every typable `e` under `Γ`, W returns `(S, τ)` such that `close(Γ, Sτ)` is principal: every other valid typing of `e` is a generic instance of it.

> **Proof:** *Sketch.* Induction on `e`, composing most general unifiers. Generalisation at `let` quantifies only variables not free in `Γ`, preventing over-generalisation; completeness follows because any derivation's instantiations factor through W's MGUs. ∎

Two boundaries matter. W is *exponential* in the worst case (Kfoury–Tiuryn–Urzyczyn), though near-linear in practice. And the theorem covers only predicative instantiation: unifying a variable with `∀a. a → a` is forbidden.

### 2.2 Ranks of polymorphism

The *rank* of a type is the maximum nesting of `∀` on the left of arrows: `∀a. a → a` is rank 1, `(∀a. a → a) → Int` rank 2. HM supports only rank 1. Full impredicative System F is more expressive, but Wells (1999) proved typability and type checking for System F *undecidable* — so the engineering problem is carving out the largest decidable, predictable fragment: predicative higher-rank types with mandatory annotations where principality fails.

| System | Rank | Impredicative | Decidable | Principal types |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hindley–Milner | 1 | No | Yes | Yes |
| Bidirectional rank-n [3] | arbitrary | No | Yes | Yes, with annotations |
| Dunfield–Krishnaswami [4] | arbitrary | No | Yes | Yes (tuned declarative spec) |
| Quick Look (GHC ≥ 9.2) [10] | arbitrary | Guarded | Yes | Yes, with call-site guidance |
| System F (full) | arbitrary | Yes | No | N/A |

## 3. Methodology

For each extension we state the *declarative* specification (accepted types), the *algorithmic* realisation (how a compiler decides acceptance), and the soundness/completeness relationship between them, following the declarative-vs.-algorithmic methodology of [4][5]. Claims are anchored in the primary literature and in GHC, where all four extensions coexist: the higher-rank algorithm of [3], the OutsideIn(X) solver [7], Quick Look impredicativity [10], and type families elaborated to System FC [8][9]. Where designs diverge — the ordered-context [4] versus worklist [5] formulations — we compare them directly.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Rank-n polymorphism: the bidirectional algorithm of Peyton Jones et al.

Higher-rank inference founders on one question: *where may polymorphism be introduced?* In HM, only `let` generalises. But `λf. (f 3, f True)` needs `f :: ∀a. a → a`, which no annotation-free algorithm can infer. The design decision of Peyton Jones, Vytiniotis, Weirich, and Shields [3] is crisp: **require annotations on bindings of rank > 1, and infer everything else**.

Types are stratified to make the algorithm syntax-directed:

```haskell
σ ::= ∀a. σ | ρ        -- arbitrary rank
ρ ::= τ | σ → σ        -- weak prenex: no ∀ at top, but under arrows
τ ::= a | τ → τ | T τ̄  -- monotypes
```

The checker threads an `Expected` type with two modes [3, §6]: `Infer` (synthesize into a mutable cell) and `Check` (verify against a known type). Checking is the innovation — it *pushes types inward*. For `tcRho (Lam x body) (Check ty)`, the algorithm decomposes `ty` into argument and result types, binds `x` at the *argument σ-type* without instantiating its quantifiers, and checks the body. Checking against `∀a. σ` first *skolemises*: `a` becomes a rigid constant no unification variable may touch, enforcing parametricity.

Application is where predicativity bites. For `App fun arg`: infer the function's ρ-type, split it into argument σ-type and result ρ-type, then **check** the argument against the polymorphic σ-type via a deep *subsumption* check `subsCheck` (σ₁ ≤ σ₂, implemented by skolemising σ₂ and instantiating σ₁, recursing under arrows). Meta variables unify only with τ-types — predicative instantiation — which keeps subsumption decidable.

> **Theorem:** *(Peyton Jones et al. [3])*. The algorithm is sound and complete w.r.t. the declarative higher-rank specification: it accepts exactly the annotated programs whose annotations restore principality, and infers principal ρ-types for unannotated subterms.

A practically vital contribution is the *"propagate types inward"* trick [3, §5.4]: `infer e` is just `check e α` for fresh α, so a single checking function suffices — and the expected type is then available at *every* node, letting the compiler report the expected type at the precise failing source location. This is the foundation of GHC's modern error reporting for higher-rank code.

### 4.2 Ordered contexts and greedy instantiation: Dunfield and Krishnaswami

Where [3] keeps metavariables in an implicit store, Dunfield and Krishnaswami [4] make the *context* the algorithmic state, with the canonical bidirectional judgments `Γ ⊢ e ⇒ A` (synthesis) and `Γ ⊢ e ⇐ A` (checking). Γ is an *ordered* list interleaving term variables, universals `α`, unsolved existentials `α̂`, solved existentials `α̂ = τ`, and scope markers. Order enforces scope: an existential may only be solved by a type well-formed *to its left*, ruling out cyclic and scope-escaping solutions. Key rules: **Sub** connects the modes (`e ⇒ B` and `B ≤ A` gives `e ⇐ A`); **∀R** introduces a rigid variable when checking against `∀α. A`; **∀L** replaces `α` with a fresh `α̂` in synthesis position — *greedy instantiation*, committing to the first plausible solution à la Cardelli's `F<:` algorithm, rather than collecting constraints.

Greediness is the decisive simplification: the algorithm is syntax-directed and obviously terminating, with no backtracking. The price is incompleteness against the most liberal declarative system — so Dunfield and Krishnaswami *tune the declarative specification to the algorithm* and prove completeness against that. Completeness by design, not by aspiration.

> **Theorem:** *(Dunfield–Krishnaswami [4])*. Checking is decidable; the algorithmic system is sound and complete w.r.t. the declarative bidirectional specification for predicative higher-rank polymorphism.

Zhao, Oliveira, and Schrijvers [5] reformulated the system as a *worklist* — an explicit judgment stack processed left-to-right — mechanically verified in Abella, eliminating context-threading bookkeeping. Both formulations agree: bidirectionality + ordered, scope-respecting existentials + greedy instantiation = tractable higher-rank inference.

### 4.3 GADTs, wobbly types, and local equalities

GADTs (Cheney–Hinze; Xi–Chen–Chen, 2003) let each constructor specify its own result type:

```haskell
data Expr a where
  Num  :: Int -> Expr Int
  Plus :: Expr Int -> Expr Int -> Expr Int
  Eq   :: Expr Int -> Expr Int -> Expr Bool
  If   :: Expr Bool -> Expr a -> Expr a -> Expr a
```

Matching `Num i` against a scrutinee of type `Expr a` brings the *local assumption* `a ~ Int` into scope for that branch only. Two pathologies follow: inference for *unannotated* GADT programs is **undecidable** [6], and GADT programs lack principal types in general — `\x -> case x of { T1 n -> n > 0 }` admits the incomparable solutions `α := Bool` and `α := γ` [7]. The response of Peyton Jones, Washburn, and Weirich [6]: **demand signatures** for GADT functions and make checking nearly trivial.

Their device is *wobbly types*: the type of an unannotated subterm is marked *wobbly*, declaratively encoding the uncertainty of incremental inference. A wobbly type may only be unified in non-committal ways; where the algorithm would need to guess — e.g. generalising a wobbly variable at `let` — it defers to the programmer's annotation. The result is decidable (unification, never search), predictable (acceptance = checkability against annotations propagated inward as in §4.1), and conservative (all HM programs still accepted). The contract all later work honours: GADTs are a *checking* problem — the checker *uses* equalities from matches rather than *discovering* them.

### 4.4 OutsideIn(X): constraints, implications, and Given versus Wanted

OutsideIn(X) [7] is the synthesis and the production realisation — a clean two-phase pipeline:

```
source program → constraint generation (syntax-directed, total)
             → mixed-prefix constraints ∀ā. ∃β̄. (Q ⊃ C)
             → constraint solving (canonicalise, react, simplify)
             → residual constraints + System FC evidence
```

The constraint language is parameterised by a domain **X** — for GHC, type equalities `τ₁ ~ τ₂` plus type-class constraints. Generation never fails; GADT matches produce the novel form, *implication constraints* `Q ⊃ C` ("under local givens `Q`, wanted `C` must hold"). From [7, §5]: with `T1 :: ∀a. (Bool ~ a) => Int -> T a`, the term `\x -> case x of { T1 n -> n > 0 }` generates `γ ~ Bool ⊃ α ~ Bool` — which has *two incomparable solutions*. OutsideIn(X) refuses to guess: its restricted implication solver discharges an implication only when the givens force a unique answer, otherwise leaving it residual.

The solver's load-bearing distinction is **Given** versus **Wanted**:

- *Given*: facts to assume — instance axioms, superclass axioms, GADT-match equalities.
- *Wanted*: goals to discharge, producing *evidence* — System FC coercions witnessing each equality.

Solving = *canonicalisation* (flattening, orienting equations) + *interaction* (a canonical Given rewrites Wanted goals) + *top-level reaction* (matching Wanteds against global axioms). Givens from an implication are never exported outward — the no-guessing rule guaranteeing principal types where they exist and predictable rejection elsewhere.

> **Theorem:** *(Vytiniotis et al. [7])*. The solver preserves solution sets across rewrites (soundness of elaboration to System FC); when it fully discharges `C`, the substitution is *principal* — every other solution is an instance of it.

The parameter X is the lasting contribution: new features become *new constraint forms plus local solver rules*, not new inference algorithms. GHC instantiates X with equalities, class constraints, and later kind, irreducibility, and type-family constraints — one solver discharging them all.

### 4.5 Type families: open, closed, and injective type-level functions

Associated type synonyms [9] attach type-level functions to classes (`type Elem c` with `type Elem [a] = a`); open type families [8] generalise them to standalone, *extensible* type-level functions whose instances any module may extend. Openness has three consequences: **no injectivity by default** (from `F a ~ F b` nothing follows — a later instance could identify them); **apartness is undecidable in general**, so overlapping equations force deferral; and every solved wanted elaborates to a **System FC coercion** applied from the family's axioms, with type safety resting on FC's consistency.

*Closed* type families (Eisenberg et al., POPL 2014) fix the equation list and try equations in order under an apartness check, recovering genuine ordered type-level pattern matching. *Injective* families [10] recover backward reasoning by declaration:

```haskell
type family F a = r | r -> a    -- result determines argument
```

GHC verifies each equation's injectivity, then inverts `F a ~ F b` to `a ~ b` — fewer ambiguous variables and errors reported at the *argument* mismatch rather than an opaque family-application mismatch. Injectivity is the type-family analogue of OutsideIn's discipline: declare the property, verify it, exploit it.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 The GHC implementation lineage

All four extensions coexist in GHC, and their integration history is empirical evidence for the designs:

- **2005–2007**: The bidirectional rank-n algorithm of [3] ships (GHC 6.6+); `Expected`-type threading and "propagate types inward" error reporting become permanent infrastructure.
- **2011–2012**: OutsideIn(X) [7] replaces GHC's solver. Generation/solving separation, Given/Wanted, and implication constraints become the uniform substrate for GADTs, classes, and families.
- **2015**: Injective type families [10] land — the solver's first sanctioned backward reasoning over type-level functions.
- **2020–2022**: Quick Look [13] ships behind `ImpredicativeTypes` (GHC 9.2), replacing the withdrawn unreliable support. Its design is telling: a *local* "quicklook" phase at call sites uses argument types and the expected result type to guide impredicative instantiation (e.g. `reverse xs` with `xs :: [∀a. a → a]` instantiates `reverse` at `∀a. a → a`), leaving the OutsideIn(X) machinery untouched — modularity demonstrated in production.
- **2019**: The worklist reformulation [5] is mechanically verified in Abella.

### 5.2 Comparative formal properties

| Property | HM / W [1] | Rank-n bidir. [3] | DK [4] / Worklist [5] | OutsideIn(X) [7] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Judgment form | `Γ ⊢ e : τ` | `⇐`/`⇒` + `Expected` | `⇒` / `⇐`, ordered ctx | generation + solving |
| Metavariables | global unifiers | global + skolems | ordered `α̂` | touchable/untouchable |
| Local equalities | No | No | No | Yes (implications) |
| Annotation burden | none | rank > 1 sigs | rank > 1 sigs | GADT signatures |
| Principality | Yes | Yes, w/ annotations | Yes (tuned spec) | Yes, where unique |
| Decidable | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes (restricted solver) |
| Mechanised metatheory | Classic | No | Yes [5] | Partial |

### 5.3 Error-message quality

Inference has a user-facing metric: *when it fails, how good is the error?* Bidirectionality contributes directly — the expected type is available at every checking node, so GHC reports mismatches against the *annotation* rather than a guessed variable [3, §5.4]. OutsideIn(X) contributes Given/Wanted: an undischarged Wanted is reported with its residual constraint (e.g. `Could not deduce (a ~ Int) arising from a GADT match`), and *untouchable* variables explain *why* the solver refused to guess. Type-family errors remain weakest — an unsolved `F a ~ Int` describes solver state, not programmer error — the gap injective families [10] and apartness reporting aim to close. The diagnosis literature (Hage's survey; Stuckey–Sulzmann–Wazny interactive debugging [12]) studies these behaviours as objects in their own right.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **The annotation burden is fundamental.** Each system buys decidability with mandatory signatures: rank > 1 [3][4], GADT definitions [6], injectivity declarations [10]. Whether the compiler can *suggest* the missing signature rather than demand it remains open.
2. **Implication solving is deliberately partial.** OutsideIn(X) discharges an implication only when forced; programs a human finds unambiguous but the solver leaves residual are regularly reported. The exact boundary of *safe* implication solving is unknown.
3. **Impredicativity stays guarded.** Quick Look [13] handles common call-site patterns but rejects exotic ones (class constraints on polymorphic types remain illegal). Full impredicativity is undecidable, so the question is *where* to draw the line.
4. **Type-level error messages lag.** Unsolved family Wanteds describe solver state; proof-relevant witnesses or apartness-aware reporting are active research.
5. **Mechanisation is incomplete.** The worklist core [5] is verified, but GHC's full solver — hundreds of canonicalisation/interaction rules plus evidence generation — has no machine-checked soundness proof.
6. **Feature interaction.** Each extension is well-behaved alone; their combination (GADTs × injective families × Quick Look) is validated by GHC's test suite, not by a unified metatheory. A compositional account is the natural next milestone.

## 7. Conclusion

The arc from Damas–Milner to OutsideIn(X) refines one insight across four decades: *separate what is known from what is sought, and never guess*. W guesses nothing because principal types exist in its fragment [1]. Bidirectional systems [3][4] make the distinction syntactic — checking versus synthesis — confining commitment to greedy, scope-respecting instantiation. GADTs [6] force the distinction local, with wobbly types marking where knowledge ends. OutsideIn(X) [7] makes it architectural: generate, then solve, with Givens and Wanteds, implications, and a solver that prefers residual constraints to unforced choices. Type families [8][9][10] extend the discipline to computation, with injectivity as the verified license for backward reasoning. With Quick Look's pragmatic impredicativity [13], these are the most successful inference engineering ever deployed — and their open problems define the field's next decade.
