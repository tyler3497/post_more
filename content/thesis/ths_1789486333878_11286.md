# Single-Cell Transcriptomics: Zero-Inflated Models, Doublet Detection, and Trajectory Inference

## Abstract

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) measures transcriptomes one cell at a time, producing sparse, over-dispersed count matrices in which most entries are zero. Extracting reliable biology from these data requires a statistical treatment of three coupled problems: modeling the count distribution (and deciding whether an explicit zero-inflation term is warranted), removing technical artifacts such as doublets, and reconstructing the continuous differentiation processes that cross-sectional snapshots imply. This article reviews the mathematical core of each problem. We derive the zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) mixture, its embedding in ZINB-WaVE and deep generative models such as scVI, and the regularized negative binomial alternative of sctransform, alongside the empirical controversy over whether droplet data are zero-inflated at all. We then survey doublet detection through the lens of artificial-doublet simulation, contrasting k-nearest-neighbor scores, gradient-boosting classifiers, and semi-supervised deep learning, with reference to systematic benchmarking results. Finally, we examine trajectory inference: graph-based pseudotime, lineage-aware principal curves, and mechanistically grounded RNA velocity, and we discuss identifiability limits that no algorithm can evade.

## 1 The Statistical Anatomy of a Single-Cell Count Matrix

A droplet-based scRNA-seq experiment yields a count matrix **Y** with entries y_{ng} recording the number of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) observed for gene g in cell n. Three features distinguish this object from bulk RNA-seq counts: extreme sparsity (typical matrices are 85–98% zeros), low per-cell depth (median library sizes on the order of a few thousand UMIs), and strong over-dispersion relative to a Poisson model. The canonical sampling model is the negative binomial (NB) distribution, parameterized by a mean μ_{ng} and a gene-specific dispersion θ_g:

> **Theorem:** Under a Gamma–Poisson hierarchy with y_{ng} | w_{ng} ~ Poisson(w_{ng}) and w_{ng} ~ Gamma with mean μ_{ng} and shape θ_g, the marginal distribution of y_{ng} is negative binomial with E[y_{ng}] = μ_{ng} and Var(y_{ng}) = μ_{ng} + μ_{ng}^2 / θ_g. The quadratic term in μ_{ng} is the signature of over-dispersion beyond technical Poisson noise.

The mean itself is modeled on the log scale as a function of cell-level covariates (library size, batch, mitochondrial content), gene-level covariates (GC content, length), and a latent biological signal. Normalization is therefore not a preprocessing afterthought but part of the generative model. Regularized NB regression (sctransform) fits one NB regression per gene, shrinks the parameters toward a kernel-smoothed trend across genes with similar abundance, and defines normalized counts from the Pearson residuals [3]. This stabilizes variance without discarding the count nature of the data and avoids the overfitting that per-gene unconstrained NB fits invite.

*UMI deduplication* deserves emphasis: by collapsing PCR duplicates into molecular counts, UMIs convert the amplification-heavy read counts into something closer to a Poisson or NB sample of the original molecules, making parametric count models substantially more honest.

## 2 Zero Inflation: Model, Machine, and Controversy

### 2.1 The ZINB mixture and its rationale

Not every zero is a biological statement. A *dropout* zero arises when a transcript was present but failed to be captured, reverse-transcribed, or sequenced. The ZINB model introduces a per-cell-per-gene mixture probability π_{ng} of excess zeros:

f_ZINB(y; μ, θ, π) = π · 1_{y=0} + (1 − π) · f_NB(y; μ, θ)

The first component is a point mass at zero (technical dropout); the second is the NB count component (biological signal, whose zeros are *biological*). Fitting this mixture requires disentangling two sources of zeros, which is identifiable only through the shape of the NB component and covariates that modulate π.

### 2.2 ZINB-WaVE: wanted variation in a zero-inflated world

ZINB-WaVE casts dimensionality reduction as a penalized maximum-likelihood problem under the ZINB likelihood [1]. The NB mean and the zero-inflation probability are each parameterized by a combination of observed cell-level covariates (batch, QC metrics), unobserved cell-level factors W (a low-rank matrix capturing both unwanted and wanted variation, in the RUV tradition), and gene-level covariates:

log(μ_{ng}) = (Xβ_μ)_n + (Vγ_μ)_g + (Wα_μ)_{ng}
logit(π_{ng}) = (Xβ_π)_n + (Vγ_π)_g + (Wα_π)_{ng}

where X holds known cell covariates, V known gene covariates, and W the unknown latent factors estimated jointly. The sample-level intercept in X acts as a global-scaling normalization factor, so ZINB-WaVE needs no pre-normalization step. Posterior quantities derived from the fit are widely reused: the posterior probability that a zero came from the NB component (an "observation weight") can be plugged into bulk DE machinery such as edgeR, DESeq2, or limma-voom to rescue their assumptions on single-cell data [2].

### 2.3 Deep generative successors

The ZINB likelihood also powers deep generative models. In scVI, a variational autoencoder maps each cell to a low-dimensional latent variable z_n; decoder networks emit the ZINB parameters (μ_{ng}, θ_g, π_{ng}) as nonlinear functions of z_n, and variational inference approximates the intractable posterior over z [2 in spirit of the arXiv generative model]. This inherits the ZINB inductive bias — the decoder is explicitly told that zeros may be technical — while gaining nonlinear embeddings and scalability to millions of cells. VeloVI and related models extend the same decoder philosophy to spliced/unspliced dynamics.

### 2.4 Is droplet data zero-inflated at all?

A landmark empirical challenge argued that droplet-based scRNA-seq is *not* zero-inflated: after accounting for sequencing depth, the observed zero fractions match NB expectations without any excess-zero component, and zero-inflation is largely a feature of plate-based protocols with different capture physics [4]. The practical consequence is asymmetric risk. Fitting an unconstrained ZINB model to UMI data adds a third parameter per observation context and invites overfitting; several authors urge that plain NB models with regularization are sufficient and safer for droplet data [3]. The field has largely converged on a pragmatic middle ground: *dropout* remains a real and useful concept (low capture efficiency is undeniable), but it is increasingly handled by variance stabilization and imputation-aware downstream methods rather than by literal zero-inflation terms in every model.

> **Theorem:** For a fixed NB mean μ and dispersion θ, the implied probability of zero is (θ / (θ + μ))^θ, which grows rapidly as μ → 0. Most sparsity in droplet data is explained by this term alone; a zero-inflation component π is justified only when the empirical zero fraction exceeds this curve systematically across genes, which holds for some protocols and not others.

## 3 Doublet Detection: Finding Two Cells in One Droplet

Droplet microfluidics co-encapsulates two cells in a fraction of droplets that grows with cell loading — rates of several percent are routine and can exceed one-third of barcodes at high throughput. Doublets masquerade as intermediate cell states, create spurious clusters, and corrupt differential expression. Because ground truth is rarely available, nearly all computational methods share one elegant idea: *simulate* artificial doublets by combining the expression profiles of randomly chosen observed cells, then score each real cell by its resemblance to the artificial population [5].

| Method | Artificial doublets | Scoring rule | Key property |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scrublet | Sum of random pairs | kNN fraction of simulated neighbors | Doublet score + bimodal threshold |
| DoubletFinder | Sum of random pairs (pN ≈ 0.25) | Proportion of artificial nearest neighbors (pANN) | pK tuned by BCmvn; homotypic adjustment |
| Solo | Sum of random pairs | Semi-supervised VAE + classifier | Deep embedding; no manual threshold |
| DoubletDetection | Sum of random pairs | Hypergeometric test on neighborhoods | p-value based calls |
| DoubletDecon | Cluster-based synthesis | Deconvolution residuals | No per-cell score |
| cxds | None | Gene co-expression | Simulation-free |

### 3.1 The kNN family: Scrublet and DoubletFinder

Scrublet embeds observed cells together with simulated doublets, builds a k-nearest-neighbor graph, and assigns each cell the fraction of its neighbors that are simulated; a bimodal score distribution separates singlets from doublets [5][6]. DoubletFinder refines the pipeline: artificial doublets at proportion pN are merged with real data, PCA is computed jointly, and the pANN statistic (proportion of artificial nearest neighbors) is thresholded using an expected doublet rate, with a correction for homotypic doublets (pairs of the same cell type, which are essentially undetectable by expression alone) [6]. ROC analyses on cell-hashing and demuxlet ground-truth datasets show pANN dominates raw UMI count as a doublet predictor.

### 3.2 Deep learning: Solo

Solo takes the simulation idea into representation learning: a variational autoencoder embeds cells unsupervised, and a feed-forward head is trained semi-supervised to distinguish simulated doublets from observed data, with the observed set treated as noisy singlets [5]. The classifier's calibrated probabilities replace heuristic thresholds, and benchmarking suggests accuracy gains over kNN scores, particularly in datasets with subtle heterotypic structure.

### 3.3 What the benchmarks actually say

A systematic benchmark across sixteen real datasets with orthogonal ground truth (cell hashing, demuxlet, MULTI-seq) found that no method dominates universally, but Scrublet, DoubletDetection, and DoubletFinder were the most robust at removing heterotypic doublets and spurious clusters across a range of assumed doublet rates, with DoubletFinder and DoubletDetection the most stable when the removal percentage was misspecified [5]. Three practical lessons emerge: (i) homotypic doublets are nearly invisible to all expression-based methods — expect residual contamination on the order of the homotypic rate; (ii) performance degrades sharply in low-heterogeneity data where simulated doublets resemble singlets; (iii) doublet removal should precede clustering, since doublets otherwise seed phantom cell types that downstream analyses then "discover."

```python
# Minimal doublet-scoring sketch: the shared core of the kNN family
import numpy as np
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors

def simulate_doublets(X, pN=0.25, rng=None):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(rng)
    n_art = int(pN * X.shape[0])
    i, j = rng.integers(0, X.shape[0], size=(2, n_art))
    return X[i] + X[j]                      # additive merging of count profiles

def pann_scores(X_real, X_art, k=25):
    X_all = np.vstack([X_real, X_art])
    nn = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=k + 1).fit(X_all).kneighbors(X_all)[1][:, 1:]
    is_art = np.arange(len(X_all)) >= len(X_real)
    return is_art[nn[:len(X_real)]].mean(axis=1)   # fraction of artificial neighbors
```

## 4 Trajectory Inference: Ordering Cells Along Biological Time

scRNA-seq destroys the cell it measures, so temporal processes must be reconstructed from asynchronous snapshots. *Trajectory inference* (TI) orders cells along *pseudotime* — a latent coordinate increasing along differentiation — and increasingly also recovers the *topology* of the process: linear, bifurcating, multifurcating, cyclic, or disconnected.

### 4.1 Pseudotime from manifold geometry

The earliest framing treats cells as points on a low-dimensional manifold and pseudotime as distance along it. Diffusion pseudotime builds a Markov transition matrix from a kNN graph with Gaussian-kernel affinities and defines pseudotime as accumulated diffusion distance from a root cell, robustly capturing nonlinear geometry through random-walk averaging. Monocle instead fits a principal graph through the data (originally via reversed graph embedding on ICA coordinates) and projects cells onto it; the position along the graph is pseudotime, and branch points in the graph define lineages. The Achilles' heel of all such methods is the *root*: pseudotime has no intrinsic arrow, and choosing the wrong initial state inverts every downstream conclusion. Any trajectory figure that does not state how the root was chosen should be read with suspicion.

### 4.2 Lineage-aware methods: Slingshot, PAGA, and streams

Slingshot [7] combines the stability of cluster-based topology with the smoothness of curve fitting: it constructs a minimum spanning tree over cluster centers, identifies lineages as paths through the tree, and fits *simultaneous principal curves* — smooth one-dimensional curves, one per lineage, shrunk toward each other where lineages share early development — then assigns pseudotime by orthogonal projection onto each curve. Because projection is orthogonal rather than graph-distance based, Slingshot's orderings proved markedly more stable under resampling than cluster-MST methods that snap cells to tree vertices [7]. PAGA takes a complementary graph-abstraction view: it quantifies connectivity between clusters via a statistical model of inter-cluster edges, producing a topology-preserving abstracted graph that scales to millions of cells and tolerates disconnected components. STREAM adds density-level visualization of how cell-type composition flows across branches. Benchmarks of dozens of TI methods converge on one guideline: match the method to the dataset's scale and expected topology, and validate any inferred trajectory with an orthogonal method, since method bias is the rule rather than the exception [8].

### 4.3 RNA velocity: dynamics from splicing kinetics

RNA velocity offers a *mechanistic* arrow of time. Standard protocols capture both unspliced (intronic) and spliced (exonic) UMIs; the ratio carries information about whether a gene is being up- or down-regulated. The classic model relates unspliced u and spliced s through transcription, splicing, and degradation rates:

du/dt = α − βu,   ds/dt = βu − γs,   velocity v = ds/dt

The steady-state estimator fits the slope of the u–s phase portrait's extreme quantiles; scVelo's dynamical model instead solves the full ODE system per gene with an EM algorithm, inferring latent time and gene-specific kinetic parameters without assuming equilibrium. Velocities are projected onto the cell–cell graph to yield transition probabilities, from which Markov-chain analysis extracts root and terminal states. The approach is powerful but fragile: violations of constant-rate assumptions, genes with multiple kinetic regimes, and noisy unspliced counts can flip inferred directions — documented cases include reversed erythroid differentiation — motivating unified-time models that jointly reconcile velocity with pseudotime ordering [9].

### 4.4 Benchmarking and choosing a method

Large-scale benchmarking (notably the dynverse effort over 45+ methods) reports that accuracy depends overwhelmingly on trajectory topology and dataset size rather than on algorithmic novelty per se [8]. Practical guidance:

1. **Linear or simple bifurcation, moderate size:** Slingshot or Monocle3 with a carefully chosen root.
2. **Atlas-scale or disconnected:** PAGA for topology, then lineage-specific refinement.
3. **Directionality uncertain:** RNA velocity as an independent arrow, cross-checked against marker-gene monotonicity.
4. **Always:** bootstrap or subsample stability checks; pseudotime–marker correlation (e.g., myogenic markers rising monotonically along a muscle trajectory) as a sanity criterion.

## 5 From Counts to Lineages: The Integrated Pipeline

The three problems are not independent — errors propagate. A coherent pipeline proceeds as follows. First, model counts with NB-based normalization (sctransform residuals or a ZINB-WaVE latent space) so that variance reflects biology rather than depth [1][3]. Second, detect and remove doublets *before* clustering or trajectory inference, since doublet-driven phantom clusters otherwise become phantom lineages; record the assumed doublet rate as a sensitivity parameter rather than a fixed truth [5][6]. Third, select highly variable genes on the normalized scale, reduce dimensions, and infer topology with a method matched to the expected structure, choosing the root from independent biological knowledge (e.g., stem-cell markers or experimental time points) [7][8]. Fourth, test trajectory-associated genes with models that respect the inferred structure — generalized additive models along pseudotime per lineage — rather than naive cluster contrasts, since cells on different branches sharing a pseudotime value are not comparable states.

## 6 Limitations

*Identifiability of zero inflation.* In droplet UMI data, the NB zero probability already explains most sparsity; π and θ compete to explain the same zeros, and without strong covariates the mixture is weakly identified [4]. ZINB fits should be accompanied by goodness-of-fit checks against the NB null, not assumed superior.

*Doublet ground truth is circular.* Benchmarks rely on hashing or genetic demultiplexing, which themselves fail on ambient-RNA-contaminated or low-heterogeneity samples; methods tuned to these benchmarks may overfit to clean, high-heterogeneity data [5].

*Trajectory inference assumes continuity.* Pseudotime is meaningful only if the sampled cells densely tile the underlying process. Gaps, batch-confounded branches, or rapid transitions produce confident but fictitious orderings, and no method can infer direction without an external root or kinetic arrow [8].

*RNA velocity's kinetic assumptions.* Constant transcription, splicing, and degradation rates per gene are routinely violated; multi-rate genes and bursty transcription distort phase portraits and can reverse inferred fate decisions [9].

*Scalability versus uncertainty.* Atlas-scale datasets force approximations (sketching, minibatching, graph abstraction) whose uncertainty is rarely propagated; pseudotime confidence intervals remain the exception, and most published trajectories present point estimates as fact.

## 7 Conclusion

Single-cell transcriptomics has matured from a descriptive technology into a statistically disciplined one, and the three problems surveyed here trace that arc. Zero-inflated modeling began as an intuitive fix for dropouts, was formalized in ZINB-WaVE and deep generative decoders, and was then productively challenged by evidence that droplet data are largely NB-distributed — a dispute that sharpened rather than weakened count modeling [1][3][4]. Doublet detection converged on the artificial-doublet paradigm, where the honest benchmark literature tells us which methods are robust and, just as importantly, which doublets (homotypic) no expression-based method can find [5][6]. Trajectory inference grew from principal curves through lineage-aware smoothing to mechanistically grounded velocity models, with benchmarking consistently redirecting attention from algorithmic novelty to topology matching, root specification, and stability [7][8][9]. The through-line is humility about identifiability: zeros can be technical or biological, doublets can hide inside real clusters, and pseudotime has no arrow until biology supplies one. Methods that expose these ambiguities — posterior weights, calibrated doublet probabilities, root-dependent orderings — serve the science better than methods that conceal them.
