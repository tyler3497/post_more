# The Neural Tangent Kernel and the Lazy–Rich Divide: Infinite-Width Limits, Kernel Regression Equivalence, and the Frontier of Feature Learning

## Abstract

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK), introduced by Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler [1], provides a rigorous lens on gradient-based training of overparameterized networks: in the infinite-width limit, the empirical kernel defined by gradient inner products converges to a deterministic limit and remains constant during training, reducing dynamics to kernel gradient descent. Yet the kernel regime is only one corner of a richer landscape. Chizat and Bach [3] showed that linearization arises from a scaling choice rather than from overparameterization alone, and the lazy-versus-rich dichotomy maps a sharp divide governed by initialization scale, with different implicit biases and sample complexities on either side. This article develops NTK theory from first principles, derives the constancy and linearization equivalences, surveys the lazy-training framework and its experimental qualifications [4][6], analyzes finite-width and depth corrections beyond the limit [5], and examines maximal-update parameterizations that restore feature learning at infinite width. We close with open questions at the edge of the NTK regime.

## 1 Intro

Modern deep learning presents a theoretical paradox: networks with millions of parameters, trained by non-convex gradient methods on comparatively modest datasets, routinely generalize. Classical statistical learning theory, which predicts catastrophic overfitting in this regime, offers little comfort. One of the most successful mathematical responses to this paradox is the *Neural Tangent Kernel* (NTK), introduced by Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler [1]. Their central observation is striking in its simplicity: as the width of a neural network tends to infinity, the kernel formed by inner products of parameter gradients converges to a deterministic limit — and, crucially, *stays constant* throughout training. The nonlinear, non-convex optimization problem then collapses onto linear dynamics in function space: kernel gradient descent on a fixed kernel.

The NTK framework proves global convergence of gradient descent for wide networks [7], relates training speed to the kernel's eigenstructure, and yields closed-form predictions via kernel regression. Yet the framework has a conspicuous blind spot: in the kernel regime, *features do not learn* — internal representations stay frozen at their random initial values, and all learning is confined to a linear readout in the tangent space. This is the *lazy* regime, and it sits in tension with the empirical reality that deep networks demonstrably acquire hierarchical, task-adapted representations.

This article develops the NTK theory from first principles and then probes its boundaries: when does the kernel description hold, what breaks it, and what lies beyond? We survey the lazy-training analysis of Chizat and Bach [3][4], the lazy–rich dichotomy, depth-induced departures at the edge of chaos [5], and parameterizations that preserve feature learning at infinite width. The emerging picture is not that the NTK is wrong, but that it describes one phase of a richer phase diagram — a phase real networks can enter or escape depending on scaling, architecture, and task.

## 2 Background

The story begins with the infinite-width *Gaussian process* correspondence: Neal (1996) showed that a single-hidden-layer network with i.i.d. random weights converges, as width grows, to a Gaussian process — the NNGP — describing the network *at initialization*. The NTK extends this to the *training trajectory*: the initial function is a Gaussian-process draw, and its entire evolution under gradient flow is governed by a deterministic kernel [1].

Consider a network f(x; θ) with parameters θ in R^p, trained on data (x_i, y_i) with the squared loss L(θ) = (1/2) Σ_i (f(x_i; θ) − y_i)^2. Under continuous-time gradient flow, θ̇ = −∇L(θ), the network outputs evolve as

df(x; θ_t)/dt = −Σ_i Θ_t(x, x_i) (f(x_i; θ_t) − y_i),

where the *empirical* neural tangent kernel is

Θ_t(x, x′) = ⟨∇_θ f(x; θ_t), ∇_θ f(x′; θ_t)⟩.

This identity is exact at any width: the functional dynamics are *kernel gradient descent* with a time-varying kernel. The infinite-width limit freezes it: with weights scaled as 1/√m, the law of large numbers concentrates each gradient inner product, Θ_t(x, x′) → Θ(x, x′) — a deterministic limit depending only on architecture, activation, and depth — and the kernel's rate of change during training vanishes, so Θ_t ≈ Θ_0 throughout [1].

Du and coauthors proved that gradient descent finds global minima of sufficiently wide networks [7], establishing the *existence* of the regime the NTK would characterize completely. Lee and coauthors then showed the sharpest form: wide networks of any depth evolve *exactly as linear models* under gradient descent, with the trained predictor matching kernel regression on the NTK [2] — turning effortless overparameterized optimization into a theorem about linear dynamics in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space.

> **Theorem:** (Informal NTK constancy, after [1].) For a fully-connected network of fixed depth with Lipschitz, non-polynomial activation and i.i.d. Gaussian weights scaled as 1/√width, the empirical NTK Θ_t converges in probability to a deterministic limit Θ as width → ∞, uniformly on compact sets, with sup_t ‖Θ_t − Θ_0‖ → 0 during gradient-flow training. If the limiting kernel matrix on the training data is positive definite, training converges exponentially to a global minimizer.

## 3 Methodology

Our analytical methodology follows the program established in [1] and sharpened in [2], organized around three pillars: *scaling*, *concentration*, and *linearization*.

**Scaling.** The choice of parameterization is the decisive modeling decision. In the *NTK parameterization*, each weight matrix W^(ℓ) in R^{m×m} enters as (σ_w/√m) W^(ℓ) with standard Gaussian entries, so that preactivations remain O(1) as m → ∞. In contrast, the *standard parameterization* folds the 1/√m scaling into the initialization variance. The NTK parameterization makes the kernel limit transparent: each of the O(m) parameter groups contributes O(1/m) to the kernel inner product, and their sum converges by the weak law of large numbers.

**Concentration.** For a depth-L ReLU network, the limiting kernel admits a recursive construction: with Σ^(1)(x, x′) = (σ_w^2/d)⟨x, x′⟩ + σ_b^2 and the arc-cosine kernels κ_0, κ_1 of Cho and Saul,

Θ^(ℓ+1)(x, x′) = Σ^(ℓ+1)(x, x′) + Θ^(ℓ)(x, x′) · κ̇(Σ^(ℓ))(x, x′),

where the dot-kernel term propagates gradient correlations backward through the layers. Concentration of the preactivation covariances (a matrix version of the law of large numbers) plus Lipschitz continuity of the activation yields uniform convergence of Θ_0 to this recursion [1].

**Linearization.** The decisive step is showing the kernel does not *move*: differentiating Θ_t in time produces terms of order 1/√m per parameter group, so total displacement over training is O(1/√m). Hence the network function is uniformly close to its first-order Taylor expansion,

f_lin(x; θ) = f(x; θ_0) + ⟨∇_θ f(x; θ_0), θ − θ_0⟩,

and gradient flow on the original loss tracks gradient flow on the linearized model — exactly kernel regression with the frozen NTK [2] — a convex problem in function space with closed-form solution:

f_*(x) = Θ(x, X) Θ(X, X)^{−1} y,

up to the initialization offset; convergence is fastest along the top eigendirections of the Gram matrix, giving a principled motivation for early stopping [1].

The following table summarizes the three methodological pillars and their mathematical content:

| Pillar | Object studied | Key tool | Limit behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scaling | Parameterization at width m | 1/√m normalization | O(1) preactivations, O(1) kernel |
| Concentration | Θ_0(x, x′) at init | Weak law of large numbers | Deterministic recursion |
| Linearization | sup_t ‖Θ_t − Θ_0‖ | Gradient-flow bounds | Kernel regression equivalence |

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Infinite-Width Limit and the Constancy of the NTK

The NTK analysis sidesteps the non-convex, high-dimensional loss landscape entirely by working in *function space*. The state variable is no longer the parameter vector but the vector of network outputs on the training set, u(t) in R^n with u_i(t) = f(x_i; θ_t). Under gradient flow with squared loss, u̇ = −η Θ_t (u − y): a linear ODE with a time-varying coefficient matrix. Constancy of Θ_t turns this into u̇ = −η Θ (u − y), solvable in closed form:

u(t) − y = exp(−ηΘt) (u(0) − y).

Every question about optimization — convergence rate, final predictor, sensitivity to initialization — reduces to spectral questions about the deterministic matrix Θ. If its smallest eigenvalue λ_min is bounded away from zero, convergence is exponential with rate ηλ_min [1][7].

The *positive-definiteness* of the limiting kernel is therefore the linchpin, proved for non-polynomial activations with data on the sphere [1]; degenerate activations collapse the kernel rank. Depth enters through the recursion above: each layer compounds covariance and gradient-correlation terms, and the kernel's condition number typically worsens with depth — a first hint that the infinite-width limit interacts nontrivially with the infinite-depth limit [5].

### 4.2 Lazy Training as a Scaling Choice

Chizat and Bach reframed the entire phenomenon with a disarmingly simple observation: *laziness is a choice of scaling, not a consequence of overparameterization* [3]. Consider a model h(w) = α (f(w) − f(w_0)) with a scale factor α. To fit O(1) targets, the linearized displacement must satisfy α‖∇f(w_0)‖‖w − w_0‖ = O(1), so ‖w − w_0‖ = O(1/α). For large α, parameters barely move; the model is well-approximated by its linearization for the whole trajectory, and training reduces to kernel regression — *regardless of how many parameters there are*. Conversely, small α forces parameters to travel far, breaking the linear approximation and entering the *rich* (or *active*, or *feature-learning*) regime.

Developed fully with Oyallon [4], this perspective is both liberating and sobering. Liberating: the kernel regime is the α → ∞ endpoint of a continuum, accessible in principle for any differentiable model. Sobering: convolutional networks trained in the lazy regime systematically *underperform* their normally-trained counterparts on vision tasks, with accuracy declining as α grows. The empirical success of deep learning on hard perceptual tasks is therefore *not* explained by lazy training — real networks learn features, and the kernel regime is the regime in which they do not.

> **Corollary:** (Lazy–linear equivalence, after [3].) Under mild smoothness assumptions, the trajectory of gradient flow on the scaled model with parameter α stays within O(1/α) of the initialization, and the gap between the true and linearized trajectories vanishes as α → ∞. The limiting dynamics are kernel gradient descent with the tangent kernel at initialization.

### 4.3 The Lazy–Rich Dichotomy: Sparsity, Signal, and Sample Complexity

The crispest answers come from simple overparameterized models where both regimes are analytically tractable. The canonical setting is sparse regression with a diagonal linear network, f(x; u, v) = ⟨u ⊙ v, x⟩: the model is linear in the parameters' *product*, so gradient descent either adjusts many coordinates slightly (kernel regime, dense solution) or grows a few coordinates from small initialization (rich regime, sparse solution).

The dichotomy is governed by the *initialization scale*. With large initialization, dynamics linearize and converge to the minimum-ℓ_2-norm interpolant — the kernel solution. With small initialization, an implicit sparsity bias emerges: coordinates compete, winners grow exponentially while losers stagnate, selecting the minimum-ℓ_1-norm interpolant. The transition is sharp, and the regimes differ in *sample complexity*: the rich regime recovers a k-sparse signal from O(k log d) samples, while the kernel regime needs order-d samples — the difference between learning and memorization.

The lesson generalizes: small initialization induces implicit low-rank bias in matrix factorization and max-margin or variation-norm bias in two-layer networks. The unifying principle is that *rich dynamics perform adaptive basis selection*, while lazy dynamics are confined to the fixed tangent-feature basis at initialization. For targets with low-dimensional or sparse structure misaligned with random features, the kernel regime pays a steep statistical price.

### 4.4 Beyond the Limit: Depth, the Edge of Chaos, and Finite-Width Fluctuations

The NTK constancy theorem takes width to infinity at *fixed* depth. Real networks are deep, and the joint limit — depth and width growing together — tells a different story. Hanin and Nica analyzed NTK dispersion for ReLU networks with depth comparable to width: the kernel's fluctuations depend sharply on the *depth-to-width ratio* and the initialization's position in the order–chaos phase diagram [5]. At the *edge of chaos* — the critical initialization where signal propagation neither explodes nor vanishes — NTK variability grows *exponentially* with depth, as it does in the chaotic phase; only the ordered phase keeps the kernel well-behaved.

The consequence is severe: the NTK of a deep network may stay constant during training *only in the ordered phase*, and the Gram matrix itself can change during training outside it [5]. This undercuts naive extrapolation of infinite-width results to deep architectures, and connects the NTK literature to the older mean-field theory of signal propagation: trainability, kernel stability, and initialization criticality are facets of one phenomenon.

Finite width brings further corrections even at fixed depth: the empirical kernel fluctuates around its limit by order 1/√m, and the trained predictor deviates from the exact kernel-regression solution by terms vanishing only as m → ∞. At practical widths the NTK prediction is an *approximation* whose error budget must be quantified per task.

### 4.5 Maximal Update and the Return of Feature Learning

Can we take a *different* infinite-width limit that preserves feature learning? Yes: the *maximal update* (μP) parameterization of Yang and Hu scales each layer's learning rate and initialization so that every layer's update is *maximal* — as large as possible without destabilizing training — as width grows. In μP, hidden-layer features evolve by O(1) amounts even at infinite width: the limit is a nontrivial dynamical system in function space, a *feature-learning* infinite-width limit.

The μP program reframes hyperparameter transfer: settings tuned on small models transfer to large ones because the limit preserves the training dynamics' qualitative character. It resolves the tension between mathematical tractability *and* representation learning by showing the kernel regime is one fixed point of a larger space of scaling limits — distinguished by sending feature updates to zero while keeping the predictor's update O(1).

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The theory above makes falsifiable predictions, and the empirical literature has tested them carefully. Three lines of evidence stand out.

**1. Linearization fidelity.** For wide networks, the nonlinear trajectory tracks its first-order Taylor expansion throughout training, with the gap shrinking as width grows [2]; trained predictions match the closed-form kernel-regression solution within the predicted finite-width error.

**2. Lazy versus feature learning, disentangled.** Geiger and collaborators train F(w, x) = α(f(w, x) − f(w_0, x)) and vary α [6]. Small α gives the *feature-learning* regime — representations change substantially, test performance is strong. Large α gives the *lazy* regime — weights barely move, performance degrades toward the kernel baseline. The crossover is visible in representation-similarity metrics such as centered kernel alignment, which stays near 1 in the lazy phase and drops in the feature phase — the experimental counterpart of the Chizat–Bach theory [3][4].

**3. Global convergence at finite width.** Gradient descent on sufficiently wide finite networks converges linearly to zero training loss [7] — the exponential convergence predicted by the kernel ODE is not an artifact of the limit.

Concretely, the following Python snippet computes the empirical NTK Gram matrix for a small two-layer ReLU network by explicit Jacobian inner products — the finite-width quantity whose limit the theory describes:

```python
import numpy as np

def relu(z):
    return np.maximum(z, 0.0)

def empirical_ntk(X, W1, b1, w2):
    """Empirical NTK Gram matrix for f(x) = w2 @ relu(W1 x + b1) / sqrt(m)."""
    m = W1.shape[0]
    Z = relu(X @ W1.T + b1) / np.sqrt(m)   # hidden features
    D = (X @ W1.T + b1 > 0).astype(float)  # activation pattern
    n = X.shape[0]
    K = np.zeros((n, n))
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            gw = float(Z[i] @ Z[j])
            gW = float(((w2 * D[i])[:, None] * X[i]).ravel()
                      @ ((w2 * D[j])[:, None] * X[j]).ravel()) / m
            gb = float((w2 * D[i]) @ (w2 * D[j])) / m
            K[i, j] = gw + gW + gb
    return K

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(8, 5))
X = X / np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
m = 2048
W1 = rng.normal(size=(m, 5))
b1 = rng.normal(size=m)
w2 = rng.normal(size=m)
K = empirical_ntk(X, W1, b1, w2)
print("Gram matrix symmetric:", np.allclose(K, K.T))
print("Smallest eigenvalue:", np.linalg.eigvalsh(K).min())
```

As m grows, K concentrates around the deterministic recursion of Section 3. The limiting recursion has a clean functional form; in Haskell, the covariance propagation for ReLU reads:

```haskell
-- Arc-cosine kernel recursion for the infinite-width covariance Sigma
-- and the NTK Theta of a ReLU network (cf. Cho & Saul; Jacot et al.)
kappa0 :: Double -> Double
kappa0 c = acos (-c) / pi

kappa1 :: Double -> Double
kappa1 c = (c * acos (-c) + sqrt (1 - c * c)) / pi

propagate :: Double -> Double -> (Double, Double) -> (Double, Double)
propagate sigW sigB (sigma, theta) =
  let sigma' = sigW * kappa1 sigma + sigB
      theta' = sigma' + theta * kappa0 sigma
  in (sigma', theta')
```

Iterating `propagate` over layers yields the deterministic pair (Σ^(L), Θ^(L)) the empirical kernel concentrates around — the mathematical heart of the NTK limit [1].

Finally, a compact summary of the empirical phase structure:

| Regime | Weight motion | Features | Predictor | Typical test behavior |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lazy (α large) | O(1/α), negligible | Frozen at init | Kernel regression | Matches NTK; degrades on perceptual tasks [4][6] |
| Rich (α small) | O(1), substantial | Adapted to task | Adaptive basis selection | Strong; implicit sparsity and low-rank bias |
| μP limit | O(1) per layer | Evolve at infinite width | Feature-learning dynamics | Transfers across widths |

## 6 Limitations

The NTK framework is a triumph of mathematical deep learning theory, but its limitations are as instructive as its successes:

1. *No representation learning.* The kernel regime cannot explain representation learning — arguably the central empirical fact about deep networks. Phenomena depending on adapting features, from hierarchical abstraction in vision to in-context adaptation in language models, lie outside the theory by construction [3][4][6].

2. *Fragility with respect to depth.* At the edge of chaos the NTK's fluctuations grow exponentially in depth and constancy can fail [5] — yet practical initializations deliberately sit near the edge of chaos for trainability.

3. *Finite-width corrections matter.* Error terms vanish as m → ∞ but give little guidance at practical widths, where the Gram matrix fluctuates, the kernel drifts, and the closed-form predictor is approximate.

4. *Loss- and optimizer-specificity.* The cleanest results concern gradient flow on squared loss; cross-entropy, adaptive optimizers, weight decay, normalization, and attention each perturb the picture, and universality does not automatically transfer to these settings.

5. *Computational tractability.* The NTK Gram matrix is n × n, so exact kernel regression costs O(n^3) — for large datasets the closed form is less practical than the gradient descent it replaces.

None of these limitations diminishes what the NTK achieved: the first rigorous, quantitative account of why overparameterized optimization works. They delineate, instead, the boundary of the kernel phase — and thereby map the territory where a future theory of feature learning must be built.

## 7 Conclusion

The NTK began as a technical device — a kernel linearizing gradient descent — and became a phase of matter for deep learning theory. In the infinite-width limit, training is kernel regression: convex, solvable, spectrally transparent [1][2]. Lazy-training analysis revealed this phase as a scaling choice rather than a law of nature [3][4]; the lazy–rich dichotomy showed the choice carries sharp statistical consequences; depth and finite width blur the phase boundary [5]; and maximal-update parameterizations redraw it, restoring feature learning to the infinite-width picture.

The practical moral is nuanced: where fixed random features suffice, the NTK predicts performance accurately; where tasks demand adaptive representations, the network must escape the kernel regime and the theory must follow. The edge of the NTK regime is thus not a failure of the theory but its most productive frontier — where kernel methods end and a future science of feature learning begins.
