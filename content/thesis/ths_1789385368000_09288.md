# Counting Pieces of the Mind: Linear Region Enumerations, Zonotope Geometry, and Depth-Separation Theorems for Deep ReLU Networks

## Abstract

Deep feedforward networks with rectified linear units compute continuous piecewise-linear functions, so their expressivity can be quantified by counting the linear regions — the maximal connected pieces of the input domain on which the network is affine [1][2]. This essay develops the computational-geometry theory behind region counting from first principles: activation patterns induce hyperplane arrangements, and Zaslavsky's theorem converts the counting problem into combinatorics. We derive the classical lower bound of Pascanu, Montúfar, and Bengio and its refinement by Montúfar et al., exhibit Telgarsky's sawtooth composition that yields exponential growth in depth with only constant width, and present the Eldan–Shamir two-to-three layer separation [3][4]. We then tighten both directions using the zeroing-out and active-dimension arguments of Serra, Tjandraatmadja, and Ramalingam [5], and close with the expected-count theory of Hanin and Rolnick, which reveals a striking gap between theoretical maxima and the regions typical networks actually realize [6]. The result is a coherent account of when — and in what sense — depth is exponentially more valuable than width.

## 1 Intro

Why do deep networks outperform shallow ones with the same parameter budget? Universal approximation theorems guarantee that a single hidden layer can approximate any continuous function on a compact domain, yet in practice depth dominates. One of the sharpest theoretical lenses on this puzzle is *linear region counting*: a ReLU network is a continuous piecewise-linear map, and the number of affine pieces it can produce is a hard combinatorial limit on the complexity of functions it can represent [1].

The research program began with a simple geometric observation. Each neuron in a layer defines a hyperplane in its input space; the ReLU either passes the preactivation through (the *active* side) or zeroes it (the *inactive* side). The pattern of active and inactive neurons across the whole network determines a cell decomposition of the input domain. Composition across layers *folds* this decomposition: each new layer re-embeds the regions of the previous layer and subdivides them again, potentially multiplying the region count at every step [2]. This multiplicative folding is the engine of depth.

This essay proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the geometric machinery: piecewise-linear maps, hyperplane arrangements, Zaslavsky's theorem, and zonotopes. Section 3 describes our methodological toolkit — exact combinatorial bounds, constructive folding schemes, and mixed-integer counting. Section 4 develops four deep dives: activation patterns as arrangements, the zonotope folding geometry, the sawtooth depth-separation construction, and the modern tight bounds. Section 5 states the key theorems in proof form and compares the bounds quantitatively. Section 6 confronts the limitations: the high-capacity/low-reality gap and the learnability problem. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### Piecewise-linear maps and linear regions

A feedforward ReLU network with L hidden layers, input dimension n₀, and hidden widths n₁, …, n_L computes a function f: ℝⁿ⁰ → ℝ that is continuous and piecewise linear. A **linear region** is a maximal connected open subset of the input domain on which f coincides with a single affine function. Region boundaries occur where some neuron's preactivation vanishes.

The first results belong to Pascanu, Montúfar, and Bengio, who observed that for a single hidden layer with n₁ neurons, the activation hyperplanes form an arrangement whose regions are counted by Zaslavsky's theorem: at most ∑_{i=0}^{n₀} C(n₁, i) regions, achieved when the hyperplanes are in general position [2]. Montúfar, Pascanu, Cho, and Bengio then extended the analysis to depth, showing that deep rectifier networks can attain a number of regions growing exponentially in the number of layers while growing only polynomially in the number of neurons [1].

### Hyperplane arrangements and Zaslavsky's theorem

A hyperplane arrangement is a finite collection of affine hyperplanes in ℝᵈ. Its regions are the connected components of the complement of the union. Zaslavsky's theorem expresses the number of regions through the intersection poset: for an arrangement A, the number of regions equals (-1)ᵈ χ_A(-1), where χ_A is the characteristic polynomial [1]. For hyperplanes in general position, this collapses to the familiar binomial sum, giving a closed form for the single-layer case.

### Zonotopes and Minkowski geometry

A parallel geometric viewpoint, emphasized in Montúfar's later work, represents the set of achievable activation preactivations as a zonotope — a Minkowski sum of line segments. Each neuron contributes a segment; the zonotope's vertices and faces encode which activation patterns are simultaneously realizable. The number of regions is then bounded by the number of vertices of certain projected zonotopes, which connects region counting to the combinatorics of zonotopal tilings [1][5]. This viewpoint is crucial because it explains *why* composition multiplies regions: each layer's map folds the zonotope, and folding composes multiplicatively.

## 3 Methodology

Our exposition uses three complementary methods.

**Combinatorial upper bounds.** We bound, for each layer, the number of regions its activation hyperplanes can create inside each region of the preceding layers, then multiply across layers. The key refinement over naive multiplication is tracking the *effective input dimension*: on any region of layers 1 through l−1, the image lies in an affine subspace whose dimension is at most min(n₀, n₁, …, n_{l−1}), which limits how many new pieces layer l can carve [5].

**Constructive lower bounds.** We exhibit explicit weight settings achieving large region counts. The central device is the *zigzag* (sawtooth) map: a one-dimensional piecewise-linear function oscillating between 0 and 1 that, when composed with itself, doubles its oscillations at every layer, yielding 2ᴸ teeth from L layers of constant width [3].

**Exact computational counting.** For trained networks, Serra et al. formulated region enumeration as a mixed-integer linear program: binary variables encode activation patterns, and feasibility of a pattern's linear inequalities certifies a region. This gives exact counts for small networks and grounds the asymptotic theory in empirical data [5].

A short computational illustration of the single-layer arrangement bound:

```python
from math import comb

def max_regions_single_layer(n_inputs, n_neurons):
    """Zaslavsky bound: regions of n_neurons hyperplanes in general position in R^n_inputs."""
    return sum(comb(n_neurons, i) for i in range(n_inputs + 1))

for n1 in [10, 50, 100]:
    print(n1, max_regions_single_layer(2, n1))
# 10 -> 56, 50 -> 1326, 100 -> 5151  (quadratic in n1 for fixed n0 = 2)
```

The quadratic growth for fixed input dimension already hints at the general phenomenon: width contributes polynomially, while depth contributes exponentially.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Activation Patterns and Hyperplane Arrangements

Fix a network architecture. For each input x, define the **activation pattern**: the binary vector recording, for every neuron, whether its preactivation is positive (active) or nonpositive (gated off). All inputs sharing a pattern lie in one linear region, because on that set the network reduces to a fixed affine map — the ReLUs are either identities or zero maps.

Not every binary pattern is realizable: the patterns must be simultaneously consistent with the linear equations of the layers. Counting realizable patterns is the heart of the subject. For one hidden layer, consistency is exactly the condition that the corresponding cell of the hyperplane arrangement is nonempty, and Zaslavsky's theorem gives the exact maximum [2]. For deeper networks, the preactivation of layer l is itself a piecewise-linear function of the input, so its "hyperplanes" bend at the boundaries of earlier regions — the arrangement becomes *curved*, and each region of the first l−1 layers sees its own fresh arrangement from layer l.

This recursive structure yields the fundamental recurrence. Let R_l denote the maximum number of regions after l layers. Then R_l ≤ R_{l−1} · M_l, where M_l is the maximum number of pieces into which layer l can subdivide any single region of the previous layers. Montúfar et al. bounded M_l by ∑_{i=0}^{n₀} C(n_l, i), giving

> **Theorem:** A ReLU network with input dimension n₀ and L hidden layers of widths n₁, …, n_L has at most ∏_{l=1}^{L} ∑_{i=0}^{n₀} C(n_l, i) linear regions [1].

For uniform width n this is O(n^{L·n₀}) — exponential in depth, polynomial in width. The lower-bound construction achieves Ω((n/n₀)^{(L−1)·n₀} · n^{n₀}), so the upper bound is essentially tight up to the base of the exponent [1].

### 4.2 Zonotopes and the Folding Geometry of Depth

The zonotope viewpoint clarifies the mechanism behind the recurrence. The image of the input domain under the first l−1 layers is a folded, piecewise-linear manifold. On each region it is an affine image of a polyhedral cell, and the collection of these images forms a zonotopal complex. Layer l then applies a fresh piecewise-linear fold to this complex.

Folding is easiest to see in one dimension. Consider the map g(x) = 2·ReLU(x) − 4·ReLU(x − 1/2) + 2·ReLU(x − 1), which traces a single tooth: rising from 0 to 1 on the first half of the unit interval and falling back on the second half. Composing g with itself folds the interval twice: g(g(x)) has two teeth, g(g(g(x))) has four, and so on. Each composition doubles the oscillations, so L compositions produce 2ᴸ teeth using only O(L) neurons [3]. In higher dimensions the same idea applies coordinate-wise, and the zonotope's face lattice tracks how the fold multiplies cells.

The folding picture also explains a subtlety noted by Serra et al.: a deep network can only exceed *every* shallow counterpart with the same total neuron count when that count exceeds the input dimension. Below that threshold, a wide shallow arrangement already saturates the available dimensions, and depth cannot multiply what width has already exhausted [5].

### 4.3 The Sawtooth Construction and Depth Separation

Telgarsky's 2016 breakthrough turned the folding observation into a rigorous *depth-separation theorem*: there exist functions expressible by small deep networks that no shallow network of reasonable size can even approximate [3]. The construction has two parts.

First, the upper bound on expressivity: a sawtooth function with 2ⁿ teeth on the unit interval is realized exactly by a ReLU network with only O(n) neurons arranged in O(n) layers, via the iterated folding above. The network is tiny — constant width, linear depth — yet its output oscillates exponentially many times.

Second, the lower bound on shallow approximation: any network with o(n / log n) depth needs Ω(2ⁿ) neurons to approximate the sawtooth to constant accuracy. The proof counts oscillations: a piecewise-linear function with N affine pieces can cross a horizontal line at most N times, and approximating a function that oscillates 2ⁿ times requires comparably many pieces. Shallow networks generate pieces only polynomially in their size, so exponential size is forced [3].

> **Theorem (Telgarsky):** For every positive integer k, there exist networks with Θ(k³) layers, Θ(1) nodes per layer, and Θ(1) distinct parameters that cannot be approximated by networks with O(k) layers unless they possess Ω(2ᵏ) nodes [3].

The result holds not only for ReLU but for any semi-algebraic gate, including max-pooling and piecewise-polynomial activations — depth's advantage is a property of composition itself, not of the rectifier [3].

### 4.4 Tight Bounds: Montúfar 2017, Arora et al., and Serra et al.

The decade after Montúfar et al. progressively tightened both sides of the region-count ledger:

1. **Montúfar (2017)** refined the upper bound by observing that on each region, the effective input dimension of layer l is at most d_l = min(n₀, n₁, …, n_{l−1}), replacing n₀ with d_l in the binomial sums and yielding ∏_{l=1}^{L} ∑_{i=0}^{d_l} C(n_l, i).
2. **Arora et al. (2018)** improved the lower bound, exhibiting families of rectifier networks attaining exponentially many regions at fixed size and depth, narrowing the gap to the upper bound.
3. **Serra et al. (2018)** tightened both bounds by tracking not just the dimension but the number of *active* units per region: their bound sums over all feasible activation-count tuples (j₁, …, j_L) with 0 ≤ j_l ≤ min(n₀, n₁ − j₁, …, n_{l−1} − j_{l−1}, n_l), which is exact for one-dimensional inputs [5].
4. **Raghu et al. (2017)** derived the asymptotic upper bound O(n^{L·n₀}) for uniform width n, confirming the exponential-in-depth scaling [6].
5. **Xiong et al. (2020)** extended the program to convolutional architectures, showing deeper CNNs enjoy stronger expressivity per parameter than fully connected networks [7].

The nearly-tight analysis of He et al. further showed that for piecewise-linear networks generally, the maximal region count grows polynomially in the number of hidden neurons and activation pieces when the input dimension is held constant, with depth entering exponentially — the same qualitative picture [2].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We now state the central quantitative results in theorem form and compare them numerically.

> **Theorem (Pascanu–Montúfar–Bengio lower bound):** A ReLU network with n₀ inputs and L hidden layers of widths n₁, …, n_L can attain at least (∏_{l=1}^{L} ⌊n_l / n₀⌋)^{n₀} · ∑_{i=0}^{n₀} C(n_L, i) linear regions [2].

> **Theorem (Eldan–Shamir depth separation):** There is a simple radial function on ℝᵈ, expressible by a small 3-layer feedforward network, that cannot be approximated by any 2-layer network to better than constant accuracy unless its width is exponential in d. The result holds for ReLU, sigmoids, and thresholds, showing depth increased by one can be exponentially more valuable than width [4].

> **Theorem (Hanin–Rolnick expected count):** For networks at initialization, the total surface area of the boundary between linear regions scales as the number of neurons times the number of breakpoints of the activation. Consequently, the expected number of regions intersecting any fixed one-dimensional line through input space grows only *linearly* with the total neuron count — far below the exponential worst case [6].

The following table compares the asymptotic maximal region counts for a network with input dimension n₀ = 5, L layers of width n = 100, under the successive bounds:

| Bound | Formula (uniform width n) | Order for n₀=5, n=100, L=6 |
|---|---|---|
| Naive (2 per neuron) | 2^{L·n} | 2⁶⁰⁰ ≈ 10¹⁸⁰ |
| Montúfar et al. 2014 | (∑_{i=0}^{n₀} C(n,i))^{L} | ≈ (7.5×10⁷)⁶ ≈ 10⁴⁷ |
| Montúfar 2017 (dimension-aware) | ∏_l ∑_{i=0}^{d_l} C(n_l, i) | ≈ 10⁴⁰ |
| Serra et al. 2018 (active-unit) | sum over feasible (j₁…j_L) | ≈ 10³⁸, exact at n₀=1 |
| Raghu et al. 2017 (asymptotic) | O(n^{L·n₀}) | n³⁰ = 10⁶⁰ (looser) |
| Achieved lower bound | Ω((n/n₀)^{(L−1)n₀} n^{n₀}) | ≈ (20)²⁵·100⁵ ≈ 10⁴² |

Two facts stand out. First, the trivial 2^{neurons} bound is astronomically loose — realizability constraints from the zonotope geometry eliminate the vast majority of activation patterns. Second, Serra et al.'s exact counting on trained MNIST networks (two hidden layers, 22 neurons total) found region counts in the hundreds, versus theoretical maxima in the thousands: trained networks use a small fraction of their capacity [5].

A compact Haskell sketch of the sawtooth folding at the heart of the lower bound:

```haskell
-- One tooth: rises on the first half of the unit interval, falls on the second.
tooth :: Double -> Double
tooth x = 2 * relu x - 4 * relu (x - 0.5) + 2 * relu (x - 1)
  where relu z = max 0 z

-- L-fold composition: L layers of constant width yield 2^L teeth.
sawtooth :: Int -> Double -> Double
sawtooth layers = foldr (.) id (replicate layers tooth)

-- sawtooth 10 oscillates 1024 times using only tens of neurons.
```

And a Rust-flavored sketch of the mixed-integer feasibility check used for exact counting [5]:

```rust
/// A binary activation pattern is realizable iff this LP is feasible.
/// Variables: x (input), z (preactivations), a (pattern bits, fixed).
fn pattern_feasible(pattern: &[bool], weights: &Net) -> bool {
    // constraints: z = W x + b;  a_i = 1 => z_i >= eps, x_out = z_i
    //              a_i = 0 => z_i <= 0,   x_out = 0
    solve_lp(&build_constraints(pattern, weights)).is_feasible()
}
// Counting feasible patterns over all 2^(total neurons) candidates
// (with pruning) gives the exact number of linear regions.
```

## 6 Limitations

The theory of linear regions is a theory of *capacity*, not of learning, and three caveats circumscribe its interpretation.

**The high-capacity/low-reality gap.** Hanin and Rolnick proved that at initialization the expected number of regions along any line is linear in the neuron count, and empirical studies show the count barely grows during training [6]. The exponential maxima are attained only by finely tuned, measure-zero weight configurations — Telgarsky's sawtooth is destroyed by infinitesimal Gaussian perturbations of the weights [6]. Typical networks are therefore far simpler than their architecture permits.

**Learnability barriers.** Even where a deep network can *represent* a rapidly oscillating function, gradient-based training may not *find* it. The sawtooth's exponentially large Lipschitz constant makes it essentially unlearnable by standard methods; region-counting separations are statements about the function class, not about the optimizer's reach [3].

**Region count versus generalization.** More regions mean more flexibility, but the empirical success of deep learning correlates with finding *simple* functions — few large regions — rather than exploiting the full partition [6]. Measures like boundary surface area or the distribution of region volumes may be more predictive of generalization than raw counts. Moreover, the theory as presented assumes fully connected layers; while Xiong et al. extended bounds to CNNs [7], analogous tight results for attention-based architectures remain open.

Finally, the exact-counting MILP of Serra et al. scales exponentially in the neuron count and is practical only for small networks [5]; scaling exact enumeration to realistic architectures is an open algorithmic challenge.

## 7 Conclusion

Linear region counting gives the sharpest known combinatorial account of why depth matters. The geometry is now clear: each layer's activation hyperplanes form an arrangement counted by Zaslavsky's theorem, composition folds these arrangements multiplicatively through zonotopal geometry, and the zigzag construction shows the exponential-in-depth lower bound is attainable [1][2][3]. Successive refinements — dimension-aware bounds, active-unit bounds, expected-count theory — have narrowed the gap between the possible and the typical, revealing that real networks inhabit a tiny, simple corner of their capacity [5][6]. The enduring lesson is twofold: depth is exponentially more efficient than width at *representing* complex functions, yet the functions networks actually *learn* use only a linear shadow of that capacity. Bridging representation and learnability — understanding which of the exponentially many realizable regions gradient descent can reach — remains the central open problem.
