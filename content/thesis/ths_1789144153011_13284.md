# The Dataflow Model for Unbounded Data: Event Time, Watermarks, Triggers, and Exactly-Once Streaming SQL

## Abstract

Unbounded, out-of-order event streams are the dominant substrate of modern analytics, yet classical batch and streaming abstractions force a cruel tradeoff between correctness, latency, and cost. This thesis develops a unified treatment of the Dataflow model [1] and its two successors in exactly-once stateful processing: barrier-aligned distributed checkpointing in the Chandy–Lamport lineage, and continuous streaming SQL over dynamic tables. We formalize event time versus processing time, watermarks as completeness predicates, and triggers as a general mechanism for balancing speculative refinement against cost, with a taxonomy of fixed, sliding, and session windows and a pane calculus for late-data correction. We then characterize exactly-once semantics as the conjunction of deterministic replay and atomic state-and-sink commitment, analyzing barrier snapshotting and two-phase commit sinks in Apache Flink. Finally, we reconstruct streaming SQL — Flink SQL's dynamic-table/changelog duality and ksqlDB's ROWTIME windowing [3] — as a declarative projection of the same model. We close with a formal latency–correctness–cost theorem, measurement methodology, and the model's enduring limitations.

## 1. Introduction

The central pathology of stream processing is that *time itself is unobservable*. A record reporting that a payment was authorized at 13:04:22 UTC carries an intrinsic timestamp — its **event time** — but the system encounters that record at some later, uncontrollable instant — its **processing time**. The two are decoupled by network transit, clock skew, mobile-device offline buffering, and human operational delays, and no amount of infrastructure can re-synchronize them after the fact. Every streaming query therefore confronts a question that has no fully satisfactory answer: *when is a window complete?*

Early systems answered with processing-time heuristics: fire the hourly aggregation when the wall clock strikes, and silently misplace the events that arrive late [1]. Batch systems answered with the opposite extreme: wait until all data is present, and sacrifice latency entirely. The intellectual contribution of Akidau et al.'s **Dataflow model** (2015) is to reject both corners and instead decompose the problem into four orthogonal, independently tunable axes [1]:

1. **What** results are computed — the relational semantics of the query.
2. **Where** in event time they are computed — the windowing function.
3. **When** in processing time they are materialized — the trigger.
4. **How** earlier results are refined — the accumulation mode and allowed lateness.

This decomposition is the thread on which the entire thesis hangs. Section 2 traces the lineage from batch MapReduce through continuous-query systems to the unified model. Section 3 specifies our formal methodology. Section 4 develops the deep dive: the Dataflow pillars, watermarks and triggers, exactly-once state management, and streaming SQL. Section 5 proves the **latency–correctness–cost tradeoff theorem** and describes an empirical validation methodology. Section 6 catalogs limitations, and Section 7 concludes with directions for the next decade of streaming systems research.

The intended audience is the graduate student or senior engineer who must *build* these systems: we therefore ground every abstraction in concrete mechanism — watermark propagation, barrier snapshotting, two-phase commit sinks, dynamic-table changelogs — and verify each claim against primary sources [1][2][3][4][5].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From Batch to Streaming: The Semantic Gap



The streaming SQL community eventually converged on the **dynamic table** as the bridging abstraction: a streaming query continuously maintains a table whose contents evolve as events arrive, and physical changelogs (insert, retract, upsert messages) carry the deltas to downstream consumers [4]. We will show in Section 4.4 that this is not a new idea but the declarative shadow of the Dataflow model's pane semantics.

### 2.2 Time Domains

Following Akidau et al. [1] and the comparative watermark study of Begoli et al. [2], we distinguish three time domains:

| Domain | Definition | Controlled by |
|---|---|---|
| **Event time** | Timestamp embedded in the record at creation | Data producer |
| **Ingestion time** | Timestamp assigned when the record enters the system | Ingestion layer (e.g., Kafka broker) |
| **Processing time** | Wall-clock time at the operator | System clock |



### 2.3 Windowing as Partitioning of Event Time



- **Fixed (tumbling) windows**: disjoint, aligned intervals `[k·w, (k+1)·w)`.
- **Sliding windows**: intervals of length *w* emitted every *s < w*; each event belongs to ⌈w/s⌉ windows.
- **Session windows**: data-dependent intervals separated by a gap timeout *g*; an event extends a session if it falls within *g* of the session's end.

*Completeness* of a window — the assertion that all its events have arrived — is undecidable in general. Triggers and watermarks supply the operational substitute.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Four Pillars of the Dataflow Model



```java
PCollection<KV<String, Double>> events = /* unbounded source */ ...;

events
  .apply("Window", Window.<KV<String,Double>>into(
      FixedWindows.of(Duration.standardHours(1))))
  .apply("Sum", Combine.perKey(Sum.ofDoubles()))
  .apply("Trigger", Window.<KV<String,Double>>triggering(
      AfterWatermark.pastEndOfWindow()
          .withEarlyFirings(AfterProcessingTime.pastFirstElementInPane()
              .plusDelayOf(Duration.standardMinutes(5)))
          .withLateFirings(AfterCount.of(1)))
      .withAllowedLateness(Duration.standardDays(2))
      .accumulatingFiredPanes());
```

This single declaration separates the four concerns cleanly:

1. **What** — `Combine.perKey(Sum)` computes per-key sums regardless of time.
2. **Where** — `FixedWindows.of(1 hour)` assigns records to event-time intervals.
3. **When** — the composite trigger fires early panes every five minutes of processing time after the first element, fires on-time when the watermark passes the window end, and refires on each late arrival.
4. **How** — `accumulatingFiredPanes()` merges late data into previously emitted results rather than discarding the pane.

> **Theorem (Decomposition Soundness, informal):** For any pipeline expressed in the what/where/when/how decomposition, there exists a *reference semantics* defined as the limit of pane materializations as processing time → ∞ and allowed lateness → ∞, equal to the batch semantics of the same query on the complete dataset.

The theorem matters because it supplies the **correctness target**: deviations introduced by early firings or finite lateness are refinements and approximations, not arbitrary alternative answers. This is the precise sense in which the model "balances correctness, latency, and cost" [1].

#### Cost Dimensions

Each axis of freedom carries a cost vector:

| Axis | Latency cost | Correctness cost | Resource cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early firings | ↓ (speculative results sooner) | Intermediate panes superseded | Extra downstream compute |
| Allowed lateness | ↑ (state held longer) | ↓ (late data incorporated) | State retention, GC pressure |
| Discarding mode | — | Late data dropped | Minimal state |
| Accumulating mode | — | Full refinement history | Pane state per window |

No single configuration dominates; the choice is a workload-dependent point on a Pareto surface. Section 5 makes this surface quantitative.

### 4.2 Watermarks, Triggers, and the Completeness–Latency–Cost Triangle

#### Watermarks as Completeness Predicates

A **watermark** *W(t)* at processing time *t* is a claim: *all records with event time ≤ W(t) have been observed* [2]. In the ideal, watermarks are monotone non-decreasing and eventually reach +∞ (for bounded inputs) or track the event-time frontier (for unbounded inputs). In practice, sources emit watermarks heuristically — bounded-out-of-orderness (watermark = max observed event time − fixed delay), periodic punctuations, or idle-source advancement.

Begoli et al. [2] provide the definitive comparative analysis of watermark semantics in Flink versus Dataflow/Beam, showing that the two systems agree on the *contract* (watermarks gate window emission and state cleanup) while differing in propagation mechanics: Flink broadcasts watermarks through the operator DAG with min-aggregation across input partitions, whereas Dataflow tracks per-key watermarks with a more granular "watermark hold" mechanism for stateful transforms.

The crucial subtlety is that **watermarks are never guarantees**. A watermark is a *lower bound on our confidence*, and records arriving with event time < current watermark are **late**. The system has exactly three responses to late data [1]:

1. **Drop it** — cheap, silently incorrect (the processing-time default).
2. **Fire a late pane** — correct eventually, at the cost of recomputation and downstream retractions.
3. **Side-output it** — quarantine for offline reconciliation.

Allowed lateness bounds how long option 2 remains available; beyond the lateness horizon, late data is dropped or side-output. Garbage collection of window state is then provably safe: no trigger can fire for a window whose state has been collected.

#### Triggers: A Complete Firing Language

Triggers generalize "fire when the watermark passes" into a composable language [1]:

- **AfterWatermark** — fire when *W(t) ≥ window end* (the correctness anchor).
- **AfterProcessingTime** — fire after a processing-time delay (speculation).
- **AfterCount** — fire after *n* elements (density-based speculation).
- **Composite triggers** — `OrFinally`, `AfterEach` (in sequence), `AfterFirst`/`AfterAll` combinators.

Repeated firings (`Repeatedly.forever(...)`) convert a trigger into a *schedule*, and the accumulation mode determines the algebra of pane combination. The expressiveness result of the model is that any reasonable "emit early, emit often, correct later" policy is encodable — and, importantly, *inspectable*, so that downstream consumers can distinguish speculative panes from final ones via pane timing metadata.

### 4.3 Exactly-Once Semantics: Checkpointing and Two-Phase Sinks

The Dataflow model defines *what* correct results are; it does not by itself guarantee them under failure. **Exactly-once semantics** is the conjunction of two independent properties:

1. **Deterministic state recovery** — after a failure, operator state is restored to a consistent snapshot, and the input is replayed from a position consistent with that snapshot.
2. **Atomic external effects** — results written to external sinks are committed atomically with the state snapshot, so no result is emitted twice or lost.

Flink's implementation is the canonical realization, and Figure 3 shows its architecture. The mechanism descends from the **Chandy–Lamport distributed snapshot algorithm**: the job coordinator injects *checkpoint barriers* into the source streams; barriers flow with the data, aligning at each operator; when an operator has received barriers from all inputs, it snapshots its state to durable storage and forwards the barrier downstream. The resulting global snapshot is *consistent* — it corresponds to a valid cut of the execution — without ever stopping the world.

```java
// Flink: enabling exactly-once checkpointing (barrier snapshotting)
StreamExecutionEnvironment env = StreamExecutionEnvironment.getExecutionEnvironment();
env.enableCheckpointing(60_000); // barriers every 60s
env.getCheckpointConfig().setCheckpointingConsistencyMode(
    CheckpointingConsistencyMode.EXACTLY_ONCE);
env.getCheckpointConfig().setCheckpointStorage(
    new FileSystemCheckpointStorage("hdfs:///flink/checkpoints"));
env.setStateBackend(new EmbeddedRocksDBStateBackend());
```

On recovery, sources reset to the offsets recorded in the snapshot and operators restore state — deterministic replay from a consistent cut yields exactly-once *internal* state. The harder half is the sink. Flink's **two-phase commit sinks** (`TwoPhaseCommitSinkFunction`) implement the second phase of a distributed transaction against the checkpoint coordinator:

1. **Pre-commit** — on snapshot, the sink flushes its buffered records to a *pending* transaction (e.g., a Kafka idempotent producer transaction, a file-sink pending file).
2. **Commit** — only after the checkpoint is acknowledged durable by *all* operators does the coordinator instruct sinks to commit; on failure before commit, the pending transaction is aborted.

> **Theorem (Exactly-Once Decomposition):** A streaming pipeline provides end-to-end exactly-once semantics iff (a) state recovery restores a consistent cut of a deterministic dataflow, and (b) every sink commits its outputs atomically with the checkpoint that produced them.

Note the corollary that "exactly-once" is a misnomer for what is really *effectively-once*: records may be processed multiple times internally (at-least-once delivery from sources), but the observable effect — state plus sink contents — is indistinguishable from single processing. Sinks that cannot participate in two-phase commit (plain file appends, non-idempotent HTTP endpoints) degrade to at-least-once regardless of the engine's internal guarantees.

#### State Backends and the Cost of Exactly-Once

Incremental checkpointing (e.g., RocksDB's SSTable-diff uploads) reduces the per-checkpoint I/O to the *delta* since the last snapshot, but barrier alignment still introduces a latency tax: an operator cannot complete its snapshot until barriers arrive on all inputs, so a single straggling partition delays the global checkpoint. Unaligned checkpoints (Flink's optimization) trade a larger snapshot for eliminating alignment stalls — another point on the latency–cost surface.

### 4.4 Streaming SQL: Dynamic Tables, Changelogs, and Joins over Streams

Streaming SQL is the declarative projection of the Dataflow model. Flink SQL's foundational abstraction is the **dynamic table**: a continuously evolving relation. A stream and a dynamic table are interconvertible [4]:

- **Stream → table**: append stream ⇒ `INSERT` changelog; keyed updates ⇒ upsert/retract changelog.
- **Table → stream**: the table's changes are encoded as a changelog with row kinds `+I` (insert), `-U`/`+U` (update before/after), `-D` (delete) [4][5].

```sql
-- Flink SQL: event-time tumbling window over a dynamic table
CREATE TABLE bids (
  bidtime TIMESTAMP(3),
  price  DECIMAL(10, 2),
  item   STRING,
  WATERMARK FOR bidtime AS bidtime - INTERVAL '5' SECOND
) WITH ( 'connector' = 'kafka', ... );

SELECT
  item,
  TUMBLE_START(bidtime, INTERVAL '1' HOUR) AS window_start,
  MAX(price) AS max_price
FROM bids
GROUP BY item, TUMBLE(bidtime, INTERVAL '1' HOUR);
```

The `WATERMARK FOR` clause is the SQL surface of Section 4.2: it declares the completeness predicate (*bidtime − 5s*), which drives both window emission and state cleanup — windows whose end precedes the watermark are finalized and their state garbage-collected.

ksqlDB makes the same concepts available with Kafka-native syntax: `ROWTIME` exposes the record timestamp, `WINDOW TUMBLING (SIZE 1 HOUR, GRACE PERIOD 2 HOURS)` encodes allowed lateness as a *grace period* (default 24 hours), and records arriving after the grace period are classified *late* and excluded [3]. The vocabulary differs; the model is identical.

#### The Changelog Duality and Non-Determinism

Flink's recent SQL evolution makes the duality explicit: `FROM_CHANGELOG` converts an append-only stream carrying an operation column into a dynamic table, and `TO_CHANGELOG` materializes a dynamic table back into an append-only stream — the first SQL-level operator that converts retract/upsert streams into append form for archival and audit [6]. This matters because **retractions are the SQL encoding of late-data refinement**: a late arrival to an already-emitted window is expressed as `-U` (retract old aggregate) followed by `+U` (emit corrected aggregate) — precisely the accumulating-pane semantics of Section 4.1.

There is a deep correctness hazard here. Flink's determinism analysis [5] shows that once a pipeline carries update messages (`-U`/`+U`/`-D`), operators must maintain state keyed by an *update key* derived from the query; if the key cannot be derived (no primary key, non-deterministic functions, lookup joins on evolving sources), the pipeline suffers **non-deterministic update (NDU)** anomalies — computation errors that no amount of checkpointing can repair, because the *logic* is unsound. Exactly-once infrastructure guarantees faithful execution of the plan; it cannot salvage a plan whose update semantics are undefined [5].

#### Joins over Streams

Stream–stream joins are windowed by construction: the `WITHIN` clause in ksqlDB [3] and interval joins in Flink SQL bound the temporal neighborhood in which two events may match, which simultaneously bounds state. A stream–table join (Flink's *temporal join*) versions the table by event time, joining each stream record against the table version valid at the record's event timestamp — the principled answer to "which snapshot of the dimension table applies to this late event." Unbounded stream–stream equi-joins without temporal bounds are expressible but pathological: state grows without bound and watermarks cannot advance past the join — a direct corollary of the completeness undecidability noted in Section 2.3.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Latency–Correctness–Cost Theorem

We now state formally the tradeoff that the Dataflow model navigates. Fix a window *w* with true event set *E(w)* (unknowable at runtime) and observed event set *Ê(w, t)* at processing time *t*.

> **Theorem (Latency–Correctness–Cost):** Let *L* be the processing-time delay before the first pane of *w* is emitted, *C* the fraction of *E(w)* incorporated into the final emitted result, and *K* the total compute+state cost of the pipeline. Then no streaming algorithm can simultaneously achieve (i) *L* below the network's *p*-th percentile event delay, (ii) *C = 1*, and (iii) *K* bounded by a constant multiple of the batch cost — unless the event-delay distribution has bounded support known a priori.

*Proof sketch.* Correctness *C = 1* requires that the final pane incorporate every *e ∈ E(w)*, including the maximum-delay event *e\**. Any algorithm emitting its first pane at latency *L* less than *delay(e\*)* must therefore emit a preliminary pane and later revise it — incurring reprocessing cost proportional to the number of revisions, which is unbounded in the worst case over adversarial delay distributions. Conversely, emitting only after *delay(e\*)* is known forces *L* above the delay tail. Bounding *K* by batch cost forbids speculative recomputation entirely, forcing the single-emission strategy and its latency. The three desiderata are jointly satisfiable only when *delay(e\*)* is bounded and known, i.e., the degenerate case. ∎



### 5.2 Measurement Methodology



1. **Watermark-lag calibration.** Replay a trace with known event-time/arrival-time skew; measure the distribution of `watermark(t) − max event time observed`. This quantifies how conservative the watermark heuristic is — the direct driver of latency under `AfterWatermark` triggers [2].
2. **Revision-cost profiling.** With allowed lateness set to *d ∈ {0, 1h, 24h}*, measure (a) end-to-end latency of first panes, (b) fraction of windows revised, (c) total records reprocessed per late arrival. This traces the Pareto frontier of the theorem above.
3. **Checkpoint-tax measurement.** Under exactly-once checkpointing at intervals *τ ∈ {10s, 60s, 300s}*, measure steady-state 99th-percentile event latency and checkpoint duration; the alignment stall appears as periodic latency spikes correlated with barrier propagation, disappearing under unaligned checkpoints at the price of larger snapshot sizes.

## 6. Limitations

No model survives contact with production unscathed. We enumerate the principal limitations honestly.

1. **Watermarks are heuristics, not contracts.** Every watermark strategy is a bet on the event-delay distribution. Idle partitions stall the global watermark (the min-aggregation problem); Flink's idle-source detection and Dataflow's per-key holds are mitigations, not cures [2]. A single poisoned partition can hold an entire pipeline's event-time progress hostage.
2. **Exactly-once ends at the sink boundary.** The two-phase commit argument of Section 4.3 requires sink cooperation. The long tail of real sinks — REST webhooks, email gateways, legacy databases — cannot participate, and pipelines touching them are at-least-once by construction, whatever the engine promises.
3. **Session windows resist distribution.** Data-dependent windowing (sessions) requires global per-key ordering knowledge; merging sessions across partitions is inherently sequential per key, limiting parallelism for hot keys and complicating watermark propagation.
4. **SQL's update-key fragility.** As Flink's determinism analysis shows, streaming SQL plans with retractions are sound only when update keys are derivable; non-deterministic functions and evolving lookup joins silently corrupt results [5]. The declarative surface hides a sharp operational cliff.
5. **The cost model is qualitative.** The Dataflow paper [1] deliberately leaves cost as an engineering judgment. There is still no widely adopted cost-based optimizer for streaming comparable to System R's for batch — trigger and lateness selection remains manual, workload-specific tuning.
6. **State is the scalability ceiling.** Exactly-once stateful processing externalizes correctness into durable state; at millions of keys with multi-day lateness horizons, state size — not CPU — becomes the binding constraint, and state-backend performance (RocksDB compaction, incremental snapshots) dominates operational life.

## 7. Conclusion

The Dataflow model [1] endures because it identified the *right* decomposition. Event time versus processing time, watermarks as completeness predicates, triggers as a firing language, and panes with accumulation modes together convert an undecidable problem — "when is the data complete?" — into a tunable engineering surface. Barrier-aligned checkpointing and two-phase commit sinks then supply the failure-recovery half of the contract, and streaming SQL (Flink SQL's dynamic tables and changelogs [4][5][6], ksqlDB's ROWTIME windowing [3]) projects the whole edifice into declarative form.

Three research frontiers stand out. First, **cost-based streaming optimization**: automatically selecting triggers, lateness horizons, and checkpoint intervals from workload statistics and SLOs, closing the loop the 2015 paper left open. Second, **transactional streaming sinks as a commodity**: extending exactly-once past the sink boundary through standardized commit protocols rather than per-connector bespoke implementations. Third, **materialized-table abstractions** — Flink 1.20's materialized tables, which maintain a query result at a declared freshness without exposing streaming/batch mechanics [7] — suggest the ultimate trajectory: the streaming model becomes infrastructure, and users simply declare *what freshness they will pay for*.

The deepest lesson is methodological. Correctness, latency, and cost cannot all be maximized; they can only be *traded*, explicitly and measurably. The Dataflow model's achievement is making the trade visible — in watermarks you can monitor, triggers you can configure, and panes whose timing metadata tells downstream consumers exactly how much to trust each result. A decade on, that remains the standard against which every streaming system is judged.
