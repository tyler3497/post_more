# Quantum Error Mitigation in the NISQ Era: Zero-Noise Extrapolation, Probabilistic Error Cancellation, and Clifford Data Regression with Scalable Overhead Analysis

## Abstract

---

## 1 Introduction

The defining tension of the NISQ era, articulated in Preskill's 2018 framing of noisy intermediate-scale quantum computing, is that quantum processors of tens to hundreds of physical qubits now exist whose gate fidelities are tantalizingly close to — but not sufficient for — fault tolerance [8]. Near-term algorithms such as the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) [8], the quantum approximate optimization algorithm, and Trotterized Hamiltonian simulation do not require logical qubits; they require only *accurate expectation values* of observables measured on noisy states. Decoherence, control imperfections, readout errors, and crosstalk systematically bias these estimates, and the bias compounds with circuit depth.

**Quantum error mitigation** addresses this bias *without* the qubit overhead of error correction. Introduced in its modern form by Temme, Bravyi, and Gambetta in 2017 [1], mitigation accepts the physical noise as given and applies classical post-processing — extra circuit executions, randomized compilation, or learned correction maps — to recover unbiased (or reduced-bias) estimators. Three approaches dominate the literature and practice:

1. **Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE)** — deliberately amplify the noise by known scale factors $\lambda \in \{1, 3, 5, \dots\}$, measure the expectation value $E(\lambda)$ at each level, and extrapolate to the $\lambda \to 0$ limit [1].
2. **Probabilistic error cancellation (PEC)** — characterize the noise channel $\mathcal{E}$, decompose the ideal operation as a *quasiprobability* mixture of noisy implementable operations, and Monte-Carlo sample the mixture with sign-weighted outcomes, cancelling the noise in expectation [1].
3. **Clifford data regression (CDR)** — generate training data from near-Clifford circuits that are classically simulable (Gottesman–Knill) yet experience similar noise, fit a linear ansatz relating noisy and exact expectation values, and apply it to the circuit of interest [2].

These methods differ sharply in their assumptions (known vs. unknown noise model), resource overhead, and bias–variance trade-offs. This thesis develops each method from first principles, compares sampling overheads as functions of qubit count $n$ and depth $d$, situates virtual distillation and learning-based hybrids as neighboring techniques, and surveys experimental demonstrations on superconducting hardware up to the 127-qubit scale.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The NISQ noise landscape

A NISQ computation prepares a state $\rho_{\text{noisy}} = \mathcal{E}_d \circ \mathcal{U}_d \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal{E}_1 \circ \mathcal{U}_1(|0\rangle\langle 0|)$ and estimates $\langle O \rangle = \mathrm{Tr}(O\rho)$ by repeated measurement. Each layer's noise channel $\mathcal{E}_k$ may include:

- **Depolarizing and Pauli noise** — stochastic bit/phase flips; the dominant model after Pauli twirling, which converts arbitrary decoherent noise into Pauli channels without changing the computation [7].
- **Amplitude damping and dephasing** — $T_1$ relaxation and $T_2$ dephasing, coherent in the Lindblad picture.
- **Coherent (unitary) errors** — systematic over/under-rotations, which are not twirled away trivially and can interfere constructively.
- **Readout errors** — classical assignment errors, mitigated separately by confusion-matrix inversion (twirled readout mitigation).
- **Crosstalk and leakage** — correlated and non-Markovian effects, particularly severe in dense superconducting layouts.

The *bias* $|\langle O \rangle_{\text{noisy}} - \langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}}|$ typically grows monotonically with depth and error rate; mitigation reduces this bias at the price of *increased estimator variance*, and the sampling overhead needed to hold variance fixed is the currency of comparison.

> **Theorem (informal — fundamental overhead bound):** For generic Markovian noise of strength $p$ per layer over $d$ layers, any *unbiased* error-mitigation protocol requires a sampling overhead that scales as $\exp(\Omega(p d))$. Exponential cost in the total noise is unavoidable; protocols differ only in constants and in whether the exponential appears in samples, circuit variants, or classical computation.

This bound, established in its sharp form by Takagi et al. (2022), is the organizing principle of Section 4.4: every method below pays the exponential somewhere.

### 2.2 Mitigation vs. correction

Quantum error *correction* (QEC) encodes logical information redundantly and actively removes entropy, enabling arbitrarily long computations at large qubit overhead. Quantum error *mitigation* performs no encoding: it reduces the bias of expectation values for circuits in the $p \cdot d \lesssim 1$ regime [1]. Mitigation cannot extend coherence-limited computation indefinitely; it extends the *useful accuracy* of shallow circuits.

### 2.3 The estimator framework

All three methods estimate an ideal expectation value $\mu = \langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}}$ from noisy data. A mitigated estimator $\hat{\mu}$ is characterized by:

- **Bias:** $b = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}] - \mu$,
- **Variance:** $v = \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}) = \sigma_0^2 \cdot C$, where $C \geq 1$ is the *sampling overhead*,
- **Shot cost:** to achieve standard error $\epsilon$, one needs $N \approx v/\epsilon^2 = C \cdot N_0$ shots versus $N_0$ unmitigated.

Reducing bias at the price of overhead $C$ is the universal trade. ZNE pays in *circuit variants × extrapolation weights*, PEC pays $\gamma^2$ *per noisy gate*, and CDR pays in *training circuits plus regression variance*. Virtual distillation, discussed in Section 4.5, pays in *copies and coherent multi-copy measurements*.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Formal problem statement

Given a target unitary circuit $U$, an observable $O$, and a noisy implementation $\tilde{U}$ with $\tilde{\mathcal{U}} = \mathcal{E} \circ \mathcal{U}$ for noise channel $\mathcal{E}$, estimate $\mu = \langle 0|U^\dagger O U|0\rangle$ from experiments on $\tilde{U}$. We assume:

1. The noise is *gate-local* and approximately Markovian (non-Markovian corrections treated as model violation).
2. Shot noise is Gaussian in the large-sample limit; total error = bias + $O(1/\sqrt{N})$.
3. For PEC, $\mathcal{E}$ is *characterized* (learned via benchmarking); for ZNE and CDR, it need not be.

### 3.2 Evaluation protocol

We evaluate each method on three axes:

| Axis | Measure |
|---|---|
| **Bias reduction** | Residual bias after mitigation vs. raw noisy bias |
| **Sampling overhead** | Multiplicative shot cost $C$ to fix variance |
| **Model dependence** | Whether noise must be characterized a priori |

Additionally we track *qubit overhead* (extra qubits required), *classical cost* (simulation, fitting), and *robustness* to model mismatch. Experiments cited are drawn from superconducting platforms (IBM Quantum), where two-qubit gate errors $\sim 10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$ and readout errors $\sim 10^{-2}$ define the operating point.

### 3.3 Software notes

Reference implementations exist in the open-source ecosystem (Mitiq for ZNE with unitary folding; Qiskit Runtime estimator options; community CDR implementations following [2]); the Python snippets in Section 5 illustrate the core mathematics (folding, quasiprobability sampling, CDR fitting) in NumPy.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Zero-noise extrapolation: amplifying noise to defeat it

**Principle.** If the noisy expectation value $E(\lambda)$ is an analytic function of a continuous noise-strength parameter $\lambda$, then measuring $E(\lambda)$ at amplified noise levels $\lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < \cdots < \lambda_m$ (with $\lambda_1 = 1$ the native level) and extrapolating to $\lambda = 0$ recovers the ideal value — provided $E(\lambda)$ is well approximated by the chosen fit model over the probed range [1][7].

**Noise scaling.** Two mechanisms dominate:

- *Unitary (gate) folding:* replace each gate $U$ by $U \mapsto U(U^\dagger U)^n$, which is logically the identity insertion $U^\dagger U$ repeated $n$ times and yields an odd-integer scale factor $\lambda = 1 + 2n$ under the assumption that noise per gate is unchanged by the insertion [7]. Global folding folds the entire circuit; *local folding* folds gates selectively, enabling non-integer effective scale factors and finer $\lambda$ grids. He et al.'s identity-insertion variant achieves the same effect at the pulse-schedule level.
- *Pulse stretching:* rescale the duration of control pulses by $\lambda$ while rescaling amplitudes inversely, amplifying decoherence per gate (Strikis et al.). This requires pulse-level access and assumes time-translation-invariant noise, but avoids the gate-count inflation of folding.

Noise-aware folding [6] improves on uniform folding by allocating fold insertions proportionally to calibrated per-gate error rates, so that the *effective* noise amplification is uniform across heterogeneous qubit pairs — important on devices where two-qubit error rates vary by an order of magnitude across the chip.

**Extrapolation models.** Let measurements $E_k = E(\lambda_k)$ have variance $\sigma^2$. Common estimators:

1. *Richardson extrapolation:* fit the unique degree-$(m-1)$ polynomial through $m$ points and evaluate at zero:
$$\hat{E}(0) = \sum_{k=1}^{m} c_k E_k, \qquad c_k = \prod_{j \ne k} \frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_j - \lambda_k}.$$
> **Theorem (Richardson exactness):** If $E(\lambda)$ is a polynomial of degree at most $m-1$ in $\lambda$, Richardson extrapolation with $m$ points is exact: $\hat{E}(0) = E(0)$. For analytic $E(\lambda)$, the residual bias is $O(\lambda_{\max}^{m})$ but the variance inflates as $\sigma^2 \sum_k c_k^2$, where the Lagrange weights $|c_k|$ grow rapidly with $m$ — the classic bias–variance knife-edge.

2. *Exponential fit:* motivated by global depolarizing noise, $E(\lambda) \approx a + b\,e^{-c\lambda}$; nonlinear least squares is more stable than high-order Richardson at large $\lambda$ but introduces model bias if the true decay is not exponential.
3. *Linear/low-order polynomial:* safest against overfitting; used when only $\lambda \in \{1, 3\}$ are affordable.

**Overhead.** ZNE's cost is $m$ circuit variants (often $m = 3$–$5$) times deeper circuits (depth scaled by $\approx \lambda_{\max}$), plus the variance amplification $\sum c_k^2$. It requires *no* noise characterization and *no* extra qubits — its model-agnostic character is its chief virtue — but it fails when $E(\lambda)$ is non-analytic in $\lambda$ (e.g., noise-induced phase transitions in the observable) or when deep folding pushes circuits past the decoherence horizon.

### 4.2 Probabilistic error cancellation: inverting noise by quasiprobability

**Principle.** Suppose the noisy implementation of an ideal gate $\mathcal{U}$ is $\tilde{\mathcal{U}} = \mathcal{E} \circ \mathcal{U}$ with *known* noise channel $\mathcal{E}$. If the inverse channel admits a decomposition into implementable (noisy) operations,
$$\mathcal{E}^{-1} = \sum_i q_i \,\mathcal{B}_i, \qquad q_i \in \mathbb{R},$$
where the $\mathcal{B}_i$ are noisy basis operations (e.g., noisy gates composed with Pauli twirls), then the ideal expectation value can be estimated by sampling: draw $\mathcal{B}_i$ with probability $p_i = |q_i|/\gamma$, execute it, multiply the outcome by $\mathrm{sgn}(q_i)\,\gamma$, where $\gamma = \sum_i |q_i|$ is the *one-norm* of the quasiprobability distribution [1]. The estimator is unbiased because $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{sgn}(q_i)\gamma \cdot x_i] = \sum_i q_i \langle x \rangle_{\mathcal{B}_i} = \langle x \rangle_{\text{ideal}}$.

> **Theorem (PEC sampling overhead):** The variance of the PEC estimator is inflated by exactly $\gamma^2$ relative to direct sampling: $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\text{PEC}}) = \gamma^2 \,\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\text{direct}})$. For a circuit with $L$ independently mitigated noisy layers with one-norms $\gamma_k$, the total overhead is $\Gamma^2 = \left(\prod_{k=1}^{L} \gamma_k\right)^2 = \exp\!\left(2\sum_k \ln \gamma_k\right)$ — exponential in circuit depth.

Since $\gamma_k \approx 1 + c\,p_k$ for per-gate error $p_k$, the overhead scales as $\exp(O(p \cdot L))$, saturating the fundamental bound of Section 2.1. PEC is the canonical *unbiased* method, and its cost is the canonical exponential.

**Noise learning.** PEC's accuracy lives or dies on the characterization of $\mathcal{E}$. Modern practice uses *sparse Pauli–Lindblad models* learned via cycle benchmarking, keeping parameters linear in $n$; van den Berg et al. demonstrated this on 127-qubit superconducting hardware (2023), the largest-scale unbiased mitigation reported to date. **Trade-offs.** PEC is unbiased given a correct noise model and needs no extra qubits — but it pays exponential sampling overhead in depth and is acutely sensitive to model mismatch, since mischaracterized coherent errors are amplified rather than cancelled by the $\gamma$ weights.

### 4.3 Clifford data regression: learning the noise map from simulable circuits

**Principle.** CDR sidesteps noise characterization by *learning* how noise distorts expectation values, using training circuits that are (a) classically simulable and (b) structurally similar to the target circuit so they experience similar noise [2]. The Gottesman–Knill theorem guarantees that circuits composed of Clifford gates (Hadamard, phase, CNOT, Pauli) are simulable in polynomial time; a *near-Clifford* circuit — the target circuit with most non-Clifford rotations replaced by the nearest Clifford — is therefore exactly simulable while remaining noise-representative.

**Protocol** [2][5]:

1. From the target circuit $U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, generate $K$ training circuits $\{V_k\}$ by substituting all but a few non-Clifford gates with Clifford gates (randomized substitution keeps the ensemble diverse).
2. For each $V_k$, compute $X_k^{\text{exact}}$ classically and measure $X_k^{\text{noisy}}$ on the quantum device.
3. Fit the linear ansatz $X^{\text{exact}} \approx a_0 + a_1 X^{\text{noisy}}$ by least squares over $\{(X_k^{\text{noisy}}, X_k^{\text{exact}})\}$.
4. Measure $X_{\text{target}}^{\text{noisy}}$ and predict $X_{\text{mitigated}} = a_0 + a_1 X_{\text{target}}^{\text{noisy}}$.

Extensions generalize the ansatz: ridge-regularized multi-feature regression [5], energy-sampling CDR [3], and Clifford-perturbation hybrids (CPDR) merging CDR training with ZNE-style noise-level regression [4].

> **Theorem (Gottesman–Knill):** Any quantum circuit composed exclusively of Clifford gates, preparation of $|0\rangle$ states, and Pauli measurements can be simulated classically in time polynomial in the number of qubits and gates. This is the computational foundation on which CDR's exact training labels rest.

**Results.** Czarnik et al. [2] reported order-of-magnitude error reduction on a 16-qubit IBMQ device for a ground-state energy problem, plus validation on a 64-qubit noisy simulator — the strongest scalability evidence among learning-based methods at the time. CDR requires no noise model, adds no qubits, and its classical cost is polynomial. Its risks are *generalization error* (the linear ansatz is an approximation; training circuits are near-Clifford, not identical, to the target) and *training cost* ($K \sim 10$–$100$ extra circuit executions, each needing both quantum and classical evaluation).

### 4.4 Comparative overhead scaling analysis

The following table summarizes asymptotic resource scaling, where $n$ = qubits, $d$ = depth, $p$ = per-layer error rate, $m$ = ZNE noise levels, $K$ = CDR training circuits, $\gamma$ = PEC one-norm per layer.

| Method | Extra qubits | Sampling overhead | Noise model needed | Bias |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ZNE (Richardson, $m$ pts) | 0 | $m \times \sum_k c_k^2$ (poly in $m$) | No | $O(\lambda_{\max}^m)$ model bias |
| PEC | 0 | $\Gamma^2 = \prod_k \gamma_k^2 = e^{O(pd)}$ | Yes (learned) | Zero (if model exact) |
| CDR (linear ansatz) | 0 | $K$ training circuits + regression variance | No | Ansatz/generalization bias |
| Virtual distillation ($M$ copies) | $(M-1)n$ | $O(1)$ extra shots; needs coherent $M$-copy measurement | No | Exponentially suppressed in $M$ |

Three observations follow. *First*, PEC alone is provably unbiased but pays the fundamental exponential $\exp(O(pd))$ in shots — practical only when $pd \lesssim 1$–$2$. *Second*, ZNE and CDR convert the exponential into *model/ansatz bias*: cheap and polynomial, but approximate. *Third*, all methods share the feasibility frontier $pd = O(1)$; beyond it, the Takagi et al. bound leaves error correction as the only scalable path.

In practice the methods are *layered*: Pauli twirling + readout mitigation as a base, ZNE or CDR for the bulk bias, and PEC on the noisiest subcircuits where characterization is affordable. Hybrid CPDR-ZNE [4] exemplifies this trend, using Clifford-perturbation training data to fit noise-level regression coefficients with ridge regularization against overfitting.

### 4.5 Neighboring method: virtual distillation

*Virtual distillation* (Huggins et al., 2021) estimates $\langle O \rangle_{\text{VD}} = \mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M O)/\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)$ from $M$ copies of the noisy state; since $\rho^M/\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)$ concentrates on the dominant eigenvector, errors are suppressed exponentially in $M$ — costing $(M-1)n$ extra qubits and a coherent multi-copy measurement.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Worked mathematics: the three estimators

**ZNE (Richardson, three points).** For $\boldsymbol{\lambda} = (1, 3, 5)$ the Lagrange weights at zero are
$$c_1 = \frac{3 \cdot 5}{(3-1)(5-1)} = \tfrac{15}{8},\quad c_2 = \frac{1 \cdot 5}{(1-3)(5-3)} = -\tfrac{5}{4},\quad c_3 = \frac{1 \cdot 3}{(1-5)(3-5)} = \tfrac{3}{8},$$
so $\hat{E}(0) = \tfrac{15}{8}E_1 - \tfrac{5}{4}E_3 + \tfrac{3}{8}E_5$ with variance inflation $\sum c_k^2 = 225/64 + 25/16 + 9/64 \approx 5.2$. Five-fold variance for cubic-exact bias cancellation is the characteristic ZNE bargain.

**PEC (single-qubit depolarizing).** For $\mathcal{E}(\rho) = (1-p)\rho + p\,I/2$, the inverse has quasiprobability $\gamma = (1+p/2)/(1-p)$ over the basis $\{I, X, Y, Z\}$-twirled noisy gates; e.g., $p = 0.01$ gives $\gamma \approx 1.015$ per gate, and for $L = 100$ noisy gates $\Gamma^2 \approx e^{2 \cdot 100 \cdot 0.015} \approx 20$ — a twenty-fold shot overhead, growing exponentially with $L$.

**CDR (least squares).** With training pairs $(x_k, y_k) = (X_k^{\text{noisy}}, X_k^{\text{exact}})$, the normal equations give $a_1 = \mathrm{Cov}(x,y)/\mathrm{Var}(x)$, $a_0 = \bar{y} - a_1 \bar{x}$; the mitigated estimate inherits variance $\sigma^2(a_0 + a_1 x_t)$ from both shot noise and the finite training set, quantified by standard regression prediction intervals.

### 5.2 Reference implementations (illustrative)

```python
import numpy as np

def richardson_extrapolate(lambdas, values):
    """Richardson extrapolation of E(lambda) to lambda=0."""
    lambdas = np.asarray(lambdas, dtype=float)
    m = len(lambdas)
    coeffs = np.array([
        np.prod([lambdas[j] / (lambdas[j] - lambdas[k])
                 for j in range(m) if j != k])
        for k in range(m)
    ])
    est = float(coeffs @ np.asarray(values))
    var_inflation = float(np.sum(coeffs ** 2))
    return est, var_inflation

# Example: quadratic decay E(l) = 1 - 0.1*l - 0.02*l**2, measured with noise
lam = [1, 3, 5]
E = [1 - 0.1*l - 0.02*l**2 + np.random.normal(0, 0.005) for l in lam]
print(richardson_extrapolate(lam, E))  # -> (~1.0, ~5.2)
```

```python
def pec_sample(quasi_coeffs, basis_outcomes, rng):
    """One PEC shot: sample basis op ~ |q|/gamma, return sign*gamma*x."""
    q = np.asarray(quasi_coeffs)
    gamma = np.sum(np.abs(q))
    probs = np.abs(q) / gamma
    i = rng.choice(len(q), p=probs)
    return float(np.sign(q[i]) * gamma * basis_outcomes[i]), gamma

def cdr_fit(x_noisy, x_exact):
    """Linear CDR ansatz: x_exact ~ a0 + a1 * x_noisy (least squares)."""
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x_noisy), x_noisy])
    (a0, a1), *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, x_exact, rcond=None)
    return a0, a1
```

### 5.3 Experimental record on superconducting hardware

- **Foundations (2017).** Temme, Bravyi, and Gambetta [1] introduced ZNE and PEC on few-qubit superconducting devices, defining the short-depth regime $pd \lesssim 1$.
- **VQE (2017–2019).** Kandala et al. [8] demonstrated hardware-efficient VQE; follow-up work combined Richardson ZNE with PEC for Heisenberg-model dynamics on one platform.
- **CDR breakthrough (2021).** Czarnik et al. [2] achieved order-of-magnitude error reduction on a 16-qubit IBMQ device and validated scaling on a 64-qubit noisy simulator.
- **Hundred-qubit PEC (2023).** Van den Berg et al. applied PEC with sparse Pauli–Lindblad models to Ising dynamics on a 127-qubit Eagle processor.
- **Hundred-qubit ZNE (2023).** Kim et al. (*Nature* 2023) used ZNE with twirled readout mitigation on 127 qubits × 60 two-qubit layers.
- **Refinements (2024–2025).** Noise-aware folding [6], Clifford-perturbation hybrids [4], energy-sampling CDR [3], and surrogate-accelerated ZNE [7].

### 5.4 Proof sketch: exponential overhead is fundamental

*Claim.* Any protocol returning an unbiased estimate of $\langle O \rangle_{\text{ideal}}$ under per-layer depolarizing noise of strength $p$ over $d$ layers needs $\Omega(e^{cpd})$ samples for some constant $c > 0$.

*Sketch.* The total-variation distance between noisy and ideal output distributions decays as $e^{-\Theta(pd)}$; channel-discrimination lower bounds then force $\Omega(e^{\Theta(pd)})$ samples for any unbiased estimator. PEC saturates this scaling, proving tightness; ZNE and CDR evade it only by accepting bias. ∎

## 7 Conclusion

Zero-noise extrapolation, probabilistic error cancellation, and Clifford data regression form a complementary triad spanning the model-agnostic to model-exact spectrum of quantum error mitigation. ZNE asks nothing of the noise model and pays in extrapolation bias; PEC asks everything and pays the fundamental exponential in sampling; CDR learns the middle ground from classically simulable proxies and pays in ansatz and generalization risk. Unified by the $\exp(\Omega(pd))$ lower bound on unbiased mitigation, their practical message is consistent: mitigation multiplies the useful depth of NISQ processors by valuable constant factors — demonstrated from 16 to 127 qubits on superconducting hardware — while the asymptotic road to scalable quantum computation still runs through error correction.

The active frontier is hybridization and automation: noise-aware folding [6], Clifford-perturbation training sets [4], energy-guided CDR sampling [3], and surrogate-accelerated ZNE [7] all point toward mitigation stacks that adapt to calibrated device data with minimal tuning. As devices cross into $pd \sim 1$ at hundreds of qubits, the question is *which combination* delivers the most accuracy per shot — and the accounting framework developed here answers it.
