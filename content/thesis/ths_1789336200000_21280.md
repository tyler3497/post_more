# Quantum Error Mitigation: Zero-Noise Extrapolation, Probabilistic Error Cancellation, and the Scaling Limits of Near-Term Quantum Devices

## Abstract

Quantum error mitigation has emerged as the principal strategy for extracting reliable expectation values from noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) processors without the prohibitive overhead of full fault tolerance. This thesis develops a unified treatment of the two dominant mitigation families: zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE), which reconstructs the zero-noise limit of an observable from measurements taken at amplified noise levels, and probabilistic error cancellation (PEC), which inverts the action of noise channels through quasi-probability decompositions into physically implementable operations. We analyze noise-scaling protocols including unitary folding and parameterized pulse stretching, extrapolation models ranging from Richardson and polynomial fits to physically bounded statistical inference, and the sampling-overhead scaling that governs PEC through the negativity of its decompositions. Drawing on theoretical lower bounds and large-scale hardware benchmarks, we argue that mitigation cost grows exponentially with circuit fault rate, establishing a finite window of applicability for NISQ devices bounded on one side by extrapolation bias and on the other by the quasi-probability sampling wall.

## 1 Introduction

The noisy intermediate-scale quantum era is defined by a paradox: processors with fifty to a thousand physical qubits exist, yet none can execute the deep, error-corrected circuits required for asymptotically guaranteed quantum advantage. Full fault tolerance demands physical-to-logical overheads of three to four orders of magnitude, placing it beyond the reach of present hardware. Between raw noisy execution and fault tolerance lies a third path — *quantum error mitigation* — a collection of classical post-processing and circuit-resampling techniques that trade additional measurement shots for reduced bias in expectation values, without requiring a single extra qubit [1].

Two ideas anchor the field. The first, *zero-noise extrapolation* (ZNE), deliberately amplifies the noise in a computation, measures how the expectation value of an observable degrades as a function of a noise scale factor, and extrapolates the fitted curve back to the zero-noise limit. The second, *probabilistic error cancellation* (PEC), characterizes the noise afflicting each gate, decomposes the mathematical inverse of that noise into a signed linear combination of implementable operations, and Monte Carlo samples from this quasi-probability distribution so that the averaged result is, in expectation, the noise-free one. Both were introduced in a single foundational paper by Temme, Bravyi, and Gambetta [1], and both remain the workhorses of the field nearly a decade later.

This thesis presents a self-contained, mathematically precise account of these two families, the engineering that makes them practical on real hardware, and — most importantly — the fundamental scaling limits that bound what any mitigation protocol can achieve.

## 2 Background

A noisy quantum computation is modeled as a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map acting on density operators. In the Markovian regime, the evolution of the system state is governed by the Lindblad master equation, and the cumulative effect of noise on a gate can be summarized by an effective noise channel N that composes with the ideal unitary channel U to give the realized operation E = N ∘ U. For near-term superconducting and trapped-ion devices, the dominant noise is well approximated by *Pauli channels* — mixtures of Pauli errors — after the application of randomized compiling or Pauli twirling, which converts coherent errors into stochastic ones at the cost of modest circuit overhead.

The object of interest in nearly all NISQ applications — variational eigensolvers, quantum machine learning, digital quantum simulation — is an *expectation value* tr(Oρ) of some observable O with respect to the prepared state ρ. Noise introduces two distinct corruptions: *shot noise*, the finite-sample statistical fluctuation that decays as one over the square root of the number of shots, and *bias*, the systematic shift of the mean away from the ideal value. Error mitigation targets bias; it cannot reduce shot noise below the standard quantum limit, and indeed every mitigation protocol *amplifies* the variance of the estimator as the price of bias reduction. This bias–variance trade-off is the central tension of the field.

The depolarizing channel provides the canonical illustration: a single-qubit depolarizing channel with error probability p maps ρ to (1 − p)ρ + p I/2, so that after L noisy gates the expectation value of a traceless observable decays roughly as (1 − p)^L. The following table situates ZNE and PEC among the broader mitigation zoo:

| Method | Extra qubits | Noise model needed | Sampling overhead | Bias |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zero-noise extrapolation | none | not required | polynomial in depth, exponential in extrapolation order | residual, model-dependent |
| Probabilistic error cancellation | none | yes, learned | exponential in circuit volume | zero (unbiased) |
| Clifford data regression | none | not required | training-set dependent | learned, approximate |
| Symmetry verification | none | not required | post-selection discard | partial |
| Virtual distillation | 2× qubits | not required | exponential in copy number | suppressed, not removed |

Unlike quantum error *correction*, which suppresses logical errors asymptotically as code distance grows, mitigation offers no such asymptotic guarantee. Its cost — measured in circuit executions — must therefore be tracked with precision, because the exponential walls arrive sooner than intuition suggests [1].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The zero-noise extrapolation pipeline

ZNE proceeds in three stages. First, *noise scaling*: the circuit is transformed into a family parameterized by a noise scale factor λ ≥ 1, with λ = 1 reproducing the original circuit and larger λ amplifying the effective noise without changing the ideal unitary. Second, *execution*: each scaled circuit is run for a fixed shot budget, yielding noisy values E(λ). Third, *extrapolation*: a parametric model is fitted to the data and evaluated at λ = 0. The method needs no noise characterization and no extra qubits; its burden lies in faithful noise scaling and a trustworthy extrapolation model [2].

### 3.2 The probabilistic error cancellation pipeline

PEC proceeds in four stages. First, *noise learning*: the noise channel of each gate (or gate layer) is characterized, increasingly via sparse *Pauli–Lindblad* models whose parameters are learned from dedicated calibration experiments with cost polynomial in the number of qubits. Second, *quasi-probability decomposition*: the inverse noise channel N⁻¹ is expanded as a real linear combination of implementable operations, N⁻¹ = Σᵢ qᵢ Bᵢ, where the coefficients qᵢ may be negative. Third, *sampling*: circuits are drawn by sampling operations Bᵢ with probability |qᵢ|/γ, where γ = Σᵢ |qᵢ| is the *negativity*, and each sampled circuit is executed. Fourth, *sign-weighted averaging*: each measurement outcome is multiplied by γ·sign(qᵢ), producing an unbiased estimator of the ideal expectation value at the cost of variance amplified by γ² per gate [1].

### 3.3 Figures of merit

Every protocol is judged on three quantities: the *bias* δ = |⟨O⟩_mitigated − ⟨O⟩_ideal|, the *variance* of the mitigated estimator, and the *sampling overhead* η — the multiplicative factor by which the shot budget must grow to reach a target precision relative to unmitigated estimation. Best-practice deployments compose mitigation with readout-error mitigation and Pauli twirling, and allocate shots across noise scale factors by importance sampling rather than uniformly [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Richardson extrapolation and the zero-noise limit

The theoretical foundation of ZNE is the perturbative expansion of the noisy expectation value in the noise strength. When the noise parameter r (for instance, the per-gate error rate) is small and the circuit depth fixed, the expectation value admits a Taylor expansion

E(r) = E* + a₁r + a₂r² + … + aₖrᵏ + O(rᵏ⁺¹),

where E* is the ideal zero-noise value. Given measurements at scaled noise strengths r, c₂r, …, cₘr, one can solve for the linear combination of the data points that cancels the first k powers of r. This is *Richardson extrapolation*, and the combination weights β are the solution of a Vandermonde system:

> Theorem: For distinct scale factors c₁ … cₘ with m ≥ k+1, there exist unique coefficients βⱼ such that Σⱼ βⱼ E(cⱼr) = E* + O(rᵏ⁺¹). The coefficients satisfy Σⱼ βⱼ cⱼᵖ = 0 for p = 1 … k and Σⱼ βⱼ = 1, and they grow in magnitude combinatorially with the extrapolation order.

The growth of the β coefficients is the source of ZNE's variance amplification. If each E(cⱼr) is estimated with variance σ², the extrapolated estimator has variance σ²Σⱼβⱼ², and Σⱼβⱼ² grows rapidly — often by orders of magnitude — as the order increases or the scale factors cluster near one. Richardson extrapolation is therefore a *bias–variance exchange*: higher order kills more bias terms but demands exponentially more shots for the same precision. The following Python routine computes the weights directly:

```python
import numpy as np

def richardson_weights(scale_factors, order):
    """Solve the Vandermonde system for Richardson coefficients."""
    V = np.vander(scale_factors, N=order + 1, increasing=True).T
    rhs = [1.0] + [0.0] * order
    return np.linalg.solve(V, rhs)

scales = [1.0, 2.0, 3.0]
beta = richardson_weights(scales, order=2)
print("Richardson weights:", beta)
```

In practice, exact Richardson extrapolation is frequently replaced by fitting parametric models — linear, polynomial, exponential, or poly-exponential in the scale factor — which trade the rigorous order-by-order cancellation for statistical stability when data are noisy [2].

### 4.2 Digital noise scaling: unitary folding and parameterized stretching

A noise-scaling protocol must amplify noise by a known factor while leaving the ideal unitary invariant. The most widely adopted *digital* approach is *unitary folding*. Given a circuit implementing U, one replaces it with U (U†U)ⁿ, which is logically identical to U but executes the noisy gates 2n+1 times as often, yielding an effective noise scale factor of λ = 2n+1 under the assumption that the noise per gate is approximately constant. Folding can be applied *globally* (the entire circuit is mirrored) or *locally* (individual gates or layers are folded), and *partial folding* — folding only a fraction of the gates — achieves fractional scale factors between 1 and 3, enabling the small-noise-baseline measurements that stabilize extrapolation [2].

An alternative is *parameterized noise scaling* (also called pulse stretching): the physical control pulses implementing each gate are stretched in time, increasing exposure to decoherence and relaxation by a calibrated factor. This scales the noise more faithfully at the physics level but requires pulse-level access that cloud platforms do not always expose. Benchmarks of digital folding report error reductions of 18× to 24× over unmitigated circuits, demonstrating ZNE's effectiveness at qubit counts well beyond early demonstrations [2].

A subtle failure mode is *noise saturation*: at large scale factors the expectation value flattens toward its fully mixed value, and any extrapolation model fitted through the saturated regime will misestimate the intercept. Best-practice studies on 104-qubit chains show that for scale factors 1, 3 and 5, exponential models outperform polynomials at large depth, whereas for tightly spaced factors near unity only linear extrapolation remains stable — a direct manifestation of the bias–variance trade-off [3].

### 4.3 Probabilistic error cancellation and quasi-probability representations

PEC is best understood as *error inversion by randomized compilation*. Suppose each noisy gate implements E = N ∘ U with known N. If the inverse channel N⁻¹ can be written as Σᵢ qᵢ Bᵢ with each Bᵢ a physical operation (typically a Pauli channel composed with the ideal gate), then sampling Bᵢ with probability proportional to |qᵢ| and weighting outcomes by γ·sign(qᵢ) yields an estimator whose mean is exactly the ideal expectation value. The proof is one line: linearity of expectation carries the signed sum through the measurement, and the sampled distribution reproduces the quasi-probability up to the normalization γ [1].

The cost is governed by the *negativity* γ = Σᵢ|qᵢ| ≥ 1, with equality only for the identity channel. For a circuit of L noisy gates with per-gate negativity γ, the variance of the PEC estimator is amplified by γ²ᴸ relative to ideal sampling — an exponential in the circuit volume. For weak depolarizing noise with per-gate error p, γ ≈ 1 + 2p, so the overhead scales as (1 + 2p)²ᴸ ≈ exp(4pL): the sampling wall is exponential in the *fault rate* pL, the expected number of errors in the circuit. This single formula explains both the power and the doom of PEC: it is exactly unbiased, but its cost explodes as circuits grow.

Modern PEC implementations learn the noise via *sparse Pauli–Lindblad models*, whose polynomially many rates are fit from calibration experiments, making PEC practical at tens of qubits where per-gate negativities stay near unity. The Haskell sketch below captures the algebraic essence:

```haskell
module QEM where

-- A quasi-probability decomposition: list of (operation, signed weight)
type QPD op = [(op, Double)]

negativity :: QPD op -> Double
negativity qpd = sum (map (abs . snd) qpd)

-- Per-layer variance amplification of the PEC estimator
layerOverhead :: QPD op -> Double
layerOverhead qpd = negativity qpd ^ (2 :: Int)

-- Sampling probabilities are the normalized absolute weights
sampleProbs :: QPD op -> [Double]
sampleProbs qpd = map ((/ negativity qpd) . abs . snd) qpd
```

And a Rust implementation of the Richardson estimator used alongside PEC-calibrated ZNE baselines:

```rust
/// Richardson extrapolation of expectation values at scaled noise levels.
pub fn richardson_estimate(values: &[f64], scales: &[f64]) -> f64 {
    let n = scales.len();
    let mut weights = vec![0.0; n];
    for (i, wi) in weights.iter_mut().enumerate() {
        let mut num = 1.0;
        let mut den = 1.0;
        for (j, sj) in scales.iter().enumerate() {
            if i != j {
                num *= 0.0 - sj;
                den *= scales[i] - sj;
            }
        }
        *wi = num / den;
    }
    values.iter().zip(weights.iter()).map(|(v, w)| v * w).sum()
}
```

### 4.4 Extrapolation models: from polynomials to physically bounded inference

The choice of extrapolation model Ê(λ) is where ZNE meets statistical inference. Polynomial models inherit Richardson's order-by-order guarantees but suffer Runge-like oscillations when data are noisy. Exponential models Ê(λ) = a + b·exp(−cλ) encode the physical intuition that expectation values decay toward a saturation value, and empirically dominate at large circuit depths where noise is strong [3]. Poly-exponential hybrids add flexibility at the cost of identifiability.

Two modern refinements deserve emphasis. First, *adaptive statistical inference* treats extrapolation as Bayesian model selection: candidate models are scored by their evidence given the data, and the zero-noise estimate is marginalized over models, regularizing the notorious instability of high-order fits [2]. Second, *physically bounded extrapolation* explicitly constrains the fitted intercept to the valid range of the observable — for example, a Pauli expectation value must lie in the closed interval from −1 to 1. Unbounded fits routinely return unphysical values when data are noisy; bounded variants substantially reduce such pathological predictions and stabilize exponential-family models across a synthetic benchmark of 180,000 circuits and 3.6 million ZNE experiments [4].

A further innovation constructs ZNE datasets from *mixed physical and logical data*: a few error-corrected points anchor the low-noise end of the fit while cheap physical points extend the baseline, cutting the estimator's variance by orders of magnitude of physical runtime in studied regimes [5].

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

The empirical record for ZNE is extensive. Digital-folding benchmarks demonstrate 18× to 24× error reductions over unmitigated circuits, with ZNE effective at larger qubit numbers than prior studies had tested [2]. On a 104-qubit chain in a 127-qubit superconducting processor, composite mitigation (readout correction, twirling, ZNE) tracks classical benchmarks out to 70 two-qubit gate depths, with exponential extrapolation winning at large depth and linear extrapolation the only stable choice for tightly spaced scale factors [3]. Physically bounded inference was validated on 180,000 synthetic circuits under device-derived noise models plus hardware GHZ and W-state runs, eliminating the unphysical-intercept pathology of naive fits [4]. Mixed physical/logical datasets on a six-spin transverse-field Ising simulation reduced estimator variance by orders of magnitude versus physical-only extrapolation [5], and mitigation has been demonstrated on *logical* qubits themselves [6].

On the theoretical side, two results are central. First, the *unbiasedness of PEC* follows directly from the quasi-probability construction, as sketched in Section 4.3. Second, the *exponential lower bound* on mitigation cost:

> Theorem: Consider any error-mitigation protocol that, given black-box access to a noisy circuit of volume V with per-gate error rate p, outputs an estimate of an observable's ideal expectation value to additive error ε with probability at least two thirds. Then the protocol must consume a number of circuit executions scaling as exp(Ω(pV))/ε² in the worst case.

*Proof sketch.* The noisy output distribution at fault rate pV is exponentially close in total variation distance to the output of a circuit in which a constant fraction of gates are replaced by depolarizing noise; distinguishing the ideal expectation value from the fully mixed value therefore requires resolving a signal attenuated by roughly (1−p)^V. Any estimator — mitigated or not — needs its statistical uncertainty below the signal size, and standard information-theoretic lower bounds (via the data-processing inequality applied to the hypothesis test between the ideal and noisy channels) force the sample complexity to grow as the inverse square of the attenuated signal, i.e., exponentially in pV. Mitigation reshapes the constants but cannot defeat the exponential, because it operates only on measurement data from the same noisy device. ∎

The practical reading of this theorem is the *mitigation window*: there exists a range of circuit volumes — roughly, fault rates pV of order unity to a few — where mitigation converts useless hardware into scientifically meaningful results, as demonstrated in the benchmarks above. Below this window mitigation is unnecessary; above it, the sampling overhead exceeds any plausible shot budget and only fault tolerance helps. The original Temme–Bravyi–Gambetta analysis already contained this warning in embryonic form: the applicable circuit size is limited by the rate at which errors are introduced [1].

## 6 Limitations

The limitations of error mitigation are not engineering details to be optimized away; several are structural. **(1) The exponential wall.** Both ZNE's variance amplification and PEC's γ²ᴸ overhead grow exponentially with the fault rate, so mitigation extends a noisy processor's reach by only a constant factor in circuit volume [1]. **(2) Noise drift.** PEC's unbiasedness assumes the learned model matches the noise at execution time; real devices drift on hourly timescales, and stale models reintroduce bias. **(3) Learning cost.** Characterizing noise to PEC's required precision needs dedicated calibration circuits that must be repeated as the device drifts. **(4) Coherent and non-Markovian noise.** The Pauli-channel assumption is enforced by twirling, which costs depth; crosstalk and leakage violate the model and are poorly mitigated. **(5) Extrapolation bias.** ZNE is only as good as its model: saturated data or wrong functional forms produce confident but wrong intercepts, only partially tamed by bounded inference [4]. **(6) Composability.** Stacking ZNE with PEC, twirling, and readout mitigation multiplies overheads and risks double-counting error sources. **(7) No asymptotic suppression.** Unlike error correction, mitigation cannot drive logical error rates arbitrarily low; it is a constant-factor technology inside a finite window [1].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Zero-noise extrapolation and probabilistic error cancellation remain the twin pillars of quantum error mitigation: the former needing no noise characterization and paying in extrapolation bias, the latter delivering unbiased estimates and paying in exponential sampling overhead. A decade of development has turned both into engineered pipelines — digital unitary folding, parameterized scaling, adaptive and physically bounded inference, sparse Pauli–Lindblad learning, hybrid physical/logical datasets — validated on hundred-qubit hardware [2][3][4][5][6]. Yet the picture is unchanged since 2017: mitigation cost scales exponentially with the circuit fault rate, defining a finite window of applicability rather than a path to arbitrary accuracy [1].

The research frontier therefore lies at the boundaries of that window. Tighter noise learning reduces PEC's negativity; better inference shrinks ZNE's bias; hybrid correction-mitigation schemes push the window's upper edge outward by anchoring fits with logical data [5][6]. But the deepest lesson is architectural: error mitigation is what makes NISQ devices scientifically useful *today*, while fault tolerance is what will make them scalable *tomorrow*. Understanding exactly where mitigation ends and correction must begin — quantitatively, in shots and in fault rates — is among the most practically important questions in quantum computing.
