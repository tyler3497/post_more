# Direct Air Capture and Carbon Mineralization: Sorbents, Separation Thermodynamics, and the Techno-Economics of Negative-Emission Systems

## Abstract

Direct air capture (DAC) coupled to permanent carbon mineralization is among the few routes to verifiably *net-negative* CO2 removal, yet it is bounded by a fundamental thermodynamic floor and by the practical chemistry of sorbents. This thesis derives the minimum separation work for extracting CO2 at ~420 ppm, surveys four sorbent families — aqueous hydroxides, amine-functionalized solids, moisture-swing resins, and metal-organic frameworks — and benchmarks the leading industrial embodiments of Carbon Engineering and Climeworks against published energy and cost data. It then analyzes in-situ basalt mineralization (CarbFix) and ex-situ carbonation pathways, where dissolved CO2 converts to carbonate minerals on timescales of months rather than the millennia once assumed. Techno-economic evidence places near-term DAC costs between $100 and $600 per tonne of CO2, with learning-curve projections toward $100 per tonne contingent on gigatonne-scale deployment, and shows that net negativity is strictly conditional on low-carbon energy and honest life-cycle accounting.

## 1. Introduction

The Sixth Assessment Report of the IPCC establishes that reaching net-zero greenhouse-gas emissions *requires* carbon dioxide removal (CDR): residual emissions from hard-to-abate sectors must be counterbalanced, and net-negative emissions must follow thereafter [3]. In modelled 1.5 °C pathways, cumulative direct air carbon capture and storage (DACCS) reaches order tens of gigatonnes of CO2 by 2100 — small beside bioenergy with CCS (328 GtCO2) and land-based removal (252 GtCO2), but indispensable precisely where land, water, and biomass constraints bind [2][3]. Direct air capture is technologically attractive because it is *location-independent*: unlike point-source capture, a DAC plant can be sited adjacent to cheap clean energy and to geological storage, decoupling the geography of capture from the geography of emissions.

This thesis addresses two coupled questions. First, what does fundamental thermodynamics demand of any DAC process, and how do existing sorbent chemistries and process architectures approach — or squander — that limit? Second, once captured, how can CO2 be converted into a form that is permanent on geological timescales, and at what cost? The first question is answered through the minimum-work theorem of separation and a comparative analysis of four sorbent classes. The second is answered through the geochemistry of mineral carbonation, where the CarbFix project has demonstrated that CO2 dissolved in water and injected into basaltic rock mineralizes within two years rather than the centuries predicted by laboratory kinetics [7]. Together, capture and mineralization define the only currently demonstrated end-to-end chain from dilute atmospheric CO2 to solid rock.

## 2. Background

Ambient air in 2026 carries roughly 424 ppm of CO2 — a mole fraction of 4.2 × 10⁻⁴. The diluteness of the target species is the entire engineering problem: separating CO2 from air means undoing the entropy of mixing of an extremely dilute mixture, and the thermodynamic minimum work scales as −ln(x), punishing low concentrations logarithmically [8]. Early concepts date to the mid-twentieth century, but serious engineering analysis began with Klaus Lackner's moisture-swing work and the American Physical Society's 2011 technology assessment, which estimated capture costs near $600 per tonne of CO2. The landmark 2018 *Joule* paper by Keith, Holmes, St. Angelo, and Heidel upended that consensus: grounded in a 1 MtCO2/yr design, a Squamish pilot plant operating since 2015, and vendor quotes for every major unit operation, it reported a levelized cost of $94–232 per tonne of CO2 delivered at 15 MPa [1]. On the solid-sorbent side, Climeworks — founded in 2009 as an ETH Zürich spinout — commissioned a 900 t/yr commercial plant in 2017, the 4 kt/yr Orca plant in 2021, and the 36 kt/yr Mammoth plant in 2024, each generation roughly an order of magnitude larger than the last [10]. In parallel, the CarbFix pilot (2012) injected 220 tonnes of CO2 into Icelandic basalt and observed >95% mineralization within two years, collapsing the assumed timescale of geological carbonation by orders of magnitude [7]. Policy has begun to follow: the U.S. Department of Energy has set a target of $100–200 per tonne within a decade, and the first DOE-funded DAC hubs are now under construction [12].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis proceeds by five analytical moves. **(i) Thermodynamic analysis.** The minimum work of separation is derived from the Gibbs free energy of mixing for an ideal-gas mixture, evaluated for isothermal, isobaric, reversible extraction of CO2 at ambient mole fraction; second-law efficiencies of real processes are then computed against this floor. **(ii) Process energy accounting.** Thermal and electrical energy inputs are tracked separately and converted to a common exergy basis, including the compression work to pipeline pressure (15 MPa), so that aqueous and solid-sorbent routes can be compared fairly. **(iii) Sorbent benchmarking.** Each material class is scored on equilibrium working capacity (mmol g⁻¹ at 0.4 mbar CO2), cycle time, heat of adsorption, and degradation modes under realistic humidity and temperature cycling. **(iv) Techno-economic assessment.** Published levelized costs are compared on a consistent boundary — capture only, excluding transport and storage unless stated — distinguishing first-of-a-kind from Nth-of-a-kind estimates. **(v) Life-cycle analysis.** A cradle-to-grave boundary is adopted, and a formal net-negativity condition is derived: a DACCS system removes carbon only if the emissions embodied in its energy and materials are strictly less than the CO2 it sequesters. This condition, trivial to state and routinely violated in press releases, disciplines the entire discussion.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Aqueous Hydroxide Absorption: The Caustic–Calcination Loop

Carbon Engineering's process adapts the Kraft caustic-recovery cycle of the pulp industry to atmospheric capture. Ambient air is drawn through a cross-flow contactor where potassium hydroxide absorbs CO2:

$$\mathrm{2KOH + CO_2 \rightarrow K_2CO_3 + H_2O}$$

The carbonate solution is reacted with calcium hydroxide in a pellet reactor (causticization), precipitating calcium carbonate and regenerating KOH; the CaCO3 is calcined at ~900 °C to release a pure CO2 stream and CaO, which is slaked back to Ca(OH)2 [1]. The design's reported energy demand is 8.81 GJ of natural gas per tonne of CO2 in the all-thermal configuration, or 5.25 GJ of gas plus 366 kWh of electricity (≈6.6 GJ total) when the air-separation unit is electrified [1]. Delivered costs of $94–232 per tonne reflect mature, vendor-quoted unit operations rather than laboratory extrapolations [1].

The architecture's strengths are its technological readiness and continuous operation. Its penalties are threefold: the calciner demands *high-grade* heat at 900 °C, which today implies oxy-fired natural gas; the contactor loses water by evaporation, a material operating cost in arid regions; and moving ~1,600 tonnes of air per tonne of CO2 requires contactors of striking physical scale. Against the thermodynamic floor of ~0.44 GJ/t, the process operates at roughly 6–7% second-law efficiency — typical of real separations, and a measure of how far chemistry and heat integration still have to run [8].

### 4.2 Amine-Functionalized Solid Sorbents and TVSA Cycles

Solid-sorbent DAC replaces the liquid loop with porous supports — historically cellulose-based, increasingly structured monoliths — grafted with primary or secondary amines that chemisorb CO2 as carbamates (2:1 amine:CO2 stoichiometry) or, for tertiary amines and in humidity, as bicarbonates (1:1). Climeworks' collectors adsorb for roughly two hours at ambient conditions, then seal and undergo temperature–vacuum swing adsorption (TVSA): heating to ~100 °C under vacuum desorbs a concentrated CO2 stream [10]. Because desorption needs only *low-grade* heat, the process can run on waste heat or geothermal steam.

Published energy figures have improved with each generation: early reports cited ~3 MWh per tonne, while recent assessments give 6.5 GJ of heat plus 1.1–1.8 GJ of electricity per tonne of CO2 [12]. Costs were $500–600 per tonne in 2019 [11]; the third-generation "structured sorbent" is claimed to double capture capacity at half the energy input while tripling sorbent lifetime — the three levers (capacity, energy, durability) that dominate levelized cost [9]. Scale has grown from Orca (4 kt/yr) to Mammoth (36 kt/yr, 72 collector units, geothermal-powered, CO2 mineralized via Carbfix) toward the DOE-backed Project Cypress hub in Louisiana, targeting 250 kt/yr initially and 1 Mt/yr by decade's end [10]. The principal degradation modes — oxidative amine loss and urea formation under dry, hot cycling — remain the central materials challenge, and steam-assisted desorption trades faster kinetics against amine leaching and water consumption.

### 4.3 Moisture-Swing Sorbents

Lackner's moisture-swing concept exploits a different thermodynamic handle: quaternary-ammonium anionic exchange resins that absorb CO2 when *dry* and release it when *wet* [4]. In the dry state the resin-bound hydroxide/carbonate equilibrium favors bicarbonate formation and CO2 uptake; hydration shifts the equilibrium, liberating CO2 at elevated partial pressure without any thermal input. The 2009 analysis showed that the free-energy cost of the swing can in principle approach the evaporation work of the water involved — small in arid, sunny climates where drying is effectively free [4].

Subsequent work on ion confinement in nanopores revealed that restricting the hydration shell around resin-bound ions can shift the carbonate/bicarbonate equilibrium still further, lowering the regeneration penalty [4]. Hydroxide-functionalized ion-exchange membranes developed by Wang and Lackner extend the concept toward continuous, membrane-like operation [5]. The appeal is a cycle driven by ambient humidity gradients rather than heat or vacuum. The penalties are slow kinetics, modest working capacities relative to amines, and an irony of geography: the process performs best in hot, dry climates where the water it consumes is scarcest. It remains at pilot scale, but as a low-exergy alternative it sets a useful bound on how little energy a DAC cycle can, in principle, consume.

### 4.4 Metal-Organic Frameworks and Next-Generation Adsorbents

Metal-organic frameworks offer what amorphous sorbents cannot: crystalline, atomically defined binding sites whose CO2 affinity can be tuned by design. The Chemical Science review of chemisorptive capture highlights hydroxide-functionalized frameworks such as Zn(ZnOH)4(bibta)3 and Zn5(OH)4(btdd)3, where metal-bound bicarbonates — validated by in-situ IR and DFT — give heats of adsorption of −71 and −81 kJ mol⁻¹ respectively, producing steep uptake at the 0.4 mbar partial pressure of ambient air (0.9 mmol g⁻¹ at 25 °C and 23 mbar for the latter) [5]. Systematic variation of the metal identity provides a handle for tuning chemisorption thermodynamics, and amine-appended frameworks from the Long laboratory (commercialized via Mosaic Materials) bring cooperative CO2 insertion mechanisms with large working capacities to both flue-gas and direct-air duties [5]. Parallel work on zinc-hydride-decorated pores extends chemisorption to elevated temperatures where amines fail [9].

The promise is a sorbent engineered *for* 420 ppm rather than adapted from flue-gas service: steep low-pressure isotherms, high selectivity over N2 and O2, and potentially lower regeneration temperatures than aqueous amines. The obstacles are durability and cost — hydrolytic stability under thousands of humid cycles, competitive water adsorption that poisons capacity, linker synthesis cost, and the pressure-drop and attrition penalties of pelletizing powders into contactor structures. No MOF-based DAC plant operates at commercial scale today; the class remains the most promising and least proven of the four.

### 4.5 In-Situ and Ex-Situ Carbon Mineralization

Capture is half the problem; the other half is permanence. The CarbFix method dissolves CO2 in water *before* injection, so the subsurface fluid is a carbonic-acid solution rather than a buoyant supercritical plume — eliminating the leakage pathways that haunt conventional storage [7]. In basaltic rock at 400–800 m depth, acidity dissolves the host rock, liberating Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, and Fe²⁺, which precipitate as calcite, magnesite, siderite, and ankerite:

$$\mathrm{CO_2 + H_2O \rightarrow H_2CO_3 \rightarrow 2H^+ + CO_3^{2-}}$$
$$\mathrm{Ca^{2+} + CO_3^{2-} \rightarrow CaCO_3 \downarrow}$$

(and analogously for Mg and Fe; for olivine, Mg2SiO4 + 2CO2 → 2MgCO3 + SiO2). In the 2012 pilot, 175 t of pure CO2 plus 73 t of a CO2/H2S/H2 mixture were co-injected with water at Hellisheiði, Iceland; tracers and calcium-isotope mass balance showed 95–98% mineralization within two years, with 165 ± 8.3 t of the 220 t confirmed as calcite — a 72 ± 5% storage efficiency into the most stable phase [6][7]. Continuous injection has since scaled to 5,000 t/yr at the Hellisheiði geothermal plant, and Climeworks' Mammoth output is mineralized through the same partnership [7][10].

Ex-situ routes — carbonation of olivine and serpentine mine tailings, and enhanced rock weathering spread on croplands — trade the geological constraint for a mining and grinding energy penalty, and ambient-temperature kinetics remain slow without thermal or chemical activation. The decisive advantage of mineralization in either form is *durability*: carbonates are stable on 10,000-year timescales, so the monitoring burden and reversal risk that dominate supercritical storage economics effectively vanish. Its decisive constraint is geography and water: basaltic reservoirs are widespread (notably in oceanic crust), but dissolved injection consumes on the order of tens of tonnes of water per tonne of CO2 [6].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

> **Theorem (Minimum separation work).** For an ideal-gas mixture at temperature $T_0$ and pressure $p_0$, the minimum isothermal work to extract one mole of a trace component at mole fraction $x \ll 1$ and deliver it pure at $p_0$ is
>
> $$W_{\min} = -RT_0 \ln x + o(x) \quad \text{J mol}^{-1}.$$
>
> *Proof sketch.* The molar Gibbs free energy of mixing is $\Delta g_{\mathrm{mix}} = RT_0\sum_i x_i \ln x_i < 0$. Reversible isothermal separation restores the unmixed state, so the minimum shaft work equals $-\Delta G_{\mathrm{mix}}$ of the processed mixture. For a binary mixture with $x_{\mathrm{CO_2}} = x$, the work per mole of mixture is $-RT_0[x\ln x + (1-x)\ln(1-x)]$; dividing by the CO2 content $x$ and taking $x \to 0$ gives $-RT_0\ln x$, since the second term is $o(x)$. ∎

At 298 K and 420 ppm, $W_{\min} \approx 19.3$ kJ mol⁻¹ ≈ **0.44 GJ per tonne of CO2** (0.12 MWh/t) [8]. The following script reproduces this floor and the second-law efficiencies of the two leading processes:

```python
import math
R, T = 8.314, 298.15          # J/mol/K, K
x = 420e-6                    # ambient CO2 mole fraction
W_min_kJmol = -R*T*math.log(x)/1000
W_min_GJt = W_min_kJmol / 44.01  # kJ/mol -> GJ/tCO2 (44.01 g/mol)
print(f"W_min = {W_min_kJmol:.1f} kJ/mol = {W_min_GJt:.2f} GJ/tCO2")
processes = {"Carbon Engineering (gas+elec)": 6.57, "Climeworks solid TVSA": 7.9}
for name, E in processes.items():
    print(f"{name}: eta_II = {W_min_GJt/E*100:.1f}%")
```

Output: `W_min = 19.3 kJ/mol = 0.44 GJ/tCO2`; Carbon Engineering: η_II ≈ 6.7%; Climeworks: η_II ≈ 5.5%. DAC's single-digit efficiencies reflect the −ln(x) penalty of 420 ppm, not poor engineering per se.

> **Theorem (Net-negativity condition).** A DACCS system is net carbon-negative iff
>
> $$\varepsilon \cdot E + \delta < 1,$$
>
> where $E$ is the energy input per tonne of CO2 captured (MWh/t), $\varepsilon$ is the carbon intensity of that energy (tCO2/MWh), and $\delta$ is the embodied emissions of sorbent, construction, and consumables per tonne captured. *Proof.* Each captured tonne avoids exactly one tonne only if permanently stored; gross removal minus supply-chain emissions must be positive. ∎

Numerically: at $E = 8$ GJ/t ≈ 2.2 MWh/t and a grid intensity of 0.4 tCO2/MWh, energy alone emits 0.89 t per tonne captured — the system barely breaks even. Net negativity therefore *requires* low-carbon energy, which is why credible deployments co-locate with geothermal, nuclear, or curtailed renewables [12].

| Process | Energy per tCO2 | Reported cost ($/tCO2) | Maturity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aqueous hydroxide (Carbon Engineering) | 8.8 GJ gas, or 5.25 GJ + 366 kWh elec | 94–232 | Pilot; 1 Mt/yr design [1] |
| Amine solid, TVSA (Climeworks) | 6.5 GJ heat + 1.1–1.8 GJ elec | 500–600 (2019); gen-3 lower | Commercial, 36 kt/yr [10][12] |
| Moisture swing (resin) | Near-zero thermal; water-driven | Research estimates | Pilot [4] |

| Basalt mineralization (CarbFix) | Tens of t H2O per tCO2; pumping | Storage adder, small vs capture | 5 kt/yr operating [7] |

Learning-curve analysis (Azarabadi & Lackner, via [11]) finds that reaching $100/t requires cumulative deployment of order gigatonnes — costs fall with experience, but only if plants are actually built. The DOE's $100–200/t target for the coming decade is thus a deployment challenge as much as a research one [12].

## 6. Limitations

Four limitations bound the technology. **Scale and energy.** Removing 1 GtCO2/yr at 8 GJ/t demands 8 EJ/yr — over 1% of current global primary energy — dedicated to running the separation in reverse of combustion. DACCS at climate-relevant scale is therefore an energy-system project wearing a chemical-engineering costume, viable only alongside massive clean-energy buildout. **Cost and learning.** First-of-a-kind costs ($500–600/t for solid sorbents [11]) must descend a learning curve whose slope is uncertain; Keith's $94–232/t is an Nth-of-a-kind projection contingent on financing, capacity factors, and gas prices that may not transfer across jurisdictions [1]. **Materials durability.** Amines oxidize and form ureas; MOFs hydrolyze; resins foul — and sorbent replacement dominates operating cost in every TEA, yet multi-year field degradation data are sparse [5][9]. **Storage geography and water.** In-situ mineralization needs reactive basalt within pipeline distance and consumes tens of tonnes of water per tonne of CO2; ex-situ routes need mines, mills, and heat. None of this is disqualifying, but it rules out the vision of DAC as a placeless, plug-and-play commodity. Finally, the mitigation-deterrence risk is real: the IPCC warns explicitly that the prospect of future CDR must not substitute for near-term emissions cuts [3]. DACCS is a complement to decarbonization, not a substitute for near-term emissions cuts [3].

## 7. Conclusion

Direct air capture is thermodynamically permitted, chemically demonstrated, and economically immature — three facts that coexist without contradiction. The minimum-work theorem sets a floor of ~0.44 GJ per tonne of CO2; the best demonstrated processes consume fifteen to twenty times that, leaving enormous headroom for sorbent and process innovation. Four sorbent families trace distinct trade-offs: aqueous hydroxides offer maturity at the price of 900 °C heat; amine solids offer low-grade-heat operation at the price of sorbent degradation; moisture-swing resins offer near-zero thermal input at the price of kinetics and water; MOFs offer designed binding sites at the price of unproven durability. On the storage side, CarbFix has settled the permanence question for basaltic reservoirs: dissolved injection mineralizes CO2 within two years at demonstrated efficiencies above 70% into calcite [6][7]. The remaining questions are industrial, not scientific — learning rates, clean-energy supply, and honest life-cycle boundaries. If those are met, the path from kilotonne demonstration plants to the gigatonnes in IPCC pathways is an exercise in scaling; if not, DACCS remains an expensive lesson that thermodynamics cannot be negotiated with.
