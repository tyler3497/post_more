## Abstract

Gaussian processes (GPs) are the canonical Bayesian nonparametric model for regression, offering exact posterior inference and analytic uncertainty quantification through the kernel trick. Their Achilles' heel is cubic scaling: exact inference inverts an *n* × *n* kernel matrix at *O(n³)* time and *O(n²)* memory, collapsing beyond roughly ten thousand points. This thesis unifies the two axes along which scalability is recovered: **kernel design** — radial basis, Matérn, and spectral mixture families with automatic relevance determination — which controls statistical capacity; and **sparse variational approximations** — FITC, DTC, Titsias' variational free energy, stochastic variational GPs (SVGP), and structured kernel interpolation (SKI/KISS-GP) — which reduce cost through *m ≪ n* inducing variables. We derive the variational lower bound, analyze convergence of the variational posterior, quantify the inducing-capacity trade-off, and examine deep kernel learning as the bridge to representation learning. Empirical results show expressive kernels with sparse inference achieving near-exact accuracy on datasets orders of magnitude beyond exact GPs, while calibration analysis exposes the residual overconfidence of variational posteriors.

## 1 Introduction

A Gaussian process defines a distribution over functions *f : X → ℝ* such that every finite set of function values is jointly Gaussian. Specified by a mean function *μ(x)* and a positive-definite covariance (kernel) function *k(x, x′)*, the GP places a prior directly on function space; conditioning on data yields a posterior that is again a GP with closed-form mean and covariance [1]. The posterior mean is a data-adaptive interpolant, and the posterior variance is a principled measure of epistemic uncertainty that expands exactly where data are absent — the reason GPs became the gold standard for Bayesian optimization, geostatistics, and probabilistic time-series modeling.

Yet the closed form is computationally fatal. The posterior requires solving linear systems in **K** + σ²**I**, costing *O(n³)* time via Cholesky factorization and *O(n²)* storage. At *n = 100,000* a single exact fit exceeds workstation budgets; at *n = 10⁶* it is fantasy. The literature has pursued two orthogonal escapes:

1. **Statistical leverage — kernel design:** covariances whose inductive biases (smoothness, periodicity, quasi-periodicity) compress structure into the model. The RBF kernel imposes infinite differentiability; Matérn kernels admit tunable finite smoothness; the spectral mixture (SM) kernel parameterizes a dense family of stationary covariances through a Gaussian mixture in the spectral domain, enabling automatic pattern discovery and extrapolation [5].
2. **Computational leverage — sparse approximations:** *m ≪ n* auxiliary inducing variables act as a low-rank information bottleneck. Titsias recast sparsity variationally, deriving a rigorous lower bound on the marginal likelihood that jointly optimizes inducing locations and hyperparameters [2]; Hensman, Fusi, and Lawrence made the bound factorize over data, enabling stochastic minibatch optimization on millions of points [3]; Wilson and Nickisch replaced global inducing-point regression with *local* kernel interpolation on structured grids, achieving *O(n)* inference via Kronecker and Toeplitz algebra [4].

The central claim of this thesis: *with expressive kernels and principled variational sparsity, GPs scale to millions of points with near-exact accuracy — but only when inducing capacity matches the kernel's statistical complexity.*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Exact Gaussian Process Regression

For data *D = {(xᵢ, yᵢ)}ᵢ₌₁ⁿ* with *yᵢ = f(xᵢ) + εᵢ*, *εᵢ ∼ 𝒩(0, σ²)*, and prior *f ∼ 𝒢𝒫(0, k)*, the joint distribution of training targets **y** and test latents **f\*** is Gaussian, and conditioning gives the posterior predictive [1]:

- **Posterior mean:** *μ\* = K(X\*, X)(K(X, X) + σ²I)⁻¹y*
- **Posterior covariance:** *Σ\* = K(X\*, X\*) − K(X\*, X)(K(X, X) + σ²I)⁻¹K(X, X\*)*

> **Theorem:** Under a GP prior and Gaussian likelihood, the posterior predictive at any finite set of test inputs is exactly Gaussian: the mean is a linear smoother of **y** depending only on kernel evaluations and the noise variance, and the covariance is the prior covariance minus the explained variance. No approximation is invoked.

The hyperparameters *θ* of the kernel are learned by maximizing the log marginal likelihood

```
log p(y | X, θ) = −½ yᵀ(K + σ²I)⁻¹y  − ½ log|K + σ²I|  − (n/2) log(2π)
```

with terms reading as *data fit*, *complexity penalty*, and *normalization* [1]: the determinant penalizes kernels spreading mass over too many function classes — an automatic Occam's razor that sparse methods must approximate without corrupting.

### 2.2 Kernel Families and the Inductive Bias They Encode

| Kernel | Form *k(τ)*, *τ = ‖x − x′‖* | Sample-path smoothness | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| RBF (SE) | *σ² exp(−τ²/2ℓ²)* | *C^∞* | Gaussian spectrum at origin; often unrealistically smooth |
| Matérn-ν | *σ² · (2^(1−ν)/Γ(ν)) (√2ν τ/ℓ)^ν K_ν(√2ν τ/ℓ)* | *⌈ν⌉ − 1* differentiable | ν = 3/2, 5/2 standard; polynomial spectrum tails |
| Rational Quadratic | *σ²(1 + τ²/2αℓ²)^(−α)* | *C^∞* per scale | Infinite mixture of RBFs over lengthscales |
| Periodic | *σ² exp(−2 sin²(πτ/p)/ℓ²)* | *C^∞* | Strict periodicity with period *p* |
| Spectral Mixture | *Σ_q w_q exp(−2π²τ²v_q) cos(2πτμ_q)* | depends on components | Gaussian mixture in spectral domain; dense in stationary kernels [5] |

The RBF kernel's spectral density is a Gaussian centered at the origin — a tiny corner of the possible space [1]. Matérn kernels have Student-*t*-like spectra with polynomial tails, giving tunable finite differentiability (*ν = 3/2* yields once-differentiable paths, a better match to spatial fields).

**Automatic relevance determination (ARD).** A per-dimension lengthscale vector, *k(x, x′) = σ² exp(−Σ_d (x_d − x′_d)²/2ℓ_d²)*, lets the marginal likelihood *prune irrelevant inputs*: dimensions whose ℓ_d grows large are switched off. ARD is the GP analogue of feature selection, though its effectiveness degrades when *D ≳ 50* as the marginal likelihood surface flattens.

### 2.3 The Scalability Bottleneck

Every exact-GP operation reduces to linear algebra on the dense matrix **K** ∈ ℝ^(n×n): Cholesky decomposition costs *O(n³)* time and *O(n²)* memory; each hyperparameter gradient needs the inverse — another *O(n³)* per step without careful caching. At *n ≈ 10⁴* this is comfortable; at *n ≈ 10⁵* the Cholesky factor alone needs ~80 GB. Scalability demands *approximating the matrix* (inducing points), *exploiting structure* (grids, Kronecker, Toeplitz), or both.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Sparse Approximations: FITC, DTC, and the Variational Turn

Introduce *m ≪ n* inducing inputs **Z** = {z_j} with latent values **u** = *f*(**Z**). The exact joint factorizes as *p(**y**, **f**, **u**) = p(**y**|**f**) p(**f**|**u**) p(**u**)*; sparse methods differ in how they approximate *p(**f**|**u**)*:

- **DTC (deterministic training conditional)** treats *fᵢ | **u*** as deterministic: *fᵢ = K_{xᵢZ} K_{ZZ}⁻¹ **u***. The prior becomes degenerate, variance is underestimated, and the objective is *not* a bound on the marginal likelihood [2].
- **FITC (fully independent training conditional)** restores exact conditional variances on the diagonal: *p(**f**|**u**) ≈ 𝒩(K_{XZ}K_{ZZ}⁻¹**u**, diag(K_{XX} − Q_{XX}))* with *Q_{XX} = K_{XZ}K_{ZZ}⁻¹K_{ZX}*. Cost stays *O(nm²)* and DTC's variance collapse is fixed, but the prior is still modified in an uncontrolled way [2].
- **VFE (variational free energy, Titsias 2009).** A variational distribution *q(**f**, **u**) = p(**f**|**u**) q(**u**)* is placed over the *augmented* model, and the evidence lower bound is maximized:

```
log p(y) ≥ E_q[log p(y | f)] − KL(q(u) ‖ p(u))  :=  L
```

The inducing inputs **Z** are *variational parameters* optimized to tighten the bound — not model hyperparameters — and the KL is minimized against the *exact* posterior [2]. This is the conceptual breakthrough: sparse approximation as *principled approximate inference* rather than *prior surgery*. The bound costs *O(nm²)*, and increasing *m* monotonically tightens it.

### 3.2 SVGP: Stochastic Variational Inference

Hensman, Fusi, and Lawrence (2013) reparameterized the variational distribution in its *natural parameters* so the bound's expected log-likelihood becomes a sum over data points — an unbiased minibatch estimator [3]. The SVGP algorithm:

1. Maintains *q(**u**) = 𝒩(**m**, **S**)* over inducing values.
2. Forms a minibatch ELBO estimate with *B ≪ n* points per step.
3. Updates natural parameters with stochastic (natural) gradients — natural gradients are especially effective for *q(**u**)*, with unit step size in the conjugate case.

Per-iteration cost drops to *O(Bm² + m³)*, *independent of n* — GPs on millions of points, demonstrated on the airline-delay dataset (*n ≈ 5.9 × 10⁶*) [3]. The framework extends to non-Gaussian likelihoods and latent-variable models, since the ELBO's expectation needs only one-dimensional quadratures for factorized likelihoods.

### 3.3 Structured Kernel Interpolation: SKI and KISS-GP

Wilson and Nickisch (2015) approximate the kernel function itself rather than summarizing data [4]:

```
k_SKI(x, z) = w_xᵀ K_{UU} w_z
```

Here **U** is a grid of *m* inducing points and **w**_x, **w**_z are *sparse* interpolation weights — local cubic interpolation uses only *4^d* neighbors per point. The Gram approximation *K_{XX} ≈ W_X K_{UU} W_Xᵀ* combines with structure-exploiting algebra:

- On a regular grid, *K_{UU}* is a **Kronecker product** of Toeplitz matrices: MVMs cost *O(m log m)* via FFT.
- *W_X* is extremely sparse: *O(n)* storage.

**KISS-GP** thus achieves *O(n)* training and *O(1)* prediction per test point — and can afford *m ≈ n* inducing points, giving *near-exact* accuracy while staying linear [4].

### 3.4 Deep Kernel Learning

Deep kernel learning composes a neural feature extractor *g_w(x)* with a base kernel: *k_DKL(x, x′) = k_base(g_w(x), g_w(x′))* [8]. The network learns a representation in which a simple stationary kernel suffices, while the GP layer retains calibrated uncertainty. With SKI's linear algebra and the SVGP objective, DKL scales representation learning to large datasets [4][8] — but the joint objective over network weights, hyperparameters, and inducing locations is highly non-convex, and *feature collapse* must be countered with careful initialization and regularization.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Kernel Design from First Principles: Bochner's Theorem

For stationary kernels, *k(x, x′) = k(τ)*, **Bochner's theorem** states that *k* is positive definite iff it is the Fourier transform of a finite positive measure — the *spectral density* *S(s)*:

```
k(τ) = ∫ S(s) e^{2πi sᵀτ} ds ,   S(s) ≥ 0
```

Kernel design is therefore *spectral density design*. The RBF kernel corresponds to a Gaussian spectrum at the origin — a tiny corner of the space [5]. Wilson and Adams' spectral mixture kernel models *S(s)* as a scale-location Gaussian mixture,

```
S(s) = Σ_q w_q [ φ(s; μ_q, σ_q²) + φ(−s; μ_q, σ_q²) ] / 2
```

with closed-form inverse Fourier transform: a sum of exponentially damped cosines [5]. Since Gaussian mixtures are dense in the space of distributions, the SM kernel is *dense in the space of stationary covariances* [7]. Consequences:

- **Pattern discovery:** learned components are interpretable — a component at frequency *μ_q* with weight *w_q* identifies a quasi-periodic pattern of period *1/μ_q* and coherence length set by *σ_q*.
- **Extrapolation:** unlike RBF kernels, which revert to the prior mean outside the data, SM kernels extrapolate discovered periodicities — shown on atmospheric CO₂ and airline passenger data [5].
- **Initialization discipline:** the SM marginal likelihood is riddled with local optima; initializing component frequencies from the empirical periodogram is essential, else optimization collapses to degenerate solutions [7].

### 4.2 Anatomy of the Variational Bound

Titsias' derivation starts from the augmented model with *q(**f**, **u**) = p(**f**|**u**) q(**u**)* [2]. Because *q* reuses the exact conditional prior, the infinite-dimensional KL collapses to a finite-dimensional one over the inducing variables, yielding the bound *log p(**y**) ≥ E_q[log p(**y**|**f**)] − KL(q(**u**)‖p(**u**))*.

Optimizing *q(**u**)* analytically for fixed **Z** gives the collapsed bound

```
L = log N(y | 0, Q_{XX} + σ²I) − (1/2σ²) tr(K_{XX} − Q_{XX})
```

with *Q_{XX} = K_{XZ}K_{ZZ}⁻¹K_{ZX}*. The trace term is the *total conditional variance* — the approximation's information loss — acting as a regularizer that stops inducing inputs from overfitting: unlike FITC/DTC objectives, **L** can only increase toward the true log marginal likelihood as **Z** is optimized [2]. Gradients cost *O(nm²)* via the matrix inversion lemma on the low-rank-plus-diagonal structure of *Q_{XX} + σ²I*.

> **Theorem (Variational protection):** Maximizing Titsias' bound over **Z** minimizes *KL(q(**f**) ‖ p(**f**|**y**))* for the implied variational process. The bound is a *certificate*: reported values are guaranteed lower bounds on the true log marginal likelihood, and model comparison between two sparse configurations is valid whenever both use the same bound [2].

Hensman's SVGP reformulation makes the data term a sum of *n* per-point expectations, each depending on **u** only through the *m*-dimensional projection *K_{xᵢZ}K_{ZZ}⁻¹**u*** — which is what legitimizes minibatching [3].

### 4.3 How Many Inducing Points? Convergence Analysis

Burt, Rasmussen, and van der Wilk (2020) showed the KL between variational and exact posteriors is controlled by the *tail sum of kernel eigenvalues* beyond the top *m* [6]:

- **Exponential eigendecay** (RBF on compact domains): *m = O(log^d n)* suffices for the KL to vanish — *polylogarithmic* in *n*.
- **Matérn-ν** (polynomial eigendecay): *m = O(n^{d/(2ν+d)})* is required — polynomial in *n*, since rougher processes need more capacity.

> **Theorem (informal, Burt et al. 2020):** Let λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ … be the Mercer eigenvalues of *k*. Then *KL(q\* ‖ p(·|**y**)) ≤ C · Σ_{i>m} λᵢ*. Approximation quality is governed by the spectral tail, not by *n* directly [6].

This explains a widely observed phenomenon: smooth kernels are cheap to approximate, while rough kernels — and spectral mixture kernels with many components — demand large *m*. It also justifies KISS-GP's design: *O(n)* algebra makes *m ≈ n* affordable, sidestepping the capacity question entirely [4].

### 4.4 Structure-Exploiting Linear Algebra

KISS-GP's scalability rests on two classical structures [4]:

1. **Kronecker structure.** On a *d*-dimensional Cartesian grid with a product kernel, *K_{UU} = K₁ ⊗ ⋯ ⊗ K_d*. MVMs exploit *(A ⊗ B)vec(X) = vec(BXAᵀ)*, cutting *O(m²)* products to *O(d·m^{1+1/d})*, and eigendecompositions factorize per dimension for fast log-determinants.
2. **Toeplitz structure.** Stationary kernels on a 1D regular grid give Toeplitz *K_{UU}*; circulant embedding enables *O(m log m)* MVMs via FFT. Block-Toeplitz-Toeplitz-block (BTTB) structure extends this to 2D.

With conjugate gradients and stochastic Lanczos quadrature for the log-determinant, marginal-likelihood optimization runs in *O(n + m log m)* per iteration [4]. The interpolation matrix *W_X* is the linchpin: local cubic interpolation gives *4^d* nonzeros per row — sparse enough for *O(n)* assembly, accurate enough on a fine grid. The curse of dimensionality reappears, however: *4^d* grows exponentially, so SKI grids are practical only for *d ≲ 5* without additive or projection structure.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Extrapolation Benchmarks: Expressive Kernels Matter

Wilson and Adams trained on atmospheric CO₂ records and airline passenger series up to a cutoff year, then extrapolated [5]: the **RBF GP** reverts to the prior mean within a few lengthscales of the data boundary, while the **SM GP** recovers the linear trend, the annual cycle, and their interaction — learned spectral components correspond to identifiable physical periodicities.

KISS-GP demonstrated near-exact inference on natural sound modeling with hundreds of thousands of points, where exact GPs were infeasible and conventional inducing methods with *m = 10³* showed visible error [4].

### 5.2 Complexity Landscape

| Method | Training time | Memory | Prediction / point | Accuracy character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exact GP | *O(n³)* | *O(n²)* | *O(n)* mean, *O(n²)* var | exact |
| DTC / SoR | *O(nm²)* | *O(nm)* | *O(m)* / *O(m²)* | degenerate prior; variance underestimated |
| FITC | *O(nm²)* | *O(nm)* | *O(m)* / *O(m²)* | heteroscedastic correction; not a bound |
| VFE (Titsias) | *O(nm²)* | *O(nm)* | *O(m)* / *O(m²)* | rigorous lower bound; KL-optimal **Z** [2] |
| SVGP | *O(Bm² + m³)* / iter | *O(Bm + m²)* | *O(m)* / *O(m²)* | stochastic; exact as *B → n* [3] |
| KISS-GP | *O(n + m log m)* | *O(n + m)* | *O(1)* | near-exact with *m ≈ n* [4] |

### 5.3 Reference Implementation Sketch

The VFE bound in NumPy-like pseudocode, showing where the *O(nm²)* structure arises:

```python
import numpy as np

def vfe_bound(X, y, Z, kernel, sigma2):
    """Titsias (2009) collapsed variational lower bound. O(n m^2)."""
    Kmm = kernel(Z, Z) + 1e-6 * np.eye(len(Z))      # m x m
    Kmn = kernel(Z, X)                              # m x n
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(Kmm)
    # A = Kmm^{-1} Kmn via two triangular solves: O(n m^2)
    A = np.linalg.solve(L.T, np.linalg.solve(L, Kmn))
    Qnn_diag = np.einsum('ij,ji->i', Kmn, A)        # diag(Q_XX)
    trace_term = np.sum(kernel.diag(X) - Qnn_diag) / sigma2
    # log N(y | 0, Q + sigma^2 I) via matrix inversion lemma
    B = np.eye(len(Z)) + A @ A.T / sigma2           # m x m
    Lb = np.linalg.cholesky(B)
    n = len(y)
    logdet = n * np.log(sigma2) + 2 * np.sum(np.log(np.diag(Lb)))
    alpha = y / sigma2 - A.T @ np.linalg.solve(
        Lb.T, np.linalg.solve(Lb, A @ y)) / sigma2
    data_fit = -0.5 * (y @ alpha + logdet + n * np.log(2 * np.pi))
    return data_fit - 0.5 * trace_term
```

> **Theorem (Complexity):** The collapsed VFE bound and its gradients w.r.t. **Z** and kernel hyperparameters cost *O(nm²)* time and *O(nm)* memory. The SVGP reformulation reduces per-iteration cost to *O(Bm² + m³)* with minibatch size *B*, independent of *n* [2][3].

### 5.4 Calibration: Uncertainty You Can Trust?

Approximate GPs can be miscalibrated in systematic ways. FITC underestimates noise variance and overconfidently extrapolates; DTC collapses posterior variance away from inducing points [2]. Variational methods are better behaved — KL minimization penalizes overconfidence — yet SVGP with small *m* still overconcentrates under kernel misspecification. Reliability diagrams and empirical coverage of 95% credible intervals should accompany every sparse GP deployment; when coverage falls below nominal, increase *m* first, enrich the kernel second.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Inducing capacity vs. kernel complexity.** The required *m* grows with the kernel's effective rank [6]: spectral mixture kernels with many components and deep kernels with high-dimensional embeddings can demand *m* in the thousands, eroding the *O(nm²)* advantage.
2. **Optimization fragility.** The VFE/SVGP objective is non-convex in (**Z**, *θ*): inducing points can collapse, hyperparameters can drift to degenerate solutions, and SM kernels need periodogram initialization [7]. Stochastic gradient noise interacts poorly with the ill-conditioned natural-parameter geometry unless natural gradients or adaptive preconditioning are used [3].
3. **Non-Gaussian likelihoods.** The collapsed bound exists only for Gaussian likelihoods. Classification and count models use the uncollapsed SVGP bound with quadrature or Monte Carlo expectations — noisier and harder to optimize [3].
4. **Calibration gaps.** Variational inference minimizes *KL(q‖p)*, the mode-seeking direction, which underestimates posterior variance in multimodal or heavy-tailed settings. Credible intervals should be empirically validated, not trusted axiomatically.
5. **Model selection with approximate bounds.** Titsias' bound is valid for fixed *m*, but comparing bounds across different *m* conflates model evidence with approximation quality — a larger bound at larger *m* may reflect tighter approximation rather than a better kernel [2].

## 7 Conclusion

Gaussian processes survived the big-data era by *approximating their Bayesian principles honestly*. Titsias [2] turned inducing-point methods from prior hacks into certified approximate inference; SVGP [3] made the certificate stochastic and therefore infinite-scale; SKI/KISS-GP [4] showed that approximating the kernel rather than the data admits linear-time near-exact inference; and spectral mixture kernels [5] demonstrated that the kernel itself — designed in the spectral domain — is the highest-leverage modeling decision, enabling pattern discovery and extrapolation no data volume alone provides.

The unified lesson is a *capacity-matching principle*: choose a kernel expressive enough for the phenomenon (spectral mixtures for quasi-periodic structure, Matérn for rough fields, deep kernels for representation learning [8]), allocate inducing capacity commensurate with the kernel's spectral tail [6], and validate calibration empirically — yielding Bayesian nonparametric models on millions of points with honest uncertainty.
