# Persistence Modules, Barcodes, and the Stability of Shape: A Formal Treatment of Persistent Homology in Topological Data Analysis

## Abstract

Persistent homology is the central construction of topological data analysis (TDA), providing a multiscale algebraic-topological descriptor of point clouds, functions, and networks. Beginning from filtered simplicial complexes — principally the Vietoris–Rips, Čech, and alpha constructions — it applies the homology functor over a field to obtain a persistence module, a functor from a totally ordered index set to vector spaces, which the Crawley-Boevey structure theorem decomposes uniquely into interval summands [1][2]. These intervals, rendered as barcodes or persistence diagrams, furnish an invariant that is stable under perturbation: the Cohen–Steiner–Edelsbrunner–Harer stability theorem bounds the bottleneck distance between diagrams by the Gromov–Hausdorff or sup-norm perturbation of the input [3][4]. This thesis develops the full pipeline from filtrations to persistence modules, proves the structure and stability theorems in their modern interleaving formulation, surveys vectorizations — persistence landscapes [5] and persistence images [6] — that bridge TDA with machine learning, examines multiparameter persistence and its computational hardness, and documents applications in materials science, neuroscience, and statistical inference [7][8]. We further discuss algorithmic practice via boundary-matrix reduction and the GUDHI/Ripser ecosystem.

## 1 Introduction

Data are increasingly high-dimensional, nonlinear, and multimodal: neural spike trains, protein configurations, single-cell expression profiles, and materials microstructures resist description by linear statistics alone. **Topological data analysis** (TDA) proposes that *qualitative, coordinate-free invariants* — connected components, loops, voids, and their higher-dimensional analogues — can serve as robust descriptors of such data [4]. Persistent homology is the workhorse of this program: rather than fixing a single scale at which to build a topological model of the data, it considers *all scales simultaneously*, recording when each topological feature appears (its **birth**) and when it disappears (its **death**) [1].

The decisive innovation of Edelsbrunner, Letscher, and Zomorodian [1] was to treat the history of a growing complex — a **filtration** — as the primary object, and to classify topological changes during growth as *signal* or *noise* by their lifetime. This simple idea has matured into a substantial mathematical field with deep connections to representation theory (through persistence modules as quiver representations) [2], metric geometry (through interleaving distances and the Gromov–Hausdorff stability of diagrams) [3], statistics (through vectorizations and limit theorems) [5][6], and computation (through efficient boundary-matrix reduction algorithms) [7].

This thesis provides a self-contained, PhD-level treatment of the subject:

1. We construct the standard filtrations — Vietoris–Rips, Čech, alpha, and sublevel-set — and analyze their approximation relations via the Nerve theorem and interleaving bounds.
2. We develop persistence modules, prove the structure theorem (unique interval decomposition), and derive barcodes and persistence diagrams.
3. We prove stability in its classical and modern (interleaving) forms, including the isometry theorem.
4. We survey vectorizations that make persistence machine-learning-compatible, and confront the hardness barrier of multiparameter persistence.
5. We document the computational pipeline and representative applications.

Throughout, citations refer to peer-reviewed sources; proofs are sketched rigorously with the essential ideas made explicit.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Simplicial homology

Let **K** be an abstract simplicial complex: a collection of finite vertex sets closed under taking subsets. The elements of cardinality *k*+1 are the *k-simplices*. Over a field **F** (typically **F**₂ for computation or ℝ/ℚ for theory), the *k*-th chain group *C_k*(**K**) is the **F**-vector space on the *k*-simplices, and the boundary operator *∂_k: C_k → C_{k−1}* is defined on a simplex by the alternating sum of its faces:

```
∂_k [v₀,...,v_k] = Σᵢ (−1)ⁱ [v₀,...,v̂ᵢ,...,v_k]
```

The identity *∂_k ∘ ∂_{k+1} = 0* holds, yielding the **simplicial homology**

> **Definition:** *H_k*(**K**; **F**) = ker *∂_k* / im *∂_{k+1}*, with **Betti number** *β_k* = dim_**F** *H_k*(**K**).

Intuitively, *β₀* counts connected components, *β₁* counts independent loops, *β₂* counts voids, and so on. Functoriality is essential: a simplicial map *f: K → L* induces a linear map *f_∗: H_k*(**K**) → *H_k*(**L**), and homotopic maps induce identical maps — the fact on which the Nerve theorem's consequences rest [8].

### 2.2 Filtrations

> **Definition:** A **filtration** of a simplicial complex **K** is a nested family ∅ = **K**_{r₀} ⊆ **K**_{r₁} ⊆ ··· ⊆ **K**_{rₘ} = **K**, indexed by real parameters *r*, such that every simplex enters at a well-defined index *f(σ)* and stays forever after.

The standard constructions for a finite metric point cloud *X* ⊆ (*M*, *d*) are:

- **Vietoris–Rips complex** Rips_r(*X*): a simplex *σ* ⊆ *X* is included iff diam(*σ*) ≤ 2*r* (convention varies by factor 2; equivalently, iff all pairwise distances ≤ *r*). It is the *clique complex* of the threshold graph — combinatorially simple but exponentially large in the worst case.
- **Čech complex** Čech_r(*X*): *σ* is included iff ∩_{x∈σ} *B*(x, *r*) ≠ ∅, where *B*(x, *r*) is the closed ball of radius *r*. By the **Nerve theorem**, Čech_r(*X*) is homotopy equivalent to the union of balls ∪_{x∈X} *B*(x, *r*) — the geometrically faithful model [8].
- **Alpha complex**: the nerve of the balls *clipped* to Voronoi cells; it is a subcomplex of the Delaunay triangulation, hence of linear size in low dimension, and homotopy equivalent to the union of balls.

These filtrations are related by explicit interleavings. For general metric data,

$$\check{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{ech}_r(X) \subseteq \mathrm{Rips}_r(X) \subseteq \check{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{ech}_{2r}(X),$$

and for Euclidean *X* the factor improves to √2: Rips_r(*X*) ⊆ Čech_{√2·r}(*X*) [8]. This sandwich is the workhorse that lets us substitute the computable Rips complex for the geometrically exact Čech complex with bounded error.

### 2.3 Functions and sublevel sets

For a tame function *f: X → ℝ*, the **sublevel-set filtration** *X_a = f⁻¹((−∞, a])* yields filtrations without any point-cloud discretization. The **Persistence Nerve theorem** [8] asserts that the filtrations of Čech complexes, alpha complexes, and sublevel sets of the distance function *d_X* share the same persistence diagram — unifying the geometric and combinatorial pictures.

## 3 Methodology

The methodological core of TDA is a four-stage pipeline:

1. **Filtration construction.** Choose a filtration adapted to the data: Rips for metric point clouds (fast, approximate), Čech/alpha for Euclidean geometry (exact, expensive), sublevel sets for functions on grids, or *flag/weight-rank* filtrations for networks and images.
2. **Boundary-matrix reduction.** Order all simplices by filtration value (breaking ties by dimension), form the *m × m* boundary matrix *D* with *D[i,j] = 1* iff *σᵢ* is a codimension-1 face of *σⱼ*, and reduce columns left to right: while column *j* is nonzero with pivot row *i* already claimed by an earlier column *j′*, add column *j′* to column *j* (over **F**₂, i.e. XOR) [1][7]. Pivot pairs (*i, j*) give (birth, death) pairs; zero columns with no pivot partner give essential (infinite) classes.
3. **Diagram extraction.** Convert pairs to a **persistence diagram** Dgm_k — a multiset of points (*b, d*) in the extended half-plane *b < d*, plus points on the diagonal Δ with infinite multiplicity to allow matchings [3].
4. **Vectorization and inference.** Map diagrams into Hilbert spaces via landscapes [5] or images [6] for downstream ML, or compute diagram distances (bottleneck, Wasserstein) directly for clustering and hypothesis testing [4][7].

The following Python sketch (using the standard GUDHI interface [7]) illustrates the pipeline end to end:

```python
import numpy as np, gudhi as gd

# 1. Filtration: sample 200 points from a noisy circle (radius 1)
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
theta = rng.uniform(0, 2*np.pi, 200)
pts = np.c_[np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)] + rng.normal(0, 0.05, (200, 2))

# 2. Vietoris-Rips complex + persistence via boundary reduction
rc = gd.RipsComplex(points=pts, max_edge_length=1.2)
st = rc.create_simplex_tree(max_dimension=2)
dgm = st.persistence()                       # list of (dim, (birth, death))

# 3. Diagram extraction: the long H1 bar is the circle's loop
h1 = [(b, d) for (dim, (b, d)) in dgm if dim == 1 and d < np.inf]
print("most persistent H1 class:", max(h1, key=lambda p: p[1]-p))

# 4. Bottleneck distance between two diagrams (stability check)
dgm2 = gd.RipsComplex(points=pts + rng.normal(0, 0.01, pts.shape),
                      max_edge_length=1.2).create_simplex_tree(
                      max_dimension=2).persistence()
print("bottleneck distance:", gd.bottleneck_distance(
      st.persistence_intervals_in_dimension(1),
      gd.SimplexTree(), ))  # placeholder: compare diagrams via gudhi
```

Complexity of naive reduction is *O(m³)* in the number of simplices, but practical behavior is near-linear thanks to sparsity and the *clearing* optimization; Ripser exploits the coboundary matrix and apparent pairs to compute Rips persistence for thousands of points routinely [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Persistence modules and the structure theorem

> **Definition:** A **persistence module** over ℝ is a functor *V: (ℝ, ≤) → Vect_**F*** — a family of vector spaces *V_r* with linear maps *v_{r,s}: V_r → V_s* for *r ≤ s* satisfying *v_{s,t} ∘ v_{r,s} = v_{r,t}* and *v_{r,r} = id*. It is *tame* (or q-tame) if each *v_{r,s}* has finite rank for *r < s*.

Applying *H_k(−; **F**)* to a filtration yields precisely such a module, the *k*-th **persistence module** of the filtration [2]. The fundamental algebraic result, due in its discrete form to Gabriel and in persistence form to Crawley-Boevey, is:

> **Theorem (Structure theorem):** Every pointwise finite-dimensional persistence module decomposes uniquely (up to permutation) as a direct sum of **interval modules** **F**[*b*, *d*), where **F**[*b*, *d*) has **F** at indices *r ∈ [b, d)* and identity maps between them [1][2].

The multiset of intervals [*bᵢ, dᵢ*) is the **barcode**; plotting (*bᵢ, dᵢ*) as points gives the **persistence diagram**. Uniqueness means the diagram is a *complete invariant* of the persistence module up to isomorphism — everything the filtration's homology can say is captured by these intervals. The **elder rule** (when two classes merge, the younger dies) makes the pairing canonical for sublevel filtrations [1].

### 4.2 The stability theorem and interleavings

Stability is what elevates persistence from an algebraic curiosity to a data-analysis tool: *small perturbations of the input produce small perturbations of the diagram*. The classical statement [3]:

> **Theorem (Stability of persistence diagrams):** Let *f, g: X → ℝ* be tame functions on a triangulable space. Then the bottleneck distance between their sublevel-set persistence diagrams satisfies *d_B*(Dgm(*f*), Dgm(*g*)) ≤ ‖*f − g*‖_∞.

The **bottleneck distance** is defined via matchings: *d_B(D₁, D₂)* is the infimum over bijections *φ: D₁ ∪ Δ → D₂ ∪ Δ* (diagonal points added with infinite multiplicity) of sup_x ‖*x − φ(x)*‖_∞, with the convention that unmatched points are matched to the diagonal. The **p-Wasserstein distance** *W_p* replaces the supremum with an ℓ_p sum, giving a finer geometry [3][4].

The modern, more general formulation uses **interleavings**. Two persistence modules *V, W* are *ε-interleaved* if there exist natural transformations *φ: V → W[ε]* and *ψ: W → V[ε]* (shift by *ε*) whose compositions equal the *2ε*-shift maps. The **interleaving distance** *d_I(V, W)* is the infimum of such *ε* [3].

> **Theorem (Isometry theorem):** For q-tame persistence modules, *d_B*(Dgm(*V*), Dgm(*W*)) = *d_I*(*V, W*) [3].

This isometry — due to Lesnick and proved in full generality by Chazal et al. and Bauer–Lesnick — is the field's centerpiece: it identifies the analytic distance on diagrams with the algebraic distance on modules, so that geometric approximation results (like the Rips/Čech sandwich of §2.2) translate *immediately* into diagram-stability bounds. For point clouds, the Gromov–Hausdorff version reads *d_B*(Dgm(Rips(*X*)), Dgm(Rips(*Y*))) ≤ 2·*d_GH*(*X, Y*) [4].

### 4.3 Vectorizations: landscapes, images, and kernels

Persistence diagrams are multisets, not vectors — they live in no Hilbert space, blocking direct use in SVMs, neural networks, and classical statistics. Three vectorizations dominate [5][6]:

| Vectorization | Construction | Stability | Key property |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Persistence landscape** [5] | For each point (*b, d*), tent function *f_{(b,d)}(t)* = max(0, min(*t−b, d−t*)); *λ_k(t)* = *k*-th largest value at *t* | 1-Lipschitz in bottleneck → sup norm | Lives in Banach/Hilbert space; SLLN & CLT hold |
| **Persistence image** [6] | Rotate diagram to (birth, persistence) coords; convolve weighted sum of Gaussians; discretize on pixel grid | Stable in 1-Wasserstein under weight conditions | Fixed-length vector; resolution parameter |
| **Persistence kernel** | e.g. multi-scale kernel *K(D₁, D₂)* via feature map into RKHS | Varies by construction | Positive-definite; works with kernel methods |
| **Betti curve / Euler curve** | *β_k(r)* as function of scale | Stable but lossy | Simple "topological fingerprint" |

Bubenik's landscape [5] is theoretically the cleanest: the map Dgm ↦ *λ* is injective enough to separate diagrams differing in their largest features, and because landscapes live in *L^p*, the full machinery of functional data analysis — means, confidence bands, two-sample tests — applies directly. Adams et al.'s persistence image [6] is the practitioner's choice for deep learning: a fixed-resolution image grid is a natural CNN input, and the weighting function *w(b, d)* can emphasize long-lived features. *Figure 4 depicts both vectorizations.*

### 4.4 Multiparameter persistence and the hardness barrier

Real data often carry *two or more* natural filtrations — e.g., scale and density, or time and threshold. A **multiparameter persistence module** is a functor *V: (ℝⁿ, ≤) → Vect_**F***. Here the paradise ends:

> **Theorem:** For *n ≥ 2*, interval-decomposability fails in general; there is no complete discrete invariant analogous to the barcode, and the interleaving distance is NP-hard to compute [8].

The representation theory of the *n*-dimensional grid quiver is *wild* for *n ≥ 2*: indecomposables are unclassifiable. Practical responses include the **rank invariant** *ρ_V(a, b)* = rank(*v_{a,b}*), **fibered barcodes** (restricting to lines of positive slope and taking 1-D barcodes along each), and **signed barcodes** / minimal presentations that are computable via Gröbner-style algorithms [8]. Multiparameter persistence remains the field's most active frontier, driven by applications where scale alone is insufficient — notably density-robust clustering and time-varying networks.

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

We collect the field's key quantitative results as a mixture of theorems with proof sketches and documented empirical findings.

**Proof sketch — structure theorem.** Discretize a tame module to a representation of an *A_n* quiver (a linear chain of arrows); Gabriel's theorem classifies indecomposable representations of Dynkin quivers, and *A_n* indecomposables are exactly intervals. Crawley-Boevey's theorem extends this to ℝ-indexed modules under pointwise finite-dimensionality [2]. Uniqueness follows from the Krull–Remak–Schmidt theorem.

**Proof sketch — stability.** The classical proof [3] proceeds by box inequalities: for *‖f − g‖_∞ ≤ ε*, sublevel sets interleave as *X^f_a ⊆ X^g_{a+ε} ⊆ X^f_{a+2ε}*, inducing an *ε*-interleaving of persistence modules; the isometry theorem converts this to *d_B ≤ ε*. The matching itself is constructed via a Hall's-marriage argument on a bipartite graph of critical values.

**Empirical result 1 — computation at scale.** Otter et al. [7] survey the software landscape (GUDHI, Ripser, Dionysus, javaPlex, Perseus) and report that Ripser's optimizations (coboundary reduction, apparent/coapparent pairs, clearing) compute Vietoris–Rips persistence for point clouds of several thousand points in seconds on commodity hardware, with memory dominated by the *1*-skeleton enumeration rather than full simplex storage.

**Empirical result 2 — materials science.** Persistent homology of atomic configurations distinguishes amorphous from crystalline packings: the *H₁* and *H₂* diagrams of silica glass vs. quartz show systematically different birth–death distributions, and diagram-based features predict physical properties (e.g., bulk modulus) in published studies surveyed by Chazal & Michel [8] and Dey & Wang [4].

**Empirical result 3 — neuroscience.** Applied to neural spike-train correlations and fMRI functional networks, persistent homology recovers geometric organization (e.g., the circular topology of head-direction cells and grid-cell toroidal structure) that linear dimensionality reduction obscures; bottleneck-distance clustering of diagrams separates behavioral states [4][8].

**Empirical result 4 — statistical validity.** For persistence landscapes, Bubenik [5] proves a strong law of large numbers and central limit theorem in Banach space, enabling confidence intervals and hypothesis tests on topological features — the foundation of *statistical* TDA. Persistence images inherit stability in the 1-Wasserstein metric under mild weight conditions [6].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Metric dependence and the Rips tax.** Vietoris–Rips persistence depends on the ambient metric and can be dominated by metric artifacts rather than intrinsic topology; the Čech complex is faithful but its high-dimensional simplices are computationally prohibitive, and the Rips–Čech sandwich only bounds the error multiplicatively.
2. **Instability of Wasserstein vs. coarseness of bottleneck.** Bottleneck distance is stable but crude (a single outlier point can saturate it); Wasserstein distances are finer but lack the clean ∞-stability guarantees and are costlier to compute.
3. **Multiparameter intractability.** As noted in §4.4, no complete discrete invariant exists for *n ≥ 2* parameters and interleaving distance is NP-hard — the single-parameter theory does not generalize gracefully.
4. **Interpretability gap.** A long bar certifies *that* a loop exists, not *which* geometric cycle represents it; optimal cycle representatives are computable but expensive, and "representative cycles" are non-canonical.
5. **Curse of dimensionality in complexes.** Rips complexes grow combinatorially with point count and intrinsic dimension; practical computation relies on dimension cutoffs (*k ≤ 2* or 3) and landmark/sparsification schemes (witness complexes, sparse Rips) that introduce their own approximation errors [7].
6. **Choice of filtration is a modeling decision.** Different filtrations of the same data can tell different stories; TDA has no canonical answer to "which filtration," and results must be validated against domain knowledge [4].

## 7 Conclusion

Persistent homology transforms the vague intuition that "data have shape" into a rigorous, computable, and stable mathematical theory. From the filtration constructions of §2 — Rips, Čech, alpha, sublevel — through the algebraic engine of persistence modules and the structure theorem, to the stability guarantees that justify the entire enterprise, the single-parameter theory is essentially complete and practically deployable [1][2][3][4]. Vectorizations [5][6] have bridged the gap to modern machine learning, and applications in materials science, neuroscience, and beyond demonstrate that topological features capture signal invisible to linear methods [7][8].

The open frontiers are clear: a computationally tractable multiparameter theory, tighter integration with deep learning (differentiable persistence), statistical methodology for diagram-valued data beyond landscapes, and principled filtration selection. The stability theorem guarantees that whatever answers emerge will be robust — and robustness, in the end, is what makes topology suitable for data.
