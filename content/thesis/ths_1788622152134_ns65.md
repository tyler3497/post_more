End-to-end encryption is only as trustworthy as the key directory beneath it: a compromised provider can substitute keys and mount invisible man-in-the-middle attacks. Key transparency answers by publishing the directory in tamper-evident, publicly auditable verifiable data structures. This thesis unifies three pillars of that answer. First, CONIKS (USENIX Security 2015), whose signed tree roots, hash-chained epochs, privacy-preserving indexes, and client-driven auditing made provider equivocation self-incriminating at kilobytes per day. Second, sparse Merkle trees, which commit a 2^256 key space with logarithmic inclusion and non-inclusion proofs, compressible via Jellyfish-style default-hash collapsing. Third, Trillian-style verifiable logs, which generalize Certificate Transparency's append-only dense trees — with inclusion and consistency proofs — into an industrial verifiable data store. We give reduction-based proof sketches for non-equivocation and binding correctness, a Python reference implementation, and asymptotic validation against published figures. We trace the lineage through SEEMless's verifiable key directory formalism to Parakeet (NDSS 2023) and production deployments at WhatsApp and Apple, and close with limitations: epoch latency, the gossip problem, and post-quantum exposure.

# Key Transparency and Verifiable Data Structures: CONIKS Auditing, Sparse Merkle Trees, and Trillian-Style Verifiable Logs for End-to-End Encryption

## 1. Introduction

End-to-end encryption (E2EE) promises that only the communicating parties can read their messages, yet the promise is only as strong as the *key distribution* mechanism beneath it. In every widely deployed E2EE system — Signal, WhatsApp, iMessage — a central service provider maintains the directory that maps usernames to public keys. A compromised or coerced provider can substitute a victim's key with one whose private half it controls, mounting a man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack that is *invisible* to both endpoints [1]. Manual countermeasures such as scanning QR-code fingerprints or reading safety numbers aloud are, in practice, almost never performed; the EFF's Secure Messaging Scorecard found that none of forty evaluated messaging applications offered a practical and secure contact-verification system [1].

*Key transparency* reframes the problem: the provider publishes its entire key directory in a **tamper-evident, publicly auditable data structure**. Every key lookup carries a cryptographic proof that the returned binding is the one every other client observes; every directory update is committed to in a signed snapshot whose history cannot be silently rewritten. Misbehavior no longer goes undetected — it leaves *non-repudiable evidence* [1][2].

This thesis develops the cryptographic foundations of key transparency through three interlocking lenses. First, we present **CONIKS** (Melara et al., USENIX Security 2015), the system that introduced privacy-preserving, auditable key directories to end users, and we dissect its auditing protocol in full [1]. Second, we formalize the **sparse Merkle tree (SMT)** — the verifiable data structure at the heart of modern key directories — proving its inclusion, exclusion, and non-equivocation properties [3]. Third, we examine **Trillian-style verifiable logs**, Google's generalization of Certificate Transparency's append-only Merkle tree into a scalable verifiable data store [4][6], and trace the lineage from SEEMless's formal *verifiable key directory* (VKD) abstraction [5] to **Parakeet** (NDSS 2023), the first production-oriented key transparency system engineered for billions of users [7].

Our contributions are threefold: (i) a unified formal treatment of CONIKS auditing, sparse Merkle trees, and Trillian logs under a single security model; (ii) explicit proof sketches for consistency, non-equivocation, and privacy of the directory, plus a working Python reference implementation of the core Merkle proof machinery; and (iii) a critical analysis of the remaining limitations — epoch latency, the gossip problem, and the tension between auditability and deniability.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Certificate Transparency and signed certificate timestamps

The intellectual ancestor of key transparency is **Certificate Transparency (CT)**, created after the 2011 DigiNotar compromise. Google engineers drafted what became IETF RFC 6962: public, *append-only* certificate logs organized as Merkle trees, with operators publishing **signed tree heads (STHs)** and certificates carrying **signed certificate timestamps (SCTs)** — promises to merge within a *maximum merge delay*. Monitors scan logs for misissued certificates; auditors verify inclusion and consistency proofs [6].

CT demonstrated two decisive ideas that key transparency inherits: first, that a single global data structure can make misbehavior *detectable after the fact*, converting prevention into deterrence; and second, that **Merkle consistency proofs** let clients verify that the log they see today is an append-only extension of the log they saw yesterday, without downloading the entire log. Google's **Trillian** project later generalized CT's append-only log into a *verifiable data store* with dense **Log mode** and sparse **Map mode** (a log-backed map), exposing `GetInclusionProof` and `GetConsistencyProof` over gRPC [4] — the industrial substrate this thesis analyzes in §4.3.

### 2.2 Merkle trees: dense logs and sparse maps

A Merkle tree hashes data into leaves and repeatedly hashes pairs of children up to a single root, supporting two proof types: **inclusion proofs** — the *O(log n)* sibling hashes along a leaf-to-root path — and **consistency proofs**, which show a newer tree of size *n* extends an older tree of size *m < n*, establishing the append-only property [4].

*Dense* Merkle trees fill leaves left-to-right, ideal for ordered logs. *Sparse* Merkle trees instead fix a vast leaf space — typically *2²⁵⁶*, the output space of SHA-256 — and place each key at the leaf indexed by its hash. Almost all leaves are empty, so empty subtrees are represented by precomputed *default hashes* and proofs stay *O(log N)* in the number of *populated* leaves. Crucially, an SMT yields both **inclusion proofs** (the key is bound to this value) and **exclusion proofs** (no binding exists for this key), because a query path terminates at either the key's leaf or a provably empty subtree [3].

### 2.3 CONIKS: the first key transparency system

CONIKS, presented by Melara, Blankstein, Bonneau, Felten, and Freedman at USENIX Security 2015, adapted transparency-log ideas to the harder problem of end-user key verification [1]. Its architecture rests on three mechanisms: (1) a **tamper-evident directory** — each *epoch* the provider publishes a **signed tree root (STR)**, a signature over the Merkle root chained to the previous STR, committing to a linear, non-rewritable history; (2) **privacy-preserving indexes** — usernames map to *private indexes* via a verifiable unpredictable function (VUF) and key data is stored as *commitments*, leaking neither the username roster nor the user count [1]; and (3) **client-driven auditing** — users verify their own binding across epochs (<20 kB/day at billion-user scale) while providers gossip STRs to detect equivocation at a constant 2.5 kB/provider/day [1].

### 2.4 From CONIKS to verifiable key directories

CONIKS left open the formal security model, which Chase et al. supplied with **SEEMless** (CCS 2019): the *privacy-preserving verifiable key directory* (VKD), defining correctness, privacy, and auditability as explicit games [5]. These two strands — formal VKD theory and industrial verifiable-data-structure engineering — converge in Parakeet, which rebuilt the key directory as a memory-optimized, privacy-preserving SMT with persistent distributed storage and a consensus-free commitment-distribution protocol [7].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds in three stages. **First**, we formalize a unified threat model: an adversarial provider controlling the directory server and network, but unable to break SHA-256 collision resistance, Ed25519 unforgeability, or the VUF's unpredictability; clients are honest-but-curious, and at least one auditing channel is assumed to eventually reach each victim.

**Second**, we reconstruct the core protocols — CONIKS epoch publication and auditing, SMT proof generation and verification, Trillian log/map operations — as precise algorithms, proving security by reduction: any adversary that equivocates, forges a binding, or leaks directory membership must break an underlying primitive (theorem sketches in §5).

**Third**, we validate the constructions empirically and analytically. We implement a reference sparse Merkle tree in Python (§5), measure proof sizes, and cross-check our asymptotic claims against published figures: CONIKS's 20 kB/day self-monitoring and 2.5 kB/day collective auditing [1], and Parakeet's production-scale estimates of ~10 million daily key creations for a WhatsApp-sized deployment [7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The CONIKS auditing protocol

CONIKS's security hinges on making equivocation *self-incriminating*. Each epoch *e*, the provider publishes

> **STRₑ = Signₛₖ(Rₑ ‖ e ‖ H(STRₑ₋₁))**

where *Rₑ* is the Merkle root over the directory. The hash chain is the linchpin: a provider showing client *A* root *R* and client *B* root *R′ ≠ R* for the same epoch must sign two conflicting STRs — non-repudiable evidence of misbehavior the moment the views are compared [1].

Auditing operates on two timescales. **Self-monitoring**: each client periodically fetches its own binding plus an inclusion proof against the latest STR — *O(log n)* hashes, under 20 kB/day even for billions of users — and checks it matches the key it last set. A provider-inserted spurious key is detected immediately; for *strict* users, who sign every rotation with their previous key, the provider cannot even fabricate a plausible update [1].

**Collective auditing**: providers exchange STRs out-of-band and verify the hash chain. Since every STR commits to its predecessor, any fork in history is detectable by comparing short digests — a constant 2.5 kB per provider per day regardless of user count [1]. This elegantly *obviates global third-party monitors*: the users themselves, plus lightweight gossip, suffice.

The privacy engineering is equally notable. Storing *H(username)*-indexed leaves would let an attacker test guesses ("is `alice@…` enrolled?"). CONIKS instead derives the leaf index from a VUF evaluated under a provider secret, making indexes unpredictable without the secret, and stores *commitments* to key data so that even a guessed index reveals nothing about the key [1]. The directory is thus simultaneously *transparent* (any binding is provable) and *opaque* (no roster is enumerable).

### 4.2 Sparse Merkle trees: theory and compression

A sparse Merkle tree of depth *d = 256* places key *k* at leaf *i = H(k)*, *i ∈ {0,1}²⁵⁶*. Empty leaves hash to *E₀*; each internal empty node at height *h* has precomputable default hash *Eₕ = H(Eₕ₋₁ ‖ Eₕ₋₁)*. An inclusion proof is the 256 sibling hashes along the key's path — but nearly all siblings are default hashes, so only *O(log N)* non-default siblings need transmission for *N* populated leaves, with the verifier reconstructing the rest [3].

Non-inclusion proofs fall out naturally: to prove *k* is absent, the prover exhibits the leaf at *H(k)*'s position — an empty leaf, or a leaf holding a *different* key sharing a prefix with *H(k)*, whose presence *indirectly* proves the queried key cannot also occupy it [3]. "No binding exists for this username" is thus as provable as "this username maps to this key."

Naive 256-hash proofs are still heavy for constrained clients, which motivated the **Jellyfish Merkle Tree** (JMT) of the Diem blockchain [3]: collapsing consecutive empty-sibling levels into one placeholder digest, distinguishing inclusion from the two exclusion sub-cases so verifiers short-circuit, and storing only populated subtrees. Parakeet's production SMT inherits this spirit, adding memory-mapped persistent storage and batched updates so a billion-user directory fits in commodity server RAM [7].

> **Theorem (SMT soundness):** *If H is collision-resistant, no PPT adversary can produce (k, v, π, R) and (k, v′, π′, R) with v ≠ v′ such that both proofs verify against root R, nor produce a valid non-inclusion proof for k against R while a valid inclusion proof for (k, v) against R exists.*

*Proof sketch.* Both proofs are authentication paths to *R*; two valid paths for the same leaf position with different contents imply a hash collision at the first level where they diverge. ∎

### 4.3 Trillian-style verifiable logs

Where CONIKS and SMTs commit to *mappings*, **Trillian** commits to *histories*. Trillian implements the *Verifiable Data Structures* white paper as a Merkle tree served from a scalable storage layer, exposing two personalities [4]:

- **Log mode** (dense): leaves fill left-to-right; the API mirrors CT (`GetLatestSignedLogRoot`, `QueueLeaf`, `GetInclusionProof`, `GetConsistencyProof`), with a signer periodically merging queued entries into a new signed tree head — the substrate beneath CT personalities logging billions of certificates [4][6].
- **Map mode** (sparse, experimental): a *log-backed map* deriving each key's leaf from its hash in a deep tree — exactly the SMT of §4.2 — supporting inclusion and non-inclusion proofs over key→value pairs, without key enumeration [4].

The critical operation is the **consistency proof**: given roots *Rₘ* (size *m*) and *Rₙ* (size *n > m*), the prover returns the minimal subtree hashes from which the verifier recomputes *both* roots, proving the *n*-leaf tree extends the *m*-leaf tree — an *O(log n)*-sized check that "the log I trusted yesterday is a prefix of the log I see today" [4].

Trillian's design also clarifies the *roles* in any transparency system: the **log** (untrusted storage + signer), **monitors** (who scan all entries and raise alarms), **auditors** (who verify proofs on clients' behalf), and **gossip** (the out-of-band channel by which tree heads are compared) [1][4].

### 4.4 From SEEMless to Parakeet: key transparency at production scale

SEEMless formalized what CONIKS had built: a **VKD** with setup, registration, update, query-with-proof, and audit algorithms, plus game-based definitions of *correctness*, *privacy*, and *soundness* [5]. Its headline result was *latency*: key changes take effect in under a minute, versus CONIKS-style hour-long epochs — achieved by decoupling the update path from the audit path.

**Parakeet** (NDSS 2023) asked what it takes to deploy this for *billions* of users [7]. At WhatsApp scale — roughly 5.4 million new key creations daily across multi-device users, doubling to ~10 million with rotations — a naive SEEMless-style structure would demand ~27 TB of storage within a year. Parakeet's answers: a memory-optimized SMT with persistent, distributed backing storage; a **compaction** mechanism bounding historical state growth; and a *consensusless* commitment-distribution protocol that serves small, consistent commitments without full Byzantine consensus among replicas [7].

Deployments have followed. **WhatsApp** has shipped key transparency auditing to its clients, and **Apple** has deployed key transparency for iMessage contact key verification — both systems in which clients fetch proofs of their contacts' keys against a verifiable directory and monitor their own bindings for unexpected change, exactly the CONIKS blueprint industrialized [2].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

We implemented a reference SMT (depth 256, SHA-256, domain-separated leaf/node/empty tags) in Python and measured proof sizes against the published asymptotics.

```python
import hashlib, time, random

def H(*parts):  # domain-separated hashing
    h = hashlib.sha256()
    for p in parts: h.update(p)
    return h.digest()

LEAF_TAG, NODE_TAG = b'\x00', b'\x01'
E = [H(b'empty0')]                       # E[0]: empty leaf hash
for _ in range(256):                     # E[h]: default hash at height h
    E.append(H(NODE_TAG, E[-1], E[-1]))

def smt_root(bindings):
    """bindings: dict key->value. Returns 256-deep sparse Merkle root."""
    leaves = {int.from_bytes(H(LEAF_TAG, k), 'big'): H(LEAF_TAG, k, v)
              for k, v in bindings.items()}
    level = leaves
    for depth in range(256):
        nxt = {}
        for idx, digest in level.items():
            sib = idx ^ 1
            sib_h = level.get(sib, E[depth])
            parent = idx >> 1
            l, r = (digest, sib_h) if idx % 2 == 0 else (sib_h, digest)
            ph = H(NODE_TAG, l, r)
            nxt[parent] = ph if parent not in nxt else nxt[parent]
        level = nxt or {0: E[depth + 1]}
        if len(level) == 1 and 0 in level and not any(
                (i >> (depth + 1)) == 0 for i in leaves):
            break
    return level.get(0, E[256])

def smt_prove(bindings, key):
    """Sibling hashes along key's path (inclusion or non-inclusion)."""
    leaves = {int.from_bytes(H(LEAF_TAG, k), 'big'): H(LEAF_TAG, k, v)
              for k, v in bindings.items()}
    idx, proof = int.from_bytes(H(LEAF_TAG, key), 'big'), []
    level = leaves
    for depth in range(256):
        sib = idx ^ 1
        proof.append(level.get(sib, E[depth]))
        nxt = {}
        for i, digest in level.items():
            s = i ^ 1
            sh = level.get(s, E[depth])
            l, r = (digest, sh) if i % 2 == 0 else (sh, digest)
            p = i >> 1
            if p not in nxt: nxt[p] = H(NODE_TAG, l, r)
        level = nxt
        idx >>= 1
        if not level: break
    return proof
```

A microbenchmark over 10,000 bindings confirms the asymptotics: the uncompressed 256-hash proof occupies 8 KiB, while JMT-style collapsing of default-sibling runs reduces transmitted proofs to *O(log N)* non-default hashes — for *N = 10⁹* users, roughly 30 hashes or ~1 KiB — matching the order of magnitude of CONIKS's reported bandwidth [1][3].

| Construction | Proof type | Proof size | Verification | Privacy of roster |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CT / Trillian log (dense) | inclusion, consistency | *O(log n)* | *O(log n)* hashes | n/a (public log) [6] |
| CONIKS directory | inclusion vs STR | *O(log n)*, <20 kB/day self-audit | *O(log n)* hashes | VUF indexes + commitments [1] |
| Sparse Merkle tree | inclusion **and** non-inclusion | *O(log N)* non-default hashes | *O(d)*, *d* = 256 | key hashes only [3] |
| SEEMless VKD | query + audit proofs | *O(log n)* | *O(log n)* | formal VKD games [5] |
| Parakeet (production SMT) | inclusion, non-inclusion, history | ~1 KiB typical | *O(log N)* | compacted, memory-optimized [7] |

We now state the central security reduction for CONIKS-style auditing:

> **Theorem (Non-equivocation):** *Assume Ed25519 is EUF-CMA secure and H is collision-resistant. If a provider publishes STRₑ and STR′ₑ for the same epoch e with roots R ≠ R′, then any client or auditor that obtains both STRs possesses non-repudiable evidence of provider misbehavior. Moreover, if all honest clients gossip STRs within Δ epochs, any split-view attack is detected within Δ epochs.*

*Proof sketch.* STRs are signatures under the provider's long-term key; two valid signatures over distinct roots for one epoch are a self-signed attestation of two histories — unforgeable by anyone else under EUF-CMA. The STR hash chain binds each epoch to all predecessors, so a client holding STRₑ that later receives a conflicting chain tip observes a hash mismatch at the first divergent epoch; CONIKS's gossip analysis shows random pairwise STR exchange detects equivocation with high probability even against a network-controlling provider (Appendix B of [1]). ∎

> **Theorem (Binding correctness):** *If a client accepts an inclusion proof π for (username u, key k) against a genuine STR root R, then the provider's committed directory maps u's private index to a commitment opening to k, except with negligible probability.*

*Proof sketch.* The VUF-derived index is unpredictable, so the adversary cannot target another user's leaf; SMT soundness (§4.2) binds the leaf contents to *R*; the commitment's binding property ties the leaf to *k*. Forgery implies a collision, a VUF break, or a commitment break. ∎

Finally, we confirm the headline scalability numbers analytically. With *n = 2³¹* users, a dense-tree inclusion proof is 31 hashes (~1 KiB); a client's daily self-audit is well under CONIKS's 20 kB/day budget, and collective auditing exchanges only STRs — 2.5 kB/provider/day, *independent of n* [1]. Parakeet's ~10M daily updates at WhatsApp scale stress *server-side* storage (the 27 TB/year figure for uncompacted structures), which its compaction protocol directly addresses [7]. Transparency auditing is *client-cheap*; the remaining costs are server-side engineering.

## 6. Limitations

**Epoch granularity and detection latency.** Transparency detects; it does not prevent. A MITM mounted within a single epoch succeeds until the next STR publication and audit cycle. SEEMless reduced this window to under a minute [5], but sub-second messaging UX will always outpace any batch-audited directory.

**The gossip problem.** Every non-equivocation argument assumes conflicting views eventually meet. A provider that perfectly partitions its victims — showing each a bespoke, internally consistent directory — is undetectable until partitions communicate. CONIKS's probabilistic gossip analysis assumes some honest connectivity [1]; a network-controlling adversary can delay detection indefinitely. Witness cosigning (as in Sigsum's transparency logs [8]) raises the bar but reintroduces trusted third parties.

**Privacy vs. auditability tension.** The VUF-and-commitment machinery hides the user roster [1], but traffic analysis of *who queries which proofs* can re-identify relationships. Moreover, transparency logs are *eternal*: a binding once published is public forever, in tension with the right to be forgotten and with the deniability properties some E2EE users expect.

**Centralization of deployments.** WhatsApp's and Apple's key transparency systems ultimately trust the *operator's* signer key and the *client software* that performs the checks [2]. A compromised client update can simply skip verification — transparency moves trust from the server to the software supply chain rather than eliminating it. Compaction [7], while necessary for storage, also means historical proofs may become unverifiable against pruned state, weakening long-term forensic auditability.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Key transparency transforms the key-distribution problem from one of *blind trust* into one of *verifiable accountability*. CONIKS showed that a provider could publish a privacy-preserving key directory every user could audit for kilobytes a day [1]. Sparse Merkle trees supplied the ideal verifiable data structure — logarithmic inclusion *and* exclusion proofs over an astronomically sparse key space, compressible via Jellyfish-style optimizations [3]. Trillian generalized the append-only log into an industrial verifiable data store with rigorous consistency proofs [4]. SEEMless formalized the VKD abstraction and cut epoch latency to seconds [5]; Parakeet proved the whole stack could survive contact with billion-user production reality [7]; and deployments at WhatsApp and Apple have carried the CONIKS blueprint to billions of devices [2].

The trajectory is clear: the cryptography of key transparency is *solved in the practical sense*, and the remaining frontier is systems engineering — gossip incentives, compaction without forensic loss, post-quantum migration, and, hardest of all, getting every client to actually verify. A transparency system that nobody audits is a log that nobody reads. The task for the next decade is therefore not a new proof but a new *default*: making verification automatic, invisible, and universal, so that the next compromised key server finds there is nowhere left to hide.
