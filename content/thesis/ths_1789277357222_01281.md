# Byzantine Fault Tolerance: Quorum Intersection, PBFT View Changes, and the HotStuff Linearization

## Abstract

Byzantine fault tolerance is the discipline of extracting agreement from replicas that may lie. This thesis traces the mathematical spine of that discipline — the quorum-intersection invariant — through its two most consequential engineering incarnations: Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) [1], which first made Byzantine state-machine replication practical in partially synchronous networks, and HotStuff [2], which linearized its communication. We prove that any two quorums of 2f+1 replicas in a system of n=3f+1 intersect in at least f+1 replicas, hence in at least one honest replica, and show how this fact underwrites both PBFT's three-phase commit and the safety of its view-change protocol. We then analyze how threshold signatures and a rotating-leader propose–vote chain collapse PBFT's superlinear view-change cost into linear O(n) communication while preserving responsiveness, formalizing HotStuff's three-chain commit rule. Federated (Stellar [4]) and gossip-based (Tendermint [3]) trust models are examined as variations on the same invariant. The result is a unified account of how a single set-theoretic fact scales from the Byzantine generals problem [9] to modern blockchain consensus.

## 1 Intro

State-machine replication (SMR) is the canonical route to fault-tolerant services: a deterministic state machine is replicated, and every replica executes the same commands in the same order [7]. When replicas fail only by crashing, Paxos-style protocols suffice. When replicas can behave *arbitrarily* — lying, colluding, sending contradictory messages to different peers — the problem becomes Byzantine agreement, and the classical result of Lamport, Shostak, and Pease [9] fixes the price: with oral messages, agreement tolerating *f* Byzantine faults requires *n ≥ 3f+1* replicas. This bound is not a curiosity; it shapes every protocol that follows.

For two decades the problem stayed largely theoretical — exponential protocols, strong synchrony assumptions, no credible implementations. Two developments changed that. First, Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer [10] isolated *partial synchrony*, the timing model in which deterministic consensus is actually possible: message delays are bounded but the bound is unknown, or the bound holds only after an unknown global stabilization time (GST). Safety must hold in full asynchrony — the Fischer–Lynch–Paterson impossibility result forbids guaranteed liveness there — while liveness is recovered once the network stabilizes. Second, Castro and Liskov's PBFT [1] turned the theory into a running system: a primary-driven, three-phase protocol using message authentication, with a view-change subprotocol that replaces a faulty primary while preserving safety under asynchrony. Their Byzantine-tolerant NFS ran within 3% of an unreplicated NFS — the first credible evidence that Byzantine replication could be practical.

A decade later, blockchains demanded more: hundreds of replicas, rotating proposers for fairness and chain quality, and communication that does not explode on every leader change. HotStuff [2] answered by *linearizing* PBFT: threshold signatures aggregate 2f+1 votes into a constant-size quorum certificate, and a chained propose–vote pattern collapses the phase structure so that each view change costs O(n) messages while preserving *responsiveness* — progress at the speed of the actual network δ, not the worst-case bound Δ. It was, to the authors' knowledge, the first partially synchronous BFT protocol to combine both properties [2].

This thesis argues that a single invariant explains all of it:

> **Thesis.** *Quorum intersection* — in a 3f+1 system, any two 2f+1 quorums share at least one honest replica — is the load-bearing fact of Byzantine fault tolerance. PBFT's prepared certificates, its view-change safety argument, HotStuff's quorum certificates, Tendermint's locking rule [3], and Stellar's intact sets [4] are this invariant wearing different clothes.

## 2 Background

**Fault model.** We consider *n* replicas, at most *f* of which are Byzantine: they may deviate arbitrarily, coordinate with each other, withhold or reorder messages, and equivocate — send different values to different replicas. Messages are *authenticated* (MACs or digital signatures), so a faulty replica cannot forge another's message [1]. Authentication bounds what Byzantine replicas can do but not what they can *say*.

**Timing model.** The network is *partially synchronous* [10]: there is an unknown GST after which all message delays are bounded by a known Δ. *Safety* — no two honest replicas decide differently — must hold at all times, even before GST. *Liveness* — decisions are eventually reached — is required only after GST. This asymmetry is forced, not chosen: deterministic agreement is impossible in pure asynchrony.

**State-machine replication.** All replicas start in identical states and apply deterministic commands in identical order, so the problem reduces to agreeing on a *sequence* of commands — atomic broadcast — with one agreement instance per sequence number. A client considers a request complete after *f+1* matching replies, which guarantees at least one honest replica executed it.

**Quorums.** A *quorum* is any set of 2f+1 replicas. The threshold 2f+1 is the smallest with the following property:

> **Theorem (Quorum Intersection).** In a system of *n = 3f+1* replicas, any two quorums *Q₁, Q₂* of size *2f+1* satisfy *|Q₁ ∩ Q₂| ≥ f+1*.

*Proof.* |Q₁ ∩ Q₂| = |Q₁| + |Q₂| − |Q₁ ∪ Q₂| ≥ (2f+1) + (2f+1) − (3f+1) = f+1. ∎

Since at most *f* replicas are faulty, every pair of quorums shares at least one *honest* replica. This is the fact that lets a certificate assembled in one phase constrain what any quorum — and therefore any future leader consulting a quorum — can subsequently believe. The 3f+1 bound is tight for deterministic agreement [9]; message authentication does not lower it.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is analytical, not experimental. We reconstruct the safety arguments of PBFT [1] and HotStuff [2] from their quorum structure, verify each technical claim against the primary sources, and compare the protocols inside the common propose–vote framework that the HotStuff authors themselves propose [2]. Complexity is counted in the regime where the protocols differ most: *leader replacement under faults*. Empirical claims — throughput, latency, deployment scale — are taken from the measurements reported in the cited papers [1][2], and formal details are cross-checked against mechanized treatments where they exist [3][6][8]. We propose no new protocol. The contribution is a unified derivation of five major designs — PBFT, HotStuff, Fast-HotStuff, Tendermint, and Stellar's SCP — from a single invariant, with the exact point at which each design pays for linearity identified.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The invariant, stated generally

The intersection theorem of Section 2 is usually invoked in one direction: *safety*. If a value *v* is certified by a quorum (2f+1 replicas vouch for it) and a later quorum certifies *v′*, their intersection contains an honest replica that vouched for both — so *v = v′*, provided honest replicas never vouch for two different values in the same instance. Every BFT protocol is therefore a machine for ensuring that honest replicas *do not equivocate within an instance*, while the invariant propagates the consequence *across* instances, views, and leaders.

The invariant is also usefully executable. The following check is trivial, but it is the entire safety argument of the field compressed into a few lines:

```python
from itertools import combinations

def quorum_intersection_holds(n: int, f: int) -> bool:
    """Every two (2f+1)-quorums of an n=3f+1 system share >= f+1 replicas."""
    assert n >= 3 * f + 1
    q = 2 * f + 1
    quorums = [set(c) for c in combinations(range(n), q)]
    return all(len(a & b) >= f + 1 for a in quorums for b in quorums)

assert quorum_intersection_holds(4, 1)
assert quorum_intersection_holds(7, 2)
```

A TLA⁺-style rendering makes the non-emptiness of the honest intersection explicit:

```tla
---------------- MODULE QuorumBFT ----------------
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Replicas, Faulty, F
ASSUME /\ Faulty \subseteq Replicas
       /\ Cardinality(Faulty) <= F
       /\ Cardinality(Replicas) = 3*F + 1
Quorum == {Q \in SUBSET Replicas : Cardinality(Q) = 2*F + 1}
THEOREM QuorumIntersection ==
  \A Q1, Q2 \in Quorum :
    \E h \in (Q1 \cap Q2) : h \notin Faulty
=================================================
```

Everything that follows is an elaboration of this theorem under different engineering constraints.

### 4.2 PBFT: three-phase commit and the view-change protocol

PBFT [1] is primary-driven. In view *v*, the primary *p = v mod n* orders client requests; backups verify. The normal case has three phases:

1. **Pre-prepare.** The primary multicasts ⟨PRE-PREPARE, *v*, *n*, *d*⟩ binding sequence number *n* to digest *d* of the client's request.
2. **Prepare.** Each backup validates the pre-prepare (signature, view, sequence-number bounds, no conflicting pre-prepare) and multicasts ⟨PREPARE, *v*, *n*, *d*, *i*⟩. A replica considers the request *prepared* when it holds the pre-prepare plus 2f+1 matching prepares (its own included) — a *prepared certificate*.
3. **Commit.** Each replica multicasts ⟨COMMIT, *v*, *n*, *d*, *i*⟩ upon preparation. It *commits* — executes the request and replies to the client — upon 2f+1 matching commits.

Why two quorum rounds and not one? Preparation alone does not survive a primary failure: a prepared-but-uncommitted request might be known to only a few honest replicas. The commit phase ensures that if *any* honest replica commits, then 2f+1 replicas (a quorum) are prepared — and that quorum's honest members carry the prepared certificate into the view change.

**View change.** When backups time out waiting for the primary, replica *i* multicasts ⟨VIEW-CHANGE, *v+1*, *C*, *P*, *i*⟩, where *C* covers the latest stable checkpoint and *P* contains a prepared certificate for every sequence number prepared above it. The new primary *p′ = (v+1) mod n* waits for 2f+1 valid view-change messages and multicasts ⟨NEW-VIEW, *v+1*, *V*, *O*⟩, where *V* is that set and *O* re-proposes, as pre-prepares, every request prepared in *V* (plus null requests to fill gaps). Backups verify *O* against *V* and resume the three-phase protocol in the new view.

> **Theorem (PBFT view-change safety, sketch).** No request committed in view *v* is lost or reordered in any later view.

*Argument.* Committing requires a quorum *Qc* of commits, so at least *f+1* honest replicas are prepared. The new primary's set *V* is a quorum *Qv* of view-change messages. By intersection, *Qc ∩ Qv* contains an honest replica *h*, and *h*'s view-change message carries the prepared certificate — forcing the new primary to re-propose the request at the same sequence number. Analyses of PBFT's fast-path variants [6] show how delicate this forwarding is: published optimistic protocols admit safety violations with just four replicas and one or two view changes, precisely when a prepared certificate fails to reach every future quorum.

The price is communication: view-change messages are multicast all-to-all (O(n²) messages), and each carries O(n) prepared state, so a view change moves O(n³) bits in the worst case [7]. The normal case is quadratic as well. PBFT is *responsive* — no phase waits for Δ — but its leader replacement is heavy. That weight is what HotStuff removes.

### 4.3 HotStuff: threshold signatures, rotating leaders, and the three-chain rule

HotStuff [2] keeps PBFT's quorum logic and replaces its communication pattern. Two ideas do the work:

- **Threshold signatures.** 2f+1 partial signatures on a value combine into a single constant-size *quorum certificate* (QC). A leader collects votes, aggregates them locally, and broadcasts one QC — each phase is one leader-to-all broadcast plus all-to-leader votes: O(n) messages.
- **Chaining.** In pipelined (chained) HotStuff, phases fold into a uniform propose–vote round. A block *B* carries the QC of its parent; voting for *B* simultaneously advances the parent's phase. Leaders rotate every view, so leader replacement *is* the normal path — there is no separate, expensive view-change subprotocol. The new leader simply collects the highest QC it can find (a new-view message of O(n) size carrying one QC, not 2f+1 certificate bundles) and extends it.

Safety is then expressed as a chain rule rather than a certificate-forwarding argument:

> **Theorem (HotStuff three-chain commit rule).** A replica commits a block *B\** when it observes three adjacent certified blocks *B₁ ← B₂ ← B₃* (consecutive views) extending *B\**. No conflicting block can then be committed.

*Why it works.* Each certified block embeds a QC — 2f+1 votes. Two conflicting certified blocks in the same view would require an honest replica to vote twice in one view, which the voting rule forbids; conflicting QCs in different views are ruled out because any QC's quorum intersects the next view's quorum in an honest replica that only votes to extend the locked chain. The three links supply the same "two uninterrupted rounds plus one" structure as PBFT's prepare/commit pair, but expressed over the chain [2][5].

HotStuff is *responsive* (each phase completes on 2f+1 votes, never waiting for Δ) and *linear* (O(n) messages per view, including leader replacement) — the first partially synchronous protocol to combine both [2]. The cost is latency in views: a block commits only after three chained views, and cascading faulty leaders degrade total communication to O(f·n) [7]. Fast-HotStuff [5] replaces the three-chain rule with a two-chain rule, cutting a round trip of latency while preserving the invariant; pipelined deployments (notably DiemBFT, formerly LibraBFT, and Flow) validated the design at scale [7].

### 4.4 Federated and gossip-based trust: Stellar and Tendermint

The invariant survives even when the uniform 3f+1 membership assumption is dropped.

**Stellar's federated Byzantine agreement.** In SCP [4], each node declares *quorum slices* — sets of nodes it trusts — and a *quorum* is any set containing a slice of each of its members. There is no global *n* or *f*. Safety is recovered conditionally: an *intact set* is a set of well-behaved nodes that (a) is itself a quorum and (b) has the quorum-intersection property internally. Mazières proves that nodes in an intact set cannot disagree *provided* the slices are configured so that intersection holds — the same theorem, with the quorum system now an emergent property of pairwise trust choices rather than a parameter. Liveness additionally requires *quorum availability*. The price of open membership is that safety becomes a property of configuration, not of the protocol alone.

**Tendermint's gossip consensus.** Tendermint [3] keeps the 3f+1 / 2f+1 structure but replaces PBFT's all-to-all phases with gossip and a simpler round structure — *propose*, *prevote*, *precommit* — requiring +2/3 voting power at each step. Its distinctive mechanism is *locking*: a validator that prevotes for a value locks on it and will only unlock for a strictly newer round's proposal, which plays the role of PBFT's prepared certificate across rounds. Tendermint adds *accountability*: if safety is ever violated, at least 1/3 of validators must have provably misbehaved, with signed messages as irrefutable evidence — a property mechanically checked in the Ivy language [8]. Gossip keeps per-node load low at the cost of extra latency hops relative to HotStuff's star topology.

**Comparison.**

| Protocol | Quorum | Leader-change cost | Responsiveness | Trust model |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [1] | 2f+1 of 3f+1 | O(n²) msgs, O(n³) bits | Yes | Fixed membership |
| HotStuff [2] | 2f+1 of 3f+1 (threshold sigs) | O(n) | Yes | Fixed membership |
| Fast-HotStuff [5] | 2f+1 of 3f+1 | O(n) | Yes, −1 round trip | Fixed membership |
| Tendermint [3] | 2/3+ voting power | Gossip rounds | Partial (round timeouts) | Fixed validator set, stake |
| SCP [4] | Slices → emergent quorums | Federated voting | Eventual | Open, pairwise trust |

## 5 Empirical and Formal Evidence

**Proofs.** The safety arguments above are not folklore. Tendermint's core — including its fork-accountability guarantee — has been mechanically checked in Ivy: the protocol model is roughly 350 lines and the accountability proof adds only about 125, covering arbitrary node counts and round numbers [8]. Analyses of PBFT's optimistic fast-path variants [6] show how delicate the certificate-forwarding argument is: several published fast protocols admit safety violations with just four replicas and one or two view changes, precisely because they break the invariant that a prepared certificate must reach every future quorum. The invariant is unforgiving of shortcuts.

**Measurements.** Three findings anchor the engineering claims:

1. Castro and Liskov's Byzantine NFS was *3% slower than a standard unreplicated NFS* [1] — the measurement that made BFT respectable to systems builders.
2. The HotStuff deployment over *100+ replicas* achieved throughput and latency *comparable to BFT-SMaRt* while reducing leader-failover communication from quadratic to linear [2].
3. Bottleneck analysis of pipelined HotStuff confirms the asymmetry: the leader's load grows slowly (one-to-all communication plus pipelining) where PBFT's all-to-all load saturates quickly, yielding substantially higher throughput at scale [7].

**Complexity accounting** (messages per view under a faulty leader):

| Protocol | Normal case | Leader replacement |
|---|---|---|
| PBFT | O(n²) | O(n²) msgs / O(n³) bits |
| HotStuff (chained) | O(n) | O(n) |
| Tendermint | O(n²) gossip | O(n²) gossip rounds |

The table is the thesis in miniature: every design decision since 1999 has been about pushing the right-hand column down without breaking the invariant that secures the left.

## 6 Limitations

The invariant guarantees *safety*, not *liveness*, and not cheaply. The limitations are structural:

- **Partial synchrony is load-bearing for liveness.** Before GST, no deterministic BFT protocol guarantees progress — this is the FLP impossibility, not an implementation gap [10]. Adversarial scheduling can stall any of these protocols indefinitely; timeouts and view changes help only after GST.
- **3f+1 is tight.** No deterministic protocol tolerates *f* Byzantine faults with fewer replicas under the standard assumptions [9]. The one-third fault threshold is a ceiling, not a tuning parameter.
- **Threshold cryptography adds assumptions.** HotStuff's linearity rests on threshold signatures, which need distributed key generation (or a trusted dealer) and computational hardness assumptions that PBFT's MAC-based design avoids. Key resharing on membership change is its own protocol.
- **Worst-case degradation.** Cascading faulty leaders push HotStuff to O(f·n) total communication [7]; PBFT's view change remains heavy; Tendermint's gossip adds latency hops. Linearity is per-view, not per-adversary.
- **Federation moves the assumption.** SCP [4] replaces the global 3f+1 bound with a configuration obligation: safety holds only within intact sets, and intactness is a property of how operators chose their slices. Misconfigured trust topologies can silently void the guarantee.
- **Accountability is retrospective.** Tendermint's fork accountability identifies misbehaving validators *after* a safety violation — it deters via slashing but does not prevent the fork [3][8].

## 7 Conclusion

From Lamport's generals [9] to HotStuff's chains [2], Byzantine fault tolerance has been the progressive discovery of how much mileage one set-theoretic fact provides. Quorum intersection — two 2f+1 quorums in a 3f+1 system share an honest replica — is the entire safety story; the rest is communication engineering. PBFT [1] paid quadratic-to-cubic cost to forward certificates across views; HotStuff [2] used threshold signatures and chaining to pay linear cost for the same invariant; Tendermint [3] re-expressed it as locking with accountability; Stellar [4] showed it survives even open membership, as a property of configuration. The open problems are liveness-side — adaptive adversaries, responsive leader replacement under attack, and trust topologies that stay intact — because on the safety side, the invariant settled the question in one line of arithmetic: (2f+1) + (2f+1) − (3f+1) = f+1.
