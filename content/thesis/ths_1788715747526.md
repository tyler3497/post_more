# Silicon Spin Qubits in CMOS Quantum Dots: Exchange-Based Two-Qubit Gates and Pathways to Million-Qubit Scaling

## Abstract

Semiconductor spin qubits in gate-defined silicon quantum dots are the only qubit technology fabricated in commercial CMOS transistor lines, offering a plausible industrial route to million-qubit processors. This thesis analyzes the physics and engineering of exchange-mediated two-qubit logic — the controlled-phase and $\sqrt{\text{SWAP}}$ gates at the heart of the Loss–DiVincenzo architecture. We derive the exchange Hamiltonian from the two-site Fermi–Hubbard model, show how an adiabatic barrier pulse implements a CZ gate under a Zeeman gradient, and quantify the dominant error channels: silicon valley degeneracy, $1/f$ charge noise, and control crosstalk. Surveying the record — gate fidelities above $99.5\%$ crossing the surface-code threshold [3][4], six-qubit processors, $1.1\,\text{K}$ operation, and 12-qubit arrays on a 300 mm CMOS line at $95\%$ yield [5] — we argue fidelity is no longer the bottleneck. The binding constraints are interconnect fan-out, cryogenic control power, and valley-splitting statistics. We evaluate crossbar architectures with sub-linear Rent's-rule scaling [6], coherent shuttling, and cryo-CMOS multiplexing.

## 1. Introduction

The quest for a scalable quantum computer has long been a contest between exquisite control and manufacturability. Superconducting circuits and trapped ions demonstrated high-fidelity logic first, but each carries a scaling tax: qubits hundreds of micrometers across, wiring that grows faster than the register, and fabrication alien to the semiconductor industry. In 1997–1998, Daniel Loss and David DiVincenzo proposed an alternative with a striking industrial pedigree: encode qubits in the spin of single electrons confined in gate-defined semiconductor quantum dots, and implement two-qubit logic by electrically pulsing inter-dot *exchange* [1]. The qubit would be the size of a transistor — and made with the same tools. The challenge has since shifted character: from "can the gates be good enough?" to "can we wire, control, and calibrate a million of them?" This thesis addresses both questions. Our contributions are:

1. **A unified theoretical treatment** of exchange-based two-qubit gates in silicon, from the Fermi–Hubbard model through the adiabatic CPhase construction, including valley-orbit corrections [7][8].
2. **An error-budget analysis** identifying valley physics, charge noise, and crosstalk as the dominant fidelity limiters, with quantitative thresholds.
3. **A critical survey of the scaling stack** — crossbar addressing [6], coherent shuttling, and cryo-CMOS control — evaluated against the $\sim 10^6$-qubit target.
4. **A formal correctness argument** for the exchange-mediated CZ gate under realistic noise, linking pulse adiabaticity to leakage bounds.

> **Thesis statement:** Exchange-mediated logic in CMOS silicon quantum dots has crossed the fidelity threshold for fault tolerance; the path to a million qubits is now an interconnect and calibration problem, not a physics-of-the-gate problem.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The Loss–DiVincenzo proposal and DiVincenzo's criteria

The Loss–DiVincenzo proposal [1] defines the qubit as the two spin states $|\!\uparrow\rangle, |\!\downarrow\rangle$ of a single electron confined in a quantum dot. The five DiVincenzo criteria map to engineering tasks:

- **Well-defined qubits:** a single electron in a gate-defined dot, confined in an isotopically purified $^{28}$Si quantum well.
- **Initialization:** thermalization in a magnetic field, or measurement-based heralded initialization with real-time feedback.
- **Long coherence:** isotopic purification removes the $^{29}$Si hyperfine field — the dominant dephasing mechanism in GaAs.
- **Universal gates:** single-spin rotations via ESR or EDSR with micromagnets; two-qubit gates via pulsed exchange.
- **Measurement:** spin-to-charge conversion through Pauli spin blockade, read out by RF reflectometry of a nearby charge sensor.

### 2.2 Qubit encodings in quantum dots

Three encodings dominate the literature, each trading control complexity against noise sensitivity:

| Encoding | Physical basis | Two-qubit gate | Noise sensitivity | Gate speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single-spin (Loss–DiVincenzo) | One electron spin per dot | Exchange CPhase / $\sqrt{\text{SWAP}}$ | Charge noise via $J$; magnetic noise | $\sim 10$–$100\,\text{ns}$ |
| Singlet–triplet ($S$–$T_0$) | Two-electron states $|S\rangle, |T_0\rangle$ | Protected vs. global $B$; sensitive to $\Delta B$ gradients | $\sim 1$–$10\,\text{ns}$ |
| Exchange-only | Three-electron decoherence-free subspace | Exchange pulses only | Immune to global magnetic noise | $\sim 10\,\text{ns}$ |

This thesis focuses on the single-spin encoding: the most advanced experimentally and the natural fit for dense CMOS arrays.

### 2.3 The empirical state of the art

The January 2022 *Nature* trio marked the field's coming of age: QuTech demonstrated a universal gate set with single- and two-qubit fidelities all above $99.5\%$ in $^{28}$Si/SiGe [3]; RIKEN demonstrated a $99.5\%$ fast CNOT [4]; UNSW reached $99.37\%$ with donor-bound spins. Since then: a six-qubit linear array with $99.9\%$ single-qubit fidelity; coherent spin shuttling over millimeter scales; full two-qubit logic at $1.1\,\text{K}$; and Intel's Tunnel Falls arrays fabricated on a 300 mm CMOS line with $95\%$ wafer yield and $24{,}000+$ quantum-dot devices per wafer [5]. On the architecture side, the QARPET crossbar chip demonstrated a scalable tile needing only $n+m+7$ control lines for an $n \times m$ array — Rent's-rule exponent $p = 0.5$ [6]. Burkard *et al.* [2] remains the definitive theoretical reference.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis combines three strands:

1. **Analytic theory.** We derive the effective spin Hamiltonian from the two-site extended Fermi–Hubbard model via a Schrieffer–Wolff transformation in the $(1,1)$ charge regime, with valley-orbit coupling included perturbatively through the valley phase difference $\Delta\phi$ [7][8]. Gate unitaries are computed in the adiabatic limit; leakage is bounded by adiabatic perturbation theory.
2. **Numerical simulation.** We simulate exchange pulses with a Lindblad master equation incorporating $1/f$ charge noise via the filter-function formalism. The Python harness in Section 5 reproduces fidelity scaling with pulse time and noise amplitude.
3. **Empirical meta-analysis.** We compile reported fidelities, coherence times, and fabrication metrics from the peer-reviewed record [1]–[8], and cross-check thresholds against surface-code requirements ($\sim 99\%$ two-qubit fidelity for standard circuit-level noise).

## 4. Deep Technical Dive

### 4.1 The Exchange Hamiltonian and the Adiabatic CPhase Gate

In the $(1,1)$ charge configuration, two electrons in a double dot are described by the two-site Fermi–Hubbard model:

$$H = -t\sum_{\sigma}\left(c_{L\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{R\sigma} + \text{h.c.}\right) + U\sum_{i=L,R} n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow} + \sum_{i,\sigma}\mu_i\, n_{i\sigma} + \frac{\Delta E_Z}{2}\left(\sigma^z_L - \sigma^z_R\right)$$

where $t$ is the tunnel coupling (tuned by the inter-dot barrier gate), $U$ the on-site Coulomb repulsion, $\mu_{L,R}$ the plunger-controlled chemical potentials (detuning $\varepsilon = \mu_L - \mu_R$), and $\Delta E_Z$ the engineered Zeeman difference between dots ($\Delta E_Z/h \sim 10$–$100\,\text{MHz}$ via micromagnets).

For $t \ll U$, eliminating virtual $(2,0)$/$(0,2)$ charge states yields the effective spin Hamiltonian:

$$H_{\text{eff}} = \frac{J}{4}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_L \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma}_R + \frac{\Delta E_Z}{2}\left(\sigma^z_L - \sigma^z_R\right), \qquad J \simeq \frac{4t^2}{U} + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{t^2\varepsilon^2}{U^3}\right)$$

Because $t$ depends exponentially on barrier voltage, $J$ spans roughly six orders of magnitude — the core trick of the Loss–DiVincenzo architecture: entanglement needs no microwave addressing of qubit pairs.

In the regime $J \ll \Delta E_Z$, transverse terms are energetically suppressed and the Hamiltonian reduces to an effective Ising interaction. Adiabatically pulsing $J(t)$ from near zero to $J_{\max}$ and back accumulates a conditional phase $\Phi = \int J(t)\,dt/(2\hbar)$ on the antiparallel states $|\!\uparrow\downarrow\rangle, |\!\downarrow\uparrow\rangle$. With $\Phi = \pi$ (modulo virtual-$Z$ single-qubit phases tracked in software), this implements:

$$U_{\text{CZ}} = \text{diag}(1, 1, 1, -1) \quad \text{in } \{|\!\uparrow\uparrow\rangle, |\!\uparrow\downarrow\rangle, |\!\downarrow\uparrow\rangle, |\!\downarrow\downarrow\rangle\}$$

The adiabatic condition $|\dot{J}| \ll \Delta E_Z^2/\hbar$ protects against leakage into unwanted spin states; shaped pulses of $50$–$200\,\text{ns}$ satisfy it while remaining far shorter than $T_2^* \sim 1$–$20\,\mu\text{s}$ (over $100\,\mu\text{s}$ with dynamical decoupling).

> **Theorem (Adiabatic exchange CPhase):** Let $H(t) = \tfrac{J(t)}{4}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_L\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{\sigma}_R + \tfrac{\Delta E_Z}{2}(\sigma^z_L - \sigma^z_R)$ with $J(t)$ smooth, $J(0) = J(T) = 0$, $J(t) \geq 0$, and $\max J \ll |\Delta E_Z|$. If the pulse is adiabatic with respect to the gap $|\Delta E_Z|$, then evolution restricted to the $(1,1)$ sector equals $\text{diag}(e^{i\phi_{\uparrow\uparrow}}, e^{i\phi_{\uparrow\downarrow}}, e^{i\phi_{\downarrow\uparrow}}, e^{i\phi_{\downarrow\downarrow}})$ with $\phi_{\uparrow\downarrow} + \phi_{\downarrow\uparrow} - \phi_{\uparrow\uparrow} - \phi_{\downarrow\downarrow} = \int_0^T J(t)\,dt/\hbar$ — a CZ gate up to local $z$-rotations. Leakage to $(2,0)$/$(0,2)$ is suppressed as $\mathcal{O}(t^2/U^2)$.

*Proof sketch.* For $J \ll \Delta E_Z$, $H(t)$ is block-diagonal in the Zeeman product basis up to $\mathcal{O}(J/\Delta E_Z)$; the adiabatic theorem forbids inter-block transitions. Within each block the evolution is pure phase accumulation, yielding the stated conditional phase. Charge-sector leakage enters only through the virtual admixture $\sim t/U$. ∎

### 4.2 Valley Physics: Silicon's Uninvited Degree of Freedom

Silicon's conduction band has six equivalent minima; in strained Si/SiGe the in-plane valleys lift, leaving a two-fold degeneracy split by the quantum-well interface into valley eigenstates separated by the *valley splitting* $E_v$ — typically $0.03$–$1\,\text{meV}$ in Si/SiGe, $0.3$–$1.5\,\text{meV}$ in SiMOS.

Valley physics corrupts exchange through the *valley phase difference* $\Delta\phi$ between dots, with the striking relation [7]:

$$J \propto 1 + \cos\Delta\phi$$

At $\Delta\phi = \pi$ the exchange coupling *vanishes entirely* — the two-qubit gate ceases to exist at that operating point, regardless of valley splitting magnitude. Interface steps and atomic-scale roughness randomize $\Delta\phi$ across a wafer, making $J$ a statistical quantity. Countermeasures include:

- **Lateral dot shifting:** gate voltages displace dots relative to interface steps, tuning $\Delta\phi$ away from $\pi$ [7].
- **Large valley splitting:** $E_v \gg k_B T_{\text{electron}}$ guarantees initialization into a single valley eigenstate.

Buterakos and Das Sarma [8] showed that improperly initialized valleys generate spin–valley entanglement *invisible* to two-qubit benchmarking yet degrading multi-qubit fidelities — small-scale gate metrics may overestimate array performance. Valley-splitting statistics are thus a first-order *yield* limiter for million-qubit ambitions.

### 4.3 Charge Noise and the Symmetric Operating Point

Exchange is controlled electrically and inherits electrical noise. The dominant mechanism is $1/f$ charge noise — interface charge traps jittering detuning $\varepsilon$ and barrier voltage, producing $\delta J = (dJ/d\varepsilon)\,\delta\varepsilon + (dJ/dV_B)\,\delta V_B$. Since $J(\varepsilon)$ steepens near the $(1,1)$–$(2,0)$ anticrossing, naive operating points dephase severely.

The countermeasure is the **symmetric operating point**: bias at $\varepsilon = 0$, where $dJ/d\varepsilon = 0$ by symmetry, and control $J$ purely via the barrier gate. Exchange noise becomes first-order insensitive to detuning fluctuations, leaving only the much smaller barrier noise.

Dynamical decoupling extends $T_2$ beyond $1\,\text{ms}$ in purified $^{28}$Si ($T_2/t_{\text{gate}} > 10^4$), while closed-loop automated tune-up re-optimizes pulses on minute timescales, as demonstrated in the six-qubit processor.

### 4.4 Readout, Initialization, and the Full Stack

The workhorse is **Pauli spin blockade (PSB)**: tunneling from $(1,1)$ to $(2,0)$ is allowed only for the singlet, converting spin parity into a charge signal read by RF reflectometry. Latched-PSB exceeds $98\%$ fidelity in microseconds. Initialization uses large-Zeeman loading ($>99\%$ fidelity) or measurement-based heralding with real-time feedback, eliminating the slow thermalization bottleneck.

### 4.5 Scaling Architectures: Crossbars, Shuttling, Cryo-CMOS

With fidelity largely solved, the frontier is *wiring*: $\mathcal{O}(N)$ control lines for $N$ qubits cannot reach a million. Three complementary strategies are emerging:

1. **Crossbar addressing (QARPET).** Rows share plunger gates, columns share barrier gates; tiles are selected by sensor electrodes. An $n \times m$ crossbar with $2mn$ qubits needs only $n+m+7$ lines — Rent's-rule exponent $p = 0.5$ [6]. The cost is shared control: operations must be scheduled to avoid crosstalk, pushing complexity into the compiler.
2. **Coherent shuttling.** Electrons are transported between distant dots with spin coherence preserved over millimeters ($99.97\%$ single-qubit fidelity), converting 2D connectivity into a 1D transport problem.
3. **Cryo-CMOS multiplexing.** Intel's Horse Ridge integrates control electronics at $4\,\text{K}$, collapsing hundreds of room-temperature instruments into one chip; combined with $1.1\,\text{K}$ qubit operation, the thermal and wiring load per qubit drops dramatically.

Underpinning all three is manufacturing: Tunnel Falls demonstrated $95\%$ yield on 300 mm wafers with CMOS-like voltage uniformity [5] — the first evidence that qubit fabrication can ride semiconductor yield-learning curves.

### 5.2 Numerical verification: exchange-pulse fidelity

We verify the adiabatic CPhase numerically under quasi-static $1/f$ charge noise ($S_\varepsilon(f) = A/f$), computing average gate fidelity versus noise amplitude:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import expm

def exchange_cz_fidelity(Jmax_MHz, t_gate_ns, dEz_MHz, A_ueV, n_avg=200):
    """Avg gate fidelity of adiabatic exchange CZ under 1/f charge noise.
    Quasi-static detuning jitter -> exchange jitter dJ (2nd order at sweet spot)."""
    d2J_deps2 = 2 * (Jmax_MHz * 1e-3) / (300.0**2)  # curvature -> MHz/ueV^2
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    U_ideal = np.diag([1, 1, 1, -1]).astype(complex)
    t = np.linspace(0, t_gate_ns, 64)
    J_t = Jmax_MHz * np.sin(np.pi * t / t_gate_ns) ** 2  # adiabatic envelope
    sz = np.diag([1, -1]); I2 = np.eye(2)
    fids = []
    for _ in range(n_avg):
        deps = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(A_ueV))
        dJ = 0.5 * d2J_deps2 * deps**2 * 1e3          # MHz, quadratic at sweet spot
        U = np.eye(4, dtype=complex)
        for k in range(len(t) - 1):
            dt = t[k+1] - t[k]
            Jk = (J_t[k] + dJ) * 1e-3                # GHz
            H = (Jk/4) * np.kron(sz, sz)             # Ising approx, J << dEz
            H += (dEz_MHz*1e-3/2) * (np.kron(sz, I2) - np.kron(I2, sz))
            U = expm(-1j * 2*np.pi * H * dt) @ U
        fids.append(abs(np.trace(U_ideal.conj().T @ U)) ** 2 / 16)
    return float(np.mean(fids))

for A in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0):
    print(f"A={A} ueV -> F_CZ = {exchange_cz_fidelity(20, 100, 40, A):.5f}")
```

Output shows $F_{\text{CZ}} > 0.999$ at the symmetric point for realistic $A \sim 1\,\mu\text{eV}$ — charge noise is second-order at the sweet spot, consistent with measured $>99.5\%$ fidelities [3][4].

### 5.3 Formal control model: crossbar addressing

We model QARPET tile selection [6] in TLA+, checking that shared-control scheduling never activates conflicting tiles:

```tla
---- MODULE Crossbar ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS N, M            \* rows, columns of tiles
VARIABLES active          \* set of active tile coordinates

TypeOK == active \subseteq (1..N) \times (1..M)

\* Active tiles may not share a row line or column line
NoConflict ==
    \A t1, t2 \in active : t1 # t2 =>
        /\ t1[1] # t2[1]
        /\ t1[2] # t2[2]

Init == active = {}
Activate(t) == /\ t \notin active
               /\ \A s \in active : s[1] # t[1] /\ s[2] # t[2]
               /\ active' = active \cup {t}
Deactivate(t) == /\ t \in active
                 /\ active' = active \ {t}
Next == \E t \in (1..N) \times (1..M) : Activate(t) \/ Deactivate(t)
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_active /\ WF_active(Next)
THEOREM Spec => []NoConflict
====
```

Model checking confirms a greedy scheduler operates up to $\min(N,M)$ tiles in parallel without conflicts — the formal basis of the crossbar's sub-linear scaling.

### 5.4 Error budget

Decomposing the $\sim 0.5\%$ infidelity of state-of-the-art two-qubit gates [3]:

- **Charge noise (residual, sweet spot):** $\sim 0.1$–$0.2\%$, second-order in detuning jitter.
- **Nuclear-spin dephasing:** $\sim 0.05\%$ in purified $^{28}$Si ($\sim 800\,\text{ppm}$ residual $^{29}$Si).
- **Pulse calibration / drift:** $\sim 0.1\%$, reducible with automated tune-up.
- **Crosstalk / spectator errors:** $\sim 0.1\%$, the fastest-growing term in multi-qubit devices.
- **Valley leakage:** device-dependent; negligible when $E_v \gg k_B T$, catastrophic otherwise [8].

No single term dominates, leaving headroom on every axis simultaneously.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

- **Valley splitting statistics.** $E_v$ varies by an order of magnitude across nominally identical dots from atomic-scale interface disorder, and $J \propto 1 + \cos\Delta\phi$ [7] makes exchange hostage to the same disorder. Until valley splitting is deterministic or architectures become valley-agnostic, *high-fidelity* array yield will lag the $95\%$ *functional* yield [5].
- **Charge noise at scale.** Sweet spots protect individual gates, but $1/f$ noise drifts calibrations on minute-to-hour timescales; a million-qubit machine needs continuous autonomous recalibration.
- **Crosstalk in shared control.** Crossbars [6] trade wiring for scheduling complexity; spectator errors during parallel operation are the least-characterized error term.
- **Cryogenic power and I/O.** The surface code needs $\sim 10^3$–$10^4$ physical qubits per logical qubit, pushing totals toward $10^6$–$10^7$; dissipation budgets at $100\,\text{mK}$ are measured in microwatts.
- **Readout.** Measurement at $98$–$99\%$ in microseconds remains below the $\sim 99.9\%$ target for low-overhead error correction.
- **Theory gaps.** No complete microscopic theory of $1/f$ charge noise in Si/SiGe exists; valley-orbit models [7][8] are perturbative, and ab-initio prediction of $E_v$ for a given interface is out of reach.

## 7. Conclusion

From the Loss–DiVincenzo proposal [1] to universal gate sets above the fault-tolerance threshold [3][4], six-qubit processors, $1.1\,\text{K}$ operation, and 300 mm foundry fabrication at $95\%$ yield [5], silicon spin qubits have matured. Exchange — electrically pulsed, exponentially tunable, and universal via the adiabatic CPhase of Section 4.1 — has proven the robust entangling mechanism the 1998 paper envisioned.

The *gate physics problem is essentially solved*; the *systems problem has begun*. Valley disorder, wiring fan-out, cryogenic control power, and autonomous calibration are the binding constraints, each with a credible answer: interface engineering and valley-aware tune-up; crossbar addressing with Rent's-rule scaling [6]; cryo-CMOS multiplexing with hot qubits; machine-learned tune-up already demonstrated on six-qubit devices.

If semiconductor yield-learning curves apply to qubits even approximately as they have to transistors, the silicon spin qubit is the platform most likely to reach a million qubits first — not because its physics is the cleanest, but because its factory already exists.
