# Linear Logic, Proof Nets, and Session Types: Cut Elimination as Concurrent Computation

## Abstract

This thesis develops the Curry–Howard correspondence for concurrency that links Girard's linear logic to session-typed message-passing processes. Beginning with the resource reading of linear propositions and the exponential modalities that control copying and discarding, we present proof nets as a parallel syntax for proofs in which cut elimination becomes local graph rewriting, certified by the Danos–Regnier correctness criterion. We reconstruct the session-types interpretation of Caires and Pfenning, where intuitionistic linear propositions are read as binary session types, processes as proofs, and cut elimination as communication, together with Wadler's classical reformulation CP, which replaces two-sided sequents by one-sided ones to obtain a symmetric calculus in which deadlock freedom is a corollary of cut elimination. A treatment of the geometry of interaction shows how cut elimination can be executed as feedback between operators, giving an algebraic, deadlock-free account of composition. We close by surveying the metatheory — subject reduction, progress, termination — and the limitations that keep linear-logic session types an active research frontier.

##1 Intro

Classical logic treats assumptions as infinitely reusable truths: once *A* is proved, it may be used any number of times, or not at all. Computation, by contrast, is a theory of *resources* — memory cells, file handles, network channels, and messages are consumed, produced, and moved, and using one twice is a bug. **Linear logic**, introduced by Jean-Yves Girard in 1987, is the logic that takes this observation seriously: it refines classical and intuitionistic logic by controlling the structural rules of contraction and weakening, so that every hypothesis must be used exactly once unless explicitly marked as reusable [1]. The payoff was enormous and unexpected. Linear logic did not merely give a finer proof theory; it gave computer science a new way to think about state, concurrency, and communication.

The central computational reading of logic is the Curry–Howard correspondence: *propositions as types, proofs as programs, normalization as evaluation*. Linear logic extends this correspondence in two decisive directions. First, its proofs admit a **parallel syntax — proof nets** — in which the artificial sequentiality of sequent derivations disappears and cut elimination becomes local graph rewriting. Second, its propositions admit a **concurrent reading — session types** — in which a formula describes not a value but a communication protocol, a proof is a process implementing that protocol, and cut elimination *is* communication between processes [4][5].

This thesis traces that arc. §2 introduces linear logic, the exponentials, and proof nets with their correctness criteria. §3 develops the session-types correspondence of Caires and Pfenning and Wadler's classical variant. §4 dives into the exponentials as shared servers, principal cuts as communication primitives, the classical–intuitionistic divide, and the geometry of interaction. §5 states the metatheoretic results that make deadlock freedom a theorem rather than a hope. §6 discusses limitations, and §7 concludes.

##2 Background

### The resource reading of the connectives

Linear logic splits the familiar connectives into finer variants. The **multiplicatives** describe parallel, independent resource use: *A ⊗ B* (tensor, "both") means we have both *A* and *B* as separate resources, while *A ⅋ B* (par, "together") means *A* and *B* are available but must be used jointly. The **additives** describe choice: *A & B* (with) offers a choice to the *consumer*, while *A ⊕ B* (plus) is a choice made by the *producer*. Linear implication decomposes as *A ⊸ B = A⊥ ⅋ B*: to produce *B* from *A*, consume *A* once and continue. Negation *(·)⊥* is involutive, so every connective has a De Morgan dual, and the units *1*, *⊥*, *⊤*, *0* complete the picture [1].

The **exponentials** *!A* ("of course *A*") and *?A* ("why not *A*") reintroduce controlled reuse: *!A* marks a resource that may be duplicated or discarded, governed by promotion, dereliction, contraction, and weakening. Intuitionistic logic embeds into linear logic by prefixing reusable hypotheses with *!*.

### Sequent calculus and cut elimination

Linear logic is presented as a sequent calculus with the usual left and right rules for each connective, plus the **cut rule**, which composes a proof of *⊢ Γ, A* with a proof of *⊢ Δ, A⊥* into a proof of *⊢ Γ, Δ*. The cut rule is the proof-theoretic analogue of lemma application — or, as we shall see, of plugging two processes together along a channel. **Cut elimination** is the rewriting procedure that removes cuts, pushing them upward until they meet the rules that introduced the cut formula (the *principal* case) and then replacing them by cuts on smaller formulas. Girard proved that cut elimination terminates and preserves provability, which yields consistency and the subformula property [1]. Crucially, cut elimination is *confluent* in its essence: the computational content of a proof does not depend on bureaucratic choices of rule order.

### Proof nets: proofs without bureaucracy

Sequent derivations suffer from *bureaucracy*: two derivations that differ only in the order of independent rule applications represent the same proof. **Proof nets** eliminate this bureaucracy by representing proofs as graphs. A proof *structure* is a hypergraph whose nodes are *links* — axiom links joining dual atoms, cut links joining dual formulas, tensor and par links combining premises, and exponential *boxes* enclosing subnets. A proof structure is a proof *net* when it arises from a sequent derivation, but the deep question is how to recognize one directly [1].

The first answer was Girard's **long-trip criterion**: label every edge and follow a deterministic walk through the net; the structure is correct when every walk returns to its start having visited each edge exactly twice in opposite directions. The more practical answer is the **Danos–Regnier criterion**: choose, for each par link, one of its two premises to keep (a *switching*); the structure is a net exactly when every switching graph is acyclic and connected [2]. Later work gave linear-time correctness checks via graph parsing and contractibility [8]. Proof nets matter because cut elimination on nets is *local*: a principal cut between a tensor link and a par link rewrites to two smaller cuts, touching only the links involved. Computation, in this picture, is graph rewriting — a striking preview of concurrent execution. Proof nets pay off in three ways:

- *Bureaucracy elimination*: derivations differing only in rule order become one net
- *Locality*: cut elimination touches only the links of the principal cut
- *Parallelism*: disjoint cuts can be eliminated concurrently, with no global scheduler

### The geometry of interaction

Girard's **geometry of interaction** (GoI) goes further: it interprets proofs as *operators*, and cut elimination as the *execution formula* — a feedback equation that resolves the interaction between a proof net and its cuts algebraically [3]. Each proof net yields a matrix *M* of partial isometries describing token paths through the net, and each cut yields a permutation *σ*; eliminating the cuts sums over all alternating paths, which converges to a normal form. Execution becomes token flow, and — in the second GoI paper, subtitled *deadlock-free algorithms* — composition of operators cannot deadlock because the algebra resolves every interaction deterministically.

##3 Methodology

### From session types to linear propositions

Session types, introduced by Honda, describe the communication behavior of channel endpoints: a session type is a protocol such as "send an integer, then receive a boolean, then terminate." The methodology of Caires and Pfenning (2010) is to read such protocols *as* linear logic propositions, via a Curry–Howard correspondence with three clauses: **propositions as session types, proofs as processes, cut elimination as communication** [4].

The technical vehicle is the judgment *Γ; Δ ⊢ P :: z : A*, read as "process *P* offers session *A* along channel *z*, using shared channels *Γ* and linear session channels *Δ*." This is exactly the two-sided sequent calculus of dual intuitionistic linear logic, with the process *P* annotating the proof. Each logical rule becomes a process construct:

| Linear proposition | Session-type reading | Process action |
|---|---|---|
| *A ⊗ B* | send *A*, continue as *B* | output on *z* |
| *A ⊸ B* | receive *A*, continue as *B* | input on *z* |
| *A & B* | offer a choice to the peer | branch on *z* |
| *A ⊕ B* | make a choice | select on *z* |
| *1* | terminated session | close *z* |
| *!A* | replicated server offering *A* | accept connections on *z* |

Duality of propositions *(·)⊥* becomes duality of session types: the two endpoints of a channel must have dual types, so that one's output is the other's input. **Cut** becomes parallel composition with a private channel: cutting a proof of *z : A* against a proof using *z : A⊥* yields *(νz)(P | Q)*, two processes connected along *z*. Cut elimination then *computes*: a principal cut between dual introductions reduces to smaller cuts, which is exactly the handshake of a send meeting a receive.

### Wadler's classical reformulation

Wadler observed that the intuitionistic two-sided presentation forces an asymmetry: output appears as ⊗ on the right but as ⅋ on the left, and similarly for input. His calculus **CP** is based on *classical* linear logic with one-sided sequents *⊢ P : Δ*, gaining perfect symmetry: a single construct for output (⊗), a single one for input (⅋), each the exact dual of the other [5]. If πDILL is intuitionistic processes, CP is "Classical Processes."

```haskell
-- Session types as a Haskell datatype: duality is negation made executable.
-- The two endpoints of a channel must have dual types: one's Send is the
-- other's Recv, which is exactly the involutive negation of linear logic.
data Session
  = Send Ty Session       -- A tensor B: output a value, continue as B
  | Recv Ty Session       -- A par B: input a value, continue as B
  | Offer Session Session -- with: the peer chooses the branch
  | Select Session Session-- plus: we choose the branch
  | End                   -- one: the session is over
  | Repl Session          -- bang: a replicated server offering sessions

dual :: Session -> Session
dual (Send a s)   = Recv a (dual s)
dual (Recv a s)   = Send a (dual s)
dual (Offer l r)  = Select (dual l) (dual r)
dual (Select l r) = Offer (dual l) (dual r)
dual End          = End
dual (Repl s)     = Repl (dual s)
```

Wadler pairs CP with **GV** ("Good Variation"), a linear functional language with session types in the style of Gay and Vasconcelos [6], and gives a translation from GV into CP that, for the first time, formally connects a standard presentation of session types with linear logic. The translation is type-preserving, and because CP inherits cut elimination, GV inherits deadlock freedom: *race and deadlock freedom follow from the correspondence to linear logic* [5]. Where traditional session-type systems prove deadlock freedom by intricate syntactic arguments about channel usage, here it falls out of a ninety-year-old proof-theoretic theorem.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The exponentials: from copying to shared servers

The exponentials are where linearity meets the realities of concurrent systems. A purely linear world has no servers: every channel is used once and discarded. The modality *!A* changes this by marking a session that may be *replicated*: a process offering *!A* is a persistent server that spawns a fresh copy of *A* for each client. Proof-theoretically, promotion encloses the server's proof in an exponential **box**, a subnet boundary that cut elimination must respect — cuts can be pushed into a box only by duplicating the entire box, which is precisely server replication made geometric.

Dereliction opens a box for a single use, contraction merges two server references, and weakening discards one. The session reading is vivid: contraction is a client sharing a server handle, weakening is forgetting a service exists. Caires and Pfenning show that this logical account captures both linear and *shared* session channels uniformly — the exponential, not an ad-hoc language feature, is the principled source of sharing [4]. The cost is that boxes complicate cut elimination and correctness: exponential proof nets need box-aware criteria, and the once-local rewriting must now manage duplication of whole subnets.

### 4.2 Principal cuts as communication primitives

The heart of the correspondence is the operational reading of principal cut reductions. Each pair of dual connectives yields one communication step:

| Cut pair | Logical reduction | Process reduction |
|---|---|---|
| *⊗* vs *⅋* | split into two smaller cuts | send meets receive; channel splits into payload and continuation |
| *⊕* vs *&* | select the chosen branch | label selection meets branch; continue with chosen arm |
| *1* vs *⊥* | both disappear | close meets wait; both endpoints terminate |
| *!* vs *?* | duplicate or discard the box | server spawns a copy for a new client, or the client request is dropped |

Consider *⊗* vs *⅋*: the left proof sends a channel of type *A* along *z* and continues as *B*; the right proof receives on *z* and continues. The cut between them reduces to a cut on the transmitted channel plus a cut on the continuation — operationally, the message is delivered and both parties proceed. *This* is why cut elimination is computation: every reduction step is a handshake, and the normal form of a proof net is a network of processes with no pending handshakes [4][5].

### 4.3 Classical versus intuitionistic: symmetry, locality, and expressiveness

The move from πDILL to CP is not merely cosmetic. In the intuitionistic system, the distinguished "offered" channel *z : A* plays a special role, and shared channels in *Γ* satisfy a **locality** condition: a shared channel received from another process cannot itself be offered as a shared service. The classical system drops this: one-sided sequents treat all channels uniformly, so CP admits processes that violate locality and are untypable in πDILL [7].

This yields a genuine expressiveness question. Using Girard's Logic of Unity as a framework, Pérez and collaborators construct a united linear logic whose session interpretation subsumes both systems, and prove that the class of intuitionistically typable processes is *strictly included* in the class of classically typable ones [7]. The classical reading is more permissive; the intuitionistic reading enforces a discipline on shared channels. Which is preferable depends on the goal: maximal expressiveness favors CP, while the locality invariant of πDILL gives stronger guarantees about where shared services may appear. Either way, the comparison is only possible because both systems expose their logic so explicitly — a triumph of the propositions-as-sessions methodology.

### 4.4 The geometry of interaction as an execution model

The geometry of interaction supplies the missing *dynamics*. Sequent calculus tells us which reductions are legal; proof nets make them local; GoI makes them *algebraic*. A process network becomes a matrix of token paths; composing two networks along a channel is matrix multiplication with feedback; the execution formula sums over all possible alternating paths, and its convergence *is* termination of the communication [3].

This matters for deadlock freedom in a deep sense. Syntactic deadlock-freedom proofs argue that no cyclic waiting dependency can arise among well-typed processes — a global, combinatorial argument. The GoI replaces it with a local, algebraic one: feedback between operators always resolves, because the underlying mathematics has no notion of "waiting forever." Mackie's GoI machine turned this into an actual abstract machine for evaluation, and the token-guided graph-rewriting tradition it spawned remains one of the cleanest bridges between proof theory and implementation [3].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The "empirical" content of this correspondence is metatheoretic: theorems, proved once about the logic, that transfer automatically to every well-typed process. We state the core results.

> Theorem: (Subject reduction) If *Γ; Δ ⊢ P :: z : A* and *P* reduces to *Q*, then *Γ; Δ ⊢ Q :: z : A*. Typing is preserved by communication [4].
> Proof: By induction on the reduction. Communication steps are exactly principal cut reductions, which the cut-elimination argument shows preserve the proved sequent; structural steps correspond to commuting conversions, which preserve typing by the admissibility of the permuted rules.

> Theorem: (Progress / deadlock freedom) A well-typed closed process is either inactive or can reduce. In CP: if *⊢ P : ·* then either *P* is the terminated process or *P → Q* for some *Q* [5].
> Proof: A closed process corresponds to a proof of the empty sequent. If the proof contains a cut, some cut is principal or can be made principal by commuting conversions, yielding a reduction. If it is cut-free, the empty sequent forces the process to be inactive. No stuck intermediate state exists, because every cut either reduces or exposes a communication — there is no third option.

> Theorem: (Termination) Cut elimination on well-typed processes terminates; every reduction sequence reaches a normal form [1][5].
> Proof: Inherited from strong normalization of cut elimination in linear logic. The exponentials are the delicate case: box duplication is measured by a multiset ordering that decreases even when a cut replicates an entire server.

> Theorem: (GV to CP translation) The translation from GV into CP preserves typing and reflects reduction: well-typed GV programs translate to well-typed CP processes whose communications simulate the source program's steps [5][6].
> Proof: By induction on GV typing derivations, mapping each session construct to its CP proof term; operational correspondence follows because each GV reduction is realized by a sequence of principal cut reductions in the image.

These results have been mechanized and extended: the Caires–Pfenning–Toninho journal version gives the full intuitionistic and classical developments with detailed operational correspondence [4], and the united-logic comparison pins down the exact expressiveness relationship [7].

##6 Limitations

1. **Binary sessions only.** The correspondence handles dyadic communication beautifully, but multiparty protocols — where three or more roles coordinate — require global types and choreographies that go beyond multiplicative linear logic. Extending propositions-as-sessions to multiparty settings remains partial.
2. **No native recursion.** Finitary linear logic describes finite protocols; realistic servers run forever. Recursive session types are added as an extra-logical fixed point, and their metatheory (productivity, fairness) is developed separately rather than inherited from cut elimination.
3. **The classical–intuitionistic gap.** CP's extra expressiveness comes at the price of the locality invariant; πDILL's discipline restricts which programs typecheck. There is no consensus on which trade-off is right for language design [7].
4. **Proof-net correctness beyond the multiplicatives.** The Danos–Regnier criterion is elegant for MLL, but additives need slices or boxes and exponentials need box-aware criteria; full correctness checking for realistic fragments is subtler and costlier than the linear-time MLL story [8].
5. **Adoption.** Session-typed languages in this tradition — SILL, SePi, C0 — exist and are elegant, but mainstream concurrent programming still relies on dynamically checked or informal protocols. The gap between a beautiful metatheory and industrial practice has not closed.

##7 Conclusion

The propositions-as-sessions correspondence is the Curry–Howard isomorphism grown up and gone concurrent. Girard's linear logic gave us resources instead of truths, the exponentials gave us controlled reuse, and proof nets gave us proofs as graphs whose cut elimination is local rewriting [1][2]. The geometry of interaction then showed that this rewriting is the feedback of operators — execution as algebra, deadlock-free by construction [3]. Caires and Pfenning turned the logic sideways into a type system for processes, Wadler symmetrized it into classical processes, and Gay and Vasconcelos's functional session types found their logical home through the GV translation [4][5][6].

The deepest lesson is methodological: *deadlock freedom need not be proved — it can be inherited*. When processes are proofs and communication is cut elimination, the theorems of proof theory become theorems about concurrent programs for free. Subject reduction, progress, and termination transfer across the correspondence without re-proof. That is the promise that keeps this line of work alive: a concurrent program whose correctness is not an accident of careful coding but a corollary of logic.
