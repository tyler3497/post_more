# Stellarator Optimization for Magnetic Confinement Fusion: From Modular Coil Geometry to Neoclassical and Turbulent Transport in Wendelstein 7-X

## Abstract

The stellarator replaces the tokamak's inductively driven toroidal plasma current with an externally imposed three-dimensional magnetic field, enabling intrinsically steady-state fusion without current-driven disruptions. This thesis develops the optimization pipeline by which modern stellarators are designed as high-dimensional inverse problems: modular-coil synthesis via the inverse Biot–Savart problem; the near-symmetry conditions of *quasisymmetry* and *quasi-isodynamicity* that tame collisionless drift orbits; drift-kinetic computation of neoclassical transport with the DKES code; and prediction of microturbulent losses with nonlinear gyrokinetic simulations in GENE. Taking Wendelstein 7-X — the world's largest optimized stellarator — as the central case study [3], and contrasting it with the quasi-helically symmetric HSX experiment and the cancelled quasi-axisymmetric NCSX, we trace each optimization criterion to a falsifiable signature: flux-surface quality with δB/B ∼ 10⁻⁵ [8], suppression of the 1/ν neoclassical regime [1], intrinsic stabilization of ion-temperature-gradient turbulence [4], and controlled island-divertor operation at ι = 1.

---

## 1. Introduction

Magnetic confinement fusion has, for six decades, been dominated by the tokamak — an axisymmetric torus in which the confining poloidal field is generated by a large toroidal plasma current. That current must be driven inductively or by auxiliary means, which forecloses true steady-state operation in a transformer-driven device and introduces an entire taxonomy of current-driven instabilities: disruptions, sawteeth, and neoclassical tearing modes. The *stellarator* sidesteps this taxonomy entirely. By abandoning axisymmetry, it generates the full confining field — including the poloidal component and hence the rotational transform ι — with external coils alone. The plasma current vanishes to leading order, the device is intrinsically steady-state, and disruptions are essentially absent [1][3].

Freedom, however, is purchased at a price. A general three-dimensional magnetic field does not even possess nested flux surfaces: resonant radial perturbations open magnetic islands, field lines can become stochastic, and collisionless guiding-centre orbits drift radially out of the device on the time scale of a single toroidal transit. Historically, the resulting *neoclassical* transport — the collisional random walk of particles on their drift orbits — was so large in classical stellarators that the concept was nearly abandoned in the 1970s. What revived it was the recognition, pioneered by Boozer, Nührenberg, Zille, and others, that the magnetic field could be *optimized*: that within the enormous space of three-dimensional equilibria there exist fields whose guiding-centre orbits are nearly as well confined as those of a tokamak, and that these fields can be found numerically by minimizing physics-based objective functions [1][2].

The optimization program reached maturity with **Wendelstein 7-X (W7-X)** at Greifswald, Germany — a five-field-period, quasi-isodynamic stellarator with 50 non-planar and 20 planar superconducting coils, major radius 5.5 m, on-axis field up to 3 T, and 30 m³ of plasma volume [3]. Completed in 2015 after more than one million assembly hours, W7-X is the first device built to test the full optimized-stellarator hypothesis: that numerically tailored three-dimensional shaping can simultaneously deliver good flux surfaces, high-β equilibrium and MHD stability, small neoclassical transport, suppressed bootstrap current, confined fast particles, and — most ambitiously — an exhaust solution via a magnetic island divertor [8].

This thesis is organized as a descent through the layers of the optimization problem, each layer introducing its own mathematical formalism, its own computational machinery, and its own experimental test.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Magnetic coordinates and the drift-kinetic ordering

The natural language of stellarator theory is *magnetic coordinates*. On any flux surface labelled by the toroidal flux ψ, one introduces poloidal and toroidal angles (θ, ζ) such that field lines are straight:

> **Theorem:** (Boozer coordinates) There exist coordinates (ψ, θ, ζ) in which the magnetic field admits the covariant representation **B** = I(ψ)∇θ + G(ψ)∇ζ + β\*(ψ,θ,ζ)∇ψ and the contravariant form **B** = ∇ψ × ∇θ + ι(ψ)∇ζ × ∇ψ, where ι is the rotational transform. In these coordinates the field strength B = |**B**| is the sole quantity entering the guiding-centre drift to leading order in the gyroradius expansion [1].

The guiding-centre drift velocity is **v**_d = (v_∥² + μB)/(ZeB³) **B** × ∇B + (μ/ZeB²) **B** × ∇B × ..., and its radial component v_d · ∇ψ determines whether trapped particles are confined. In a tokamak, axisymmetry guarantees that the canonical toroidal momentum is conserved and trapped ("banana") orbits remain radially localized. In a general stellarator, no such invariant exists, and particles trapped in local magnetic wells — *ripple-trapped* particles — drift radially outward at the full ∇B drift speed, producing the infamous **1/ν regime** in which the diffusion coefficient *increases* as collisionality *decreases*: D ∼ ε_eff^(3/2) v_d²/ν. Optimization is, at its core, the systematic elimination of this regime [1].

### 2.2 Neoclassical transport regimes

Collisional transport in a stellarator spans five asymptotic regimes in the normalized collisionality ν\* = ν_eff R/(v_th ι):

| Regime | Collisionality | Diffusion scaling | Dominant orbits |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1/ν | ν\* ≪ ε_h^(3/2) | D ∝ ε_eff^(3/2) v_d² / ν | Ripple-trapped |
| √ν | intermediate | D ∝ √ν | Boundary layer |
| Banana | ν\* ≪ 1 | D ∝ ν q²ρ²/ε^(3/2) | Toroidally trapped |
| Plateau | ν\* ∼ 1 | D ∝ v_th qρ²/R | Passing |
| Pfirsch–Schlüter | ν\* ≫ 1 | D ∝ ν q²ρ² | Collisional |

Here ε_h measures the helical ripple and **ε_eff**, the *effective ripple*, is the single scalar proxy that quantifies 1/ν transport; reducing ε_eff to the ∼1% level is the principal neoclassical target of W7-X-class optimization [1][6].

### 2.3 Turbulence and gyrokinetics

Even with neoclassical transport suppressed, microinstabilities driven by temperature and density gradients — the ion-temperature-gradient (ITG) mode, the trapped-electron mode (TEM), and the electron-temperature-gradient (ETG) mode — drive *turbulent* transport that, in optimized stellarators, becomes the dominant loss channel, exactly as in tokamaks [4]. The governing framework is **gyrokinetics**: averaging over the fast gyromotion reduces the six-dimensional Vlasov–Maxwell system to five dimensions (three spatial, v_∥, μ), yielding a tractable but still formidably expensive nonlinear system solved by codes such as **GENE** (Jenko *et al.*) on massively parallel machines [5].

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology mirrors the actual stellarator design workflow, which proceeds in four coupled stages:

1. **Fixed-boundary equilibrium optimization.** A target plasma boundary is parameterized in Fourier harmonics. The MHD equilibrium code *VMEC* computes nested-flux-surface equilibria, and a Levenberg–Marquardt or differential-evolution optimizer minimizes a weighted objective χ² = Σᵢ wᵢ (fᵢ − fᵢ^target)² over physics targets: ε_eff, magnetic well depth, Mercier stability, bootstrap current, fast-particle proxies, and island width metrics [8].

2. **Coil synthesis.** Given the optimized boundary and its surface current potential, the coil-design codes (*NESCOIL*, *FOCUS*, *REGCOIL*) solve the ill-posed inverse Biot–Savart problem: find filamentary or winding-surface currents, constrained to lie outside the plasma and to feasible engineering envelopes, reproducing the target normal field on the boundary. Tikhonov regularization trades field accuracy against coil curvature, coil–coil spacing, and electromagnetic forces [3][8].

3. **Neoclassical evaluation.** The *DKES* (Drift Kinetic Equation Solver) code computes the mono-energetic transport coefficients D_ij(ν/v, E_r/vB) by solving the linearized drift-kinetic equation in a flux-tube domain; convolution with a Maxwellian yields the full neoclassical fluxes, and the ambipolar radial electric field E_r is found from Σ_a Z_a Γ_a(E_r) = 0 [1][6].

4. **Turbulence evaluation.** Linear and nonlinear *GENE* simulations in stellarator flux tubes (via the *GIST* geometry interface) compute ITG/TEM/ETG growth rates and saturated heat fluxes; quasilinear proxies can be embedded directly in the optimization loop [2][5].

> **Theorem:** (Intrinsic ambipolarity — Helander & Simakov, 2008) Stellarator neoclassical transport is *not* intrinsically ambipolar: the radial charge flux vanishes only for specific values of E_r, *unless* the field is quasisymmetric, in which case ambipolarity holds for arbitrary E_r and no neoclassical E_r is driven [1][6].

This theorem is the reason W7-X experiments exhibit spontaneous transitions between *ion-root* (negative E_r, high collisionality) and *electron-root* (positive E_r, low-density ECRH) regimes — including core electron-root confinement (CERC) — and why predicting E_r self-consistently is inseparable from predicting confinement [6].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The modular coil design problem

W7-X's confining field is produced by **50 non-planar, fully three-dimensional modular coils** plus 20 planar coils, all wound from NbTi superconductor in aluminium jackets, carrying 12.8 kA to reach 3 T on axis, with roughly 425 tonnes of cold mass held at 4 K inside a 16 m cryostat [3]. Each non-planar coil is a unique, twisted D-shaped winding whose shape encodes the optimization targets: the coils are the physical embodiment of the objective function.

Mathematically, coil design inverts the Biot–Savart law. If **K**(θ,ζ) is a surface current density on a prescribed winding surface outside the plasma, the normal field it produces on the plasma boundary S is B_n(**x**) = (μ₀/4π) ∫ **K**(**x′**) × (**x** − **x′**) · **n̂** / |**x** − **x′**|³ dS′. The inverse problem — find **K** such that B_n matches the VMEC target — is severely ill-posed: high-spatial-frequency current components produce fields that decay exponentially with distance, so unconstrained inversion demands absurd, unmanufacturable current filaments. Regularization penalizes the current density, coil curvature κ, and minimum coil–plasma and coil–coil distances, and the art of the subject lies in choosing penalties that keep the coils buildable without sacrificing the physics [8].

The tolerances are punishing. W7-X demonstrated nested flux surfaces with measured relative field errors **δB/B < 10⁻⁵** [8] — a triumph of metrology and assembly (each coil positioned to sub-millimetre accuracy over metre scales) that was a prerequisite for everything else: a single misplaced coil module would have opened islands at low-order rational ι values and destroyed the divertor topology.

### 4.2 Quasisymmetry, quasi-isodynamicity, and omnigenity

The deepest idea in stellarator optimization is that a three-dimensional field can *mimic* a symmetry where it matters — in the drift orbits. **Quasisymmetry** demands that B depend on the angles only through a single helicity:

- *Quasi-axisymmetry* (QA): B = B(ψ, θ) — mimics a tokamak; the NCSX design.
- *Quasi-helical symmetry* (QH): B = B(ψ, θ − N_fp ζ) — the HSX experiment.
- *Quasi-poloidal symmetry* (QP): B = B(ψ, ζ) — theoretically attractive, never built.

In Boozer's formulation, exact quasisymmetry restores an invariant of the guiding-centre motion and with it tokamak-like orbit confinement [1]. W7-X instead implements the weaker but more coil-friendly condition of **quasi-isodynamicity**: the contours of maximum B close poloidally, trapped particles precess poloidally rather than drifting radially, and — crucially — the bootstrap current vanishes in the ideal limit, since all internal parallel currents except the diamagnetic current are minimized [8]. The general umbrella concept is **omnigenity** (Cary–Shasharina): the bounce-averaged radial drift vanishes for all trapped particles, ∂⟨v_d·∇ψ⟩_b/∂α = 0, which quasisymmetry and quasi-isodynamicity both satisfy as special cases [1].

The W7-X optimization criteria, as codified by the design team, were sevenfold [8]:

1. nested flux surfaces with small magnetic islands;
2. good equilibrium at ⟨β⟩ ≈ 4%;
3. MHD stability (magnetic well, Mercier criterion) at ⟨β⟩ ≈ 4%;
4. small neoclassical transport in the collisionless regime;
5. minimized bootstrap current;
6. improved fast-particle confinement;
7. feasible modular coils.

Criteria 4–6 are the kinetic core; criterion 7 is the engineering leash that keeps the optimizer honest.

### 4.3 Neoclassical transport and DKES

The workhorse of stellarator neoclassical theory is **DKES**, which solves the drift-kinetic equation for the distribution function perturbation at fixed energy (speed v) and normalized collisionality ν/v and electric field E_r/vB, returning the mono-energetic diffusion matrix D_ij. The physical fluxes follow from energy convolution:

Γ_a = −n_a Σ_j L^a_1j A_j, Q_a = −n_a T_a Σ_j L^a_2j A_j,

where A_j are the thermodynamic forces (density, temperature, and E_r gradients) and L_ij = (2/√π) ∫₀^∞ dx x² e^(−x²) x^(2i+2j−3) D_ij(x) in the standard normalization [1].

Two subtleties dominate. First, the *radial electric field* is not imposed but solved from ambipolarity, and in W7-X's low-collisionality ECRH plasmas the solution bifurcates to the **electron root** (E_r > 0), which further suppresses ion transport — a neoclassical transport barrier of purely geometric origin [6]. Second, DKES is local and linear; at reactor scale, *global* neoclassical effects (finite orbit widths, solved by codes like *KNOSOS* and *SFINCS*) and momentum transport must be checked, since the 1/ν scaling is derived in the small-gyroradius limit [1].

A compact illustration of the effective-ripple proxy can be coded directly:

```python
import numpy as np

def effective_ripple(theta, zeta, B, iota, n_theta=64, n_zeta=128):
    """Proxy for the 1/nu neoclassical transport coefficient.

    eps_eff^(3/2) ~ < (B_max - B_min)/B_0 > weighted by the
    fraction of ripple-trapped particles; D_11 ~ eps_eff^1.5 * vd^2 / nu.
    """
    B0 = B.mean()
    # helical ripple amplitude along field lines
    ripple = (B.max(axis=0) - B.min(axis=0)) / B0
    # trapped-particle weighting: particles trapped where dB/ds = 0
    trapped_frac = np.mean(B > B0, axis=0)
    eps_eff = np.mean(ripple * trapped_frac)
    return float(eps_eff)

# Wendelstein 7-X standard configuration achieves eps_eff ~ 0.01,
# roughly an order of magnitude below classical stellarators.
```

Minimizing functionals of this type inside the VMEC loop is precisely what reduced W7-X's 1/ν transport by more than an order of magnitude relative to its predecessor W7-AS [8].

### 4.4 Gyrokinetic turbulence and GENE

With neoclassical losses tamed, turbulence becomes the confinement bottleneck — and stellarator turbulence differs qualitatively from tokamak turbulence. Using the massively parallel **GENE** code, Xanthopoulos *et al.* [4][5] demonstrated two *intrinsic* stabilization mechanisms in optimized stellarators:

1. **Localization upshift.** Where the turbulence scale is small compared with the equilibrium variation scale, the strongest fluctuations form narrow band-like structures on the flux surface; surface-averaged transport is far below the peak-drive estimate, upshifting the ITG critical gradient relative to naive mixing-length predictions.
2. **Eddy suppression.** Without scale separation, large eddies are suppressed relative to the equilibrium scale, reducing the *stiffness* of the heat-flux dependence on ∇T_i.

These effects were first quantified for the QUASAR (NCSX-derived quasi-axisymmetric) configuration and then systematically for W7-X, where nonlinear GENE simulations reproduce the experimentally observed **ion-temperature clamping** at T_i ∼ 1.5 keV in ECRH plasmas: the ITG-driven flux is so stiff above threshold that additional heating power flows to the electrons while T_i saturates, matching the measurements of Beurskens *et al.* [5]. A dedicated optimization study showed that minimizing neoclassical proxies *also* reduces ITG and ETG fluxes — the geometric features that suppress ripple transport (smooth |B| variation, favourable curvature) simultaneously improve microstability [5].

The frontier is **direct microstability optimization**: Jorge *et al.* [2] coupled linear gyrokinetic calculations of the quasilinear heat flux into the stellarator optimization loop itself, minimizing the sum of the turbulent proxy and the quasisymmetry deviation at each iteration. This closes the loop between the two transport channels and points toward configurations optimized for *total* transport rather than neoclassical transport alone — the necessary step for a reactor, where both channels matter.

### 4.5 Divertor islands, NCSX, and HSX

The exhaust problem is solved in W7-X by exploiting the very islands optimization otherwise avoids. With ι ≈ 1 at the plasma edge, a chain of **5/5 magnetic islands** forms; field lines in the islands are guided to ten divertor target plates, and control coils fine-tune the island size and position. This *island divertor* — tested uncooled in OP1.2 (2017–2018) and now water-cooled for steady-state OP2 operation — is the stellarator's answer to the tokamak divertor, and its viability hinges on the field-error control of §4.1 [3][8].

The sister experiments sharpen the picture. **HSX** (Helically Symmetric Experiment, University of Wisconsin) is the only operating quasi-helically symmetric stellarator; by breaking its symmetry with auxiliary coils, experimenters directly measured the neoclassical degradation that optimization removes — the cleanest experimental proof that quasisymmetry works as advertised [1]. **NCSX** (National Compact Stellarator Experiment, Princeton) pursued quasi-axisymmetry at low aspect ratio with large bootstrap current; its modular coils proved unmanufacturable within budget and the project was cancelled in 2008 — a cautionary tale about criterion 7, and the reason modern optimization (notably the *SQuID* quasi-isodynamic reactor studies [7]) treats coil feasibility as a first-class objective rather than an afterthought.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

W7-X's experimental record now tests every layer of the optimization. Flux-surface mapping with electron beams confirmed the vacuum field to δB/B < 10⁻⁵ [8]. In OP1.2, density reached 2×10²⁰ m⁻³, energy content exceeded 1 MJ in 100-second discharges, and a record triple product of 6×10²⁶ degree·s·m⁻³ was achieved in June 2018 [3]. Critically, a 2021 reanalysis of X-ray imaging crystal spectrometer data showed neoclassical heat transport *below* the levels that had troubled earlier interpretation — direct evidence that the magnetic cage optimization functions as designed [3].

The OP2 campaigns (from February 2023, water-cooled divertor) demonstrated the steady-state mission: an eight-minute discharge with gigajoule energy turnover, and in 2025 a six-minute, 1.8 GJ run with combined pellet fuelling and pulsed heating — exceeding long-pulse records of superconducting tokamaks [3]. The upcoming OP2.4 campaign (August–December 2026) targets higher heating power at reactor-relevant collisionality, the regime in which the 1/ν optimization must finally prove itself at performance [3].

Formally, the transport analysis closes as follows. DKES-based predictive modelling with self-consistent E_r reproduces the electron-root CERC plasmas and the ion-root high-density regimes within experimental uncertainty [6]; nonlinear GENE flux-tube simulations reproduce the ITG stiffness and T_i clamping [5]; and the residual discrepancy — the factor-of-two-level mismatch in absolute turbulent fluxes — is consistent with known sensitivities to the E_r shear and to electromagnetic effects neglected in the electrostatic simulations. The chain from coil currents to confinement time is, for the first time in stellarator history, quantitatively unbroken.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Turbulence-optimized design is immature.** Nearly all built stellarators were optimized against neoclassical proxies; direct turbulence optimization [2] is computationally ferocious (each GENE evaluation costs 10⁴–10⁵ CPU-hours) and has not yet produced a built device. Whether a configuration can simultaneously minimize ε_eff, ITG/TEM drive, *and* fast-particle losses remains the central open question [7].
2. **Fast-particle confinement at reactor β.** Alpha particles in a reactor are collisionless and deeply trapped; their confinement depends on the detailed |B| structure in ways ε_eff does not capture. W7-X's ⟨β⟩ ≈ 4% stability target is untested experimentally — achieved β values remain well below it [8].
3. **Coil cost and complexity.** Non-planar superconducting coils dominate device cost (W7-X: €1.06 billion total site cost [3]). Reactor studies (HELIAS 5-B, ARIES-CS, SQuIDs [7]) consistently find that coil feasibility, maintenance access, and force management — not plasma physics — set the economic viability boundary.
4. **The E_r prediction problem.** Ambipolar E_r is exquisitely sensitive to the ratio of electron to ion neoclassical fluxes; small errors in DKES coefficients flip the root, and with it the confinement regime [6].
5. **Island divertor detachment.** Steady-state detached operation with acceptable target heat loads (<10 MW/m²) at reactor power density is not yet demonstrated; OP2 is the testing ground [3].

## 7. Conclusion

Stellarator optimization has matured from a mathematical curiosity into an engineering discipline with a verified experimental record. The logical chain — *modular coils → near-symmetric |B| → suppressed 1/ν transport (DKES) → intrinsic turbulence mitigation (GENE) → island-divertor exhaust* — now rests on hardware: W7-X confines plasmas with the field errors, the neoclassical transport, and the steady-state capability its optimizers promised [3][8]. The complementary experiments, HSX's symmetry-breaking tests and NCSX's coil-driven cancellation, bound the design space from both sides [1].

What remains is the step from physics demonstration to reactor: optimizing turbulence as aggressively as neoclassical transport [2], proving fast-particle confinement at ⟨β⟩ of several percent, and — hardest of all — making the coils affordable [7]. The stellarator's fundamental advantage has not changed since Spitzer's figure-8: no plasma current, no disruptions, intrinsic steady state. Optimization is what finally made that advantage usable. The next decade, culminating in a quasi-isodynamic pilot-plant design, will determine whether it is also economical.
