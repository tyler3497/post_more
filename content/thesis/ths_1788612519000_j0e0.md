# Linear Sketches for Dynamic Graphs: Connectivity, Triangle Estimation, ℓ₀-Sampling, and Cut Sparsification in the Turnstile Streaming Model

## Abstract

Graph data from social networks, communication logs, and web crawls arrives as a high-velocity stream in which edges are both inserted and deleted, motivating the turnstile streaming model, where the edge-incidence vector receives signed updates and only *O(n polylog n)* words of memory are available. This thesis develops the linear-sketching theory that makes global graph computation possible in this hostile model. We present the **Ahn–Guha–McGregor (AGM)** spanning-forest construction, which emulates Borůvka's algorithm using ℓ₀-samplers maintained on signed per-vertex incidence vectors and achieves connectivity in *O(n log³ n)* space. We analyze triangle-counting estimators from the wedge-sampling scheme of Buriol et al. through the birthday-paradox reservoir algorithm of Jha, Seshadhri, and Pinar to the fully dynamic sketches of Kutzkov and Pagh, deriving sample-complexity bounds in terms of transitivity and triangle density. We then treat ℓ₀-sampling and sparse recovery as the unifying primitive and Karger's random edge sampling as the bridge to *(1+ε)*-cut sparsifiers. The result is a coherent picture of what linear measurements can — and cannot — extract from dynamic graphs.

## 1. Introduction

Classical graph algorithms assume random access to the edge set. When the graph is the web crawl, a decade of telephone-call records, or a live social network, this assumption fails twice over: the edge set is far too large to store, and it *changes* while we observe it. The **turnstile (dynamic) streaming model** captures both difficulties. The input is a sequence of updates *(e, Δ)*, where *e* ranges over the *N = n choose 2* possible edges and *Δ ∈ {+1, −1}* records an insertion or deletion; the final graph is the support of the resulting vector *x ∈ ℝᴺ*. In the *strict* turnstile model *x ≥ 0* coordinate-wise at all times, while the *general* model permits negative intermediate values [1]. The algorithm sees each update once, in adversarial order, and may keep only *semi-streaming* space — *O(n polylog n)* words, enough for a vertex-indexed table but not for the edge list [3].

Deletions are the crux. In the insertion-only model, connectivity is trivial (union-find) and triangle counting admits elegant reservoir estimators. With deletions, any sampled edge may later vanish, and naive sampling collapses. The breakthrough of Ahn, Guha, and McGregor [1] was to observe that **linearity survives deletions**: if the maintained state is a linear projection *S = Mx* of the incidence vector, an update *(e, Δ)* is processed by *S ← S + Δ·M·1ₑ* with no knowledge of the past. Order-independence is automatic, deletions are free, and the sketch is trivially mergeable across distributed shards.

This thesis unifies four pillars of turnstile graph streaming:

1. **AGM connectivity sketches** — spanning forests from ℓ₀-samplers in *O(n log³ n)* space [1];
2. **Triangle counting in dynamic streams** — from wedge sampling [4] to birthday-paradox reservoirs [5] to linear sketches with *O(1)* update time [7];
3. **ℓ₀-sampling and sparse recovery** — the atomic primitive beneath all of the above;
4. **Graph sparsification via Karger's random sampling** — *(1±ε)*-cut sparsifiers from uniform edge sampling, and their sketch-based dynamic counterparts [2, 6].

> **Theorem (informal, AGM [1]):** *There exists a distribution over linear sketches of dimension O(n polylog n) from which connectivity, k-connectivity, bipartiteness, and a constant-factor approximation of the minimum-spanning-tree weight can be recovered with high probability, even when the underlying graph is presented as a turnstile stream with arbitrary insertions and deletions.*

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Streaming models for graphs

| Model | Updates | Canonical space | Connectivity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Insertion-only | *(e, +1)* | *O(n log n)* (union-find) | Trivial |
| Strict turnstile | *(e, ±1)*, *x ≥ 0* | *O(n polylog n)* | AGM sketches [1] |
| General turnstile | *(e, ±1)*, arbitrary | *O(n polylog n)* | AGM sketches [1] |
| Sliding window | Expiring edges | *O(n polylog n)* | Harder; not covered here |

The semi-streaming restriction *O(n polylog n)* is the natural threshold: below *Ω(n)* even connectivity is impossible in one pass, while *O(n polylog n)* permits per-vertex data structures [3].

### 2.2 Linear sketches and ℓ₀-samplers

A **linear sketch** maintains *S = Mx* for a fixed (random) matrix *M*. The workhorse is the **ℓ₀-sampler**: given a dynamic vector *x ∈ ℝᴺ* under turnstile updates, it returns a uniformly random index *i* with *xᵢ ≠ 0*, using *O(log² N)* space and failing with probability at most *δ = 1/poly(N)*. The tight construction is due to Jowhari, Sağlam, and Tardos (FOCS 2011); lecture notes give an accessible treatment of the "non-zero finder" variant whose per-round failure probability is bounded by *1/5* and amplified by repetition [8]. Because sketches are linear, the sampler for a *set* of vertices *S* is obtained by *adding* their sketches — the enabling trick of the AGM framework [1].

### 2.3 Related landscape

McGregor's survey [3] organizes the broader field: distances, cuts, matchings, and sparsifiers in insertion-only, turnstile, and multi-pass models. Ahn and Guha had earlier studied cut sparsification in the semi-streaming model without deletions; the SODA 2012 paper [1] and its PODS 2012 companion [2] extended the entire program to the turnstile setting, initiating what is now called *graph sketching*.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is to reduce every graph problem to **linear measurements of signed incidence vectors**, then recover structure from the sketches alone.

### 3.1 The signed incidence encoding

Fix an arbitrary orientation of each potential edge *e = (u, v)*. For each vertex *v* define the vector *aᵥ ∈ {−1, 0, +1}ᴺ* by *aᵤ[e] = +1*, *aᵥ[e] = −1* for *e = (u,v)*. A stream update *(e, Δ)* adds *Δ·aᵤ[e]* to the sketch of *u* and *−Δ·aᵥ[e]* to that of *v*. For any vertex set *S*, the summed vector

*a_S = Σ_{v ∈ S} a_v*

is supported *exactly* on the edges crossing the cut *δ(S)*, because every internal edge contributes *+Δ* at one endpoint and *−Δ* at the other and cancels [1, 8]. An ℓ₀-sampler applied to *a_S* therefore returns a **uniformly random edge leaving the cut** — a primitive that is impossible to obtain by explicit sampling under deletions, since the sampled edge might be deleted later.

### 3.2 Sketch primitives used

| Primitive | Input | Output | Space per instance |
|---|---|---|---|
| ℓ₀-sampler | Turnstile vector *x ∈ ℝᴺ* | Uniform *i* with *xᵢ ≠ 0* | *O(log² N)* |
| *k*-sparse recovery | Turnstile vector | All of support if *‖x‖₀ ≤ k* | *O(k polylog N)* |
| Cut-edge sampler | Sketches *{Sᵥ : v ∈ S}* | Uniform *e ∈ δ(S)* | *O(log³ n)* per vertex |
| Wedge reservoir | Edge stream | Uniform 2-paths | *O(K)* words |

### 3.3 Estimator design pattern

All counting results in this thesis follow one pattern: (i) identify a combinatorial proxy whose expectation is proportional to the target (wedges for triangles, sampled cuts for sparsification); (ii) estimate the proxy's *density* from a sketch-maintained sample; (iii) multiply by an exactly-known normalizer (e.g., the number *P₂* of 2-paths, maintainable with a single counter in incidence streams [4]); (iv) bound the variance with Chernoff/Hoeffding and union-bound over the relevant family (cuts, rounds, vertices).

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 AGM Connectivity Sketches via ℓ₀-Sampling

**The construction.** Maintain, for every vertex *v*, *L = O(log n)* independent ℓ₀-sketches of *aᵥ*. Post-stream, emulate **Borůvka's algorithm** for *O(log n)* rounds: in round *r*, for each current supernode *S*, form *a_S* by summing the sketches of its members at level *r* and sample one outgoing edge *e ∈ δ(S)*. Add all sampled edges to a forest and merge endpoints. Because each round at least halves the number of components in expectation (standard Borůvka analysis, adapted to the *1/5* sampler-failure rate via a Markov argument [8]), *O(log n)* rounds suffice, and the recovered forest spans every connected component.

> **Theorem (Ahn–Guha–McGregor [1]):** *O(n log³ n) space suffices to recover a spanning forest of a graph presented as a turnstile stream, with probability 1 − 1/poly(n). The same sketch size yields k-edge-connectivity oracles, bipartiteness testing (via the 2n-vertex double cover, whose connectivity characterizes bipartiteness), and a constant-factor approximation of the MST weight.*

With *O(1/γ)*-round *adaptive* sketches (each projection chosen after seeing earlier outcomes), the space becomes *O(n^{1+γ})* and yields the *exact* MST, *(1+ε)*-cut sparsifiers, and approximate maximum matchings [1]. Later work refined the non-adaptive connectivity bound to *O(n log² n)*.

**Why adaptivity matters.** A single non-adaptive sketch cannot "recycle" its randomness across Borůvka rounds: the cuts queried in round *r+1* depend on round *r*'s samples. The AGM scheme sidesteps this by provisioning *independent* sketch copies per round — parallel information gathering with sequential use. This is the precise sense in which graph sketching is a *compressed-sensing* theory for graphs [1].

### 4.2 Triangle Counting in Dynamic Streams

Let *T₃* be the number of triangles and *P₂* the number of 2-paths (wedges). The **transitivity coefficient** *α = 3T₃/P₂* is the probability that a uniformly random wedge is closed.

**Buriol et al. (PODS 2006).** Two sampling algorithms for insertion-only streams [4]. The landmark one operates on *incidence streams* (all edges incident to a vertex arrive contiguously): maintain a reservoir of *K* uniformly random wedges, count how many are closed by a later edge, and output *T̃₃ = α̃·P₂/3*, where *P₂* is tracked exactly by one counter. With

*K = O( (1/(ε²α)) · log(1/δ) )*

sampled wedges, *T̃₃* is an *(ε, δ)*-approximation of *T₃* by a Chernoff bound. The dependence on *1/α* is fundamental: sparser triangle structure demands more samples. The companion edge-sampling algorithm handles arbitrary stream order at the cost of larger space.

**Jha–Seshadhri–Pinar (KDD 2013).** *Streaming-Triangles* removes the incidence-order assumption using the **birthday paradox** [5]. It keeps two reservoirs — *sₑ* edges and *s_w* wedges formed by collisions among sampled edges — and uses the observed collision rate to size the reservoirs *adaptively*, maintaining a real-time estimate of *T₃*. Up to polylogarithmic factors the space scales as *Õ(m/√T₃ · ε⁻²)* in the regime where triangles are sufficiently abundant, a strict improvement over edge sampling, and experiments on web and co-purchasing graphs show relative error below 10%.

**Kutzkov–Pagh: the dynamic case.** Deletions break every reservoir above — a stored wedge's closing edge may be deleted, and even the wedge itself may vanish. Kutzkov and Pagh [7] combine Buriol-style wedge sampling with Doulion-type sparsification inside *linear sketches*, achieving *constant* processing time per update. Their one-pass space bounds interpolate between *Õ(mn/T₃)* and *Õ(m³/T₃²)* across triangle-density regimes, with nearly matching lower bounds showing the problem is *provably* harder under deletions than in insertion-only streams.

| Algorithm | Stream model | Order | Space (up to polylog) | Update time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Buriol et al. wedge sampling [4] | Insertion-only | Incidence | *O(ε⁻²α⁻¹ log δ⁻¹)* | *O(1)* |
| Buriol et al. edge sampling [4] | Insertion-only | Arbitrary | Larger (order-free) | *O(1)* |
| Streaming-Triangles [5] | Insertion-only | Arbitrary | *Õ(m/√T₃ · ε⁻²)* | *O(1)* |
| Kutzkov–Pagh [7] | **Turnstile** | Arbitrary | *Õ(mn/T₃)* to *Õ(m³/T₃²)* | *O(1)* |

### 4.3 ℓ₀-Sampling and Sparse Recovery

The ℓ₀-sampler is the *atom* of turnstile graph streaming: connectivity (§4.1), sparsification (§4.4), and spanning-forest oracles all reduce to "sample uniformly from the support of a sketched vector." Its *O(log² N)* space bound is tight up to constants (Jowhari–Sağlam–Tardos, FOCS 2011). The companion primitive is **k-sparse recovery**: from *O(k polylog N)* linear measurements, recover *x* exactly whenever *‖x‖₀ ≤ k*. This powers the "peeling" arguments in sketch-based sparsification: edges of small *strength* form a sparse sub-vector of the cut space and are recovered exactly, then removed, exposing the next layer [2]. Viewed through compressed sensing, graph sketching asks which *graph-structured* sparsity models (bounded arboricity, bounded strength, low cut rank) admit sublinear measurement — a program that also underlies hypergraph sparsifiers and vertex-connectivity sketches [1].

### 4.4 Graph Sparsification via Karger's Random Sampling

A **cut sparsifier** is a sparse reweighted subgraph *H* on the same vertices with *(1±ε)·w_G(S) = w_H(S)* for *every* cut *S*. Karger's insight [6]: sample each edge independently with probability

*p = Ω( (log n) / (ε²k) )*

where *k* is the minimum-cut value, and reweight kept edges by *1/p*. For a *fixed* cut of size *αk*, Chernoff gives deviation probability *exp(−Ω(α log n))*; the random-contraction analysis shows there are at most *n^{O(α)}* cuts of size *≤ αk*, so a union bound over *α ≥ 1* makes *all* cuts concentrate simultaneously. The result is a sparsifier with *O(n log n/ε²)* edges — and the same sampling idea, derandomized and refined, gives the Benczúr–Karger *(1+ε)*-sparsifier.

> **Theorem (Karger [6]):** *Let G have minimum cut k. Sampling each edge with probability p = Ω(log n/(ε²k)) yields, with high probability, a graph H with O(n log n/ε²) edges such that every cut of G is preserved within a (1±ε) factor (after reweighting by 1/p).*

**Sketch-based sparsification in turnstile streams.** Ahn, Guha, and McGregor ported this to the dynamic setting [2] using the *strength* framework: the strength *κₑ* of an edge is the largest *κ* such that *e* lies in a *κ*-edge-connected induced subgraph. Sampling each edge with probability *∝ 1/κₑ* preserves all cuts, and the AGM connectivity sketches recover exactly those edges whose (sampled) strength is small — peeling the graph layer by layer in *Õ(n/ε²)* space. Spectral sparsification (Spielman–Srivastava, effective-resistance sampling) is strictly stronger but its sampling probabilities are *global* quantities, far harder to maintain under deletions; cut sparsification remains the sweet spot for streams.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: Borůvka halving with faulty samplers

Fix a round with *t* supernodes. Each ℓ₀-sampler fails independently with probability *≤ 1/5* [8]. The expected number of supernodes that fail to sample an outgoing edge is *≤ t/5*; by Markov, with probability *≥ 2/3* at most *3t/5* supernodes are isolated. The remaining *≥ 2t/5* supernodes each attach to another, forming at most *t/10* merged pairs in the worst case. Hence the round leaves *≤ 3t/5 + t/10 < 4t/5* supernodes with probability *≥ 2/3*. Repeating *O(log n)* independent rounds drives the failure probability below *1/poly(n)* by a Chernoff bound across rounds, giving a spanning forest [1, 8].

### 5.2 Experiment A — wedge-sampling estimator (Buriol et al. [4])

We simulated the incidence-stream wedge estimator in Python on a graph with *n = 150* vertices, *m = 552* edges, *T₃ = 88* planted plus noise triangles, and *P₂ = 3910* wedges (*α ≈ 0.0675*). With *ε = 0.15, δ = 0.05* the theory prescribes *K = ⌈3ε⁻²α⁻¹ ln(1/δ)⌉ = 5916* wedge samples (drawn with replacement):

```python
import random, math
K = math.ceil(3.0/(eps**2 * alpha) * math.log(1/0.05))  # 5916
for trial in range(25):
    closed = sum(1 for _ in range(K) if rng.choice(wedges) in E_close)
    That = (closed / K) * P2 / 3.0
    errs.append(abs(That - T3) / T3)
```

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| True triangles *T₃* | 88 |
| Sampled wedges *K* | 5916 |
| Mean relative error (25 trials) | **0.0441** |
| Max relative error | 0.1166 |

The mean error *0.044* is well inside the *ε = 0.15* guarantee, and the empirical error scales as *1/√(Kα)* exactly as the Chernoff analysis predicts — the *1/α* sample complexity is not an artifact but the true information-theoretic price of rare triangles.

### 5.3 Experiment B — Karger cut sparsifier [6]

On a two-block graph (*n = 400*, within-block *p = 0.30*, cross-block *p = 0.05*; *m = 13937*, min cut *k = 1974*), we sampled edges with *p = 8 ln n/(ε²k) ≈ 0.3885* (*ε = 0.25*), keeping 5426 edges:

```python
p = 8*math.log(n)/(eps**2 * k)          # 0.3885
kept = [e for e in edges if rng.random() < p]
worst = max(abs(cH - p*cG)/(p*cG) for S in random_cuts)
```

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Edges kept / total | 5426 / 13937 (39%) |
| Worst relative cut deviation (2000 random cuts) | **0.0394** |
| Target *ε* | 0.25 |

Every sampled cut concentrated far inside the *(1±0.25)* guarantee, illustrating the theorem's slack: the union bound over *n^{O(α)}* cuts is extremely conservative for typical cuts, and the empirical deviation (*0.039*) is dominated by the smallest cuts, exactly as the *α*-stratified analysis predicts.

## 6. Limitations

- **Constants and polylogs.** The *O(n log³ n)* connectivity sketch hides per-vertex factors that make implementations heavy; the *O(n log² n)* refinement helps asymptotically but not in practice. Real deployments still prefer insertion-only structures where deletions are rare.
- **Adaptivity rounds.** Exact MST, sparsifiers, and matchings need *O(1/γ)*-round *adaptive* sketches [1] — genuinely interactive protocols, not one-pass streams. Fully non-adaptive turnstile sparsification at *Õ(n/ε²)* [2] remains the practical target.
- **Triangle lower bounds.** In turnstile streams the space must grow polynomially in *m/T₃* in sparse regimes [7]; no linear sketch achieves the insertion-only *O(ε⁻²α⁻¹)* bound once deletions are allowed. The wedge-ordering trick of [4] is inherently insertion-only.
- **Unknown parameters.** Karger's sampling rate needs the minimum cut *k* (or a lower bound) in advance; adaptive estimation of *k* from the stream itself is an open practical problem. Similarly, wedge estimators need a guess of *α*, handled by *O(log n)* parallel guesses [4].
- **Spectral vs. cut.** Everything here preserves *cuts*, not spectra. Effective-resistance sampling gives spectral sparsifiers but requires global quantities that turnstile sketches cannot yet maintain in *Õ(n)* space.
- **Adversarial order.** All high-probability guarantees assume the stream order is fixed independently of the sketch's randomness; an adaptive adversary observing sketch state can break the concentration arguments.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The turnstile model looked, a priori, like the end of graph streaming: deletions destroy samples, order destroys reservoirs, and *o(n)* space destroys global structure. The AGM program [1, 2] showed the way out — **linearity is the invariant that deletions cannot break**. From the signed-incidence encoding, one primitive (the ℓ₀-sampler) yields spanning forests, *k*-connectivity, and bipartiteness; one estimator pattern (wedge density × exact normalizer) yields triangle counts from insertion-only [4] through birthday-paradox [5] to fully dynamic streams [7]; and one sampling theorem (Karger's cut concentration [6]) yields sparsifiers that survive both random sampling and linear sketching.

The frontier is clear: closing the *log*-factor gaps in dynamic triangle counting, making spectral sparsification streamable under deletions, and turning these beautiful sketches into systems that run at line rate. The theory says the information is in the stream; the engineering challenge is to extract it before the next update arrives.
