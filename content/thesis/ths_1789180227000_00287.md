# The Geometry of Impossibility: Calibration, Equalized Odds, and Constrained Empirical Risk Minimization in Algorithmic Fairness

## Abstract

Group fairness in machine learning asks when a predictor can be called *fair* to protected groups simultaneously. We survey the impossibility theorems that answer in the negative. Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan [1] proved that no risk score can be simultaneously calibrated within groups and balanced across positive and negative classes unless predictions are perfect or base rates are equal; Chouldechova [2] independently showed that predictive parity and error-rate balance are incompatible under unequal base rates. These results explain the structure of the celebrated ProPublica–COMPAS dispute as a mathematical necessity rather than an empirical accident. We formalize the three families of group criteria — *independence*, *separation*, and *sufficiency* — quantify their mutual exclusivity, and then study the constructive response: Hardt, Price, and Srebro's post-processing for equalized odds [3], and Agarwal, Beygelzimer, Dudik, Langford, and Wallach's reductions approach that recasts constrained empirical risk minimization as a two-player game with provable convergence [4]. We evaluate these algorithms on the COMPAS and FICO case studies, trace the accuracy–fairness Pareto frontier, and conclude with the levelling-down critique [6] and the limits of oblivious fairness [3][8].

## 1. Introduction

Consider a binary decision problem: a lender predicts loan default, a judge predicts recidivism, an employer predicts job performance. Each individual is described by features *X*, belongs to a protected group *A* ∈ {0, 1} (race, gender), and has a binary outcome *Y* ∈ {0, 1}. A predictor produces either a binary decision *Ŷ* or a risk score *S* ∈ [0, 1]. The fairness question is deceptively simple: *when is the predictor fair to both groups?*

The intuitive answers turn out to be mutually exclusive. Demanding that selection rates be equal across groups (*demographic parity*) conflicts with demanding that error rates be equal (*equalized odds*) when the groups have different base rates *pₐ = P(Y = 1 | A = a)*. Demanding that a risk score mean the same thing across groups (*calibration*) conflicts with demanding that qualified members of each group face the same chance of selection. These are not failures of imagination or engineering — they are theorems.

The stakes are concrete. In 2016, ProPublica investigated the COMPAS recidivism instrument and found that it exhibited a false-positive rate for Black defendants nearly twice that of white defendants [2]. Northpointe, COMPAS's developer, responded that the instrument was *calibrated*: a score of 7 meant roughly the same recidivism probability in both groups. Both claims were correct. Kleinberg et al. [1] and Chouldechova [2] showed, nearly simultaneously, that both sides were measuring real properties that *cannot* coexist when base rates differ. The dispute was never about arithmetic errors; it was about which impossible requirement to relax.

This thesis develops the full mathematical picture. **Section 2** formalizes the three canonical families of group fairness criteria. **Section 3** presents the methodology of constrained empirical risk minimization (ERM) — how fairness is operationalized as an optimization problem with statistical constraints. **Section 4** proves and interprets the impossibility theorems. **Section 5** turns to constructive algorithms: optimal post-processing for equalized odds and the reductions approach to fair classification, with empirical evaluation. **Section 6** examines limitations, including levelling down and the critique of oblivious measures. We conclude in **Section 7**.

## 2. Background: The Three Families of Group Criteria

Let (*X*, *A*, *Y*) be drawn from a joint distribution *D*, with *A* ∈ {0, 1} the protected attribute and *Y* ∈ {0, 1} the ground-truth label. A binary classifier *Ŷ = h(X)* and a score *S = s(X)* induce, for each group *a*, the confusion-matrix quantities:

| Quantity | Symbol | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Base rate | *pₐ* | *P(Y = 1 \| A = a)* |
| True positive rate | TPRₐ | *P(Ŷ = 1 \| Y = 1, A = a)* |
| False positive rate | FPRₐ | *P(Ŷ = 1 \| Y = 0, A = a)* |
| Positive predictive value | PPVₐ | *P(Y = 1 \| Ŷ = 1, A = a)* |
| Selection rate | *rₐ* | *P(Ŷ = 1 \| A = a)* |

The standard *independence / separation / sufficiency* taxonomy organizes group criteria into three statistical families [7]:

1. **Independence** — *Ŷ* ⊥ *A*. The decision is independent of group membership. The canonical criterion is *demographic* (or *statistical*) *parity*: *r₀ = r₁*. This is the formalization of the "four-fifths rule" lineage in disparate-impact doctrine.

2. **Separation** — *Ŷ* ⊥ *A* \| *Y*. The decision is independent of group *given the true label*. This yields *equalized odds*: TPR₀ = TPR₁ **and** FPR₀ = FPR₁, and its one-sided relaxation, *equality of opportunity*: TPR₀ = TPR₁ [3]. Separation asks that the qualified and the unqualified be treated identically across groups.

3. **Sufficiency** — *Y* ⊥ *A* \| *Ŷ* (or given score *S*). The label is independent of group given the prediction. The canonical criterion is *calibration*: *P(Y = 1 \| S = s, A = a) = s* for all *s, a*, and its thresholded cousin, *predictive parity*: PPV₀ = PPV₁.

> **Theorem (informal):** The three families — independence, separation, and sufficiency — are mutually exclusive for imperfect predictors whenever base rates differ across groups.

A perfect predictor (*Ŷ = Y* almost surely) trivially satisfies all three, since conditioning on *Y* collapses everything. Equal base rates (*p₀ = p₁*) dissolve the tension algebraically. Real deployments satisfy neither escape hatch: credit, hiring, and criminal-justice data exhibit persistently unequal base rates, and no predictor is perfect. The impossibility theorems quantify exactly what must give.

## 3. Methodology: Fairness as Constrained Empirical Risk Minimization

How is a fairness criterion *enforced* rather than merely *measured*? The modern formulation, due to Agarwal et al. [4], casts fairness as constrained ERM. Let *H* be a hypothesis class and *ℓ* a loss. For a collection of *moment* constraints *Mⱼ(h) ≤ cⱼ* (each a linear functional of the confusion statistics, e.g., *|TPR₀ − TPR₁| ≤ ε* or *|r₀ − r₁| ≤ ε*), the fair learning problem is:

$$\min_{h \in H}\ \ \widehat{\text{err}}(h)\quad \text{s.t.}\quad \widehat{M}_j(h) \le \hat{c}_j\ \ \forall j.$$

Since the constraints are non-convex in general, Agarwal et al. reduce the problem to a *sequence of cost-sensitive classification problems* via the Lagrangian:

$$\mathcal{L}(h, \lambda) = \widehat{\text{err}}(h) + \sum_j \lambda_j \, \bigl(\widehat{M}_j(h) - \hat{c}_j\bigr), \qquad \lambda_j \ge 0.$$

The learner chooses a (possibly randomized) classifier *Q* over *H*; the auditor chooses multipliers *λ*. Exponentiated gradient ascent on *λ* interleaved with best-response cost-sensitive classification by the learner converges, in *O(1/ν²)* iterations, to a randomized classifier *Q̄* satisfying [4]:

- **Near-optimality:** *err(Q̄) ≤ OPT + O(ν)*,
- **Near-feasibility:** *Mⱼ(Q̄) ≤ cⱼ + O(ν)* for all *j*,

where *OPT* is the error of the best exactly-feasible classifier. This *reductions approach* is powerful because it is *oracle-efficient*: any cost-sensitive learner becomes a fair learner. The Fairlearn library implements exactly this algorithm [6].

An older, more surgical methodology is Hardt et al.'s *post-processing* [3]: given any fixed score *S*, solve for group-specific (possibly randomized) thresholds — i.e., operating points (*FPRₐ*, *TPRₐ*) on each group's ROC curve — that equalize error rates while minimizing Bayes risk. Because the problem is linear in the confusion-matrix entries, the optimum lies at vertices of the feasible polytope, and randomization between at most two thresholds per group suffices. Post-processing needs no retraining, but it *does* require access to *A* at decision time — a legal and practical complication we return to in Section 6.

## 4. Deep Dive: The Impossibility Theorems

### 4.1 The Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan Theorem

Kleinberg et al. [1] study a *risk assignment*: a partition of individuals into bins, each bin *b* carrying a score *ν_b* interpreted as the predicted probability of belonging to the positive class. Three conditions are formalized:

1. **Calibration within groups:** for each group *t* and bin *b*, the expected number of group-*t* members in bin *b* who are positive equals *ν_b* times the expected number of group-*t* members in *b*.
2. **Balance for the positive class:** the average score of positive-class members is equal across groups.
3. **Balance for the negative class:** the average score of negative-class members is equal across groups.

> **Theorem (Kleinberg, Mullainathan, Raghavan, 2016):** No risk assignment can simultaneously satisfy calibration within groups, balance for the positive class, and balance for the negative class, except in two degenerate cases: (i) *perfect prediction*, where each bin is homogeneous (score 0 or 1 and all members share the label); or (ii) *equal base rates*, where *P(Y = 1 | A = 0) = P(Y = 1 | A = 1)*.

The proof is an elegant counting argument. Let *μ_t* be the average score of group *t*, *N_t* the group size, *p_t* the base rate, and *x* the common average score of positive-class members (equal across groups by balance for the positive class), *y* the common average score of negative-class members. Total score in group *t* is *N_t μ_t = N_t (p_t x + (1 − p_t) y)*. Calibration forces the total score to equal the number of positives, *N_t p_t*. Dividing through gives *p_t x + (1 − p_t) y = p_t*, i.e., *p_t (1 − x) = (1 − p_t) y*. If *x ≠ 1* and *y ≠ 0* (the imperfect case), then *p_t/(1 − p_t) = y/(1 − x)* is constant in *t*, forcing *p₀ = p₁*. The degenerate escapes are *x = 1, y = 0* (perfect separation) or trivially constant scores. The argument is *approximate-robust*: satisfying all three conditions *approximately* forces the data to *approximately* satisfy one of the degenerate cases [1].

### 4.2 Chouldechova's Incompatibility for Threshold Classifiers

Chouldechova [2] proves the binary-decision analogue. For a threshold classifier, *predictive parity* (PPV₀ = PPV₁) and *error-rate balance* (FPR₀ = FPR₁ and FNR₀ = FNR₁) cannot jointly hold under unequal base rates. The algebra is immediate from Bayes' rule:

$$\text{PPV}_a = \frac{p_a \cdot \text{TPR}_a}{p_a \cdot \text{TPR}_a + (1 - p_a) \cdot \text{FPR}_a}.$$

If error rates are balanced (TPR₀ = TPR₁ = *t*, FPR₀ = FPR₁ = *f*), then PPVₐ is a strictly monotone function of the base rate *pₐ* (for any imperfect classifier with *f > 0, t < 1*). Hence PPV₀ = PPV₁ implies *p₀ = p₁*. Conversely, equalizing PPV while base rates differ *forces* error-rate imbalance — precisely the ProPublica finding. On the COMPAS data (threshold 5), the instrument achieves near-equal PPV across groups (≈ 63% vs ≈ 59%) but pays with FPR of 44.9% for Black defendants versus 23.5% for white defendants, and FNR of 28.0% versus 47.7% [2]. The two stories — "the instrument is calibrated" and "the instrument has disparate error rates" — are *the same mathematical fact viewed from opposite sides*.

### 4.3 Equalized Odds: Optimal Post-Processing

Hardt et al. [3] accept separation as the target and ask: given a score *S*, what is the best *Ŷ* derived from (*S*, *A*) satisfying equalized odds? The answer exploits ROC geometry. For each group, plot its ROC curve (TPR vs. FPR as the threshold varies); the achievable (FPR, TPR) pairs, with randomization, form the convex hull. Equalized odds requires a *common* operating point across groups, so the feasible set is the intersection of the two hulls; minimizing expected loss (a linear objective in TPR/FPR) picks the optimal common point. Each group's predictor is then a *randomized threshold* mixing at most two deterministic thresholds.

A crucial subtlety: the equalized-odds point generally lies *below* each group's individually optimal point — fairness *costs* accuracy, and the cost is distributed. Hardt et al. illustrate this on FICO credit scores: equalizing opportunity requires group-dependent thresholds, shifting the cost of errors from the disadvantaged group to the decision maker, who is incentivized to improve the underlying score [3]. This reframing — fairness constraints as *incentive design* — is one of the paper's most durable contributions.

### 4.4 Calibration versus Equalized Odds: The Sharpest Edge

Pleiss et al. (2017) sharpened the tension between sufficiency and separation for *calibrated* scores. A calibrated score is often treated as the "fair" gold standard: the number means the same thing for everyone. Yet calibration *plus* unequal base rates *implies* that any thresholded decision rule has unequal error rates — the score's *meaning* is fair while its *consequences* are not. Worse, common "fixes" that preserve calibration, such as group-specific thresholds, break calibration's *interpretability* guarantees: the score no longer means the same thing. The upshot is normative: *calibration answers "is the model's belief fair?" while equalized odds answers "are the model's consequences fair?"* — and the impossibility theorems prove these are different questions with incompatible answers [1][7].

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 The Accuracy–Fairness Pareto Frontier

Every fairness constraint induces a *Pareto frontier* in (error, violation) space. The reductions algorithm traces it by varying the constraint bound *ε*: at *ε = 0* (exact parity) the learner pays maximal accuracy cost; as *ε → ∞* we recover unconstrained ERM. Empirically, on the UCI Adult income dataset, enforcing demographic parity within *ε = 0.01* via the exponentiated-gradient reductions algorithm costs roughly 2–4 points of accuracy relative to the unconstrained baseline, while post-processing for equalized odds on a fixed score costs somewhat more because it cannot adapt the representation [4]. The frontier is *convex* (randomization convexifies it), which is why the Lagrangian approach can sweep it efficiently.

### 5.2 COMPAS Revisited: Numbers Under the Theorem

| Metric (threshold 5) | Black defendants | White defendants | Equal? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base rate *p* | 51.4% | 39.4% | **No** — root cause |
| PPV (predictive parity) | 63.0% | 59.1% | ≈ Yes |
| FPR | 44.9% | 23.5% | **No** (1.9×) |
| FNR | 28.0% | 47.7% | **No** (reversed) |
| Accuracy | 63.8% | 67.0% | ≈ Yes |

*Table: COMPAS statistics illustrating Chouldechova's theorem [2]. With unequal base rates, near-equal PPV mathematically forces the observed FPR/FNR disparities.*

### 5.3 A Reductions Sketch in Code

The following implements the core loop of Agarwal et al.'s exponentiated-gradient algorithm for demographic parity, illustrating how fairness constraints enter as *Lagrangian penalties* on confusion-matrix moments:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

def fair_reductions(X, y, a, eps=0.01, T=50, eta=0.5):
    """Exponentiated-gradient for demographic parity (sketch of [4])."""
    n = len(y)
    lam = np.zeros(2)          # dual vars: +1/-1 disparity directions
    Q = []                     # ensemble of classifiers (randomized hypothesis)
    for t in range(T):
        # 1. Learner best-response: cost-sensitive classification.
        #    Lagrangian tilt: weight each example by its disparity contribution.
        w = np.ones(n)
        w[a == 1] *= (1 + lam[0] - lam[1]) / (a == 1).mean()
        w[a == 0] *= (1 - lam[0] + lam[1]) / (a == 0).mean()
        h = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(X, y, sample_weight=w)
        Q.append(h)
        # 2. Auditor: measure constraint violation of the *average* ensemble.
        pred = np.mean([hh.predict(X) for hh in Q], axis=0) >= 0.5
        r1 = pred[a == 1].mean(); r0 = pred[a == 0].mean()
        viol = np.array([r1 - r0 - eps, r0 - r1 - eps])   # moment vector
        # 3. Exponentiated gradient ascent on the dual variables.
        lam = lam * np.exp(eta * viol)
        lam = lam / lam.sum() if lam.sum() > 0 else np.full(2, 0.5)
    return Q  # predict by sampling h ~ Uniform(Q)
```

Convergence theory guarantees that the uniform mixture *Q̄* over the *T* iterates satisfies *err(Q̄) ≤ OPT + O(√(log T / T))* and *violation ≤ ε + O(√(log T / T))* — the *O(1/ν²)* iteration bound of [4]. In practice, Fairlearn's `ExponentiatedGradient` wraps this loop around any scikit-learn estimator [6].

### 5.4 What the Guarantees Do — and Do Not — Buy

The reductions guarantee is *relative*: it promises near-optimality among classifiers satisfying the constraint, *not* that the constraint is the right one. If the chosen criterion is demographic parity, the algorithm will find the most accurate demographically-parity-satisfying classifier — even when parity is normatively dubious (e.g., it may *lower* selection of qualified members of the disadvantaged group to match a lower rate elsewhere, the *levelling-down* objection [6]). The theorems of Section 4 tell us which criteria *can* coexist; the algorithms of this section find the best point on the frontier *given* a choice. Choosing the frontier point — which criterion, which *ε* — remains irreducibly a *policy* decision, not a mathematical one.

## 6. Limitations

**Levelling down.** Enforcing parity by degrading the advantaged group is the most trenchant critique of group fairness in practice. Mittelstadt et al. demonstrate that standard toolkits (Fairlearn, AI Fairness 360) routinely achieve parity *partly by reducing performance for the advantaged group*, and that fairness reporting — which collapses two group-level performance numbers into a single disparity statistic — makes this invisible [6]. A fairness intervention that helps no one and harms someone satisfies the equation but arguably fails the purpose.

**Oblivious measures.** All criteria discussed here are *oblivious*: they depend only on the joint distribution of (*Ŷ*, *Y*, *A*), not on causal structure [3]. Two predictors with identical confusion matrices can embody very different mechanisms — one correcting historical bias, one laundering it. Causal approaches (counterfactual fairness, path-specific effects) address this but require strong, untestable structural assumptions [8].

**The choice of *A* and intersectionality.** Group criteria require pre-specified protected groups; fairness achieved separately on race and gender need not hold at their intersection (*fairness gerrymandering*; Kearns et al., 2018). Rich-subgroup approaches (multicalibration; Hébert-Johnson et al., 2018) extend calibration to exponentially many overlapping subgroups, at the cost of sample complexity.

**Dynamic and long-term effects.** Static error-rate parity ignores feedback: lending decisions change creditworthiness itself. Liu et al. (2018) showed that one-shot fairness interventions can *harm* the protected group in the long run when decisions alter the underlying distribution — a loan granted under parity pressure that defaults damages the borrower's future score. The impossibility theorems are statements about a fixed distribution; real deployments move the distribution.

**Access to *A* at decision time.** Post-processing for equalized odds requires group membership at inference — legally fraught in lending and hiring, and practically unavailable. The reductions approach needs *A* only at training time, a meaningful operational advantage [4].

## 7. Conclusion

The impossibility theorems of Kleinberg et al. [1] and Chouldechova [2] — and their generalization to broad families of statistical measures [5] — are among the most consequential negative results in applied machine learning: they prove that the most natural formalizations of "fair" — calibrated beliefs, equal error rates, equal selection — are *jointly unsatisfiable* outside degenerate cases. This does not render fairness hopeless; it renders it *explicit*. Every deployment must choose which criterion to privilege, at what accuracy cost, on which frontier point — and that choice is a value judgment that mathematics can discipline but not make.

The constructive program — Hardt et al.'s optimal post-processing [3], Agarwal et al.'s reductions [4], multicalibration (Hébert-Johnson et al., 2018) — gives us the machinery to implement whatever choice we defend, with provable guarantees on the accuracy cost of the constraint. But the levelling-down critique [6], the limits of oblivious measurement [3], and long-term dynamics (Liu et al., 2018) remind us that satisfying an equation is not the same as achieving justice. The geometry of impossibility maps the terrain; navigating it remains human work.
