# Certified Adversarial Robustness: Randomized Smoothing with Tight Lipschitz Certificates, Interval Bound Propagation Training, and Deterministic Smoothing Guarantees

## Abstract

This thesis presents a unified treatment of certified adversarial robustness: proving that no perturbation within a specified threat model can alter a classifier's prediction. We develop three complementary pillars of certification in depth. First, randomized smoothing a la Cohen et al., deriving the tight l2 robustness radius for any base classifier robust to Gaussian noise, including the Monte Carlo certification protocol with Clopper-Pearson confidence bounds and a Neyman-Pearson tightness proof. Second, interval bound propagation as a scalable training-time certified defense, from Gowal et al.'s original formulation through CROWN-IBP stabilization and epsilon-annealed training dynamics. Third, deterministic smoothing via Levine and Feizi's splitting-noise derandomization, eliminating probabilistic certificates for l1 threat models. We integrate Lipschitz-based certificates, showing how local Lipschitz bounds tighten margin-based guarantees during end-to-end training. Empirical results include 49% certified top-1 accuracy on ImageNet at l2 radius 0.5 and 7.02% verified error on MNIST at l-infinity epsilon 0.3. We close with fundamental limitations: the curse of dimensionality, the l-infinity barrier, and Monte Carlo certification cost.

## 1. Introduction

Deep neural networks remain vulnerable to *adversarial examples*: inputs perturbed by imperceptible noise that induce confident misclassification [7]. While **empirical defenses** — most notably projected gradient descent (PGD) adversarial training — improve robustness against known attacks, they offer no guarantee against *stronger, not-yet-known* adversaries. History is unkind to empirical defenses: nearly every proposed defense that lacked a certificate was eventually broken by adaptive attacks.

**Certified defenses** take a different stance. Instead of resisting particular attacks, they *prove* that the classifier's prediction is constant within a neighborhood of the input. Formally, a classifier *f* is **(ε, p)-certifiably robust** at *x* if

> *f(x + δ) = f(x)* for all *δ* with *‖δ‖ₚ ≤ ε*.

The challenge is to make such proofs **scalable** (deep networks, high-dimensional inputs like ImageNet), **non-vacuous** (the certified radius ε must be meaningfully large), and **training-compatible** (certification must be optimizable during learning rather than bolted on afterward).

This thesis unifies the three dominant families of certification:

1. **Randomized smoothing** [1]: convolve any base classifier with Gaussian noise to obtain a smoothed classifier with a provable ℓ₂ certificate.
2. **Interval bound propagation** [2, 3]: propagate interval bounds through the network during training, yielding cheap per-layer guarantees that compose.
3. **Deterministic smoothing** [4]: derandomize smoothing for ℓ₁ threat models via *splitting noise*, eliminating Monte Carlo uncertainty.

We additionally develop the **Lipschitz certificate** perspective [6], which certifies robustness through the margin–Lipschitz ratio, and show how randomized smoothing and Lipschitz bounds are connected through the geometry of the smoothed decision surface.

### Contributions

- A self-contained derivation of the **tight** randomized smoothing certificate, including a proof sketch via the Neyman–Pearson lemma.
- A rigorous treatment of IBP forward bounds, the CROWN-IBP [3] backward refinement, and the stability dynamics of certified training.
- An exposition of deterministic smoothing with splitting noise (DSSN) [4], the first derandomized smoothing scheme for norm-based threat models.
- A unified margin–Lipschitz certification framework with local bound propagation and end-to-end training.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Threat Models and the Certification Problem

Given an input *x ∈ ℝᵈ* and classifier *f: ℝᵈ → 𝒴*, an **adversarial example** is *x + δ* with *f(x + δ) ≠ f(x)* for small *δ*. The perturbation set is typically an ℓₚ ball: *Bₚ(x, ε) = {x + δ : ‖δ‖ₚ ≤ ε}*. Certification asks: *prove* that *Bₚ(x, ε)* contains no adversarial example. This is a **universal quantification** problem — infinitely many δ must be ruled out — which is why certification requires mathematical structure rather than sampling.

### 2.2 Exact Verification and Its Limits

Early certification work pursued **exact verification**: mixed-integer programming or complete SMT-based search over the perturbation set. These methods are *complete* but scale only to networks with thousands of neurons — hopeless for ResNet-scale models. The practical literature therefore moved to **sound but incomplete** methods: bound propagation (IBP, CROWN), convex relaxations (the convex outer adversarial polytope [7]), and randomized smoothing.

### 2.3 Empirical Defenses Are Not Certificates

PGD adversarial training minimizes the *empirical* robust loss *max_{δ∈B} ℓ(f(x+δ), y)*. It produces strong defenses in practice but **certifies nothing**: stronger attacks routinely break PGD-trained models. Moreover, existing verification methods give *vacuous* bounds for PGD-trained networks — a sobering signal that empirical and certified robustness live in different regimes.

### 2.4 The Smoothing Lineage

Randomized smoothing was first proposed under the name **PixelDP** [8] using a differential-privacy lens; Li et al. subsequently strengthened the guarantee; Cohen, Rosenfeld, and Kolter [1] then proved the first **tight** ℓ₂ guarantee, turning smoothing into the first certified defense feasible on ImageNet. Levine and Feizi [4] later derandomized the scheme for ℓ₁ certification.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The Smoothed Classifier and Its Certificate

Given any base classifier *f* and noise level *σ > 0*, define the **smoothed classifier** *g*:

$$g(x) = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{Y}} \; \mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)}\big[f(x + \varepsilon) = c\big].$$

That is, *g(x)* returns the class most likely under isotropic Gaussian corruption of *x*. The remarkable property of *g* is that its prediction can be *verified constant* within an ℓ₂ ball around *x* by merely estimating class probabilities.

> **Theorem (Cohen et al. [1], tight ℓ₂ certificate):** Let *c_A* be the most probable class with probability *p_A*, and let *p_B* be the runner-up probability. If *g* classifies *x* as *c_A*, then *g(x + δ) = c_A* for all *‖δ‖₂ < R*, where
>
> $$R = \frac{\sigma}{2}\left(\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - \Phi^{-1}(p_B)\right),$$
>
> with *Φ⁻¹* the inverse standard Gaussian CDF. When *p_A > 1/2*, this simplifies to *R = σ·Φ⁻¹(p_A)*. This bound is **tight**: a worst-case classifier attaining equality exists.

*Proof sketch.* The argument is a direct application of the **Neyman–Pearson lemma**. The worst-case classifier consistent with the observed probabilities *(p_A, p_B)* is a *half-space* classifier: classify as *c_A* precisely the region of a half-space separated by a hyperplane orthogonal to the perturbation direction *δ*. For this extremal classifier, the probability mass of the Gaussian that crosses the boundary under shift *δ* is exactly governed by the Gaussian CDF, and the certified radius emerges from inverting it. Any other classifier consistent with *(p_A, p_B)* is at least as robust. ∎

### 3.2 Monte Carlo Certification Protocol

The smoothed classifier cannot be evaluated exactly; instead, certification proceeds statistically:

1. **Sample** *n₀* noisy inputs *f(x + εᵢ)* to identify the likely top class *ĉ_A*.
2. **Sample** *n* noisy inputs to count class frequencies; apply the **Clopper–Pearson** exact binomial interval to obtain a lower bound *p̲_A* on *P(f(x+ε) = ĉ_A)* with confidence *1 − α*.
3. **Abstain** if *p̲_A ≤ 1/2*; otherwise certify radius *R = σ·Φ⁻¹(p̲_A)*.

This is a *high-probability* certificate (failing with probability ≤ *α*), which motivates the deterministic methods of §4.3.

### 3.3 Interval Bound Propagation

IBP [2] certifies ℓ∞ robustness by propagating **axis-aligned interval boxes** through the network. For layer *k* with pre-activation bounds *[l⁽ᵏ⁾, u⁽ᵏ⁾]*, an affine layer *z = Wx + b* propagates exactly:

$$[\underline{z}, \overline{z}] = \big[W^+ \underline{x} + W^- \overline{x} + b,\; W^+ \overline{x} + W^- \underline{x} + b\big],$$

where *W⁺ = max(W, 0)* and *W⁻ = min(W, 0)* elementwise. Monotone activations apply elementwise; for ReLU:

$$\mathrm{ReLU}([\underline{z}, \overline{z}]) = [\max(0, \underline{z}),\; \max(0, \overline{z})].$$

The network is certified at *x* if the lower bound of the true class logit exceeds the upper bounds of all other logits. The IBP **training loss** is the cross-entropy computed against these *worst-case* bounds, directly optimizing verifiable robustness.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Geometry of Tight Smoothing Certificates

The tightness of the Cohen et al. radius deserves emphasis: prior guarantees (PixelDP, Li et al.) were **loose** — the smoothed classifier was *provably always* more robust than the guarantee indicated. Tightness means no analysis using only *(p_A, p_B, σ)* can do better; the extremal half-space classifier saturates the bound.

Two properties drive practical performance:

- **The noise level σ trades accuracy for radius.** Large σ grows *R* linearly but degrades the base classifier's accuracy under noise, shrinking *p_A*. Optimal σ is task-dependent (σ = 0.25–1.0 on CIFAR-10; σ = 0.5 on ImageNet).
- **Training the base classifier under Gaussian noise** (Gaussian data augmentation) is essential: off-the-shelf classifiers are not robust to large Gaussian perturbations. Denoised smoothing [5] addresses this for *pretrained* models by prepending a learned denoiser trained to preserve the predicted label, converting pretrained ResNet-50s into certifiably robust models (e.g., from 4% to 33% certified accuracy on ImageNet at ℓ₂ radius 127/255).

A subtle statistical point: the certified radius is monotone in *p̲_A*, so certification quality hinges on the **Clopper–Pearson lower bound**, which tightens as *O(1/√n)* with *n* Monte Carlo samples. Typical protocols use *n = 100,000*, making certification roughly five orders of magnitude more expensive than a single forward pass — the central cost of randomized smoothing.

### 4.2 IBP Training Dynamics and CROWN-IBP Stabilization

Pure IBP training [2] is remarkably effective yet suffers from **instability**: at initialization, interval bounds are vacuous (they explode exponentially with depth), and the IBP loss provides no useful gradient. The standard remedy is an **ε-ramp**: start training with ε = 0 and linearly anneal to the target perturbation radius, allowing bounds to tighten before they are optimized.

**CROWN-IBP** [3] combines the best of both worlds: a fast IBP *forward* bounding pass with a tight linear-relaxation (CROWN) *backward* bounding pass. The forward IBP pass computes cheap intermediate bounds; the backward CROWN pass then derives tight linear upper/lower bounds on the output in terms of the input. This yields:

| Method | MNIST ε=0.3 verified err | CIFAR-10 ε=8/255 verified err |
|---|---|---|
| Pure IBP [2] | ~7.5% | ~68% |
| CROWN-IBP [3] | **7.02%** | **66.94%** |
| Convex polytope [7] | ~8.6% | — |

These are *verified* errors — strictly stronger statements than PGD empirical errors, since they bound the worst case over the entire perturbation set.

```python
# IBP forward bound propagation (PyTorch sketch)
def ibp_affine(x_l, x_u, W, b):
    Wp, Wm = W.clamp(min=0), W.clamp(max=0)
    z_l = x_l @ Wp.t() + x_u @ Wm.t() + b
    z_u = x_u @ Wp.t() + x_l @ Wm.t() + b
    return z_l, z_u

def ibp_relu(z_l, z_u):
    return z_l.clamp(min=0), z_u.clamp(min=0)
```

Note the elegance: IBP is *differentiable* (clamps aside), so the certified loss backpropagates through the bound computation itself — the network learns to be *verifiable*, not merely robust.

### 4.3 Deterministic Smoothing with Splitting Noise

Randomized smoothing's certificate is **probabilistic** (Monte Carlo) and **expensive** (*n = 10⁵* samples per input). Levine and Feizi [4] asked: can smoothing be *derandomized*? Their answer, **Deterministic Smoothing with Splitting Noise (DSSN)**, works as follows:

1. **Split** the input space randomly into a partition, then return the *center of the subdivision* occupied by the input — a **non-additive** noise mechanism.
2. Because the noise components need not be independent (unlike additive Gaussian smoothing), smoothing can be performed effectively in **one dimension**.
3. For **quantized data** (e.g., 8-bit images), the total number of distinct noisy samples is small enough to enumerate **exhaustively** — yielding a **deterministic** certificate with no Monte Carlo failure probability.

DSSN provides substantially larger ℓ₁ certificates than prior uniform-noise smoothing on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet, establishing a new state of the art, while certificate computation becomes *orders of magnitude faster* due to determinism. The conceptual leap is that smoothing need not be additive: any randomized mapping *x ↦ x̃* whose distribution shifts gracefully under perturbation induces a certificate, and quantization makes the distribution's support finite.

A related derandomization appears in **(de)randomized smoothing against patch attacks**, where structured ablation (classifying small blocks/bands of the input) admits exhaustive enumeration and hence deterministic certificates against adversarial patches — a physically realizable threat model.

### 4.4 Lipschitz Certificates: Margins, Local Bounds, and Training

A classifier with **Lipschitz constant** *L* under norm *‖·‖ₚ* satisfies *|f(x) − f(x′)| ≤ L·‖x − x′‖ₚ*. For the margin function *g_{c,j}(x) = f_c(x) − f_j(x)* between the top class *c* and runner-up *j*, robustness is certified whenever

$$\varepsilon \le \frac{g_{c,j}(x)}{L}.$$

The challenge is that global Lipschitz constants of deep networks (products of spectral norms) are **astronomically loose**. The remedy is **local Lipschitz bounds**: using box propagation to bound each neuron's activation range, then computing a data-dependent Lipschitz constant valid in *Bₚ(x, r)*. Training with these local bounds as a plug-in module (the **Local-Lip** framework [6]) improves certified accuracy on CIFAR-10 from 51.3% (prior SOTA) to **54.3%** with ReLU networks, and transfers to MaxMin activations and TinyImageNet.

```haskell
-- Certified radius from margin and Lipschitz constant (Haskell sketch)
certifiedRadius :: Double -> Double -> Double
certifiedRadius margin lipschitz
  | lipschitz <= 0 = 1/0          -- vacuous: infinite certificate
  | otherwise      = margin / lipschitz
```

Lipschitz certificates and randomized smoothing are deeply connected: Gaussian smoothing provably *reduces* the Lipschitz constant of the smoothed classifier, and the tight smoothing radius can be reinterpreted as a Lipschitz-type guarantee with an effective constant determined by σ and the class probabilities. **Gloro** and **BCP** losses operationalize this by regularizing the local Lipschitz bound during training.

There is a unifying moral: *every* certified defense is, at bottom, a statement that the classifier's output cannot change faster than some controlled rate within the threat region — whether that rate is controlled by noise (smoothing), by interval arithmetic (IBP), or by explicit regularization (Lipschitz training).

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark Results

| Defense | Dataset | Threat model | Certified metric |
|---|---|---|---|
| Randomized smoothing [1] | ImageNet | ℓ₂, r = 0.5 | **49%** certified top-1 |
| Randomized smoothing [1] | CIFAR-10 | ℓ₂, r = 0.5 | ~62% certified accuracy |
| Denoised smoothing [5] | ImageNet | ℓ₂, r = 127/255 | 33% (from 4% pretrained) |
| CROWN-IBP [3] | MNIST | ℓ∞, ε = 0.3 | **7.02%** verified error |
| CROWN-IBP [3] | CIFAR-10 | ℓ∞, ε = 8/255 | **66.94%** verified error |
| DSSN [4] | CIFAR-10 / ImageNet | ℓ₁ | SOTA ℓ₁ certificates |
| Local-Lip [6] | CIFAR-10 | ℓ₂ | **54.3%** certified accuracy |

The headline result remains Cohen et al.'s: randomized smoothing was the **first** certified defense shown feasible on ImageNet, where competing ℓ₂ certification approaches are not viable.

### 5.2 Proof: Tightness of the Smoothing Radius

We sketch why *R = σ·Φ⁻¹(p_A)* (for *p_A > 1/2*) cannot be improved. Consider perturbation direction *δ* and the extremal classifier *f** that classifies as *c_A* exactly the half-space *{z : δᵀz ≥ b}*, with *b* chosen so that *P(f*(x + ε) = c_A) = p_A*. Under shift *δ*, the mass of the Gaussian on the *c_A* side decreases; the smoothed prediction flips precisely when this mass drops below 1/2. By the Neyman–Pearson lemma, among all classifiers with top-class probability *p_A*, the half-space maximizes the adversary's ability to flip the prediction — hence the radius at which *any* classifier could flip is an upper bound on certifiable robustness, and the Gaussian CDF inversion shows this bound is exactly *σ·Φ⁻¹(p_A)*. Since the half-space classifier attains it, the bound is tight. ∎

### 5.3 Proof: Soundness of IBP Bounds

By induction on layers: the affine bound is exact interval arithmetic (each output coordinate is a linear function, so its minimum over the input box is attained at a vertex, which the *W⁺/W⁻* decomposition computes exactly). Monotone activations preserve soundness elementwise. Therefore the final interval *[l, u]* on the logits **soundly over-approximates** the true reachable set: *f(x + δ) ∈ [l, u]* for all *‖δ‖∞ ≤ ε*. If *l_{c} > u_{j}* for all *j ≠ c*, no adversarial example exists. ∎

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **The curse of dimensionality.** Randomized smoothing certificates degrade in high dimensions: the ℓ₂ radius that can be certified shrinks relative to the input dimension, and ℓ∞ certification via smoothing is fundamentally limited — the required noise level destroys accuracy before meaningful ℓ∞ radii are reached. This is not a failure of analysis but an information-theoretic barrier for smoothing-based approaches.

2. **The ℓ∞ gap.** IBP and its descendants certify ℓ∞ robustness, but verified errors on CIFAR-10 (∼67% at ε = 8/255) remain far from empirical PGD-trained performance — the **verified–empirical gap**. Tighter relaxations (CROWN, DeepPoly) narrow it at steep computational cost.

3. **Monte Carlo cost.** Standard randomized smoothing needs ∼10⁵ forward passes per certified input. Deterministic smoothing [4] mitigates this for ℓ₁, but ℓ₂ derandomization at ImageNet scale remains open.

4. **Probabilistic vs. deterministic guarantees.** Monte Carlo certificates fail with probability α (typically 0.001). In safety-critical settings, even this residual risk — and the subtleties of the Clopper–Pearson sequential testing protocol — demands the deterministic alternatives of §4.3.

5. **Adaptive adversaries and threat-model mismatch.** Certificates are only as meaningful as their threat model. ℓₚ balls poorly capture semantic perturbations (rotations, lighting, patches), and derandomized patch defenses are only beginning to address physically realizable attacks.

6. **Training stability.** IBP training requires careful ε-annealing; CROWN-IBP mitigates but does not eliminate the instability, and certified training remains substantially more expensive than standard training.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Certified adversarial robustness has matured from a theoretical curiosity into a practical engineering discipline. **Randomized smoothing** provides the only currently feasible path to ImageNet-scale ℓ₂ certificates, with a *tight* radius that no analysis can improve. **Interval bound propagation** and its stabilized descendant **CROWN-IBP** deliver the strongest ℓ∞ verified accuracies by making verifiability itself the training objective. **Deterministic smoothing with splitting noise** removes the Monte Carlo asterisk for ℓ₁ threat models, and **local Lipschitz training** shows that explicit smoothness regularization is a powerful plug-in across architectures.

The unifying principle is worth restating: a certificate is a *proof*, and proofs require structure — noise with a known distribution, intervals with sound arithmetic, or functions with bounded variation. The central open challenge is closing the verified–empirical gap without sacrificing scalability: certificates that are simultaneously tight, cheap, deterministic, and valid for realistic threat models. Until then, certified defenses remain the only honest answer to the adversarial arms race — they are the defenses that say, mathematically, what they guarantee.
