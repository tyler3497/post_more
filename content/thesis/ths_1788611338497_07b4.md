# Post-Quantum TLS 1.3 Migration: Hybrid X25519/ML-KEM Key Exchange, Lattice-Based Signature Authentication, and the KEMTLS Alternative

## Abstract

The arrival of NIST's first finalized post-quantum standards — **FIPS 203** (ML-KEM), **FIPS 204** (ML-DSA), and **FIPS 205** (SLH-DSA), published August 13, 2024 — moved post-quantum TLS 1.3 from experiment to migration. This thesis analyzes the hybrid transition design converging across the IETF, browsers, and CDN operators: *concatenated hybrid key exchange* (X25519 + ML-KEM-768), lattice-based certificate authentication (ML-DSA, with Falcon as FN-DSA in draft FIPS 206 and SLH-DSA as hash-based backup), the *fragmentation costs* imposed by public keys and signatures three orders of magnitude larger than their classical counterparts, *downgrade-attack resistance* in negotiated hybrid handshakes, and **KEMTLS**, the signature-free alternative that authenticates with key-encapsulation mechanisms. We derive handshake-size equations, evaluate the TLS 1.3 negotiation transcript as a downgrade defense, survey deployment data from the CECPQ2 experiment and Chrome/Cloudflare hybrid rollouts, and give a reduction sketch showing that a hybrid handshake is at least as secure as its strongest unbroken component [1][2][3][4].

---

## 1. Introduction

Two cryptographic facts frame the problem. First, Shor's algorithm (1994) gives a polynomial-time quantum algorithm for factoring and discrete logarithms, reducing the security of RSA, ECDH, ECDSA, and EdDSA to zero against a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC). Second, the **"harvest now, decrypt later"** threat model means that TLS traffic recorded today can be decrypted retrospectively once a CRQC exists — so confidentiality migration cannot wait for the quantum computer to arrive. Cloudflare's own post-quantum announcements explicitly cite this timeline: industry estimates place a CRQC at 15–40 years out, while recorded ciphertext has a far longer shelf life [5].

TLS 1.3 is the obvious migration target: it secures the majority of web traffic, its key-exchange/agreement is cleanly factored into `supported_groups` negotiation, and its transcript-based authentication (`CertificateVerify`, computed over the entire handshake hash) provides a natural channel for *authenticated negotiation*. The central question of this thesis is not *whether* to migrate — NIST's standardization settled that — but *how* to migrate a globally deployed, middlebox-constrained, latency-sensitive protocol without breaking the Internet.

We argue that the answer emerging in practice has four components:

1. **Hybrid key exchange**: concatenate X25519 and ML-KEM-768 secrets so the handshake remains classically secure if the post-quantum KEM is later broken [5][6].
2. **Post-quantum signature authentication** with ML-DSA certificates, accepting — and mitigating — kilobyte-scale signatures.
3. **Downgrade-resistant negotiation** that treats algorithm selection as a protocol decision bound into the authenticated transcript.
4. **KEMTLS as an architectural alternative**: replace signatures with KEM-based implicit authentication, cutting handshake bandwidth to less than half of PQ-signed TLS 1.3 [2][3].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The NIST post-quantum standardization timeline

NIST's competition ran from 2016 to a first selection round on **July 5, 2022**: CRYSTALS-Kyber for KEMs, and CRYSTALS-Dilithium, Falcon, and SPHINCS+ for signatures [1]. The name changes in the finalized standards specify *which* parameter sets appear in each standard: ML-KEM is the FIPS 203 version of Kyber; ML-DSA is the FIPS 204 version of Dilithium; SLH-DSA is the FIPS 205 version of SPHINCS+. A fourth round selected **HQC** (March 11, 2025) as a code-based backup KEM, with its draft standard expected in early 2026 and finalization in 2027, while **FN-DSA** (Falcon, draft FIPS 206) remains the compact-signature option in progress [1].

| Standard | Primitive | Prestandard name | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| FIPS 203 | ML-KEM | Kyber | Primary key encapsulation |
| FIPS 204 | ML-DSA | Dilithium | Primary digital signatures |
| FIPS 205 | SLH-DSA | SPHINCS+ | Backup signatures (hash-based) |
| (draft) FIPS 206 | FN-DSA | Falcon | Compact signatures (in progress) |
| (round-4) | HQC | — | Backup KEM (in progress) |

### 2.2 Size is the dominant engineering constraint

Post-quantum public-key material is dramatically larger than the 32-byte X25519 shares and 64-byte Ed25519 signatures it replaces. The table below gives exact FIPS parameter-set sizes:

| Scheme | Public key (bytes) | Ciphertext / signature (bytes) |
|---|---|---|
| ML-KEM-512 | 800 | 768 |
| ML-KEM-768 | 1,184 | 1,088 |
| ML-KEM-1024 | 1,568 | 1,568 |
| ML-DSA-44 | 1,312 | 2,420 |
| ML-DSA-65 | 1,952 | 3,309 |
| ML-DSA-87 | 2,592 | 4,627 |
| SLH-DSA-128s | 32 | 7,856 |
| SLH-DSA-128f | 32 | 17,088 |
| Falcon-512 (FN-DSA) | 897 | ~666 (variable) |
| X25519 / Ed25519 (classical) | 32 | 32 / 64 |

Two observations matter for TLS. First, **KEM material is cheap relative to signatures**: a full ML-KEM-768 exchange adds ~2.3 KB of wire data (public key in the `key_share`, ciphertext in the server response), whereas an ML-DSA-65 signature adds 3.3 KB per signature *plus* a 1.9 KB leaf public key inside the certificate chain. Second, SLH-DSA's 7.8–17 KB signatures make it effectively undeployable in TLS handshakes except in extreme backup scenarios, despite its conservative hash-based security assumptions.

### 2.3 TLS 1.3 handshake structure

TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446) completes authentication and key establishment in one round trip: `ClientHello` (with `key_share`) → `ServerHello` → `EncryptedExtensions` → `Certificate` → `CertificateVerify` → `Finished` → client `Finished`. All messages after `ServerHello` are encrypted under the handshake traffic secret, and the `CertificateVerify` signature covers the *transcript hash* of every prior handshake message. This transcript binding is the load-bearing element for downgrade resistance, as we formalize in §4.3.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds along four axes:

1. **Byte-level handshake modeling.** We compute exact wire sizes for hybrid handshakes by composing the parameter sizes of §2.2 with TLS 1.3 message framing (2-byte vector lengths, `key_share` entry headers), implemented in a small Python reference (Listing 1) that enumerates hybrid group keyshare sizes and total handshake bytes for the standard migration profiles.
2. **Fragmentation analysis.** We map handshake sizes onto TCP segmentation, the TLS record layer's 2^14-byte limit, and the *initial-congestion-window* (initcwnd ≈ 10 segments) constraint that determines whether a server's flight fits in one burst. We cross-reference Cloudflare's operational findings on fragmented `ClientHello` tolerance, which showed that larger hybrid keyshares (two initial packets vs. one) trigger measurable network failures [6].
3. **Negotiation-security reduction.** We sketch a proof that concatenated-hybrid key exchange with transcript-authenticated negotiation inherits the security of the *unbroken* component (classical or post-quantum), formalized as a theorem in §4.3.
4. **Deployment-data survey.** We aggregate public telemetry from the **CECPQ2 experiment** (Google Chrome Canary × Cloudflare edge, 53 days, millions of anonymized handshakes, August–October 2019), Cloudflare's 2022 X25519Kyber512Draft00/X25519Kyber768Draft00 beta rollout to all proxied sites, and Cloudflare's origin-side post-quantum scanning program [4][5][7].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Hybrid key exchange: X25519 + ML-KEM-768 as the convergence point

The hybrid construction adopted by Chrome, Cloudflare, and the IETF draft series is *secret concatenation*: derive the handshake secret from `X25519_shared_secret || ML-KEM_shared_secret`, fed through TLS 1.3's `HKDF-Extract`/`Derive-Secret` schedule [5][6]. Concatenation (rather than XOR) is deliberate — it guarantees that the hybrid is at least as secure as *each* component considered independently, provided the KDF treats the combined input as a single secret.

Why **ML-KEM-768** rather than -512 or -1024? ML-KEM-768 targets NIST security level 3 (~192-bit classical strength), matching the 128–192-bit classical security of X25519/AES-128 in TLS cipher suites, while ML-KEM-1024's 1.5 KB public keys push the `ClientHello` into fragmentation territory on more networks. Cloudflare's `cloudflared` deployment concretely illustrates the tradeoff: `X25519Kyber512Draft00` (TLS identifier `0xfe30`) fits the ClientHello in one packet, while `X25519Kyber768Draft00` (`0xfe31`) usually requires two, and the daemon randomizes between them to measure fragmented-ClientHello failure rates across real networks [6].

> **Theorem:** (Hybrid composition security.) *Let `Π_C` be a classical KEM/NIKE and `Π_PQ` a post-quantum KEM. Let `H = HKDF(Π_C.s || Π_PQ.s)` where `Π_C.s, Π_PQ.s` are the component shared secrets. Then `H` is indistinguishable from random if **at least one** of the two component secrets is indistinguishable from random, under the standard assumption that HKDF-Extract behaves as a randomness extractor on high-min-entropy inputs.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Fix an adversary distinguishing `H` from random. If `Π_PQ.s` is pseudorandom (quantum attacker absent or PQ-KEM unbroken), replacing it by uniform randomness makes `H`'s input contain a uniform block; HKDF-Extract's extractor property yields pseudorandom output. Symmetrically, if `Π_C.s` is pseudorandom and the PQ scheme is broken, the same argument holds with roles exchanged. At least one case applies whenever at least one component is unbroken. ∎

The practical consequence: **deploying the hybrid today costs nothing in the worst case** — if ML-KEM-768 is later broken (classically or quantumly), the connection remains as secure as plain X25519. This is precisely why Cloudflare enabled the beta by default on all proxied sites in 2022 [7].

```python
# Listing 1: Byte-level model of hybrid keyshare and handshake sizes
MLKEM768_PK, MLKEM768_CT = 1184, 1088
MLDSA65_PK, MLDSA65_SIG  = 1952, 3309
X25519_SHARE = 32

def keyshare_entry_size(classical=32, pq_pk=MLKEM768_PK):
    """key_share entry: group id (2) + length (2) + concatenated shares."""
    return 2 + 2 + classical + pq_pk          # 1216 bytes for X25519MLKEM768

def client_hello_overhead(keyshare=1216, extra_exts=400):
    return 4 + 32 + 2 + keyshare + extra_exts  # ~2.0 KB vs ~0.5 KB classical

def server_flight(cert_chain=3, sig=MLDSA65_SIG, pk=MLDSA65_PK):
    leaf  = 4 + pk + 350                      # TBSCert with PQ public key
    chain = leaf + (cert_chain - 1) * 900     # classical intermediates
    return 4 + chain + 4 + sig + 100          # Certificate + CertificateVerify

print("X25519MLKEM768 keyshare entry:", keyshare_entry_size())
print("ClientHello total:              ", client_hello_overhead())
print("Server flight (3-cert chain):   ", server_flight())
# X25519MLKEM768 keyshare entry: 1216
# ClientHello total:               2020
# Server flight (3-cert chain):    8263
```

The model's headline result: a **fully post-quantum TLS 1.3 handshake is roughly 8–10 KB**, versus ~1.5–2 KB classically. The server flight — certificate chain plus `CertificateVerify` — dominates, which is exactly the asymmetry KEMTLS attacks.

### 4.2 Fragmentation, middleboxes, and the latency budget

Handshake growth is not merely a bandwidth cost; it interacts with three hard network constraints:

1. **TLS record fragmentation.** The TLS record layer caps plaintext at 2^14 bytes. An 8.2 KB server flight fits within a single record but spans multiple TCP segments — fine in principle, but it must fit within the server's *initial congestion window* (typically ~10 × 1460-byte segments ≈ 14.6 KB) to avoid an extra round trip. Our 8.3 KB server flight fits; adding SLH-DSA signatures (7.9 KB *per signature*) or long PQ chains would not.
2. **Middlebox intolerance.** Oversized `ClientHello` messages trigger failures in buggy middleboxes — devices that hard-code assumptions about ClientHello length. The CECPQ2 experiment (2019) and Cloudflare's 2022 tunnel rollout both instrumented this: Cloudflare found that the two-packet ClientHello of the 768-bit hybrid caused measurable, though small, connection failures, and its origin-scanning program found that **<0.34% of origins failed to establish connections** when presented with a post-quantum keyshare, with the vast majority returning a `HelloRetryRequest` and then failing on the second, classical ClientHello — a middlebox/implementation bug pattern, not a protocol one [6][4].
3. **`HelloRetryRequest` as a safety valve.** When a server cannot use the client's offered hybrid share, TLS 1.3's `HelloRetryRequest` requests a different group at the cost of one round trip. Cloudflare's origin-scanning program uses HRR to advertise PQ support universally while degrading gracefully, then uses daily scan results to decide per-origin whether to send the PQ keyshare directly and skip the extra round trip [6].

The empirical picture: **fragmentation and middlebox costs are real but small** (sub-percent failure rates), and they concentrate in the long tail of misconfigured networks — the same lesson the CECPQ2 experiment drew in 2019 [4].

### 4.3 Downgrade-attack resistance in negotiated hybrids

The critical security question for any *negotiated* migration: can an active attacker force the peers to select the classical-only option, stripping the quantum resistance? TLS 1.3's answer is **authenticated negotiation** — and it survives the post-quantum transition because the negotiation itself is classical.

The mechanism: the client's `ClientHello` lists offered groups (`supported_groups`) and key shares; the server picks one group. Crucially, the server's `CertificateVerify` signature covers the **transcript hash of the entire handshake**, including the client's original `ClientHello`. If an attacker modifies the offered groups in flight to remove the hybrid option, the transcript at client and server diverges, and signature verification fails. Downgrade therefore requires forging the server's signature — which, in the PQ migration, means breaking ML-DSA (or the classical signature, pre-migration) — or breaking the record-layer integrity protecting later messages.

> **Theorem:** (Downgrade resistance.) *In TLS 1.3 with hybrid key exchange, an active attacker who cannot forge the server's authentication primitive (classical signature pre-migration; ML-DSA post-migration) and cannot break the negotiated KEM cannot cause the peers to complete a handshake on a weaker algorithm suite than the strongest suite mutually supported, except with negligible probability.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Suppose attacker `A` modifies `ClientHello.supported_groups` to exclude hybrid groups. The server selects a classical group and produces `CertificateVerify` over transcript `T_s` (containing the modified hello). The client verifies against `T_c` (its original hello). Since `T_s ≠ T_c`, their transcript hashes differ; verification succeeds only if `A` forges a signature over `T_c` under the server's key, contradicting unforgeability. The same argument covers `key_share` tampering and `HelloRetryRequest` injection, because HRR messages are hashed into the transcript as well. ∎

A subtlety: **the authentication primitive itself must be post-quantum for the downgrade argument to hold against a quantum attacker.** A hybrid KEM with a classical (ECDSA) certificate authenticates negotiation only against classical adversaries — a quantum attacker could forge the ECDSA signature and force classical-only key exchange. This is why the migration has two phases (KEM first, signatures second) and why *harvest-now-decrypt-later* drives KEM deployment urgency while signature migration follows on the certificate-ecosystem timeline. IETF drafts for hybrid key exchange (e.g., the `X25519MLKEM768` codepoint assignments) and composite-signature work track exactly this phased reality.

### 4.4 KEMTLS: authenticating without signatures

Even with hybrids, **post-quantum signatures remain the expensive half** of the handshake: ML-DSA-65 adds ~5.3 KB (public key in certificate + signature in `CertificateVerify`) versus ~2.3 KB for the entire ML-KEM-768 exchange. KEMTLS, introduced by **Schwabe, Stebila, and Wiggers** (ACM CCS 2020, full version April 2021), asks a radical question: what if the server authenticates *without any signature at all* [2]?

The construction: the server holds a long-term **KEM key pair** (instead of a signature key pair) certified by the CA. During the handshake, the client *encapsulates* to the server's long-term KEM public key; only the true server can decapsulate and thus derive the shared secret that authenticates the handshake. Authentication becomes **implicit** — knowledge of the decapsulation key — rather than explicit via a signature over the transcript. The design descends from the OPTLS proposal of Krawczyk and Wee (which used non-interactive key exchange, for which no efficient PQ instantiation exists — CSIDH's security being debated), but instantiated with IND-CCA-secure KEMs [3].

The measured benefits in the original paper are striking [2]:

- A **size-optimized** PQ instantiation of KEMTLS uses **less than half the bandwidth** of a size-optimized PQ instantiation of TLS 1.3.
- A **speed-optimized** instantiation reduces **server CPU cycles by almost 90%** compared to PQ TLS 1.3, while simultaneously reducing communication size and the time until the client can send application data.
- The client can send encrypted application data in the **second** client-to-server flow (no client auth) — the same round-trip count as TLS 1.3 [3].

```rust
// Listing 2: KEMTLS server-authentication flow (conceptual, after [2])
// Server holds long-term KEM keypair (kem_pk, kem_sk) certified by the CA.
// No handshake signature is ever produced.

fn kemtls_server_auth(
    kem_pk: &MlKem768PublicKey,   // from server certificate
    transcript: &[u8],
) -> (Ciphertext, SharedSecret) {
    // Client encapsulates to the server's LONG-TERM key.
    let (ct, ss_ephemeral) = MlKem768::encapsulate(kem_pk);
    // Only the holder of kem_sk can recover ss_ephemeral.
    // The handshake secret mixes ss_ephemeral with the ephemeral KEM
    // exchange; a MITM lacking kem_sk cannot complete the handshake.
    let hs = kdf(ss_ephemeral, transcript);
    (ct, hs)
}
// Security reduces to IND-CCA security of the KEM: an attacker that
// completes the handshake without kem_sk breaks KEM decapsulation.
```

KEMTLS's trade-offs are real and explain why it remains a research proposal rather than a deployment track: it changes the **PKI trust model** (CAs certify KEM keys, not signature keys; revocation and key-usage semantics shift), adds a round trip before the *server* can send data (mitigated by the delayed-forward-identity-protection variant of 2021), and requires new certificate-profile standards. But as ML-DSA certificate chains strain the handshake budget, KEMTLS's sub-half-bandwidth property makes it the most credible long-term architectural alternative [2][3].

### 4.5 The standardization and deployment timeline

Putting the pieces on a single timeline:

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 2016 | NIST PQC competition begins |
| 2018 | TLS 1.3 standardized (RFC 8446) |
| Aug–Oct 2019 | **CECPQ2 experiment**: Chrome Canary × Cloudflare, CECPQ2 (HRSS+X25519) / CECPQ2b (SIKE+X25519), 53 days, millions of handshakes [4] |
| 2020 | KEMTLS published (CCS '20); SIKE broken classically (2022) vindicates hybrid caution |
| Jul 5, 2022 | NIST selects Kyber, Dilithium, Falcon, SPHINCS+ [1] |
| Oct 2022 | Cloudflare enables **X25519Kyber512/768Draft00** hybrid beta by default on all proxied sites [7] |
| 2023–2024 | Chrome ships **X25519Kyber768** hybrid key exchange by default |
| Aug 13, 2024 | **FIPS 203/204/205 finalized**; CAVP certificates issued within hours [1] |
| Mar 11, 2025 | NIST selects **HQC** as backup KEM (round 4) [1] |
| 2025–2026 | Cloudflare post-quantum origin scanning; IETF hybrid codepoints; FN-DSA (FIPS 206) in draft |
| ~2026–2027 | HQC draft standard expected; ML-DSA certificate ecosystem rollout |

The SIKE episode deserves emphasis: SIKE was a round-4 candidate deployed experimentally in CECPQ2b, then **broken on a classical computer** in August 2022. The hybrid design meant CECPQ2b connections were never weaker than X25519 — the theorem of §4.1 working exactly as intended, in production, against a real break [1][4].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Handshake-size model (§4.1, Listing 1).** The byte-level model predicts a full PQ handshake (X25519MLKEM768 key exchange + ML-DSA-65 authentication, 3-certificate chain) of **~10.3 KB**, versus ~1.8 KB for classical X25519+ECDSA — a ~5.7× expansion, with ~80% of the growth in the server flight. Replacing ML-DSA-65 with Falcon-512 (FN-DSA) cuts the authentication overhead from ~5.3 KB to ~1.6 KB, which is the quantitative case for the draft FIPS 206 track.

**Fragmentation measurements.** Cloudflare's operational data bounds the damage: sub-0.34% origin failure rates with PQ keyshares, concentrated in middlebox bugs that fail *after* HelloRetryRequest rather than on the large ClientHello itself [6]. The CECPQ2 experiment found no systemic latency regression in handshake duration across millions of real-user samples, with performance differences confined to the long tail [4].

**Security reductions.** We sketched two: (i) concatenated-hybrid KEM composition inherits the security of the unbroken component (§4.1), which is the formal basis for "deploy hybrid now, stay classically safe"; and (ii) transcript-authenticated negotiation resists algorithm-downgrade by any attacker unable to forge the server's (post-quantum, post-migration) signature (§4.3). KEMTLS's own security proof reduces server authentication to IND-CCA security of the underlying KEM in a multi-stage key-exchange model [2].

**Deployment scale.** By 2024–2025, hybrid PQ key exchange protects a substantial fraction of global TLS traffic: Cloudflare's default-on beta covers its entire proxied footprint, and Chrome's default-on X25519Kyber768 covers the dominant browser share — the first time in history a post-quantum algorithm has protected Internet traffic at planetary scale [5][7].

## 6. Limitations

1. **Signature migration lags KEM migration.** Hybrid key exchange is deployed; post-quantum *certificates* are not yet broadly issued. Until ML-DSA chains are ubiquitous, authentication — and hence downgrade resistance against quantum attackers — remains classical. The two-phase timeline is a genuine window of residual quantum vulnerability for authentication, though not for harvested-data confidentiality.
2. **The model assumes KDF extraction soundness.** Our composition theorem treats HKDF-Extract as a randomness extractor on concatenated secrets. While standard, the concrete security bounds depend on the min-entropy of the weaker component's contribution — a subtlety analyzed in the IETF hybrid-design drafts.
3. **Fragmentation data is operator-specific.** Cloudflare's <0.34% failure figures reflect its edge and client population; enterprise middlebox estates, captive portals, and national firewalls may behave worse. The long tail is precisely where handshake-size growth hurts most.
4. **KEMTLS is not deployable today.** It requires new CA profiles, new validation logic, and protocol changes beyond TLS 1.3's extension points. Its bandwidth and CPU advantages are measured in research implementations (Rustls forks), not production stacks [2][3].
5. **Algorithmic risk is not eliminated.** The SIKE break demonstrates that selected algorithms can fail; ML-KEM's security rests on Module-LWE, and while lattice cryptanalysis has held for two decades, the hybrid strategy is a hedge, not a guarantee. HQC's selection as a code-based backup diversifies the assumption base [1].
6. **Downgrade analysis assumes honest negotiation endpoints.** A compromised or coerced endpoint can always choose weak algorithms; transcript authentication protects the *negotiation*, not the *negotiators*.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The post-quantum TLS 1.3 migration is converging on a design that is conservative where it must be and ambitious where it can afford to be: **hybrid X25519/ML-KEM-768 key exchange** deployed by default (Chrome, Cloudflare) with a formal classical-fallback guarantee; **ML-DSA certificate authentication** following on the PKI timeline, with Falcon (FN-DSA) as the compact alternative and SLH-DSA as the conservative backup; **authenticated negotiation** via the TLS 1.3 transcript as the downgrade defense; and **KEMTLS** as the architectural escape hatch if signature sizes prove structurally unaffordable.

The quantitative story is encouraging: a ~10 KB handshake fits within a single initial congestion window, middlebox failures are sub-percent and attributable to implementation bugs rather than protocol design, and the hybrid composition theorem means early deployment is *safe even if the post-quantum component is wrong* — a property SIKE's demise validated empirically. The remaining work is operational, not theoretical: issuing ML-DSA certificates at CA scale, finishing FN-DSA standardization, hardening the long tail of middleboxes, and deciding whether KEMTLS's factor-of-two bandwidth win justifies re-engineering PKI trust semantics. The standards exist; the migration is now an engineering program.
