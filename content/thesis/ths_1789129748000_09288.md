# From Neural Radiance Fields to 3D Gaussian Splatting: Volumetric Rendering, Differentiable Rasterization, and the Real-Time View Synthesis Frontier

## Abstract

Photorealistic novel-view synthesis from a sparse set of photographs has become a central achievement of modern 3D vision. Neural Radiance Fields reframed the problem as fitting a continuous five-dimensional volumetric function — volume density together with view-dependent radiance — parameterized by a multilayer perceptron and rendered through classical differentiable volume ray marching. While the original formulation delivered unprecedented fidelity, it demanded many hours of per-scene optimization and seconds per frame, igniting a research arc: anti-aliasing through integrated positional encodings and conical frustums, acceleration via multiresolution hash encodings and sparse voxel grids, and the explicit turn to 3D Gaussian Splatting, which couples anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives with a tile-based differentiable rasterizer to reach real-time frame rates at state-of-the-art quality. This article unifies these developments under a single lens — image formation as a transmittance-weighted radiance integral — derives the quadrature and projection operators that make each representation differentiable, and traces the quality–speed Pareto frontier connecting implicit volumes to explicit splats.

## 1 Introduction

The problem of *novel-view synthesis* is deceptively simple to state: given a finite collection of calibrated photographs of a static scene, produce photorealistic images of that scene from viewpoints never observed. Its lineage stretches from early image-based rendering and light-field capture through multi-view stereo, each generation trading capture density against reconstruction fidelity. What changed in 2020 was not the goal but the *representation*. Mildenhall et al. proposed to encode the scene not as geometry plus textures, nor as a discretized voxel grid, but as a continuous function

$$
F_\Theta : (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \mapsto (\sigma, \mathbf{c}),
$$

mapping a 3D position $\mathbf{x}$ and a 2D viewing direction $\mathbf{d}$ to a volume density $\sigma$ and an emitted RGB radiance $\mathbf{c}$, approximated by a fully-connected neural network with weights $\Theta$ [1]. Rendering a pixel then reduces to numerically integrating this field along a camera ray — a procedure drawn directly from the classical volumetric rendering literature [2]. Because every operation in the integration is differentiable, the network can be optimized end-to-end from a simple photometric loss, requiring no 3D supervision whatsoever. The result, termed a *Neural Radiance Field* (NeRF), surpassed all prior view-synthesis quality while introducing a representation of striking mathematical economy: the entire scene is a few megabytes of weights.

That economy concealed two severe costs. First, the representation was *implicit and queried at render time*: every pixel required hundreds of neural-network evaluations, pushing frame times into seconds and training into days. Second, the method was *scale-blind*: a single ray per pixel made it prone to aliasing whenever training and test views observed the scene at different resolutions. The subsequent literature — compressed into roughly four years — can be read as a systematic assault on these two weaknesses:

1. **Anti-aliasing.** Mip-NeRF replaced rays with conical frustums and introduced the *integrated positional encoding*, extending the scene representation to a continuously-valued scale [3].
2. **Acceleration.** Instant NGP replaced monolithic MLPs with a multiresolution hash table of trainable features decoded by a tiny network, compressing training from hours to seconds [4]; Plenoxels showed that sparse voxel grids with spherical-harmonic coefficients and no neural components at all could match NeRF quality while training two orders of magnitude faster [5].
3. **Hybrid anti-aliasing.** Zip-NeRF married the frustum reasoning of Mip-NeRF 360 to grid-based models, cutting error rates by up to 77% relative to Instant NGP while training 24× faster than Mip-NeRF 360 [6].
4. **The explicit turn.** 3D Gaussian Splatting abandoned ray marching entirely, representing the scene as a set of anisotropic 3D Gaussians rendered by a fast, differentiable, tile-based rasterizer — the first method to deliver state-of-the-art quality at real-time (≥30 fps) 1080p rates [7]. Mip-Splatting then repaired the aliasing that splatting's screen-space dilation reintroduced, via a 3D smoothing filter and a 2D Mip filter [8].

This article presents these advances not as a catalog but as a coherent theory. Section 2 reviews volumetric image formation; Section 3 derives the differentiable rendering machinery common to all ray-based methods; Section 4 dissects the four turning points above; Section 5 formalizes empirical and theoretical guarantees; and Sections 6–7 discuss open limitations and the road ahead.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The volume rendering integral

Long before neural networks entered the picture, Kajiya and Von Herzen formulated light transport through participating media as an integral equation over ray paths [2]. In the *emission–absorption* simplification — no in-scattering — the radiance observed along a ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$, $t \in [t_n, t_f]$, is

$$
C(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\,\sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t), \mathbf{d})\,dt,
$$

where the *transmittance*

$$
T(t) = \exp\!\left(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\,ds\right)
$$

is the probability that a photon emitted at distance $t$ reaches the camera unabsorbed. The density $\sigma$ plays a dual role: it is simultaneously the emission rate and the extinction coefficient, which is precisely why optimizing density from color observations is well-posed — geometry and appearance are coupled through a single scalar field.

> **Theorem (Differentiability of the emission–absorption integral).** *If $\sigma$ and $\mathbf{c}$ are $C^1$ functions of the scene parameters $\Theta$, then the rendered color $C(\mathbf{r}; \Theta)$ is $C^1$ in $\Theta$, and its gradient is given by the path integral of the parameter derivatives weighted by the same transmittance:*

> $$\frac{\partial C}{\partial \Theta} = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\left[\frac{\partial \sigma}{\partial\Theta}\,\mathbf{c} + \sigma\,\frac{\partial \mathbf{c}}{\partial\Theta} - \sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t))\int_{t_n}^{t}\frac{\partial\sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))}{\partial\Theta}\,ds\right]dt.$$

> *Proof sketch.* Apply the Leibniz rule under the integral sign; the inner integral arises from differentiating $T(t) = \exp(-\int \sigma)$, i.e., $\partial T/\partial\Theta = -T \int_{t_n}^t \partial\sigma/\partial\Theta$. Integrability of all terms follows from boundedness of $\sigma, \mathbf{c}$ on the compact ray segment. ∎

This theorem is the entire foundation of the field: it says that *any* sufficiently smooth parametric density/radiance model can be fit to images by gradient descent through rendering. The choice of parameterization — neural network, voxel grid, hash table, or Gaussian mixture — is what distinguishes the methods below.

### 2.2 The plenoptic function and view dependence

The 5D input $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d})$ of NeRF is a parameterization of the *plenoptic function*: the radiance flowing through every point in every direction. Decomposing color into a position-dependent component and a direction-dependent component is essential — it is what allows the same surface point to appear specular from one angle and diffuse from another. All later methods retain this factorization, whether through direction-conditioned MLPs, spherical harmonics, or view-dependent Gaussian color.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Differentiable quadrature

The integral of Section 2.1 has no closed form for arbitrary fields, so NeRF approximates it by quadrature. The ray is partitioned into $N$ bins and one sample drawn per bin (stratified sampling):

```python
def render_ray(ray_o, ray_d, t_bounds, model, N=192):
    # Stratified samples along the ray
    t_vals = torch.linspace(*t_bounds, N + 1)
    t = t_vals[:-1] + torch.rand(N) * (t_vals[1:] - t_vals[:-1])
    pts = ray_o + t[:, None] * ray_d
    sigma, rgb = model(pts, ray_d.expand_as(pts))   # F_Theta
    delta = t_vals[1:] - t_vals[:-1]
    alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigma * delta)          # opacity of each segment
    # Front-to-back compositing: C = sum_i T_i * alpha_i * c_i
    T = torch.cumprod(torch.cat([torch.ones(1), 1.0 - alpha + 1e-10]), 0)[:-1]
    weights = T * alpha
    return (weights[:, None] * rgb).sum(0), weights.sum()
```

The discrete weights $w_i = T_i(1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i))$ form a proper probability distribution over depth ($\sum_i w_i \le 1$, with the deficit representing background), so the expected termination depth $\sum_i w_i t_i$ doubles as a depth estimator. Hierarchical sampling exploits this: a *coarse* network proposes a piecewise-constant proposal distribution along the ray, and a *fine* network concentrates its samples where the coarse weights are large — an importance-sampling scheme that spends MLP evaluations where they matter.

### 3.2 Positional encoding and the spectral bias

A vanilla MLP mapping $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ directly to density suffers from *spectral bias*: gradient descent fits low-frequency functions exponentially faster than high-frequency ones, so fine geometric detail never emerges. NeRF's remedy is the Fourier feature map

$$
\gamma(p) = \big(\sin(2^0\pi p), \cos(2^0\pi p), \dots, \sin(2^{L-1}\pi p), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi p)\big),
$$

applied coordinate-wise. This is not a heuristic: Rahaman et al.'s analysis of neural tangent kernels shows that lifting inputs into a high-dimensional harmonic basis rebalances the eigenspectrum so that high spatial frequencies become learnable [1]. The choice of maximum frequency $L$ is, however, a commitment to a single scale — the seed of the aliasing problem addressed in Section 4.1.

### 3.3 Optimization objective

All ray-based methods minimize a photometric loss over rays sampled from the training views:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{R}} \left\lVert \hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) - C_{\text{gt}}(\mathbf{r}) \right\rVert_2^2,
$$

plus regularizers (e.g., distortion or sparsity penalties in later work). Notably, no geometric supervision is used; the density field that emerges is the one whose renderings best explain the images — a remarkable instance of *analysis by synthesis*.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 From rays to frustums: Mip-NeRF and integrated positional encoding

NeRF casts an infinitesimally thin ray per pixel, yet a pixel observes a *cone* of the scene whose footprint grows with distance. When the same surface is imaged at two resolutions, the point-sampled network must represent contradictory signals at the same coordinate, producing aliasing or blur. Supersampling — many rays per pixel — is the classical cure, but at hundreds of MLP queries per ray it is computationally prohibitive.

Mip-NeRF's insight is that the encoding, not the sampling, can carry the scale information [3]. Each ray interval is approximated as a conical frustum, modeled as a multivariate Gaussian with mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$; the *integrated positional encoding* (IPE) replaces $\gamma(\mathbf{x})$ with its expectation under that Gaussian:

$$
\gamma_{\text{IPE}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})}[\gamma(\mathbf{x})].
$$

Because the expectation of sinusoids under a Gaussian has a closed form — each frequency band is attenuated by $\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}(2^k\pi)^2 \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}))$ — the network automatically low-passes its representation for distant, large-footprint frustums and preserves detail for near ones. The covariance's axial and radial components are derived in closed form from the cone geometry, so the entire scheme adds negligible cost. The payoff was substantial: Mip-NeRF reduced average error by 17% over NeRF on the original benchmark and 60% on a new multiscale variant, while being 7% faster and half the parameter count [3]. Conceptually, it upgraded the scene representation from a function of *position* to a function of *position and scale* — the neural analog of mipmapping.

### 4.2 Escaping the MLP: hash encodings and sparse voxels

Even anti-aliased, NeRF's deeper problem was compute: every sample evaluation ran a ~256-wide MLP. Two 2022-era lines of work attacked the parameterization itself.

**Multiresolution hash encoding (Instant NGP).** Müller et al. observed that the MLP's capacity was largely wasted memorizing high-frequency spatial detail that could instead be *stored* [4]. Their input encoding maintains $L$ grids at geometrically increasing resolutions; each grid vertex holds a short trainable feature vector, hashed into a fixed-size table when the grid exceeds the table capacity. A query concatenates trilinearly-interpolated features from all levels and feeds them to a tiny 2–3 layer MLP. Hash collisions are inevitable at fine levels, but the multiresolution structure lets the network disambiguate them: a collision at one level is resolved by agreement across the others, and gradient averaging over colliding entries acts as an implicit importance weighting. Implemented in fully-fused CUDA kernels, the scheme trains high-quality radiance fields in *seconds* and renders 1080p frames in tens of milliseconds — several orders of magnitude faster than NeRF [4]. The lesson: the MLP is best used as a *decoder* of learned spatial features, not as the spatial memory itself.

**Plenoxels: radiance fields without neural networks.** Fridovich-Keil et al. pushed this logic to its endpoint, asking whether the network is needed at all [5]. A *Plenoxel* is a sparse 3D voxel grid in which each occupied vertex stores one density value and spherical-harmonic coefficients (9 per color channel) for view-dependent color; trilinear interpolation yields continuous $\sigma$ and $\mathbf{c}$, optimized directly by gradient descent with total-variation regularization. The result matched NeRF's visual quality while training two orders of magnitude faster. Plenoxels proved that the *differentiable volume renderer*, not the MLP, was NeRF's essential invention — the neural network was merely one choice of function approximator.

```haskell
-- Transmittance-weighted compositing as a pure left fold:
-- the state (T, acc) threads occlusion through the samples.
composite :: [(Double, (Double, Double, Double))] -> (Double, Double, Double)
composite samples = acc
  where
    (_, acc) = foldl step (1.0, (0,0,0)) samples
    step (t, (r,g,b)) (sigma, (cr,cg,cb)) =
      let alpha = 1 - exp (-sigma)        -- segment opacity (unit spacing)
          w     = t * alpha               -- transmittance-weighted contribution
      in (t * (1 - alpha), (r + w*cr, g + w*cg, b + w*cb))
```

### 4.3 The explicit turn: 3D Gaussian Splatting

Ray marching, however accelerated, still integrates along rays — inherently sequential per pixel and wasteful in empty space. Kerbl et al. abandoned the volumetric query model entirely [7]. A scene is represented as a set of *anisotropic 3D Gaussians*, each with a mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, a covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^\top\mathbf{R}^\top$ (parameterized by a rotation quaternion and a scale vector), an opacity $o$, and spherical-harmonic color coefficients. Rendering proceeds by *splatting*:

1. **Projection.** Each 3D Gaussian is projected to a 2D screen-space Gaussian. The covariance transforms as $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}' = \mathbf{J}\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{J}^\top$, where $\mathbf{W}$ is the view matrix and $\mathbf{J}$ the Jacobian of the projective mapping — the standard EWA (elliptical weighted average) splatting approximation.
2. **Tiling and sorting.** The screen is divided into 16×16 tiles; Gaussians overlapping each tile are instantiated per tile and depth-sorted by a GPU radix sort, giving a globally consistent front-to-back order within each tile.
3. **Alpha blending.** Pixels accumulate $\mathbf{C} = \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i \prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j)$, where $\alpha_i$ is the opacity-weighted 2D Gaussian response — structurally identical to the discrete volume-rendering equation of Section 3.1, but over *primitives sorted in depth* rather than samples along a ray.

Optimization interleaves gradient descent (all parameters are differentiable through the rasterizer, including the sorting-adjacent blending) with *adaptive density control*: Gaussians with large view-space positional gradients are *split* (over-reconstruction) or *cloned* (under-reconstruction), while near-transparent ones are pruned. Starting from the sparse SfM point cloud, the representation thus grows to fit scene complexity — a discrete, topological counterpart to NeRF's continuous fitting.

The payoff was the field's long-sought milestone: with training times competitive with the fastest prior methods, 3D Gaussian Splatting matched or exceeded the quality of Mip-NeRF 360 while rendering at well above 30 fps at 1080p [7] — the first method to be simultaneously state-of-the-art in quality *and* real-time. The conceptual shift is worth stressing: the volume integral was not approximated faster; it was *replaced* by a primitive-based renderer whose compositing math coincides with volume rendering in the limit of many small, sorted kernels.

### 4.4 Anti-aliasing the splats: Zip-NeRF and Mip-Splatting

Every rendering paradigm eventually confronts the sampling theorem, and splatting was no exception. Two complementary fixes emerged.

**Zip-NeRF** addressed aliasing in the *grid-based NeRF* lineage [6]. Mip-NeRF's frustum reasoning was incompatible with grid encodings, so Barron et al. combined multisampling of a *multiscale* hash grid — features are downsampled by prefiltering across pyramid levels, with the sample count scaled to the cone footprint — with a normalized weight-decay-like loss on the grid. The result cut error rates by 8–77% relative to Instant NGP and Mip-NeRF 360 while training 24× faster than the latter [6]: anti-aliasing and acceleration, achieved together.

**Mip-Splatting** diagnosed two distinct aliasing sources in 3DGS itself [8]: (i) nothing constrains the *3D* frequency content of the Gaussians, so zooming in reveals primitives smaller than the training sampling rate — a Nyquist violation in object space; and (ii) the screen-space *dilation* filter (a minimum 2D size imposed for optimization stability) injects high-frequency energy in image space. The fix is a two-filter design with a clean signal-processing interpretation: a **3D smoothing filter** lower-bounds each Gaussian's size by the maximal sampling frequency induced by the input views (a 3D low-pass matched to the capture), and a **2D Mip filter** replaces dilation with an approximation of the pixel box filter. Together they restore alias-free rendering across scales — the splatting analog of Mip-NeRF's frustum — without sacrificing real-time performance [8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

The field's progress is best read as movement along a quality–speed Pareto frontier, summarized below with paper-reported figures (Blender synthetic / Mip-NeRF 360 scenes; single-GPU training):

| Method | Training | Rendering | Headline result |
|---|---|---|---|
| NeRF [1] | ~10–20 h | ~0.03 fps | Baseline photorealistic NVS |
| Mip-NeRF [3] | ~10–20 h | ~0.03 fps | −17% error vs NeRF; −60% on multiscale |
| Plenoxels [5] | ~10 min | ~real-time* | Matches NeRF quality; ~100× faster training |
| Instant NGP [4] | seconds | tens of ms @1080p | Orders-of-magnitude speedup via hash grids |
| Zip-NeRF [6] | ~1–2 h | ~seconds/frame | −8–77% error vs iNGP/Mip-NeRF 360; 24× faster training than Mip-NeRF 360 |
| 3DGS [7] | ~5–50 min | ≥30 fps @1080p | SOTA quality, first real-time method |
| Mip-Splatting [8] | ~5–50 min | ≥30 fps @1080p | Alias-free splatting across scales |

\* Plenoxel rendering is real-time for moderate resolutions on GPU.

Beyond benchmarks, two theoretical properties deserve emphasis. First, *consistency*: as the number of stratified samples $N \to \infty$ and bin widths $\to 0$, the discrete compositing of Section 3.1 converges to the volume integral of Section 2.1 for any continuous field, so all ray-based methods are consistent estimators of the same image-formation model — their differences are purely in the parameterization and sampling of $F_\Theta$. Second, the anti-aliasing results (Mip-NeRF, Zip-NeRF, Mip-Splatting) are all instances of a single principle: **prefilter the scene representation below the Nyquist limit of the sampling process before rendering**, whether that prefiltering is analytic (integrated positional encoding), architectural (multiscale grids), or geometric (3D smoothing filters). Methods that violate this principle exhibit exactly the predicted failure modes — jaggies, missing content on zoom-out, and shimmer on zoom-in [3, 6, 8].

## 6 Limitations

The arc from NeRF to splatting has not removed several fundamental limitations:

- **Per-scene optimization.** Every method discussed fits parameters to one scene; generalization across scenes requires separate meta-learning or feed-forward architectures, and remains far from the quality of per-scene fitting.
- **View-dependent phenomena under sparse views.** Specularities, transparency, and refraction are modeled as view-conditioned radiance, which is under-constrained when input views are few — producing plausible but physically incorrect highlights.
- **Lighting and relighting.** All these methods bake illumination into emitted radiance; they cannot relight scenes or separate material from lighting without additional priors.
- **Unbounded and dynamic scenes.** Large-scale outdoor captures strain primitive budgets and grid memory alike, and truly dynamic scenes (topology-changing motion) remain an active research frontier with no consensus representation.
- **Geometric fidelity vs. photometric fidelity.** The density fields that best explain images are not always the truest geometry — floaters and background collapse persist, and extracted meshes lag dedicated reconstruction methods.
- **Memory and editing.** A trained 3DGS scene may hold millions of Gaussians; the representation is compact relative to its quality but opaque to artists — editing remains far less intuitive than mesh-based pipelines.

## 7 Conclusion

In six years, view synthesis traveled from an MLP evaluated hundreds of times per ray to millions of anisotropic Gaussians rasterized in milliseconds — yet the mathematics barely changed. The transmittance-weighted integral of Kajiya and Von Herzen [2] underwrites every method surveyed here; what evolved was *where the scene lives* (network weights, hash tables, voxel grids, Gaussian parameters) and *how the integral is evaluated* (stratified quadrature, frustum expectations, tile-based splatting). The deepest lesson is a design principle: **differentiable rendering is the primitive; the representation is the variable.** Mip-NeRF taught the field to respect scale, Instant NGP and Plenoxels taught it to store rather than memorize, and 3D Gaussian Splatting taught it that the fastest volume renderer is the one that stops marching rays. The frontier now lies in lifting these real-time, anti-aliased representations toward relightable, editable, and dynamic worlds — where the next unification, between inverse rendering and generative modeling, is already taking shape.
