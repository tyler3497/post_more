## Abstract

CRYSTALS-Dilithium, standardized by NIST as the Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (ML-DSA) in FIPS 204, is the primary post-quantum replacement for classical signatures threatened by Shor's algorithm [1]. This thesis presents a self-contained treatment of Dilithium's design, analysis, and engineering. We formalize the Module Learning With Errors (MLWE) and Module Short Integer Solution (MSIS) problems underlying its security, and show how the Fiat–Shamir with aborts paradigm, instantiated with uniform rejection sampling, yields compact signatures without Gaussian samplers [2]. We dissect the scheme's Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) arithmetic over the cyclotomic ring R_q = Z_q[x]/(x^256+1) with q = 8380417, explain how the modulus's special form enables O(n log n) polynomial multiplication, and survey the three standardized parameter sets with their size–security trade-offs. We then analyze Dilithium's side-channel attack surface — from single-trace power analysis of secret-key unpacking to correlation attacks on the challenge multiplication c·s1 — and evaluate masking, shuffling, and hedged-signing countermeasures [4][5]. We conclude with implementation guidance and open problems for hardened, formally verified deployments.

## 1 Introduction

The arrival of large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computers would collapse the security of RSA, DSA, and ECDSA via Shor's algorithm, breaking virtually every deployed digital-signature infrastructure on the planet. NIST's Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) standardization process, launched in 2016, set out to identify drop-in replacements grounded in problems believed hard even for quantum adversaries. In August 2024, NIST published its first three finalized standards, with CRYSTALS-Dilithium selected as the **primary signature scheme**, renamed **ML-DSA** (Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm) in FIPS 204 [1]. A companion hash-based scheme, SLH-DSA (FIPS 205), serves only as a conservative backup, underscoring the confidence placed in Dilithium.

Dilithium belongs to the family of **lattice-based signatures** derived from the Fiat–Shamir heuristic applied to lattice identification schemes, a lineage tracing back to Lyubashevsky's 2009/2012 "Fiat–Shamir with aborts" construction [2]. Its distinguishing design choice — sampling all randomness uniformly from small power-of-two intervals rather than from discrete Gaussians — eliminates the most fragile component of earlier schemes (Gaussian sampling) and yields an implementation that is both fast and comparatively easy to protect against side channels. Every polynomial operation takes place in the ring R_q = Z_q[x]/(x^256+1) with the 23-bit prime q = 8380417, a modulus deliberately chosen so that a negacyclic Number Theoretic Transform exists and polynomial multiplication runs in O(n log n) time.

This thesis develops Dilithium from first principles through deployment. Section 2 reviews lattices and the module problems underpinning security. Section 3 presents the scheme's three algorithms and explains the standardization deltas between CRYSTALS-Dilithium v3.1 and FIPS 204. Section 4 is the technical core: the hardness foundations, the rejection-sampling signing loop, the NTT arithmetic, and the parameter sets. Section 5 covers empirical performance and the security proof strategy. Section 6 addresses the scheme's genuine limitations, with emphasis on the side-channel threat model and what provably and practically hardens implementations. Section 7 concludes with guidance for engineers deploying ML-DSA.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lattices and hard problems

A *lattice* L ⊂ R^m is the set of integer linear combinations of m linearly independent basis vectors. Lattice cryptography bases its security on problems such as finding short nonzero vectors (SVP, SIS) or decoding close to a lattice point in the presence of noise (LWE) — problems conjectured to be hard for both classical and quantum computers in the worst case, with average-case instances (the ones used in cryptography) provably at least as hard as worst-case instances via reductions due to Ajtai, Regev, and successors.

Dilithium works over **module lattices**: lattices defined by matrices over the polynomial ring R_q rather than over the integers. The two relevant problems are:

- **Module-LWE (MLWE):** given A ∈ R_q^{k×ℓ} (uniform) and t = As_1 + s_2 with s_1, s_2 sampled from a small distribution, recover (s_1, s_2) — or, in decisional form, distinguish (A, As_1+s_2) from (A, uniform). MLWE hides the long-term secret key.
- **Module-SIS (MSIS):** given A ∈ R_q^{k×ℓ}, find a short nonzero vector z with Az = 0 (in the relevant norm). A refined variant, **SelfTargetMSIS**, additionally requires the short solution to be tied to a target derived from a hash — this is what an existential forger must solve [2].

Module (rather than plain ring) structure provides a *security–efficiency dial*: increasing the module rank (k, ℓ) scales security without changing the ring dimension n = 256, which fixes the NTT-friendly arithmetic.

### 2.2 Fiat–Shamir with aborts

Classical Schnorr signatures prove knowledge of a discrete logarithm via a three-move protocol (commit, challenge, respond) compiled into a non-interactive signature by hashing the commitment to derive the challenge. Lyubashevsky transported this template to lattices: the prover commits to w = Ay for a random masking vector y, receives challenge c, and responds with z = y + c·s. The problem is that z leaks the secret s, since it is distributed as a shift of y by c·s. The fix — **Fiat–Shamir with aborts** — is *rejection sampling*: the signer repeats the loop, discarding candidate responses whose distribution would depend on the secret, until the output response is statistically independent of s. Dilithium is arguably the most successful industrial realization of this idea.

### 2.3 The ring R_q and why q = 8380417

All arithmetic in Dilithium occurs in R_q = Z_q[x]/(x^256+1) with q = 8380417 = 2^23 − 2^13 + 1. This prime satisfies q ≡ 1 (mod 512), so a primitive 512th root of unity r = 1753 exists in Z_q, and x^256+1 splits into linear factors over Z_q. Consequently every ring element has an NTT-domain representation in which multiplication is pointwise — the engine of the scheme's performance, treated in depth in §4.3.

## 3 Methodology

We analyze Dilithium as standardized: CRYSTALS-Dilithium v3.1 as submitted to NIST, with the deltas introduced by FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) noted explicitly [1]. Our treatment combines (i) the scheme's pseudocode specification, (ii) the reductionist security arguments of Ducas et al. and Kiltz–Lyubashevsky–Schaffner, (iii) published performance measurements of reference and optimized implementations, and (iv) the side-channel literature, including single-trace key-recovery attacks and masked implementations evaluated on ARM Cortex-M4 and FPGA targets [4][5][6].

**The three algorithms.** Key generation samples a uniform matrix A ∈ R_q^{k×ℓ} from a seed ρ, draws secret vectors s_1 ∈ S_η^ℓ and s_2 ∈ S_η^k with coefficients in [−η, η], and computes t = As_1 + s_2. The public key is (ρ, t_1), where t_1 = Power2Round_q(t, d) keeps only the high-order bits of each coefficient (compression by d dropped bits); the secret key additionally stores the low bits t_0 along with (s_1, s_2) and key material.

Signing is a *rejection loop*. Each iteration samples a masking vector y uniformly from [−(γ_1−1), γ_1−1]^ℓ, computes w = Ay, extracts the high bits w_1 = HighBits_q(w, 2γ_2), and derives the challenge c = H(μ ∥ w_1), a sparse polynomial with exactly τ coefficients in {−1, 1}. The candidate response is z = y + c·s_1. The iteration is accepted only if ‖z‖_∞ < γ_1 − β, the low bits r_0 = LowBits_q(w − c·s_2, 2γ_2) satisfy ‖r_0‖_∞ < γ_2 − β, and the hint vector h = MakeHint_q(−c·t_0, w − c·s_2 + c·t_0, 2γ_2) contains at most ω ones. Otherwise the loop restarts. The signature is σ = (z, h, c).

Verification recomputes w_1' = UseHint_q(h, Az − c·t_1·2^d, 2γ_2) and accepts iff ‖z‖_∞ < γ_1 − β, c equals H(μ ∥ w_1'), and h has at most ω ones. Correctness follows because Az − c·t_1·2^d = w − c·s_2 + c·t_0 (up to the hinted carries), so the verifier reconstructs the same high bits the signer committed to.

**FIPS 204 deltas.** ML-DSA standardizes three parameter sets (ML-DSA-44/65/87, corresponding to NIST security categories 2/3/5) and modifies the submission in security-relevant ways [1]: the key-generation randomness is lengthened, signing uses a *hedged* procedure in which the per-signature randomness ρ' is derived from the secret key, the message, *and* a fresh 256-bit randomizer (with an optional deterministic variant), and the pseudocode now mandates a check for malformed hints during unpacking — without which the scheme fails to be *strongly* existentially unforgeable.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Module-LWE and Module-SIS: the hardness foundations

Dilithium's security rests on two legs, one per attack direction.

> **Theorem (informal, [2]):** If MLWE_{q,k,ℓ,η} is hard and SelfTargetMSIS_{q,k,ℓ+1,ζ} is hard, then Dilithium is existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack (EUF-CMA) in the (quantum) random-oracle model.

The proof proceeds in two stages. First, the public key t = As_1 + s_2 is replaced by a uniform vector; any adversary distinguishing this change breaks decisional MLWE. With a uniform public key, the signing oracle can be simulated without the secret: because rejection sampling makes z independent of (s_1, s_2), the simulator programs the random oracle and outputs statistically valid transcripts. A forger that succeeds against the simulated scheme therefore produces, by the forking lemma, two signatures (z, c) and (z', c') on the same commitment with c ≠ c', yielding A(z − z') = (c − c')·t and hence a short nonzero vector in the kernel of [A | I | t] — a solution to MSIS/SelfTargetMSIS with norm bound ζ ≈ 4γ_1. Concrete hardness is estimated with the standard lattice-estimation methodology (BKZ with sieving/enumeration cost models), giving the claimed NIST categories. Notably, the Module-SIS bound degrades gracefully: forging requires finding vectors far shorter than what the best known algorithms achieve at the stated dimensions.

### 4.2 Fiat–Shamir with aborts: signing and rejection sampling

The rejection loop is the scheme's beating heart, and its design reflects a deliberate trade-off between signature size and signing time. In each iteration:

1. **Mask:** y ← S_{γ_1−1}^ℓ, sampled uniformly — no Gaussians anywhere.
2. **Commit:** w = Ay; w_1 = HighBits_q(w, 2γ_2); c = H(μ ∥ w_1) ∈ B_τ.
3. **Respond:** z = y + c·s_1.
4. **Test:** accept iff ‖z‖_∞ < γ_1 − β and ‖r_0‖_∞ < γ_2 − β (with r_0 the low bits of w − c·s_2) and the hint weight ≤ ω.

Why does this work? Since c·s_1 has coefficients bounded by β = τ·η in absolute value, conditioning on ‖z‖_∞ < γ_1 − β makes z's distribution *exactly uniform* on the acceptance box, independent of the secret shift c·s_1. The verifier learns nothing about s_1 beyond what the public key already reveals. The acceptance probability per iteration is roughly ((2(γ_1−β)−1)/(2γ_1−1))^{256ℓ} ≈ e^{−256ℓβ/γ_1}; with the standardized parameters this gives an expected **≈4–7 repetitions** before acceptance (about 4.25 for Dilithium2), a modest and predictable cost. The r_0 check serves correctness: it guarantees that subtracting c·s_2 does not disturb the high bits the challenge was bound to, keeping ‖r_0‖_∞ < γ_2 − β ensures the hint mechanism reproduces w_1 exactly at verification.

*Figure 2 — The Dilithium signing loop: masking, commitment, challenge, response, and the rejection-sampling checks that restart the loop when a candidate would leak the secret.*

Two subtleties deserve emphasis. First, *hints*: because the public key drops d low bits of t, the verifier works with t_1·2^d rather than t, and the signer must communicate the carries this approximation introduces. MakeHint_q produces a sparse binary hint vector h (at most ω ones) that lets UseHint_q recover the correct high bits — an elegant compression trick that saves roughly d·256·k bits of public key at the cost of a few hundred hint bits per signature. Second, *deterministic versus hedged signing*: v3.1 defaults to deriving the masking randomness deterministically from (key, message); FIPS 204's hedged mode additionally mixes fresh randomness, so identical messages yield distinct signatures. Hedging is not merely defense-in-depth — as §6 discusses, it meaningfully raises the bar for certain side-channel attacks [5].

### 4.3 NTT arithmetic over R_q = Z_q[x]/(x^256+1)

Polynomial multiplication dominates Dilithium's cost — key generation, signing (Ay, c·s_1, c·s_2), and verification (Az) are all matrix–vector products over R_q. Schoolbook multiplication would cost O(n^2) = 65,536 coefficient multiplications per product; the NTT reduces this to O(n log n).

Because q ≡ 1 (mod 512), the polynomial x^256+1 factors as ∏_{i=0}^{127}(x^2 − r^{2·brv(128+i)+1}) over Z_q, where brv denotes 7-bit bit-reversal and r = 1753 is a primitive 512th root of unity. Dilithium's NTT therefore maps each polynomial to 128 linear factors — the "NTT domain" is not n pointwise values but n/2 = 128 linear polynomials, and multiplication in this domain is 128 independent products of degree-1 polynomials (each costing a few modular multiplications), followed by one inverse transform. Concretely:

```python
# Negacyclic NTT multiplication in R_q = Z_q[x]/(x^256 + 1), q = 8380417
q, n = 8380417, 256
def ntt_mul(f, g):
    fh, gh = ntt(f), ntt(g)          # 128 linear factors each
    hh = [pointwise_mul(a, b) for a, b in zip(fh, gh)]
    return intt(hh)                   # inverse transform, x^256+1 reduction built in
```

The modulus's shape q = 2^23 − 2^13 + 1 is no accident either: 23-bit coefficients fit comfortably in 32-bit registers, and reduction modulo q admits a fast specialized routine (a variant of Montgomery/Barrett reduction tuned for this q). The reference implementation keeps polynomials in Montgomery form in the NTT domain and converts lazily, so that ExpandA's output matrix Â is generated once and reused across signing iterations. On modern x86-64 with AVX2, optimized implementations sign in well under 100 µs for ML-DSA-44; on the ARM Cortex-M4 — the embedded benchmark target — the pqm4 project reports signing times on the order of a few million cycles, with the NTT and SHAKE (used for ExpandA, ExpandMask, and H) dominating the profile.

### 4.4 Parameter sets and the FIPS 204 standard

The standardized parameter sets balance signature size against the concrete MLWE/MSIS hardness estimates:

| Parameter set | NIST category | (k, ℓ) | η | τ | γ_1 | γ_2 | pk (B) | sig (B) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA-44 | 2 | (4, 4) | 2 | 39 | 2^17 | (q−1)/88 | 1,312 | 2,420 |
| ML-DSA-65 | 3 | (6, 5) | 4 | 49 | 2^19 | (q−1)/88 | 1,952 | 3,309 |
| ML-DSA-87 | 5 | (8, 7) | 2 | 60 | 2^19 | (q−1)/88 | 2,592 | 4,627 |

Several regularities are visible: γ_2 = (q−1)/88 = 95,232 is fixed so that the decomposition w = w_1·2γ_2 + w_0 has w_0 ∈ [−γ_2, γ_2]; β = τ·η bounds ‖c·s_1‖_∞; ω ∈ {80, 55, 75} caps the hint weight; and d ∈ {13, 14} sets the public-key compression. ML-DSA-44 targets AES-128-equivalent security (category 2), ML-DSA-65 targets AES-192 (category 3), and ML-DSA-87 targets AES-256 (category 5); there is deliberately no category-1 or category-4 set, mirroring the original Dilithium2/3/5 submission levels. At roughly 2.4–4.6 kB per signature, ML-DSA is an order of magnitude larger than ECDSA but far smaller than its hash-based backup SLH-DSA — the size–speed Pareto point that made it NIST's primary choice [1][3].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

**Performance.** The reference C implementation already achieves competitive speeds, and optimized variants (AVX2, ARM Cortex-M4 assembly, FPGA) push signing below 0.1 ms on desktop-class hardware. The signing loop's expected ~4–7 iterations mean runtime has a small, well-characterized variance; key generation is a one-time cost dominated by ExpandA (SHAKE-128) and the NTT of s_1, s_2; verification needs only two NTT-domain matrix–vector products and is the fastest operation. Memory footprint is modest: the expanded matrix Â (k·ℓ·256 32-bit values in the NTT domain) is the largest object, fitting easily in embedded SRAM at the cost of recomputation trade-offs explored by the pqm4 implementations.

**Proof sketch.** The EUF-CMA argument [2] runs as follows. *Game 0* is the real scheme. *Game 1* replaces the MLWE public key t with uniform — indistinguishable under decisional MLWE. *Game 2* simulates signing without the secret: because of rejection sampling, real signatures are distributed identically to simulated ones in which z is uniform in the acceptance box and c is a random B_τ element, with the random oracle programmed to match. *Game 3* applies the forking lemma to a successful forger, extracting two valid signatures sharing a commitment but with distinct challenges; their difference yields a vector v with Av = 0 (up to the hint terms) and ‖v‖_∞ ≤ ζ, solving SelfTargetMSIS. The reduction is *tight* in the number of signing queries — a property established by Kiltz, Lyubashevsky, and Schaffner — meaning the concrete parameters need no multiplicative security loss for Q_s queries, unlike many Fiat–Shamir signatures. In the quantum random-oracle model the argument goes through with the standard rewinding caveats, and NIST's selection reflects confidence in the underlying assumptions' quantum hardness.

**Interoperability evidence.** FIPS 204's publication was accompanied by Known Answer Tests and the ACVP (Automated Cryptographic Validation Protocol) test vectors; multiple independent implementations (liboqs, the pq-crystals reference code, Bouncy Castle, and hardware cores) interoperate on the standardized encodings, and the mandatory malformed-hint check noted in §3 was added precisely because an early draft omission broke strong unforgeability [1].

## 6 Limitations

**Side-channel attack surface.** The security proof models a black box; physical implementations leak. The literature documents a mature and still-growing attack surface:

- *Single-trace key recovery.* Wang, Ngo, Gärtner, and Dubrova demonstrated deep-learning-assisted profiled power analysis against the **secret-key unpacking** in signing on an ARM Cortex-M4, recovering large fractions of s_1 and s_2 from a single trace and completing the key via linear algebra on t = As_1 + s_2 or via lattice reduction — with success approaching 100% given multiple traces [4]. This reframes public-key compression (the dropped bits t_0) as a security-relevant implementation detail.
- *Randomness-leakage attacks.* Liu et al. showed that leaking even a single bit of the masking randomness y per signature (with probability > 1/2) suffices for full key recovery; follow-up work instantiated this against real Cortex-M4 implementations with a "public template attack" reaching 95% per-trace success on unprotected code and 62% against first-order masked code, recovering a Dilithium2 key from 10,000 traces in about 30 minutes [7].
- *Correlation attacks on c·s_1.* Hardware analyses target the NTT-domain pointwise multiplication ĉ ∘ ŝ_1, where the public challenge c meets the secret s_1 — a textbook CPA/DPA setting, with the 2×2 butterfly structure of FPGA implementations offering parallel leakage points [6].

![Side-channel attack vs countermeasures](/thesis/ths_1788733801000_2c8e-3.webp)

*Figure 4 — The side-channel threat model: unprotected signing leaks through power/EM emanations at the challenge multiplication and key unpacking, while masking, shuffling, and constant-time rejection sampling raise the attack cost by orders of magnitude.*

**Countermeasures and their cost.** The standard defenses are *masking* (splitting secrets into Boolean/arithmetic shares), *shuffling* (randomizing operation order, including shuffled NTTs), *hiding* (noise, jitter), and constant-time coding of the rejection checks so that the accept/reject decision does not leak through timing. Azouaoui et al. built a fully masked Dilithium signing implementation and measured the price: roughly a **1.3× slowdown for deterministic signing and 1.53× for randomized signing** when the commitment w_1 is also kept masked through decomposition, with masked hashing alone consuming ~9–14% of runtime [5]. Hedged (randomized) signing additionally deprives multi-trace attacks of the fixed-randomness alignment they exploit — one of the concrete reasons FIPS 204 made hedging the default [1][5].

**Other limitations.** Signature and key sizes (2.4–4.6 kB signatures) strain constrained protocols and embedded storage; the scheme is stateful only in the mild sense of the hedged randomizer, but deterministic variants demand a high-quality RNG failure be non-catastrophic (hedging addresses exactly this). The hardness assumptions, while well-studied, lack the decades of cryptanalytic attention RSA enjoys — module lattices are younger, and the tightness of the reduction does not protect against a future algorithmic breakthrough in lattice reduction. Finally, Dilithium signatures are *not* inherently blind or threshold-friendly; distributed-signing variants exist but add protocol complexity.

## 7 Conclusion

CRYSTALS-Dilithium — now ML-DSA under FIPS 204 — earned its position as the primary post-quantum signature standard through a rare combination of virtues: a clean reductionist security story rooted in Module-LWE and Module-SIS, a rejection-sampling design that avoids Gaussian samplers entirely, NTT arithmetic tuned to a hand-picked modulus for O(n log n) performance, and parameter sets spanning NIST categories 2, 3, and 5 at practical sizes [1][2][3]. For engineers, the deployment checklist is clear: use a validated implementation, keep signing **hedged**, enforce constant-time rejection checks, and — for devices exposed to physical attackers — budget the ~1.3–1.5× cost of masking and shuffling, since the side-channel literature shows unprotected Dilithium falls to single-trace and few-thousand-trace attacks [4][5][6][7]. Open problems remain: formally verified masked implementations with machine-checked leakage bounds, tighter concrete analysis of the SelfTargetMSIS assumption, and threshold/aggregate variants that preserve the scheme's simplicity. Dilithium does not end the post-quantum transition, but it is its load-bearing signature — and understanding it at the level of this thesis is now table stakes for cryptographic engineering.
