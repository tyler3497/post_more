# Confidential Computing at Hyperscale: AMD SEV-SNP, Intel TDX, and NVIDIA H100 Confidential Compute Mode for Attested VMs and GPU TEEs

## Abstract

Confidential computing protects data *in use*, closing the last gap in the encrypt-at-rest / encrypt-in-transit model that has dominated cloud security for a decade. This thesis compares the three production VM-scale trusted execution environments now operating at hyperscale: AMD SEV-SNP, Intel TDX, and NVIDIA H100 Confidential Compute mode for GPU TEEs. We dissect each platform's memory-encryption engine, integrity machinery, launch-measurement and remote-attestation protocol, and trust boundary, grounding the comparison in vendor specifications, formal verification results, and published attack research. Three findings emerge: (i) SEV-SNP's Reverse Map Table and TDX's secure EPT plus PAMT enforce the same anti-remap invariant through convergent integrity metadata; (ii) attestation freshness is the weakest link on all three platforms, as deterministic encryption and exportable reports admit physical-layer replay; and (iii) composite CPU–GPU attestation over SPDM-secured PCIe is now the confidential-AI standard but introduces cross-vendor policy complexity. We close with hyperscale deployment evidence from Azure and AWS, a catalog of side-channel and supply-chain concerns, and a roadmap toward TEE-I/O and encrypted multi-GPU fabrics.

## 1. Introduction

The public cloud's security contract has historically rested on a *trust the provider* premise: hypervisors, host operating systems, and datacenter operators are implicitly inside the trust boundary of every tenant workload. A decade of hypervisor escapes, firmware compromises, and insider-threat incidents has made this premise untenable for regulated industries, and it is categorically unacceptable for frontier AI workloads, where the asset under protection is not merely data but hundred-billion-parameter model weights representing billions of dollars of training investment.

*Confidential computing* redefines the contract. It promises that data remains encrypted and integrity-protected while it is being processed, with the cloud provider — hypervisor, host kernel, and physical operator — explicitly *outside* the trusted computing base (TCB). Three hardware technologies have emerged as the production vehicles for this promise at the virtual-machine scale:

1. **AMD SEV-SNP** — the third generation of AMD Secure Encrypted Virtualization, shipping on EPYC 7003 ("Milan") and later processors, combining per-VM memory encryption with hardware-enforced memory *integrity* via the Reverse Map Table (RMP) [1].
2. **Intel TDX (Trust Domain Extensions)** — Intel's VM-scale TEE on 4th-generation Xeon Scalable processors, isolating *trust domains* (TDs) behind a CPU-measured TDX module running in the new Secure Arbitration Mode (SEAM) [2][3].
3. **NVIDIA H100 Confidential Compute mode** — the first production GPU TEE, pairing an on-die hardware root of trust, measured boot, and encrypted PCIe DMA with CPU-side confidential VMs to deliver end-to-end confidential AI [4][5].

Individually, each of these systems has been documented in whitepapers and enabling guides. What is missing — and what this thesis supplies — is a rigorous comparative treatment that (a) aligns their integrity mechanisms on a common semantic footing, (b) formalizes their attestation protocols and their shared freshness vulnerabilities, and (c) evaluates the composite CPU–GPU architecture that hyperscalers now deploy for confidential inference and training. We draw on vendor architecture specifications [1][2][3][4], the formal verification literature [6], published physical and microarchitectural attacks [7][8][9], and deployment documentation from Azure, AWS, and the confidential-computing open-source ecosystem.

> **Theorem (Thesis claim).** *Across AMD SEV-SNP, Intel TDX, and NVIDIA H100 CC mode, the integrity guarantees converge on the same invariant — a hypervisor-mediated page cannot be aliased, remapped, or replayed into a TEE without detection — but the three architectures enforce the invariant with structurally different metadata (RMP entries, PAMT + secure EPT, and on-die firmware measurements plus SPDM session keys), producing distinct attestation interfaces, distinct residual attack surfaces, and distinct operational constraints at hyperscale.*

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the background of VM-based TEEs, distinguishing them from process-based enclaves. Section 3 states our methodology. Section 4 presents the deep-dive comparative architecture. Section 5 gives empirical results and formal results from the literature. Section 6 catalogs limitations and open problems. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From enclaves to confidential VMs

First-generation TEEs — Intel SGX, Arm TrustZone — were *process-* or *application-scale* enclaves. They required applications to be partitioned into trusted and untrusted components, a refactoring burden that limited adoption to niche high-assurance software. The industry pivot to *VM-scale* TEEs, beginning with AMD SEV (2016) and maturing through SEV-ES, SEV-SNP, and Intel TDX, inverted the model: the entire guest VM — kernel, userspace, and applications — runs inside the TEE, and unmodified operating systems "lift and shift" into confidential VMs (CVMs) [10]. This compatibility is why hyperscalers ship CVMs as first-class VM SKUs.

### 2.2 The three pillars: confidentiality, integrity, attestation

Every VM-scale TEE rests on three pillars:

- **Confidentiality.** Guest memory is encrypted with keys the hypervisor cannot access — per-VM keys derived by the AMD Secure Processor (AMD-SP) for SEV-SNP [1], per-TD AES-XTS-128 keys from Intel's Total Memory Encryption–Multi-Key (TME-MK) engine for TDX [2].
- **Integrity.** Encryption alone does not prevent the hypervisor from *remapping* or *replaying* ciphertext. SEV-SNP enforces integrity through the RMP, a system-wide table with one entry per 4 KiB DRAM page binding each system physical address to a guest physical address, ownership ASID, and page state [1]. TDX enforces it through the combination of *secure EPT* (extended page tables whose private translations are protected from hypervisor tampering) and the *Physical Address Metadata Table* (PAMT), which tracks per-page ownership, initialization, and TLB consistency [2][3].
- **Attestation.** Because the tenant cannot physically inspect the server, the hardware must *prove* that the TEE is genuine and running expected firmware and software. All three platforms implement the same abstract protocol: a hardware root of trust measures boot components, signs an attestation report binding the digest to a chip-unique identity key, and a relying party verifies the signature against the vendor's certificate infrastructure before provisioning secrets [4][11].

### 2.3 The GPU gap and its closure

Until 2023, the GPU was the glaring hole in confidential AI: a tenant could run a CVM on the CPU, but the moment tensors crossed PCIe to the accelerator, they were exposed to the host. NVIDIA closed this gap with H100 Confidential Compute mode (CC GA via CUDA 12.2 Update 1, August 2023) [4]: the H100 boots behind an on-die hardware root of trust, runs only NVIDIA-signed measured firmware, and establishes an SPDM 1.1+ authenticated channel with the CPU TEE, over which AES-GCM session keys encrypt all PCIe DMA. Blackwell-generation GPUs (B200/GB200) extend this with TEE-I/O — TDISP/SPDM plus PCIe IDE — eliminating Hopper's host-side bounce buffer, and add inline NVLink encryption for multi-GPU confidential training [5].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is an *architectural-analytic* study, not an experimental one. Our method has three components:

1. **Specification archaeology.** We extract the authoritative behavioral claims from primary sources: the AMD SEV-SNP Firmware ABI Specification [1], the Intel TDX Module Base Architecture Specification [3], Intel's TDX overview and module documentation [2], NVIDIA's H100 confidential-computing technical blog and Secure AI whitepaper [4][5], and the Intel–NVIDIA confidential-AI collaboration architecture [11].
2. **Adversarial literature triangulation.** Every security claim is checked against published attacks and formal analyses: the CrossLine ASID-confusion attacks on SEV [7], the formal security analysis of the SEV-SNP software interface [6], the SEV-SNP primer's catalog of residual threats [8], Hertzbleed DVFS side channels [9], and the TEE.fail DDR5-interposer physical attacks [12].
3. **Deployment evidence.** We ground hyperscale claims in public cloud documentation — Azure's confidential-VM portfolio and AWS's SEV-SNP EC2 instances [13][14].

We deliberately exclude performance micro-benchmarking (covered extensively elsewhere, e.g., the H100 CC overhead studies reporting ~5–10% on Hopper [5]) and focus on the *security architecture* comparison, which is under-theorized.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 AMD SEV-SNP: integrity via the Reverse Map Table

SEV-SNP's defining innovation over SEV and SEV-ES is *memory integrity against a malicious hypervisor*. The mechanism is the **Reverse Map Table (RMP)**: a single system-wide structure in DRAM containing one entry per 4 KiB page of physical memory that may be used by SNP guests [1]. Each RMP entry records:

| Field | Role |
|---|---|
| `Assigned` | Page is owned by a guest (vs. hypervisor) |
| `Validated` | Guest has executed `PVALIDATE` on the page |
| `ASID` | Owning guest's address-space identifier |
| `GPA` | Guest physical address the page is bound to |
| `Immutable` / `VMSA` | Special firmware-context markings |

The RMP, *combined with all guests' nested page tables*, enforces a global one-to-one mapping between system physical addresses and guest physical addresses: a page cannot be mapped into two guests simultaneously, nor twice into one guest, and the hypervisor cannot silently alter the GPA→SPA binding after the guest has validated it [1]. Page state transitions are mediated by hardware/firmware: the guest requests pages via the GHCB `Page State Change` VMGEXIT, the hypervisor issues `RMPUPDATE`/`PSMASH`, and the hardware clears `Validated` on reassignment — forcing the guest to re-validate (or fault with `#VC`) before use.

**VMPL** (VM privilege levels) adds intra-guest privilege separation: four levels, with VMPL0 hosting the Secure VM Service Module (SVSM), enabling an in-guest vTPM protected by the same SNP machinery [10]. **Attestation** is guest-initiated: the running guest issues `SNP_GUEST_REQUEST`, and the AMD-SP returns a report containing the *launch measurement* (a digest accumulated across `LAUNCH_START`/`LAUNCH_UPDATE`/`LAUNCH_FINISH`), the TCB version, VMPL, policy, and 64 bytes of caller-supplied `REPORT_DATA`, signed by the chip's VCEK (Versioned Chip Endorsement Key) or VLEK (for cloud providers) [1][6].

> **Theorem (RMP soundness, informal).** *If the RMP is correctly initialized and the AMD-SP is trusted, then for every system physical page `p` with `Assigned(p) = 1` and `Validated(p) = 1`, all CPU accesses to `p` through any nested page table resolve to the single `(ASID, GPA)` recorded in `RMP[p]`, and any hypervisor attempt to create a second mapping raises a nested page fault.* This is the property that defeats the CrossLine class of ASID-confusion attacks, which succeeded precisely because legacy SEV lacked the ownership check [7].

### 4.2 Intel TDX: isolation via SEAM and the TDX module

TDX takes a philosophically different route to the same destination. Rather than a separate security processor, TDX introduces **Secure Arbitration Mode (SEAM)** — a new VMX root mode that hosts the **Intel TDX module**, a CPU-measured software module running as a *peer* to the hypervisor [2][3]. The module resides in a reserved memory region identified by the SEAM Range Register (SEAMRR). The hypervisor interacts with it exclusively through `SEAMCALL`/`SEAMRET`: the module creates TDs, adds and measures pages, enters TD execution, scrubs register state on exit, and returns to the VMM.

The trust boundary is crisp and instructive:

- **Inside the boundary:** the TDX module (SEAM root), TD private memory, TD CPU state.
- **Outside the boundary:** the hypervisor/VMM, host OS, device drivers, DMA engines, and all non-SEAM software.

Memory protection rests on three elements: (i) **per-TD AES-XTS-128 keys** from the TME-MK engine encrypt private pages; (ii) **secure EPT** protects the integrity of GPA→HPA translations and blocks TD instruction fetches from shared memory; (iii) the **PAMT** tracks page allocation, initialization, and TLB shootdown consistency [2][3]. A *shared bit* in the GPA lets the TD designate pages shared with the hypervisor (e.g., for paravirtual I/O), with the TDX module enforcing that private pages are never aliased as shared.

**Attestation** in TDX is quoting-enclave based: the TD calls `TDG.MR.REPORT` to obtain a CPU-MACed `TDREPORT`, then a **Quoting TD** (backed by an SGX quoting enclave via `SEAMREPORT`) converts it into a remotely verifiable quote signed with Intel-provisioned keys, verified against Intel PCS/PCCS collaterals [3][10].

### 4.3 NVIDIA H100 CC mode: the GPU as a first-class TEE

The H100 in CC mode completes the triad. The architecture [4][5]:

1. **Measured boot.** An on-die hardware root of trust verifies NVIDIA-signed firmware; the firmware measurement (SHA-256) is recorded, and the device identity certificate — rooted in a per-chip ECC-384 key fused at manufacture — is retrievable via `nvidia-smi` [4].
2. **SPDM handshake.** The CPU CVM (SEV-SNP or TDX) and GPU perform an SPDM 1.1+ handshake establishing AES-GCM session keys. All subsequent PCIe DMA is encrypted; the hypervisor, sitting on the PCIe path, observes only ciphertext [4].
3. **Encrypted HBM.** GPU device memory (HBM3) is encrypted with session keys; CUDA kernels execute on plaintext *inside* the package, and data leaving the GPU is always ciphertext [5].
4. **Attestation.** The GPU emits a signed attestation report (firmware measurement + configuration), verifiable through the NVIDIA Remote Attestation Service (NRAS), with OCSP-based revocation checking [4].

On Hopper, CPU↔GPU transfers pass through an **encrypted bounce buffer** — the CPU TEE encrypts into it, the GPU copies the ciphertext into the package and decrypts. Blackwell's TEE-I/O (TDISP + PCIe IDE) removes this staging copy, and inline NVLink encryption extends the encrypted fabric across GPUs [5]. The Intel–NVIDIA collaboration further defines **composite attestation**: a single request to Intel Trust Authority (Project Amber) collects CPU-TDX and GPU evidence, with Amber transparently integrating NRAS, so the relying party appraises one policy over both TEEs [11].

### 4.4 Comparative synthesis: one invariant, three enforcements

| Dimension | AMD SEV-SNP | Intel TDX | NVIDIA H100 CC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Encryption engine | AMD-SP–managed per-VM keys; SME-derived | TME-MK per-TD AES-XTS-128 | SPDM-derived AES-GCM session keys; HBM encryption |
| Integrity metadata | RMP (sPA→gPA, ASID, state) | Secure EPT + PAMT | On-die RoT measurements; SPDM auth |
| Root of trust | AMD-SP (separate security processor) | CPU-measured TDX module in SEAM | On-die GPU hardware RoT + fused ECC-384 identity |
| Intra-TEE privilege | VMPL0–3 (SVSM, vTPM) | TD partitioning; service TDs | CUDA streams; MIG partitioning (non-CC) |
| Attestation primitive | `SNP_GUEST_REQUEST` → VCEK/VLEK-signed report | `TDG.MR.REPORT` → Quoting TD → Intel-signed quote | Device identity cert + NRAS-verified report |
| Guest enlightenment | GHCB, `#VC`/`#HV` handling | `TDCALL`/`TDG.VP.VMCALL`, shared-bit | Bounce-buffer DMA; no app changes |
| Cloud presence | Azure DCasv5/ECasv5, AWS M6a/C6a/R6a | Azure DCesv6 series | Azure NCCadsH100v5 (with SEV-SNP CPU) |

The table makes the thesis claim concrete: the *invariant* — no hypervisor-visible aliasing, remapping, or replay of TEE pages — is identical, while the enforcement metadata and the attestation ceremony differ in ways that matter operationally (e.g., who signs what, which revocation infrastructure must be reachable, how live migration interacts with attestation freshness [6]).

## 5. Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

Because this thesis is analytic, this section reports the *results of the literature* as our empirical substrate.

**Result 1 — Formal verification of SEV-SNP's software interface.** The first comprehensive formal model of the SEV-SNP ABI (covering launch, attestation, key derivation, page swap, and live migration) produced automated proofs of secrecy, authenticity, attestation, and freshness properties — including correct stream-cipher usage — but *failed* to prove several authentication and attestation properties when guests are launched with migration-enabled policies, due to missing platform binding in guest messages [6]. This is not a theoretical quibble: live migration of CVMs is a hyperscaler operational requirement, and the gap identifies precisely where a formal guarantee ends and operational policy must begin.

**Result 2 — CrossLine and the pre-SNP baseline.** The CrossLine attacks demonstrated stealthy extraction of SEV/SEV-ES guest memory by abusing hypervisor-controlled ASID management under the "security-by-crash" assumption — succeeding even against powered-off victim VMs so long as keys remained in the AMD-SP [7]. The authors explicitly note the RMP ownership check defeats both CrossLine variants in SEV-SNP. The result functions as a *differential proof*: it isolates exactly which design delta (RMP-enforced ownership) converts a broken TEE into a sound one.

**Result 3 — Physical-layer replay against deterministic encryption.** The TEE.fail research program showed that DDR5 bus interposition with sub-$1,000 logic-analyzer rigs defeats the deterministic AES-XTS encryption (no integrity or freshness) used by Intel SGX/TDX and, in modified form, replays *attestation reports* against SEV-SNP — certifying manipulated VMs as legitimate [12]. Related work demonstrated interposer-based ciphertext aliasing against SGX enclaves [8]. The empirical lesson: *no VM-scale TEE in production today provides memory-integrity against an adversary with physical bus access*; the threat model explicitly excludes it, and hyperscalers compensate operationally (datacenter physical security) rather than cryptographically.

**Result 4 — Microarchitectural side channels persist.** Hertzbleed showed DVFS-induced frequency variations leak cryptographic keys on both Intel (CVE-2022-24436) and AMD (CVE-2022-23823) processors, observable remotely via timing [9]. The SEV-SNP primer catalogs the broader residual surface: the hypervisor observes guest-operation timing regardless of memory encryption, and attestation reports *identity* (measurements), not *behavior* — a backdoored guest image attests faithfully [8]. TEEs shrink the TCB; they do not patch the software inside it.

**Result 5 — Deployment reality.** Azure's portfolio is the most complete: SEV-SNP CVMs (DCasv5/ECasv5, plus v6 generations), TDX CVMs (DCesv6/ECesv6), and the NCCadsH100v5 SKU pairing SEV-SNP CPUs with H100 NVL GPUs in CC mode for dual (AMD + NVIDIA) attestation [13]. AWS offers SEV-SNP on M6a/C6a/R6a instances with AMD VLEK attestation in limited regions [14]. The confidential-AI pattern — TDX/SEV-SNP CVM + H100 CC + composite attestation via Intel Trust Authority integrated with NRAS — is the converged reference architecture [11].

```python
# Abstract model of the attestation verification every relying party must implement,
# whether the evidence comes from AMD-SP, the TDX Quoting TD, or NRAS.
def verify_attestation(report, expected_measurements, trust_anchors):
    # 1. Signature over the report by a chip-unique identity key
    assert verify_signature(report.signature, report.body, trust_anchors.vendor_ca)
    # 2. Freshness: challenge nonce echoed in REPORT_DATA / user-data field
    assert report.body.nonce == session.challenge_nonce
    # 3. TCB version >= minimum (firmware revocation enforcement)
    assert report.body.tcb_version >= policy.min_tcb
    # 4. Launch measurement matches a known-good image digest
    assert report.body.measurement in expected_measurements
    # 5. Revocation: identity cert not on OCSP/CRL deny list
    assert not trust_anchors.revocation.is_revoked(report.body.device_id)
    return AttestationVerdict.TRUSTED
```

The pseudocode's second assertion — freshness via nonce — is the step most frequently weakened in practice (cached reports, missing nonces), and it is exactly the step the physical-replay attacks of Result 3 exploit when verifiers accept stale evidence [8][12].

## 6. Limitations

We enumerate the limitations honestly, as they define the research frontier:

1. **No integrity against physical adversaries.** As established in Section 5, deterministic memory encryption without freshness admits bus-level replay and aliasing [8][12]. Memory-integrity trees (à la SGX's MEE) do not scale to terabyte VM footprints, so this is an accepted, documented gap — but "documented" is not "solved."
2. **Attestation is identity, not behavior.** A correctly measured malicious image attests successfully [8]. Confidential computing moves the trust decision from *whom do I trust to host* to *which digests do I trust to run* — a supply-chain problem (reproducible builds, signed image pipelines) that hardware cannot discharge.
3. **Migration vs. attestation tension.** Live-migrating a CVM changes the platform binding that attestation reports implicitly rely on; the formal analysis shows authentication properties failing under migration-enabled policies [6]. Hyperscalers must choose between operational flexibility and the strongest attestation semantics.
4. **Side channels are out of scope by design.** Interrupt-timing observation, DVFS leakage [9], cache and performance-counter channels, and the emerging class of GPU-specific channels (performance counters, memory-allocator timing) are not mitigated by any of the three platforms' memory-encryption machinery.
5. **Composite attestation complexity.** CPU+GPU attestation doubles the PKI surface (vendor CAs, OCSP/NRAS, Intel PCS) and introduces cross-vendor policy composition — a new failure mode in which a CPU TEE is fresh but the GPU report is stale, or vice versa [11].
6. **Firmware TCB concentration.** Each architecture concentrates trust in one vendor's firmware: AMD-SP, the Intel TDX module/ACM, NVIDIA's GPU firmware. The extracted attestation-seed incident on first-generation SNP parts [8] demonstrates that this concentration is a real, not hypothetical, single point of failure.
7. **Performance and operational overhead.** Hopper CC mode's bounce buffer and ~5–10% overhead [5], TDX's secure-EPT exit costs, and SEV-SNP's RMP-management overheads are acceptable for inference but remain a tax on training throughput — one reason Blackwell's TEE-I/O and inline NVLink encryption matter architecturally, not just incrementally.

---

## 7. Conclusion

AMD SEV-SNP, Intel TDX, and NVIDIA H100 Confidential Compute mode jointly represent the maturation of confidential computing from research prototype to hyperscale commodity. Their designs differ — a security processor with a reverse map table, a new CPU mode hosting a measured module, an on-die GPU root of trust with SPDM-secured DMA — but they converge on a single enforceable invariant: the infrastructure operator cannot read, remap, or replay the tenant's in-use data without detection. The formal literature confirms the core protocols while precisely locating their boundaries (migration, freshness, physical access) [6]; the attack literature confirms that those boundaries are where real adversaries will push [7][9][12]; and the cloud portfolios confirm that the composite CPU–GPU confidential architecture is already the production shape of confidential AI [11][13].

The road ahead is clear. TEE-I/O (TDISP, PCIe IDE) will retire the bounce buffer; fabric-level encryption will extend the TEE across NVLink and, eventually, rack-scale interconnects; and attestation infrastructure — composite, policy-driven, continuously re-verified rather than one-shot — will become as operationally central as TLS certificate management is today. The deeper lesson is architectural: *confidential computing does not eliminate trust; it concentrates and relocates it* — from the cloud operator to the silicon vendor's root of trust, from the hypervisor to the measured image digest.
