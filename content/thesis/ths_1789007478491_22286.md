# BLAKE3: Tree Hashing Modes, SIMD Parallelism, and the Bao Verified Streaming Construction

## Abstract

BLAKE3, designed by Jack O'Connor, Samuel Neves, Jean-Philippe Aumasson, and Zooko Wilcox-O'Hearn and published in January 2020, reframes cryptographic hashing as a parallel tree computation rather than a serial chain [1]. This thesis reconstructs the design from its specification: a BLAKE2s-derived compression function reduced to seven rounds, a binary Merkle tree over 1024-byte chunks keyed by a 64-bit chunk counter and a domain-separation flag word, and three modes — hashing, keyed hashing, and key derivation — unified by a single extendable-output construction. We analyze how the tree structure unlocks two independent axes of parallelism, SIMD *hash-many* lanes and thread-level subtree hashing, and how the same tree yields Bao, a verified streaming format in which arbitrary byte ranges can be decoded against a logarithmic-size Merkle proof. We review the paper's benchmark methodology and independent commodity-hardware measurements, sketch the security arguments for the reduced round count and the tree iteration mode, and close with limitations — thinner cryptanalytic margins than BLAKE2s and unsuitability for password hashing — alongside open problems in formally verified tree hashing.

## 1. Introduction

For three decades, mainstream hash functions were serial machines. Merkle–Damgård designs (MD5, SHA-1, SHA-2) and the SHA-3 sponge process message blocks in strict sequence: block *i+1* cannot begin until block *i* has been absorbed. This serialization caps throughput at the latency of a single compression-function evaluation and forecloses incremental verification — to check byte 900,000 of a file you must first hash bytes 0 through 899,999.

BLAKE3 breaks the chain. It keeps the well-studied ARX round function of BLAKE2s but replaces serial iteration with a binary tree: the input is split into 1024-byte chunks, each chunk is hashed independently, and pairs of 32-byte chaining values are merged upward until a single root remains [1]. Because chunks are independent, they can be hashed in any order, in parallel, and on demand. The official project page distills the consequences: BLAKE3 is faster than MD5, SHA-1, SHA-2, SHA-3, and BLAKE2; it resists length extension unlike SHA-2; its inner Merkle tree makes it parallelizable across threads and SIMD lanes and enables verified streaming and incremental updates; and one algorithm serves as hash, PRF, MAC, KDF, and XOF [2]. The official Rust and C implementations back this up with SIMD backends for SSE2, SSE4.1, AVX2, AVX-512, NEON, and WASM, runtime CPU feature detection on x86, and optional multithreading [3].

This thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 traces the lineage from BLAKE through BLAKE2 and surveys the cryptanalytic context. Section 3 describes the reconstruction methodology. Section 4 dissects the compression function, the tree mode and its flag-based domain separation, the three hashing modes, SIMD and thread parallelism, and the Bao verified-streaming construction. Section 5 reviews empirical performance and the security arguments; Section 6 catalogues limitations and open problems.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 From BLAKE to BLAKE2

BLAKE, designed by Aumasson and co-authors, was one of five finalists in the NIST SHA-3 competition. BLAKE2 (2013), standardized in RFC 7693, simplified and accelerated the design: BLAKE2s uses 32-bit words and 10 rounds, BLAKE2b uses 64-bit words and 12 rounds, and the compression function takes a chaining value, a message block, an offset counter *t*, and finalization flags *f* [4]. Its quarter-round *G* mixes two message words into four state words with modular additions, XORs, and rotations — the ARX pattern that BLAKE3 inherits verbatim, rotation constants included.

### 2.2 Parallel and tree hashing before BLAKE3

BLAKE3 was not the first to parallelize hashing — BLAKE2bp/BLAKE2sp fanned work across parallel lanes, Skein's UBI chaining supported tree modes, and KangarooTwelve built a tree over a sponge — but it was the first to make the *tree* the default and only iteration mode, so that parallelism, streaming verification, and incremental updates fall out of a single construction rather than bolted-on variants [1].

### 2.3 Cryptanalytic context

ARX designs have absorbed two decades of differential and linear cryptanalysis. The relevant baseline is not the full functions, which remain unbroken, but round-reduced variants: for example, higher-order differential meet-in-the-middle techniques achieve preimages on only 2.75 rounds of BLAKE and pseudo-preimages on 7.5 rounds of its compression function — a small fraction of the deployed round counts [5]. BLAKE3's security argument leans on this gap: cut the rounds for speed, but keep the cryptanalytic distance between the best published attack and the full function comfortably large.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is an analytical reconstruction, not a new implementation. The primary source is the BLAKE3 specification and design rationale maintained by the BLAKE3 team [1]. Structural claims about the tree algorithm are cross-checked against the official reference implementation in Rust — deliberately small and readable, discussed in Section 5.1 of the paper — and the optimized Rust and C implementations that carry the SIMD and threading machinery [3]. The Bao streaming layer is reconstructed from its canonical implementation and its deliberately minimal second implementation in Python, together with the official test vectors that pin the encoding [6]. Performance claims follow the paper's benchmark figures and an independent commodity-hardware evaluation; the TLA+ invariant in Section 4.2 and the security sketches in Section 5 are original to this thesis.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Compression Function: BLAKE2s at Seven Rounds

The BLAKE3 compression function takes five inputs and is, but for the round count, the BLAKE2s compression function:

| Input | Size | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `h[0..8]` | 256 bits | input chaining value (or IV, or key) |
| `m[0..16]` | 512 bits | message block, consumed as round keys |
| `t0, t1` | 64 bits | counter: the chunk index for leaves, 0 for parents |
| `b` | 32 bits | number of message bytes in this block |
| `d` | 32 bits | domain-separation flag word |

The 16-word state is initialized from the chaining value, the BLAKE2s IV constants (`0x6a09e667, 0xbb67ae85, …` — the fractional parts of the square roots of the first primes, shared with SHA-256), the counter, the byte count, and the flags. It then undergoes **7 rounds** of the BLAKE2s round function, versus 10 in BLAKE2s [1][4]. Each round applies the quarter-round *G* to the four columns and then the four diagonals of the 4×4 state, with the message schedule reusing BLAKE2s's permutations σ:

```rust
fn g(state: &mut [u32; 16], a: usize, b: usize, c: usize, d: usize, x: u32, y: u32) {
    state[a] = state[a].wrapping_add(state[b]).wrapping_add(x);
    state[d] = (state[d] ^ state[a]).rotate_right(16);
    state[c] = state[c].wrapping_add(state[d]);
    state[b] = (state[b] ^ state[c]).rotate_right(12);
    state[a] = state[a].wrapping_add(state[b]).wrapping_add(y);
    state[d] = (state[d] ^ state[a]).rotate_right(8);
    state[c] = state[c].wrapping_add(state[d]);
    state[b] = (state[b] ^ state[c]).rotate_right(7);
}
```

The round function, the IV, the message permutations, and even the rotation constants are inherited unchanged; the *only* cryptographic novelty at this layer is deleting three rounds [1]. The output state has 16 words: words 0–7 form the 32-byte chaining value used by parent nodes, while all 16 words (64 bytes) serve as output blocks for the root and the XOF. Seven rounds were chosen so that the best known differential characteristics still fall far short of the full permutation, preserving a margin the designers judged comfortable while removing 30% of BLAKE2s's per-block work [1].

### 4.2 The Tree Mode: A Binary Merkle Tree with a Stack Discipline

Iteration is a binary tree over 1024-byte chunks. Chunk *i* is compressed with counter *t = i*; pairs of chaining values are compressed as 64-byte parent blocks with counter 0; the process repeats to a single root [1]. An incremental hasher never materializes the tree: it keeps a *stack* of chaining values, at most ⌈log₂ n⌉ deep (54 entries suffice for the maximum 2⁶⁴-byte input), merging equal-height subtrees bottom-up exactly as binary addition carries bits:

```python
def push_cv(stack, cv, height):
    # stack holds (height, chaining_value); merge while the top matches
    while stack and stack[-1][0] == height:
        _, left = stack.pop()
        cv = compress_parent(left, cv)  # counter=0, flags=PARENT
        height += 1
    stack.append((height, cv))
```

![Binary Merkle tree hashing in BLAKE3: 1024-byte chunks hashed with per-chunk counters merge pairwise through chaining values to a single root](public/thesis/ths_1789007478491_22286-0.webp)

The stack discipline admits a crisp invariant, stated here in TLA+:

```tla
---------------------------- MODULE Blake3Stack ----------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Sequences
CONSTANT MaxHeight
VARIABLES stack  \* sequence of [h |-> height, cv |-> chaining value]

TypeOK == \A i \in 1..Len(stack) : stack[i].h \in 0..MaxHeight
NoDupHeight == \A i, j \in 1..Len(stack) : i /= j => stack[i].h /= stack[j].h
StrictlyIncreasing == \A i \in 1..Len(stack) - 1 : stack[i].h < stack[i+1].h
Inv == TypeOK /\ NoDupHeight /\ StrictlyIncreasing
=============================================================================
```

*Inv* holds initially (empty stack) and is preserved by `push_cv`: merging consumes the matching-height entry before appending, so no height ever duplicates and heights increase toward the top. The final fold over the stack yields exactly the root of the full binary tree — the incremental and batch computations coincide by construction.

Node types are distinguished not by position but by the flag word *d*:

| Flag | Bit | Set on |
|---|---|---|
| `CHUNK_START` | 1 ≪ 0 | first block of a chunk |
| `CHUNK_END` | 1 ≪ 1 | last block of a chunk |
| `PARENT` | 1 ≪ 2 | interior node hashing two chaining values |
| `ROOT` | 1 ≪ 3 | the final root node |
| `KEYED_HASH` | 1 ≪ 4 | every node in keyed mode |
| `DERIVE_KEY_CONTEXT` | 1 ≪ 5 | context-string hashing |
| `DERIVE_KEY_MATERIAL` | 1 ≪ 6 | key-material hashing |

> Theorem: (Domain separation.) The sets of compression-function inputs used for chunk leaves, parent nodes, root nodes, and the three modes are pairwise disjoint.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Any two nodes of different type differ in the flag word *d* (leaf blocks carry `CHUNK_START`/`CHUNK_END`, interior nodes `PARENT`, the root additionally `ROOT`), and the mode bits `KEYED_HASH`, `DERIVE_KEY_CONTEXT`, `DERIVE_KEY_MATERIAL` separate the three modes from each other and from unkeyed hashing. Distinct chunks additionally differ in the counter *t*. Since the compression function is deterministic, disjoint input domains imply disjoint computation domains. ∎

This flag discipline is what makes length extension impossible: an attacker cannot graft a suffix onto a digest because the final block of the original message carries `CHUNK_END | ROOT`, and no valid node in an extended message reproduces that flag combination at the same counter [1].

### 4.3 One Primitive, Three Modes

The same tree, keyed three ways:

| Mode | IV | Flags | Provides |
|---|---|---|---|
| `hash` | standard IV | chunk/parent/root flags | collision-resistant digest, XOF |
| `keyed_hash` | 256-bit key as IV | `KEYED_HASH` on all nodes | PRF / MAC, no HMAC nesting needed |
| `derive_key` | derived from context string | `DERIVE_KEY_CONTEXT`, `DERIVE_KEY_MATERIAL` | KDF with domain separation |

In `keyed_hash` the secret key *replaces* the IV and the `KEYED_HASH` bit is set throughout, which domain-separates MAC computation from plain hashing at the compression-function layer — there is no need for the HMAC double-hash ritual [1]. In `derive_key`, the context string (the project recommends a hardcoded, globally unique, application-specific string such as `"[application] [timestamp] [purpose]"` [2]) is itself hashed with `DERIVE_KEY_CONTEXT` to produce the effective IV, and the input key material is hashed under `DERIVE_KEY_MATERIAL`; different contexts therefore yield independent subkeys even from identical key material. Output is extendable: the 256-bit digest is simply the first 32 bytes of the XOF stream, and callers may read arbitrarily many bytes without knowing the length in advance [1].

One explicit non-mode: BLAKE3 is *not* a password hash. It is designed to be fast, and password hashing must be slow; the project directs users to Argon2 [2].

### 4.4 SIMD Parallelism and Multithreading

The tree buys two orthogonal axes of parallelism. The first lives *inside* the compression function call: because chunks are independent, the implementation compresses several chunks with a single SIMD instruction stream — the `hash_many` interface. One vector of 32-bit lanes holds lane *j* of *N* parallel states, so a single AVX2 instruction performs eight additions across eight chunks at once:

| Backend | Vector width | Parallel chunks |
|---|---|---|
| SSE2 / SSE4.1 | 128-bit | 4 |
| NEON (AArch64) | 128-bit | 4 |
| AVX2 | 256-bit | 8 |
| AVX-512 | 512-bit | 16 |

Runtime CPU feature detection on x86 selects the widest available backend, and portable scalar code covers everything else [3]. The paper's measurements show this regime engaging at inputs of about 2 KiB and above: below one chunk, BLAKE3 closely tracks BLAKE2s, since there is nothing to parallelize [1].

The second axis is thread-level: the input is split into subtrees, each hashed by a worker thread (via Rayon in the Rust implementation), and the resulting chaining values are merged exactly as the single-threaded stack would merge them [1][3]. Because subtree roots are just chaining values, threading changes no wire format — a multithreaded hash equals the single-threaded hash bit for bit. The two axes compose: each thread runs `hash_many` over its own subtree, so on a 16-lane AVX-512 machine with *T* threads the design exposes up to 16*T* compression instances in flight.

### 4.5 Bao: Verified Streaming on the BLAKE3 Tree

Bao, originally Jack O'Connor's verified-streaming format for BLAKE2 and since ported to BLAKE3, turns the inner Merkle tree outward [6]. *Encoding* a file computes the BLAKE3 tree and persists the interior chaining values — the "tree bytes" — in one of two layouts:

| Encoding | Layout | Use case |
|---|---|---|
| Combined | content bytes interleaved with tree bytes | self-contained archive |
| Outboard | tree bytes in a sidecar file | avoid duplicating huge inputs; verify slices of a remote file |

*Decoding* is streaming verification: as each chunk arrives, the decoder recomputes its chaining value and checks it against the tree, so a corrupted or malicious chunk is rejected immediately without reading the rest of the file [6]. The price is overhead: 32 bytes of tree per 1024-byte chunk, roughly 3%. Iroh's `bao_tree` variant groups 16 chunks (16 KiB) per group node, cutting the outboard to about a sixteenth of standard Bao's size [7]. Because the tree is a Merkle tree, *slices* need only a logarithmic-size proof: `decode_slice` extracts an arbitrary byte range together with the sibling chaining values on the path to the root, and verifies the range without the prefix — the primitive behind verified range requests and resumable downloads [8]. The canonical Rust implementation ships a `bao_bin` command-line tool on crates.io, and the repository carries a second, deliberately minimal Python implementation plus official test vectors, making the format straightforward to reimplement [6].

> Theorem: (Streaming soundness.) Assuming the compression function is collision-resistant, a Bao decoder with trusted root *R* outputs a byte stream *S* only if *S* equals the encoded content.
>
> *Proof sketch.* The decoder recomputes every chaining value on the path from each output chunk to the root and compares against the encoded tree; any accepted chunk therefore chains to *R*. Producing an accepted stream differing from the committed content would require two distinct preimages of some chaining value — a collision. ∎

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

**Benchmark methodology.** The paper measures throughput in cycles per byte on an AWS `c5.metal` instance (dual Intel Xeon Platinum 8275CL, Cascade Lake-SP, AVX-512), sweeping input sizes from 64 bytes to 512 KiB single-threaded and scaling threads to 48 for the multithreaded figure [1]. The resulting curve passes through distinct regimes: up to 1 KiB BLAKE3 mirrors BLAKE2s; from roughly 2 KiB the SIMD chunk-parallelism engages and the curve drops steeply; threading then scales throughput into the tens of GiB/s. Across the sweep, BLAKE3 leads BLAKE2b, BLAKE2s, BLAKE2bp, SHA-256, SHA-512, and KangarooTwelve — the gap widening with input size as parallelism compounds [1].

**Independent confirmation.** A commodity-hardware study on an AMD EPYC 7501 system compared Blake3 against SHA-256 and SHA-512 across thread counts, finding Blake3 generating roughly an order of magnitude more hashes per second on a single thread, with the advantage persisting as threads scale, alongside better CPU utilization and cache behavior attributed to the chunked, SIMD-friendly access pattern [9].

**Security arguments.** Three claims carry the design: (i) the 7-round permutation retains a large margin because the best published cryptanalysis of the BLAKE/BLAKE2 round function reaches only a few rounds [5]; (ii) the flag-based domain separation of Section 4.2 makes the tree an indifferentiable construction — distinct node types and modes never share compression-function inputs, so the tree inherits the compression function's collision and preimage resistance up to the standard tree-hashing bounds; (iii) the XOF output blocks are domain-separated from chaining values by the `ROOT` flag and per-block counter, so extended output cannot be confused with interior tree nodes [1].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Thinner margins.** Seven rounds is an aggressive cut: BLAKE2s ships 10, BLAKE2b 12. The margin argument is relative — "far from the best attack" — and any future breakthrough in ARX cryptanalysis would reach BLAKE3's round count before its predecessors' [5]. Conservative applications that hash short, latency-sensitive inputs gain little from the tree and might prefer the deeper margin of BLAKE2s.

**Not a password hash, not a standard.** Speed is the point, so BLAKE3 must never be used for password storage — Argon2 remains the recommendation [2]. It is also not NIST-standardized; formal standing rests on the team's specification and the community cryptography specification process rather than a FIPS document.

**Quantum and multi-target bounds.** Grover's algorithm halves the 256-bit preimage strength to 128 bits; multi-target preimage attacks gain a further logarithmic factor, standard caveats shared with all 256-bit hashes. Tight, concrete bounds for the tree mode under multi-target adversaries are not published.

**Bao overhead.** Standard Bao pays ~3% tree overhead and complicates storage layouts with sidecar outboards; chunk-group variants trade proof granularity for size [7]. Verified streaming for *mutable* content — authenticated appends and edits without re-encoding — remains ad hoc.

**Open problems.** (1) A machine-checked indifferentiability proof for the flag-separated tree mode, ideally verified against the reference implementation. (2) Optimal chunk-size theory: 1024 bytes balances SIMD lane amortization against proof granularity, but no published analysis derives it from first principles. (3) Hardware acceleration: dedicated instructions exist for SHA-2 and SHA-3 on several platforms; BLAKE3 relies on general SIMD, and its ARX structure resists the kind of instruction-set support that AES enjoys. (4) Formally verified, constant-time SIMD backends — the current assembly is hand-tuned and audited, not proven.

## 7. Conclusion

BLAKE3's thesis is architectural: take a trusted ARX round function, spend the saved rounds on a tree, and let parallelism, streaming verification, and multi-mode keying emerge from the iteration mode rather than from separate constructions. The compression function is deliberately unoriginal — BLAKE2s with three rounds removed — because the novelty sits one level up, in the counter-and-flag discipline that turns a Merkle tree into a hash, a MAC, a KDF, and a verified streaming format simultaneously [1]. The empirical record supports the bet: on modern SIMD hardware it outruns every standardized alternative by a wide margin [1][9]. The open questions are commensurately architectural — verified proofs of the tree mode, principled chunk sizing, and hardware support — and they define the research program this design invites.
