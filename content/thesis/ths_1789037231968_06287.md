# Against Active Adversaries: From Yao's Garbled Circuits and GMW to Beaver Triples and the SPDZ Offline/Online Architecture for Maliciously Secure Multi-Party Computation

## Abstract

Secure multi-party computation (MPC) enables mutually distrusting parties to evaluate a joint function of their private inputs while revealing nothing beyond the output. This thesis traces the discipline's central arc: from the foundational semi-honest constructions — Yao's garbled circuits [1] and the Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson Boolean-circuit compiler [2] — to the modern malicious-security regime anchored by Beaver's multiplication triples [4] and the SPDZ offline/online architecture [3]. We formalize *authenticated* additive secret sharing under a global MAC key, prove the correctness of triple-based online multiplication, and quantify how garbling optimizations (free XOR, half gates [6]) and oblivious-transfer extension [5][9] moved MPC from theoretical feasibility to gigabyte-scale engineering. We close by mapping the open frontiers: the impossibility of fairness under dishonest majority, preprocessing bottlenecks in triple generation [8], and the asymptotic limits of garbled-circuit size.

## 1 Introduction

In 1982, Andrew Yao posed the founding vignette of secure computation: two millionaires wish to learn *who is richer* without disclosing their wealth [1]. Within five years, Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson proved the field's **completeness theorem**: assuming trapdoor permutations exist, *every* efficiently computable function can be evaluated securely with an honest majority [2].

The *against whom* clause is decisive. Protocols proven secure against **semi-honest** (honest-but-curious) adversaries collapse when a participant deviates, sends malformed messages, or aborts strategically. Bridging the gap demanded two revolutions:

1. **The arithmetic turn.** Beaver's multiplication triples [4] pushed the expensive part of secure multiplication into an input-independent *preprocessing* phase, leaving an online phase costing essentially two field-element openings per gate.
2. **The authenticated turn.** The SPDZ protocol [3] fused triples with information-theoretic message authentication codes under a secret global key, achieving active security against *any* number of corruptions — a dishonest majority — with abort as the only concession.

This thesis develops these ideas from first principles, unifies them in a single formal framework, and evaluates where the paradigm stands and where it strains.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The ideal/real paradigm

Modern MPC security is defined by *comparison to an ideal world*. In the ideal world, parties hand their inputs to an incorruptible trusted party, which computes the function and returns the outputs. A protocol is secure if, for every real-world adversary, there exists an ideal-world simulator whose interaction with the environment is computationally indistinguishable from the real execution [7]. This *simulation paradigm* cleanly separates correctness, privacy, and the subtler properties of **fairness** (all parties receive output or none do) and **guaranteed output delivery**.

Adversary models form a strict hierarchy of strength:

- **Semi-honest.** Corrupted parties follow the protocol exactly but attempt to infer extra information from their view.
- **Covert.** Parties deviate only if the probability of being caught is negligible; a middle ground useful for deterrence.
- **Malicious (active).** Corrupted parties may deviate arbitrarily.

Corruption structure matters equally. **Honest-majority** protocols (e.g., BGW) tolerate fewer than half (semi-honest) or a third (malicious) of corrupted parties. **Dishonest-majority** protocols tolerate up to *n − 1* corruptions but must surrender fairness: Cleve's 1986 impossibility result shows that fair general computation is impossible without an honest majority. The achievable notion is *security with abort*: honest parties either obtain correct output or detect cheating and abort.

### 2.2 Cryptographic building blocks

Three primitives underpin everything that follows.

**Oblivious transfer (OT).** In 1-out-of-2 OT, a sender holds *(m₀, m₁)* and a receiver holds a choice bit *b*; the receiver learns *m_b* only, while the sender learns nothing about *b*. Kilian showed OT is *complete* for MPC. The practical breakthrough was **OT extension** [5]: from κ "base" OTs, parties derive *m ≫ κ* effective OTs at the cost of a couple of hash evaluations each. The ALSZ optimizations [9] cut communication roughly in half again, making OT extension the workhorse of modern two-party protocols.

**Secret sharing.** Shamir's polynomial scheme splits a secret into *n* shares such that any *t + 1* reconstruct it while any *t* reveal nothing. For dishonest-majority MPC, the field instead uses *additive* sharing over a large prime field 𝔽ₚ: each party *Pᵢ* holds *xᵢ* with *x = Σᵢ xᵢ*. Addition gates are then free (shares add locally), while multiplication requires interaction — the bottleneck Beaver's triples eliminate [4].

**Garbled circuits.** Yao's construction [1] encrypts the truth table of every gate so that an evaluator holding exactly one wire label per input wire can decrypt exactly one row per gate, learning nothing else. Four decades of optimization followed: *point-and-permute*, *free XOR* (XOR gates at zero ciphertext cost [6] via a global offset), *half gates* (two ciphertexts per AND gate, provably optimal in their model [6]), and the Rosulek–Roy *slicing-and-dicing* scheme that beat the half-gate lower bound with *1.5κ + 5* bits per AND gate.

| Garbling technique | Ciphertexts per AND gate | XOR cost | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classical Yao | 4 | 4 | [1] |
| GRR row reduction | 3 | 3 | Pinkas et al. |
| Free XOR | 3 | **0** | Kolesnikov–Schneider |
| Half gates | **2** | **0** | [6] |
| Slicing and dicing | *1.5κ + 5* bits | **0** | Rosulek–Roy 2021 |

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *synthetic* study: rather than proposing a new protocol, it reconstructs the semi-honest-to-malicious trajectory inside one consistent formalism, proves the load-bearing lemmas, and compares the protocol families on common asymptotic and concrete metrics. Our method has three components:

1. **Formal reconstruction.** Each protocol (Yao, GMW, Beaver/SPDZ) is presented with explicit sharing semantics, a precise statement of the security theorem, and a proof sketch identifying exactly where semi-honest security breaks and what the malicious compiler must add.
2. **Complexity accounting.** We tabulate communication, round complexity, and cryptographic operations per multiplication/AND gate, separating *offline* (input-independent, amortizable) from *online* cost — the distinction that makes SPDZ practical [3].
3. **Worked micro-protocols.** Executable pseudocode in Python, Haskell, Rust, and TLA⁺ grounds the algebra: Beaver-triple multiplication, the GMW AND-gate share update, the SPDZ MAC check, and a model-checked safety invariant.

Notation: parties are *P₁…Pₙ*; 𝔽ₚ is a prime field with *p > 2^κ*; ⟦x⟧ denotes an authenticated SPDZ sharing; κ is the computational security parameter.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Yao's garbled circuits and the anatomy of a garbled gate

The garbler (say *P₁*) assigns to every wire *w* two random κ-bit labels, *k_w⁰* and *k_w¹*, representing the Boolean values 0 and 1. For a binary gate *g* with input wires *a, b* and output wire *c*, the garbled gate is the four doubly-encrypted rows

> γ_{i,j} = Enc_{k_a^i}(Enc_{k_b^j}(k_c^{g(i,j)})),  *i, j ∈ {0,1}*,

randomly permuted so row position reveals nothing about the underlying truth values. The evaluator *P₂* receives the labels for *P₁*'s input bits directly and obtains its own input labels via 1-out-of-2 OT — learning exactly one label per wire, and *P₁* learning nothing about *P₂*'s choice bits. Decryption proceeds gate by gate; only one row per gate decrypts successfully, so the evaluator learns exactly one label per wire and maps the final labels back to plaintext bits.

The proof's crux is a simulator argument: the evaluator's view is indistinguishable from random, because every unopened ciphertext is semantically secure and OT hides the unchosen label. The *failure mode* under malicious garblers is immediate — a corrupted garbler can garble a *different* circuit and the evaluator cannot tell — which motivates the cut-and-choose compilers of Section 4.5.

```python
# Toy garbling of one AND gate (XOR-based double encryption, point-and-permute)
import os
def enc(key: bytes, msg: bytes) -> bytes:  # one-time-pad stand-in for Enc
    return bytes(x ^ y for x, y in zip(key, msg))

k_a = {0: os.urandom(16), 1: os.urandom(16)}
k_b = {0: os.urandom(16), 1: os.urandom(16)}
k_c = {0: os.urandom(16), 1: os.urandom(16)}
rows = []
for i in (0, 1):
    for j in (0, 1):
        ct = enc(k_a[i], enc(k_b[j], k_c[i & j]))
        rows.append((ct, (i ^ 1, j ^ 1)))  # permute: store with select bits
# Evaluator holding k_a[x], k_b[y] trial-decrypts; exactly one row verifies.
```

### 4.2 The GMW Boolean-circuit compiler

Where Yao centralizes garbling in one party, the Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson protocol [2] distributes evaluation across all *n* parties. Every wire value *v* is XOR-shared: each *Pᵢ* holds *vᵢ* with *v = ⊕ᵢ vᵢ*. The invariant is maintained gate by gate:

- **XOR gates are free.** Each party locally sets *cᵢ = aᵢ ⊕ bᵢ*; correctness follows from the linearity of XOR.
- **AND gates need OT.** Parties must convert shares *(aᵢ, bᵢ)* into fresh shares *cᵢ* with *⊕ᵢ cᵢ = (⊕ᵢ aᵢ)(⊕ᵢ bᵢ)*. Expanding the product, each cross-term *aᵢbⱼ* (for *i ≠ j*) is computed by a small two-party subprotocol: *Pᵢ* picks a random mask *r*, and *Pⱼ* uses 1-out-of-2 OT with choice bit *bⱼ* to obtain *r ⊕ aᵢbⱼ*. Summing masks and OT outputs yields the output shares.

```haskell
-- GMW AND-gate: share update for party i (pseudocode over GF(2))
-- a_i, b_i: my input shares; otResult j: OT output obtained from party j
gmwAnd :: Bit -> Bit -> [(Bit, Bit)] -> Bit
gmwAnd a_i b_i crossTerms =
  let local  = a_i .&. b_i          -- my own product term
      masked = foldr xorBits 0 crossTerms  -- OT-derived cross terms + masks
  in local `xorBits` masked
  -- invariant: XOR over all parties of results = (XOR a_i)(XOR b_i)
```

GMW needs *O(depth)* rounds and *O(n²)* OTs per AND gate — expensive, but it established the compiler paradigm: *any* semi-honest protocol can be lifted to malicious security by forcing each party to commit to its randomness and prove (in zero knowledge) that every message is consistent with the protocol and its committed inputs [2].

### 4.3 Beaver triples and the arithmetic black box

Beaver's 1991 insight [4] is disarmingly simple. Suppose the parties hold additive shares of a random triple *(a, b, c)* with *c = ab*, generated *before* inputs are known. To multiply secret-shared *x* and *y*:

1. Each party locally computes shares of *ε = x − a* and *δ = y − b* and broadcasts them, opening the masked values *ε, δ*.
2. Since *a, b* are uniform and independent of the inputs, *ε* and *δ* leak nothing about *x, y*.
3. Each party computes its share of *z = xy* via the identity

> *xy = (a + ε)(b + δ) = ab + εb + δa + εδ = c + εb + δa + εδ.*

Every term is either a share of a known-shared value scaled by a public constant, or the public constant *εδ* (added by one designated party). **Multiplication costs one triple plus two openings** — no cryptography in the online phase at all. The triple itself must be generated securely — the *offline phase* — via somewhat-homomorphic encryption (SPDZ [3]), OT-based generation (MASCOT [8]), or pseudorandom correlation generators.

```rust
// Beaver online multiplication, additive shares over a prime field (2 parties)
// Beaver online multiplication, additive shares over a prime field (2 parties)
const P: u64 = 1_000_000_007;
fn mul_share(x: u64, y: u64, a: u64, b: u64, c: u64, eps: u64, del: u64, leader: bool) -> u64 {
    // z_i = c_i + eps * b_i + del * a_i (+ eps*del if leader)
    let mut z = (c + eps * b % P + del * a % P) % P;
    if leader { z = (z + eps * del % P) % P; }
    z // z_0 + z_1 = x * y (mod P); x,y never opened
}
```

The triple itself must be generated securely — the *offline phase*. Beaver's original construction used generic MPC; the modern story is *how cheaply* triples can be minted: somewhat-homomorphic encryption (SPDZ [3]), OT-based generation (MASCOT [8]), or pseudorandom correlation generators that compress *n* triples into short seeds.

### 4.4 SPDZ: authenticated sharing and the offline/online split

SPDZ [3] answers the question GMW left open: *malicious security with a dishonest majority and practical efficiency*. Its core is the **authenticated share**. A global MAC key *α ∈ 𝔽ₚ* is itself secret-shared (*α = Σᵢ αᵢ*), and a value *x* is shared as

> ⟦x⟧ = ( (x₁,…,xₙ), (γ₁,…,γₙ) ),  where *x = Σᵢ xᵢ* and each party holds *γᵢ*, a share of *γ(x) = α·x*.

Addition is local on both components; multiplication uses a Beaver triple *whose components are themselves authenticated* (⟦a⟧, ⟦b⟧, ⟦c⟧ with *c = ab*), so the online phase is information-theoretic — no computational assumptions are used after preprocessing.

Cheating is caught by the **MAC check**. Before any output is revealed, the parties commit to shares of a random linear combination of all opened values' MAC relations, then open. If any share was tampered with, the combination is nonzero except with probability *1/p*, and the protocol **aborts**. Batching makes the check's amortized cost negligible.

The offline phase mints authenticated triples using *somewhat homomorphic encryption* (the BGV scheme): parties encrypt their shares, homomorphically form *Enc(ab)*, prove plaintext knowledge in zero knowledge, and threshold-decrypt — with MACs attached under the shared key *α*. MASCOT [8] later replaced SHE with pure OT-based triple generation, trading public-key homomorphisms for fast symmetric-key operations.

```tla
---- MODULE MacCheck ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANT Parties
VARIABLE opened, macShare, committed
Check ==
  /\ \A p \in Parties: committed[p] = TRUE   \* commit before any opening
  /\ LET sigma == Sum({macShare[p] : p \in Parties}) IN
     IF sigma = 0 THEN "accept" ELSE "abort"
\* Safety: no party learns whether others' shares were valid before committing.
====
```

| Protocol | Adversary | Corruptions | Online comm. per mult. gate | Preprocessing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yao GC [1] | semi-honest | 1 of 2 | *O(κ)* bits (garbled table) | none |
| GMW [2] | semi-honest | *n − 1* | *O(n²κ)* (OTs) | none |
| BGW | malicious | *t < n/3* | *O(n²)* field elems | none |
| SPDZ [3] | malicious | *n − 1* (abort) | **2 field elems** + amortized MAC check | SHE/OT triples |

### 4.5 Malicious-security compilers

Three compiler families lift semi-honest protocols to active security:

1. **The GMW compiler** [2]: commit to inputs and randomness, then attach zero-knowledge proofs that each message follows the protocol. Generic and conceptually clean, but proof overhead historically made it impractical.
2. **Cut-and-choose for garbled circuits.** The garbler produces *s* garbled circuits; the evaluator opens a random subset for checking and evaluates the rest, taking the majority output. Lindell–Pinkas [7] gave the first full malicious 2PC from this template; with *s = 40* the cheating probability is *2⁻⁴⁰*. Cheaper variants (*authenticated garbling*) fold MACs into wire labels so a single circuit suffices.
3. **Authenticated preprocessing (SPDZ lineage).** Instead of compiling the online phase, authenticate *everything the online phase touches*: shares carry MACs, triples are generated by an actively secure offline protocol (SHE with ZK proofs [3], or OT-based MASCOT [8]), and a final batched MAC check converts any deviation into a detectable abort. This is the dominant paradigm in deployed malicious MPC (MP-SPDZ, SCALE-MAMBA), because the online phase stays information-theoretic and the heavy cryptography is reusable across computations.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

We now state and prove the lemma on which the entire SPDZ online phase rests.

> **Theorem:** (Beaver triple multiplication) *Let x, y be additively shared over 𝔽ₚ and let (a, b, c) be shares of uniform random a, b with c = ab. After publicly opening ε = x − a and δ = y − b, each party can locally compute a share zᵢ such that Σᵢ zᵢ = xy, and the opened values (ε, δ) are statistically independent of (x, y).*

> *Proof sketch.* Correctness: *xy = (a+ε)(b+δ) = c + εb + δa + εδ*; each party sets *zᵢ = cᵢ + εbᵢ + δaᵢ* (with *P₁* adding *εδ*), and summation gives *xy* by linearity of sharing. Privacy: *(ε, δ)* is a one-time-pad encryption of *(x, y)* under keys *(a, b)* uniform in 𝔽ₚ², hence reveals nothing. ∎

The SPDZ MAC check adds active security: any adversary that modifies a share changes *σ = Σ rⱼ(α·mⱼ − γⱼ)* to a nonzero field element except with probability *1/p* over the random challenge *r*, because *α* is uniform and unknown to the adversary. The commit-before-open ordering is essential — without it, a rushing adversary could adjust its commitment after seeing honest shares.

```python
# End-to-end Beaver multiplication over a prime field, with privacy check
P = 1_000_000_007
import random
def share(v): s = random.randrange(P); return s, (v - s) % P
x, y = 123456, 654321
x0, x1 = share(x); y0, y1 = share(y)
a0, a1 = share(a := random.randrange(P)); b0, b1 = share(b := random.randrange(P))
c0, c1 = share((a * b) % P)
eps = (x0 + x1 - a0 - a1) % P; delta = (y0 + y1 - b0 - b1) % P  # opened
z0 = (c0 + eps * b0 + delta * a0 + eps * delta) % P
z1 = (c1 + eps * b1 + delta * a1) % P
assert (z0 + z1) % P == (x * y) % P, "Beaver identity failed"
print("xy =", (z0 + z1) % P, "| inputs never reconstructed")
```

On the concrete side, the performance story is one of steady improvement: OT extension [5][9] cut public-key operations per gate to a small constant; half gates [6] halved garbled-circuit bandwidth; and the SPDZ online phase is so cheap that the *network*, not cryptography, is the bottleneck. The durable lesson is architectural, not numerical: **push cryptography into reusable preprocessing, keep the online phase information-theoretic.**

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **No fairness under dishonest majority.** Cleve's impossibility result is absolute: with *n − 1* corruptions, an adversary can always learn the output while denying it to honest parties. Security-with-abort is the ceiling, and *identifiable* abort (naming the cheater) costs extra machinery.
2. **Preprocessing is the bottleneck.** SPDZ's online phase is essentially free, but minting millions of authenticated triples via SHE [3] or OT [8] dominates total cost. Silent preprocessing via PCGs promises relief, but actively secure PCG-based triple generation remains delicate.
3. **Circuit dependence.** Garbled circuits must be regenerated per computation; only the *function*, not the data, can be reused. Reusable garbled circuits exist but rely on heavyweight assumptions.
4. **Assumption fragility.** Free-XOR garbling needs circular correlation-robust hash functions; OT extension needs random oracles. Post-quantum MPC replaces these with lattice assumptions at significant concrete cost.
5. **Scale.** Protocols with *O(n²)* per-gate communication degrade sharply past dozens of parties; sublinear-communication MPC for hundreds of parties is largely open.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The trajectory from Yao's 1986 garbled circuits [1] through the GMW compiler [2] to Beaver triples [4] and SPDZ [3] is the story of MPC growing up: from *feasibility* (anything computable can be computed privately), to *generality* (semi-honest compilers for any protocol), to *practicality under attack* (malicious security with a dishonest majority at information-theoretic online cost). The offline/online split — expensive, reusable, input-independent preprocessing feeding a lightweight, authenticated online phase — has become the field's dominant design pattern, and OT extension [5][9] plus garbling optimizations [6] supply the symmetric-key engine that makes it run. The remaining frontiers are fundamental rather than incremental: fairness is impossible, preprocessing must get quieter, and the assumption base must survive the quantum transition.
