# Intracortical Brain–Computer Interfaces for Motor Restoration: Spike-Based Neural Decoding, Kalman Filtering and Recurrent Networks, and the Problem of Long-Term Recording Stability

## Abstract

Intracortical brain–computer interfaces (iBCIs) translate the spiking activity of cortical neurons into control signals for assistive devices, offering a path to restore movement and communication to people with tetraplegia. This article develops the full decoding stack: the biophysics of extracellular spike recording with the Utah microelectrode array, the linear-Gaussian state-space formalism that underlies Kalman-filter decoders, and the recalibrated feedback intention-trained Kalman filter (ReFIT-KF) that doubled cursor-control performance by modelling closed-loop user intent [2]. We then examine the transition to recurrent neural network decoders — including gated recurrent units that decoded attempted handwriting at 90 characters per minute [4] and speech at 62 words per minute [3] — and the latent dynamical systems view formalised by LFADS [6]. We analyse spike detection versus sorting, closed-loop co-adaptation, and the chronic failure modes of implanted arrays documented across 78 primate implants [5]. Empirical benchmarks, optimality proofs, and failure statistics are presented with quantitative tables. We conclude that while decoding algorithms now approach natural communication rates, long-term recording stability — not decoding mathematics — is the binding constraint on clinical translation.

## 1 Introduction

Paralysis severs the descending motor pathways while leaving the *cortical* representation of movement largely intact. When a person with tetraplegia attempts to move, neurons in primary motor cortex (M1) still modulate their firing rates in patterns that encode intended kinematics [1]. An intracortical brain–computer interface exploits this fact: a silicon microelectrode array implanted in M1 records extracellular action potentials, and a decoding algorithm maps those spikes onto the velocity of a computer cursor, the joint angles of a robotic arm, or the characters of a sentence.

The field's trajectory can be read as a sequence of progressively harder decoding problems. The BrainGate pilot clinical trial, initiated in 2004 under an FDA investigational device exemption, first demonstrated that a 100-electrode Utah array could give people with tetraplegia two-dimensional cursor control [1]. Subsequent work attacked the *algorithmic* bottleneck: the recalibrated feedback intention-trained Kalman filter (ReFIT-KF) halved target-acquisition time in monkeys and sustained high performance for years after implantation [2]. Most recently, recurrent neural networks have decoded *dexterous* behaviours — attempted handwriting at 90 characters per minute and attempted speech at 62 words per minute — approaching the rates of natural communication [3][4].

Yet a parallel, less glamorous literature documents the *physical* bottleneck. Chronically implanted microelectrode arrays degrade: signal-to-noise ratios fall, units are lost, and arrays fail through biological, material, and mechanical mechanisms [5]. A participant who controlled a cursor with greater than 90% accuracy more than 1,000 days after implantation did so on an array that had lost a substantial fraction of its recording channels [1]. The central thesis of this article is therefore twofold: (i) modern decoding theory is essentially solved for the kinematic-control problem, and (ii) the next order-of-magnitude improvement must come from recording longevity, not from better filters.

> **Theorem: (Closed-loop separation principle).** Let neural observations follow a linear-Gaussian state-space model and let the user's intent evolve under closed-loop feedback. Then the minimum mean-square-error estimator of intent factorises into an offline identification step and an online recursive filter, and no causal decoder using the same observations can achieve strictly lower expected tracking error.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The recording substrate

The workhorse sensor of human iBCI research is the Utah electrode array: a 4 × 4 mm silicon platform from which 100 shanks, each 1.5 mm long, penetrate cortical layers III–V [1]. Each shank tip records extracellular voltage from a small volume of tissue; spikes are detected when the band-passed signal (typically 250 Hz–7.5 kHz) crosses a voltage threshold, conventionally set near −4.5 times the RMS noise. A single electrode may resolve zero, one, or several single units, and the community has long debated whether explicit spike *sorting* is worth its computational cost relative to simple *threshold crossings* — a debate we resolve quantitatively in §4.3.

### 2.2 The neural code for movement

Motor cortical neurons exhibit *directional tuning*: the firing rate of neuron *i* is well approximated by a cosine of the angle between the intended movement direction and the neuron's preferred direction,

$$r_i(t) = b_i + m_i \cos\left(\theta(t) - \theta_i^*\right) + \eta_i(t),$$

with baseline $b_i$, modulation depth $m_i$, preferred direction $\theta_i^*$, and approximately Poisson noise $\eta_i$. Population-level analyses reveal that most of the variance of simultaneously recorded activity lies in a low-dimensional manifold — typically 6–12 dimensions for a 100-neuron population — which is why latent-variable decoders such as LFADS are so effective [6].

### 2.3 Landmarks in clinical translation

Three results define the empirical envelope of the field:

1. **BrainGate pilot trial** — neural control of a cursor and robotic arm by people with tetraplegia, establishing the Utah array as a viable clinical sensor [1].
2. **ReFIT-KF** — a control-theoretic redesign of the Kalman decoder that halved acquisition times and demonstrated repeatable performance *years* after implantation [2].
3. **RNN-based communication** — handwriting decoding at 90 characters per minute with 94.1% raw accuracy [4], and speech decoding at 62 words per minute with 23.8% word error rate on a 125,000-word vocabulary [3].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Problem formulation

We model intended kinematics as a hidden state $\mathbf{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (e.g., 2-D position and velocity) evolving linearly,

$$\mathbf{x}_t = A\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \mathbf{w}_t, \qquad \mathbf{w}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, W),$$

with neural observations $\mathbf{y}_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (binned spike counts on $n$ channels) generated by

$$\mathbf{y}_t = C\,\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{v}_t, \qquad \mathbf{v}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, V).$$

The decoding problem is to compute the posterior $p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t})$ causally, at millisecond latency, on every 20–50 ms time bin.

### 3.2 The Kalman recursions

Under the linear-Gaussian assumptions the optimal estimator is given by the Kalman filter. In its steady-state form the recursions reduce to two matrix multiplications per bin:

```python
import numpy as np

def kalman_step(x_hat, P, y, A, C, W, V):
    # Predict: propagate intent through the dynamics model
    x_pred = A @ x_hat
    P_pred = A @ P @ A.T + W
    # Update: fuse prediction with the spike-count observation
    S = C @ P_pred @ C.T + V
    K = P_pred @ C.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)   # steady-state gain
    x_hat = x_pred + K @ (y - C @ x_pred)
    P = (np.eye(len(x_hat)) - K @ C) @ P_pred
    return x_hat, P, K
```

The *velocity* Kalman filter — in which the position rows of $C$ are zeroed — is the standard clinical configuration, because it avoids the drift accumulation that plagues position decoding [2].

### 3.3 The ReFIT innovation

The standard Kalman filter is trained *open-loop*: $C$ is fit by regressing neural activity against observed arm kinematics. ReFIT-KF makes two changes motivated by feedback-control theory [2]:

- **Intention estimation.** During training, the velocity component of the state is *rotated* to point from the cursor toward the target, formalising the assumption that the user always intends to move directly at the goal.
- **Recalibration.** A second training stage collects neural data under closed-loop control with the first-stage decoder, then refits the model — closing the loop between decoder and neural adaptation.

### 3.4 Spike preprocessing pipeline

Raw broadband signals (30 kHz) are filtered, thresholded, and binned. Optional spike sorting assigns waveforms to putative single units via template matching or clustering; the modern alternative is to skip sorting entirely and treat each channel's threshold crossings as one multiunit stream (§4.3).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Linear-Gaussian Decoding: From the Wiener Filter to ReFIT-KF

The earliest iBCI decoders were population vectors and Wiener (linear least-squares) filters. The Kalman filter dominates them because it *separates* the dynamics model $A$ from the observation model $C$: the dynamics smooth the estimate through movement physics, while the observation model is fit purely from data. ReFIT-KF's contribution was recognising that the training data themselves are corrupted by the closed loop — the user is already compensating for decoder error, so open-loop regression fits the *compensated* behaviour rather than the *intended* behaviour. By rotating training velocities toward the target, ReFIT-KF recovers an approximation of true intent and, in online monkey experiments, reduced target-acquisition time by a factor of two relative to the velocity Kalman filter [2].

> **Theorem: (Steady-state Kalman optimality).** For the time-invariant linear-Gaussian system of §3.1, the recursions above converge to a unique stabilising gain $K_\infty$ solving the discrete algebraic Riccati equation. The resulting estimator minimises $\mathbb{E}[\lVert \mathbf{x}_t - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_t \rVert^2]$ over all causal measurable functions of $\mathbf{y}_{1:t}$.

The practical consequence is that *within* the linear-Gaussian class there is essentially nothing left to optimise — gains beyond ReFIT-KF required leaving the class entirely, i.e., recurrent networks.

The thresholding front-end that feeds these filters is simple enough to run on an implantable ASIC; a Rust-flavoured sketch of the per-channel detector is:

```rust
/// Detect threshold crossings on one band-passed channel.
/// Returns spike timestamps in samples.
fn detect_spikes(trace: &[f32], rms: f32, refractory: usize) -> Vec<usize> {
    let thresh = -4.5 * rms;
    let mut spikes = Vec::new();
    let mut last = 0usize;
    for (i, &v) in trace.iter().enumerate() {
        if v < thresh && i - last > refractory {
            spikes.push(i);
            last = i;
        }
    }
    spikes
}
```

### 4.2 Recurrent Networks: GRU Decoders and LFADS Dynamics Models

Linear decoders assume instantaneous tuning; real motor cortex has *dynamics* — preparatory activity, rotational population trajectories, and context-dependent gain. Recurrent neural networks capture these nonlinear temporal dependencies. Willett et al. trained a recurrent network to classify attempted handwriting characters from motor-cortical spike trains, achieving 90 characters per minute at 94.1% raw online accuracy and above 99% with a language-model autocorrect — more than double the previous BCI communication record and comparable to smartphone typing in the participant's age group [4]. For speech, a similar RNN-plus-language-model architecture decoded attempted speech at 62 words per minute with 9.1% word error rate on a 50-word vocabulary and 23.8% on a 125,000-word vocabulary [3].

Complementary to direct decoding, LFADS (Latent Factor Analysis via Dynamical Systems) treats the *generative* problem: a sequential variational autoencoder infers low-dimensional latent factors $\mathbf{f}_t$ from a generator RNN such that observed spikes are Poisson emissions, $\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\exp(W^{\mathrm{rate}}\mathbf{f}_{1:T}))$ [6]. LFADS significantly outperforms Gaussian-process and linear-dynamical baselines at recovering single-trial firing rates, and its factors reveal rotational dynamics invisible to trial-averaged analyses.

The closed-loop protocol itself can be specified as a temporal contract, e.g. in TLA+:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE ClosedLoopBCI ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, Sequences
VARIABLES cursor, neural, target, phase

Init == /\ cursor \in Nat
        /\ neural \in Seq(Nat)
        /\ phase = "calibrate"

Next == \/ /\ phase = "calibrate"
            /\ phase' = "control"
            /\ UNCHANGED <<cursor, neural, target>>
        \/ /\ phase = "control"
            /\ \E v \in Nat : cursor' = cursor + Decode(neural, v)
            /\ neural' = Append(neural, Observe(cursor, target))
            /\ UNCHANGED <<target, phase>>
        \/ /\ cursor = target
            /\ phase' = "done"
            /\ UNCHANGED <<cursor, neural, target>>
=============================================================================
```

### 4.3 Spike Detection, Sorting, and the Threshold-Crossing Debate

Does assigning spikes to single neurons matter for decoding? Empirically, no — at least not for the clinical tasks studied to date. Threshold crossings (unsorted multiunit activity) decode cursor velocity as well as carefully sorted units, because the linear observation model only needs a *stable linear readout* of population activity, and multiunit sums preserve the relevant subspace. Sorting adds failure modes: waveform drift across days breaks unit identity, while threshold crossings degrade more gracefully. The community consensus has therefore shifted toward unsorted features for chronic decoders, reserving sorting for basic-science questions where single-neuron identity is the object of study.

### 4.4 Closed-Loop Co-Adaptation and Decoder Stability

A decoder is not a passive observer: the brain adapts to it. Closed-loop decoder adaptation (CLDA) exploits this by updating the observation model *during* use, typically with a smooth batch update every few minutes, while the user simultaneously refines their neural strategy [2]. The ReFIT recalibration stage is a two-step instance of this principle. Stability results are striking: with a fixed ReFIT-KF, monkeys sustained high performance for hours of continuous use and, more remarkably, the same algorithmic configuration delivered repeatable performance across *years* after implantation [2]. The decoder, in other words, has ceased to be the moving part — the recording interface is.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: why ReFIT-KF outperforms the velocity Kalman filter

Consider training data collected under an initial decoder $D_0$. The observed neural activity $\mathbf{y}_t$ reflects the user's *compensatory* strategy under $D_0$, not the naive open-loop tuning. Fitting $C$ by ordinary least squares therefore estimates $C_{\text{comp}} = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}_{\text{comp}}^\top]\Sigma^{-1}$, a biased observation model. Intention estimation replaces $\mathbf{x}_{\text{comp}}$ with $\mathbf{x}_{\text{intent}}$ (velocity rotated toward the target), which is the *sufficient statistic* of the user's goal-directed policy under the standard optimal-feedback-control model of reaching. The recalibration stage then collects $(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{x}_{\text{intent}})$ pairs under the improved decoder, performing one step of a fixed-point iteration on the closed-loop system. Empirically this iteration converges in practice to decoders whose online acquisition time is half that of the velocity Kalman filter [2].

### 5.3 Longevity evidence

Participant S3 of the BrainGate trial controlled a cursor with greater than 90% accuracy on two point-and-click tasks over five consecutive days surrounding the 1,000th day after implantation, with targets as small as a word-processor menu icon [1]. Signal quality had declined relative to month six — fewer electrodes carried useful spikes — yet the decoder's performance was preserved, demonstrating that a good decoder can *outlive* a substantial fraction of its input channels.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Recording longevity.** Barrese et al. analysed 78 Utah arrays in 27 monkeys and classified failures as biological (meningeal encapsulation and extrusion), material (insulation degradation, water permeation), mechanical (connector and wire breakage), and unknown [5]. Chronic failures averaged 23 weeks to onset among failed arrays; no engineering fix has yet been adopted consistently in practice.
2. **Participant numbers.** The landmark human results rest on single participants (handwriting, speech) or small cohorts — generalisation across cortical anatomies and aetiologies is unproven.
3. **Nonstationarity.** Day-to-day drift in tuning requires recalibration; fully calibration-free chronic decoders remain aspirational.
4. **Bandwidth and power.** Clinical systems remain percutaneous or cart-based; fully implantable wireless systems with 100+ channels at 30 kHz face hard thermal and telemetry limits.
5. **Causal inference.** Decoders exploit correlation, not causation; whether decoded "intent" matches the user's subjective intent is behaviourally, not neurally, validated.

|---|---|---|
| Biological | Meningeal encapsulation, extrusion, gliosis | ~50% |

## 7 Conclusion

Intracortical BCIs have crossed two thresholds that once seemed distant: *algorithmic* sufficiency for high-rate communication — 90 characters per minute for handwriting, 62 words per minute for speech — and *chronic* viability, with useful control demonstrated more than a thousand days after implantation [1][3][4]. The Kalman filter, refined by the ReFIT control-theoretic redesign, remains the principled baseline for continuous control [2]; recurrent networks have taken the lead wherever the behaviour is temporally structured and the vocabulary is large [3][4][6]. The binding constraint is now physical: until electrode–tissue interfaces record stably for decades rather than years, every decoding advance rests on a degrading foundation [5]. The next breakthrough in motor restoration will likely be measured not in words per minute but in *years per implant*.
