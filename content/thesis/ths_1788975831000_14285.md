## 1. Introduction

The parameter spaces of statistical models are usually treated as flat Euclidean domains: optimizers take steps from ordinary gradients, confidence regions are drawn from asymptotic normality, and reparametrization is a notational convenience. This flat view is geometrically wrong. A parameter vector is merely a coordinate chart for a more natural object — the *statistical manifold*, the collection of probability distributions the model can express. Two points are close not when their parameter vectors are close, but when the corresponding distributions are statistically hard to distinguish. The object measuring this closeness is the Fisher information matrix, whose geometric role is that of a Riemannian metric — the Fisher–Rao metric. Under it, ordinary gradient descent follows the steepest direction with respect to an arbitrary, parametrization-dependent ruler, whereas the *natural gradient* follows the steepest direction measured by the statistical ruler itself.

The program of *information geometry*, founded by C. R. Rao and N. Čencov [1] and developed decisively by Shun-ichi Amari and Hiroshi Nagaoka [2], replaces the Euclidean picture with a dualistic structure: a Riemannian metric $g$ plus a one-parameter family of affine connections $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$, dual in pairs, generalizing the Levi–Civita connection ($\alpha = 0$). The exponential ($\alpha = 1$) and mixture ($\alpha = -1$) connections are flat precisely on exponential and mixture families — the workhorse model classes of statistics. When a connection and its dual are both flat, the manifold is *dually flat*, with dual affine coordinates $\theta$ (natural) and $\eta$ (expectation) linked by Legendre duality between the log-partition potential $\psi(\theta)$ and its conjugate $\varphi(\eta)$ (negative entropy). Here the Kullback–Leibler divergence becomes a *canonical divergence* obeying a generalized Pythagorean theorem, and maximum likelihood, the EM algorithm, and iterative projections acquire clean geometric interpretations.

The geometric viewpoint delivers algorithms with properties unavailable to Euclidean methods. The natural gradient $\tilde{\nabla} L = G^{-1} \nabla L$, where $G$ is the Fisher information matrix, is *covariant*: the update direction transforms as a proper contravariant vector, so arbitrary smooth reparametrizations leave the trajectory invariant [3]. Kakade's natural policy gradient [4] carries this invariance into reinforcement learning and connects gradient steps to policy iteration. Martens and Grosse's Kronecker-factored approximate curvature (K-FAC) [5] makes the Fisher-preconditioned update tractable at scale via a block-Kronecker structure in the Fisher blocks of multilayer networks, while trust-region methods such as TRPO replace explicit Fisher inversion with a KL-constrained surrogate [6].

Section 2 reviews the background: statistical manifolds, the Fisher metric, Čencov's invariance theorem, Eguchi's divergence theory, and the Amari–Čencov α-connections. Section 3 derives the natural gradient from the local quadratic model of KL divergence, develops the dually flat geometry of exponential families (dual flatness and Pythagorean theorems), and interprets EM as alternating projection. Section 4 covers computational realization — the Fisher of neural networks, K-FAC and related approximations, natural policy gradients and trust regions, and natural-gradient variational inference. Section 5 summarizes empirical results and guarantees; Section 6 discusses limitations and open problems; Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Statistical manifolds

A *statistical model* is a parametrized family of probability densities $S = \{ p(x; \xi) : \xi \in \Xi \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \}$, with the map $\xi \mapsto p(\cdot; \xi)$ injective and smooth. Under regularity, $S$ is an $n$-dimensional differentiable manifold with $\xi$ as coordinates. The tangent space $T_{p(\xi)}S$ is spanned by the *score functions* $s_i(x; \xi) = \partial \log p(x; \xi)/\partial \xi^i$, which satisfy $\mathbb{E}_\xi[s_i] = 0$; a tangent vector $X = \sum_i a^i \partial_i$ is represented by the zero-mean random variable $\sum_i a^i s_i$.

### 2.2 The Fisher–Rao metric

The *Fisher information matrix* $g_{ij}(\xi) = \mathbb{E}_\xi[s_i s_j] = -\mathbb{E}_\xi[\partial_i \partial_j \log p]$ defines, when positive definite, an inner product $g(X,Y) = \sum_{i,j} a^i b^j g_{ij}(\xi)$ on each tangent space, making $S$ a Riemannian manifold [7]. This is the **Fisher–Rao metric**.

> **Theorem (Fisher–Rao from KL divergence).** Let $D(p \| q) = \mathbb{E}_p[\log(p/q)]$ be the Kullback–Leibler divergence. Then, under regularity,
>
> $$D(p(\xi) \| p(\xi + d\xi)) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j} g_{ij}(\xi) \, d\xi^i d\xi^j + o(\|d\xi\|^2).$$
>
> Thus the Fisher metric is the Riemannian metric induced by the infinitesimal KL divergence, in the sense of Eguchi's theory of divergence functions [7].

The crux: the Hessian of KL divergence at the diagonal is a genuine metric tensor, so every coordinate-independent geometric fact about statistical models should be expressible in Fisher–Rao terms.

### 2.3 Čencov's invariance theorem

Why the Fisher metric rather than any other? Čencov's theorem gives a definitive answer [1]. A *statistic* $\kappa: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ induces a coarse-grained model $S_\kappa$ via push-forward; if $\kappa$ is *sufficient*, no information about $\xi$ is lost. A metric is *invariant* if it is preserved under sufficient statistics.

> **Theorem (Čencov, 1982).** Up to a constant positive factor, the Fisher–Rao metric is the *unique* Riemannian metric on statistical models over finite sample spaces that is invariant under sufficient statistics [1].

In words: any geometrically meaningful notion of distance on a statistical manifold, if it must respect the data-processing structure of statistics, is forced to be the Fisher metric.

### 2.4 The Amari–Čencov α-connections

A Riemannian metric determines one canonical connection — the Levi–Civita connection — but information geometry exploits a richer *dualistic structure*: the one-parameter family of $\alpha$-connections $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$, defined via Eguchi's divergence-based formula [7] from the $\alpha$-divergences. The essential property is **duality**:

$$X g(Y, Z) = g(\nabla^{(\alpha)}_X Y, Z) + g(Y, \nabla^{(-\alpha)}_X Z),$$

so $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$ and $\nabla^{(-\alpha)}$ are dual with respect to $g$. The $0$-connection is the Levi–Civita connection; the $1$-connection is the **exponential connection** $\nabla^{(e)}$ and the $-1$-connection the **mixture connection** $\nabla^{(m)}$. The difference between the $\alpha$-connections is captured by the symmetric Amari–Čencov 3-tensor $T_{ijk} = \mathbb{E}_\xi[s_i s_j s_k]$. together forming a *dualistic structure* in the sense of Amari–Nagaoka [2].

> **Theorem (Dual flatness, Amari 1985).** If a statistical manifold is $\alpha$-flat — i.e., there exist coordinates in which all $\alpha$-Christoffel symbols vanish — then it is also $(-\alpha)$-flat [2].

Exponential families are $1$-flat (e-flat) and therefore $(-1)$-flat (m-flat), with dual affine coordinate systems obtained for free.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Exponential families and Legendre duality

An *exponential family* in canonical form is $p(x; \theta) = \exp(\langle t(x), \theta \rangle - \psi(\theta) + k(x))$, with natural parameters $\theta$, sufficient statistic $t(x)$, and strictly convex log-partition function $\psi$. The Fisher metric is the Hessian of the potential: $g_{ij}(\theta) = \partial_i \partial_j \psi(\theta)$. The *expectation parameters* $\eta = \nabla \psi(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[t(X)]$ form a diffeomorphism whose inverse is the gradient of the *conjugate potential* $\varphi(\eta) = \sup_\theta\{\langle\theta,\eta\rangle - \psi(\theta)\}$, so $\theta = \nabla\varphi(\eta)$ and $G^{-1}(\theta) = \nabla^2\varphi(\eta)$. This is the **dually flat** structure: $\theta$ is affine for $\nabla^{(e)}$, $\eta$ is affine for $\nabla^{(m)}$, and the metric is the Hessian of either potential [2].

### 3.2 Canonical divergence and the generalized Pythagorean theorem

For a dually flat manifold, the KL divergence coincides with the *canonical divergence* (Bregman divergence of $\psi$):

$$D(p \| q) = D_\psi(\theta_p, \theta_q) = \psi(\theta_p) - \psi(\theta_q) - \langle \nabla\psi(\theta_q), \, \theta_p - \theta_q \rangle.$$

> **Theorem (Generalized Pythagorean theorem).** Let $p, q, r$ be points of a dually flat manifold such that the $m$-geodesic from $p$ to $q$ is orthogonal (in the Fisher metric) at $q$ to the $e$-geodesic from $q$ to $r$. Then
>
> $$D(p \| r) = D(p \| q) + D(q \| r).$$

This is the workhorse of the geometry: alternating minimizations decompose divergences additively exactly when successive projections are orthogonal. The **EM algorithm** is the canonical example [2]: the E-step is an $m$-projection of the empirical distribution onto the model submanifold and the M-step an $e$-projection onto the data manifold, each step strictly decreasing the divergence with the Pythagorean decomposition quantifying progress.

### 3.3 Derivation of the natural gradient

Consider minimizing a smooth objective $L(\xi)$ over the statistical manifold $S$. Ordinary gradient descent picks the displacement $d\xi$ of fixed Euclidean length $\|d\xi\|^2 = \epsilon^2$ minimizing the first-order model $L(\xi) + \nabla L \cdot d\xi$, giving $d\xi^* = -\eta\nabla L$. But the Euclidean constraint is geometrically meaningless — it depends on the arbitrary coordinates $\xi$. The natural constraint bounds the *distributional* change: $D(p(\xi) \| p(\xi+d\xi)) = c^2/2$, i.e. $d\xi^\top G(\xi)\,d\xi = c^2$ to second order by the Fisher–Rao expansion. Minimizing $\nabla L \cdot d\xi$ subject to this constraint gives $d\xi^* \propto -G(\xi)^{-1}\nabla L(\xi)$. The **natural gradient** is therefore [3]

$$\tilde{\nabla} L(\xi) \;=\; G(\xi)^{-1} \nabla L(\xi), \qquad \xi_{t+1} = \xi_t - \eta \, G(\xi_t)^{-1} \nabla L(\xi_t).$$

> **Theorem (Parametrization invariance).** Let $\xi = \xi(\zeta)$ be a smooth invertible reparametrization. Then the natural gradient transforms as a contravariant vector, and the update traces the *same curve on the manifold* regardless of parametrization [3].

*Proof sketch.* With Jacobian $J$, $G_\zeta = J^\top G_\xi J$ and $\nabla_\zeta L = J^\top\nabla_\xi L$, so $G_\zeta^{-1}\nabla_\zeta L = J^{-1}G_\xi^{-1}\nabla_\xi L$ — a contravariant vector — while the ordinary gradient picks up $J^\top$. ∎

For exponential families there is a striking simplification. Since $G(\eta) = G(\theta)^{-1}$ and $\nabla_\eta L = G(\theta)^{-1}\nabla_\theta L$, the natural gradient in expectation parameters coincides exactly with the ordinary gradient in natural parameters: $\tilde{\nabla}_\eta L = G(\eta)^{-1}\nabla_\eta L = \nabla_\theta L$. Natural-gradient ascent in $\eta$ *is* ordinary-gradient ascent in $\theta$ — the geometry does the preconditioning automatically [2].

### 3.4 The natural gradient as a Riemannian gradient

The natural gradient is simply the **Riemannian gradient** of $L$ under the Fisher–Rao metric: the unique tangent vector $\mathrm{grad}_g L$ with $g(\mathrm{grad}_g L, X) = dL(X)$ for all $X$. Hence it is intrinsically defined, extends naturally to Riemannian second-order methods, and may use the generalized Gauss–Newton matrix — which coincides with the Fisher for exponential-family outputs with matching loss (softmax cross-entropy, Gaussian MSE) — connecting it to classical second-order optimization [5].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Fisher information of neural networks

For a network defining $p(y \mid x; \theta)$, the *empirical Fisher* averages $\nabla_\theta\log p\,\nabla_\theta\log p^\top$ over data, while the *true Fisher* averages over the model distribution; for exponential-family outputs (softmax, Gaussian) both coincide with the generalized Gauss–Newton matrix [5]. The $P \times P$ Fisher ($P$ = parameter count) forbids direct inversion, but its layer-wise block structure admits structure-exploiting approximations.

Martens and Grosse's **Kronecker-factored approximate curvature (K-FAC)** [5] approximates each layer's Fisher block as a Kronecker product. For a fully connected layer with weight matrix $W$, input activations $a$ and backpropagated pre-activation derivatives $s$:

$$\mathrm{vec}(\nabla_W \ell) = a \otimes s, \quad \Rightarrow \quad G_W = \mathbb{E}[(a\otimes s)(a\otimes s)^\top] \approx \mathbb{E}[aa^\top] \otimes \mathbb{E}[ss^\top] = A \otimes S,$$

under the key approximation that activations and backpropagated derivatives are independent. Inversion factorizes: $(A \otimes S)^{-1} = A^{-1} \otimes S^{-1}$, reducing a $(d_{\text{in}}d_{\text{out}})^2$ inversion to two small ones. Extensions cover convolutions (KFC) [5], recurrent networks, and transformers; damped variants $(A\otimes S + \lambda I)^{-1}$ preserve the factorization via eigendecomposition. K-FAC inherits affine reparametrization invariance of activations and trains networks in an order of magnitude fewer iterations than SGD [5].

### 4.2 Natural policy gradients and trust-region methods in reinforcement learning

Kakade's **natural policy gradient (NPG)** [4] applies the natural gradient to the expected return $J(\theta)$, with the Fisher averaged over the state-visitation distribution: $F(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s,a}[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a|s)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a|s)^\top]$. Kakade showed the natural direction moves the policy toward the *greedy* policy under the compatible value function — one step of policy iteration — rather than merely improving the current policy, explaining drastic speedups on problems like Tetris where vanilla policy gradients plateau [4].

Direct Fisher inversion being infeasible, practical methods approximate the same geometry. **TRPO** maximizes a KL-constrained surrogate, $\max_\theta \mathbb{E}[(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}})\hat{A}]$ s.t. $\bar{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\text{old}} \| \pi_\theta) \le \delta$, whose Lagrangian yields $\Delta\theta \propto F^{-1}g$ — a trust-region implementation of the natural step [6]. **PPO** clips the surrogate instead, avoiding second-order machinery, while conjugate-gradient NPG with Fisher–vector products implements the exact direction implicitly [3]. In the tabular softmax parametrization, NPG is exactly *entropic mirror descent* on the simplex, giving clean global convergence rates [4].

KL trust regions come with approximate monotonic-improvement guarantees (Kakade–Langford / Schulman et al.), which underlie the practical success of TRPO/PPO-style methods [6].

### 4.3 Natural gradient variational inference

In variational inference we fit $q(z;\lambda)$ to an intractable posterior by minimizing $\mathrm{KL}(q \| p(\cdot\mid x))$. When $q$ is an exponential family, $\tilde{\nabla}_\eta \mathcal{L} = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}$ (Section 3.3), so natural-gradient ELBO updates take a simple fixed-point form [8]; for conjugate models this is stochastic variational inference (SVI), where the natural gradient equals the gap between expected sufficient statistics and current parameters. Khan and Lin's **conjugate-computation variational inference (CVI)** [8] generalizes this: Gaussian or exponential-family approximations admit natural-gradient updates computed by conjugate Bayesian updates, unifying SVI, expectation propagation, and Newton-like methods — with no explicit $G^{-1}$ ever formed.

### 4.4 Convergence theory: why curvature helps and where it breaks

The natural gradient preconditions away parametrization-induced ill-conditioning: the effective Hessian is $G^{-1/2}HG^{-1/2}$, and for well-specified exponential-family models $H \approx G$ at the optimum, so the effective condition number tends to $1$ — locally behaving like Newton's method. Caveats in practice:

1. **Singular models.** Deep networks are singular: $G$ is rank-deficient, so the Fisher–Rao "metric" is only a pseudo-metric. Watanabe's singular learning theory shows the geometry is governed by the real log-canonical threshold, not Fisher invertibility; damping $\lambda I$ patches this but distorts the geometry.
2. **Stochastic noise.** Minibatch Fisher estimates are noisy and rank-deficient; inversion amplifies noise in small-eigenvalue directions, which is why damping, eigenvalue clipping, and factor-update schedules matter [5].
3. **Curvature $\ne$ Hessian.** For misspecified models or non-matching losses, $G \ne H$ and the natural gradient is not quasi-Newton; the GGN/Fisher coincidence requires exponential-family outputs with the canonical link and matching loss [5].
4. **Per-iteration cost.** Even K-FAC costs several times an SGD step. Wall-clock wins require the iteration-count reduction to dominate — true for ill-conditioned problems (RNNs, deep autoencoders), but not always for well-tuned normalized architectures.

A minimal, correct reference implementation of the exact natural gradient for a small exponential-family model illustrates the mechanics:


import numpy as np

def natural_gradient_step(theta, grad_fn, fisher_fn, eta=0.1, damping=1e-4):
    """One exact natural-gradient step: theta <- theta - eta * (G + lI)^{-1} g."""
    g = grad_fn(theta)          # ordinary (Euclidean) gradient of the loss
    G = fisher_fn(theta)        # Fisher information matrix at theta
    n = G.shape[0]
    # Solve (G + damping*I) d = g instead of forming the inverse explicitly.
    d = np.linalg.solve(G + damping * np.eye(n), g)
    return theta - eta * d      # covariant update direction

# Example: Bernoulli model p(x; theta) = theta^x (1-theta)^(1-x), x in {0,1}.
# Fisher information: G(theta) = 1 / (theta * (1 - theta)).
def bernoulli_grad(theta, x_bar):
    return -(x_bar / theta - (1 - x_bar) / (1 - theta))  # d/dtheta of NLL

def bernoulli_fisher(theta):
    return np.array([[1.0 / (theta * (1 - theta))]])

theta = 0.2
for _ in range(50):
    theta = natural_gradient_step(theta, lambda t: bernoulli_grad(t, 0.7),
                                  bernoulli_fisher, eta=0.5)[0]
    theta = float(np.clip(theta, 1e-6, 1 - 1e-6))
print("theta* =", theta)  # converges to the sample mean 0.7


Because the update direction is $G^{-1}g$, the same trajectory is traced whether we parametrize by $\theta$ (probability), by log-odds, or by any other smooth chart — rewriting the loop in logit coordinates gives iterates coinciding up to the chart map.

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

Decades of experiments validate the geometric predictions; we summarize the principal evidence and matching theorems.

| Domain | Method | Key result | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blind source separation / ICA | Natural gradient on $GL(p)$ | Equivariant, faster and more stable than Euclidean gradient | [3] |
| Multilayer perceptrons | Exact natural gradient (small nets) | Plateau phenomenon of backprop largely disappears | [3] |
| Deep networks | K-FAC | 10–20× fewer iterations than tuned SGD on autoencoders/CNNs | [5] |
| Deep networks | Random-net Fisher analysis | Layer-wise diagonalization justifies cheap approximations | [9] |
| Reinforcement learning | Natural policy gradient | Dramatic speedup on Tetris vs. vanilla PG; link to policy iteration | [4] |
| Reinforcement learning | TRPO / PPO | Approximate monotonic-improvement guarantees; strong continuous control | [4, 10] |
| Variational inference | SVI / CVI natural gradients | Closed-form updates for conjugate models; faster than Euclidean VI | [8] |
| Statistics | EM as alternating projection | Geometric convergence analysis via Pythagorean decomposition | [2] |

Key guarantees: **invariance** under reparametrization [3] (K-FAC preserves affine invariance of activations [5]); the **Pythagorean decomposition** for alternating $e$-/$m$-projections [2]; **efficiency** — the exponential-family MLE attains the Cramér–Rao bound, and the natural gradient in $\eta$ equals the ordinary gradient in $\theta$ [2]; and **mirror-descent equivalence** of tabular-softmax NPG [4].

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Computational cost.** The exact Fisher is intractable for modern models; every practical method is an approximation (diagonal, block-diagonal, Kronecker, low-rank, CG-implicit), each sacrificing some of the theory's invariance. No known approximation is simultaneously cheap, provably close to the true natural direction, and fully parametrization-invariant.

**Damping and trust regions.** Practical implementations add $\lambda I$ or enforce a KL trust region; the interplay between damping (which interpolates toward the Euclidean gradient) and the natural step length is poorly understood, and adaptive damping schedules are heuristic [5].

**Singular models.** Deep networks, mixtures, and latent-variable models are singular: the Fisher is degenerate and the standard asymptotic theory (and Čencov's finite-alphabet proof) does not directly apply. Watanabe's algebraic geometry of singular models is the right language, but its integration with natural-gradient optimization remains incomplete.

**Stochastic regime.** Minibatch noise interacts with curvature inversion: small eigenvalues of the estimated Fisher amplify noise. Variance-reduced Fisher estimates, and the relationship to adaptive first-order methods (Adam approximates a *diagonal* natural gradient under some assumptions), are active research areas.

**Alternative geometries.** Wasserstein geometry, Stein geometry, quantum information geometry (SLD/Bogoliubov metrics), and infinite-dimensional nonparametric information geometry (the $L^p$-Fisher–Rao metrics [1]) extend the program; unifying them with deep-learning optimization is largely open.

**Open problems.** (1) Scalable Fisher approximations with explicit error bounds on the natural direction. (2) Convergence theory for stochastic natural gradient on singular models. (3) Dually flat parametrizations for modern architectures such as transformers. (4) The precise relationship between natural gradients, mirror descent, and adaptive optimizers' implicit biases.

## 7. Conclusion

Information geometry replaces the flat, coordinate-bound view of statistical models with a canonical Riemannian structure — the Fisher–Rao metric, uniquely singled out by Čencov's invariance theorem — and a dualistic family of affine connections whose flat members organize exponential and mixture families into dually flat manifolds. Divergences become canonical, projections orthogonal, the EM algorithm alternating projection, and optimization covariant: the natural gradient descends along the steepest direction measured by the statistical ruler itself. From Amari's 1998 formulation through Kakade's natural policy gradient, Martens and Grosse's K-FAC, and Khan and Lin's natural-gradient variational inference, the same geometric idea has repeatedly delivered faster, more stable, more principled algorithms than their Euclidean counterparts — at the price of curvature estimation, which remains the central computational challenge. When the object being optimized is a *distribution* rather than a vector, the algorithm should respect the geometry of distributions: a principle that will outlive any particular approximation.
