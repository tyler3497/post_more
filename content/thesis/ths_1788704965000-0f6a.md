# Exoplanet Detection via Transit Photometry and Radial Velocity: Kepler/TESS Pipeline Systematics, Box Least Squares Periodograms, and Atmospheric Characterization with JWST Transmission Spectroscopy

## Abstract

Detecting exoplanets through the combined observables of transit photometry and radial-velocity (RV) spectroscopy is the most mature discovery pipeline in modern astrophysics. NASA's *Kepler* mission demonstrated that photometry at 20 parts per million recovers transits across 156,000 stars [1], while *TESS* targeted the brightest 200,000 stars to enable mass measurement and atmospheric follow-up [3]. At the algorithmic core sits the Box Least Squares periodogram of Kovács, Zucker & Mazeh (2002), the optimal matched filter for periodic box-shaped dimmings [2], embedded in *Kepler*'s pipeline of Bayesian systematics cotrending, wavelet-based transit search, and data-validation tests [4]. Photometry yields the radius ratio and RV the projected mass; their union gives the bulk density. Extended into the wavelength domain with *JWST*, transmission spectroscopy yields atmospheric composition, as demonstrated by the WASP-39 b spectrum with Na, H2O, CO2, and CO detected at 19-sigma, 33-sigma, 28-sigma, and 7-sigma, plus photochemically produced SO2 [5][6]. This thesis treats detection statistics, pipeline systematics, periodogram theory, Keplerian inference, and atmospheric retrieval as one coherent estimation problem, deriving the governing equations and identifying the fundamental limits of each stage.

## 1 Introduction

The detection and characterization of extrasolar planets has matured, in barely three decades, from a heroic observational pursuit into a systematized, high-throughput enterprise [7]. Two complementary observables anchor nearly all of that progress: the periodic dimming of a star as a planet transits its disk, and the periodic Doppler displacement of the stellar spectrum as the star reflexively orbits the common barycenter. The first technique yields the planet-to-star radius ratio; the second yields a lower bound on the planetary mass. Together they deliver the bulk density — the single most diagnostic physical quantity available for an unresolved world — and, when extended into the wavelength domain, the transmission spectrum that encodes atmospheric composition.

The modern era began with radial velocity: the 1995 discovery of 51 Pegasi b by Mayor and Queloz, followed by the first transiting exoplanet, HD 209458 b, whose photometric transits (Charbonneau et al. 2000; Henry et al. 2000) inaugurated the era of radius measurement. NASA's *Kepler* mission, launched in March 2009, monitored roughly 156,000 stars with a differential photometric precision of 20 ppm over 6.5 hours at 12th magnitude — . *Kepler* returned thousands of planetary candidates and demonstrated that compact multi-planet systems and sub-Neptune planets are the Galaxy's most common architectures. The *Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite* (*TESS*), launched in April 2018, pivoted the strategy from statistical completeness to follow-up amenability, surveying the nearest and brightest stars across nearly the entire sky so that every discovery is amenable to mass measurement and atmospheric characterization [3].

The third pillar arrived with the *James Webb Space Telescope*: transmission spectroscopy at 0.6–12 µm with signal-to-noise ratios far exceeding Hubble's, exemplified by the Early Release Science spectrum of WASP-39 b, in which multiple molecules were detected at high significance and a 4.05 µm feature was attributed to photochemically produced SO₂ [5][6]. This thesis treats the full chain — detection statistics, pipeline systematics, periodogram theory, Keplerian radial-velocity inference, and atmospheric retrieval — as a single coherent estimation problem.

## 2 Background

**Transit geometry.** For a planet on a circular orbit of semi-major axis *a* around a star of radius *R*★, a transit is visible when the orbital inclination *i* satisfies cos *i* < (*R*★ + *R*p)/*a*, so the geometric transit probability is approximately *p* ≃ *R*★/*a* — roughly 10% for a hot Jupiter at 0.05 AU but only about 0.5% for an Earth analog at 1 AU. During transit the fractional flux decrement, to zeroth order in limb darkening, is

δ = (*R*p/*R*★)² = k²,

so a Jupiter analog produces δ ≈ 1% while an Earth analog around a Sun-like star produces δ ≈ 84 ppm, the signal *Kepler* was designed to recover. The full transit duration (first to fourth contact) for a circular orbit is

T₁₄ = (P/π) · arcsin{ (*R*★/*a*) · √[(1+k)² − b²] / sin *i* },

with impact parameter b = (*a* cos *i*)/*R*★. Flat-bottomed transits (b ≲ 1 − k) yield the totality duration T₂₃ via the substitution (1+k) → (1−k), and the ingress/egress duration τ = (T₁₄ − T₂₃)/2 directly constrains *b* and hence breaks degeneracies with limb darkening.

Limb darkening — the center-to-limb decline of specific intensity — rounds the transit floor and must be modeled; Mandel & Agol (2002) derived exact analytic transit light curves for quadratic and nonlinear limb-darkening laws. The quadratic law is conventionally written

I(μ)/I(1) = 1 − u₁(1−μ) − u₂(1−μ)²,

with μ = cos θ the emergent angle [7].

**Radial-velocity geometry.** The stellar reflex velocity projected on the line of sight follows a Keplerian curve

v_r(t) = K [cos(ν(t) + ω) + e cos ω] + γ,

where ν is the true anomaly, ω the argument of periastron, *e* the eccentricity, γ the systemic velocity, and *K* the semi-amplitude:

K = (2πG/P)^{1/3} · (M_p sin *i*) / (M_★^{2/3} √(1−e²)).

A hot Jupiter induces K ≈ 150–200 m s⁻¹; an Earth analog at 1 AU induces K ≈ 9 cm s⁻¹, below even the ~30 cm s⁻¹ long-term floor of state-of-the-art spectrographs such as ESPRESSO. Because transiting systems have sin *i* ≈ 1, RV follow-up of transiting candidates converts the radius measurement into a true mass and hence a bulk density.

## 3 Methodology

The end-to-end methodology of a modern transit survey comprises four stages:

1. **Photometric extraction and systematics correction.** Raw pixel time series are converted to aperture photometry; instrumental systematics — thermal transients, pointing drift, reaction-wheel desaturation, scattered light, and (for *Kepler*) quarterly rolls — are removed by cotrending against basis vectors derived from the ensemble of light curves (the Presearch Data Conditioning, PDC, module with Bayesian maximum-a-posteriori cotrending) [4].
2. **Transit search.** The detrended flux is searched over a grid of trial periods, epochs, and durations using a matched-filter statistic — operationally the Box Least Squares (BLS) algorithm [2] in most community pipelines, and a wavelet-based adaptive matched filter producing the Multiple Event Statistic (MES) in the *Kepler* Transiting Planet Search (TPS) [4].
3. **Vetting and validation.** Threshold-crossing events are subjected to a battery of data-validation tests: odd–even depth consistency, secondary-eclipse search, centroid offset measurement (to reject background eclipsing binaries), ghost diagnostics, and bootstrap false-alarm probability estimation.
4. **Confirmation and characterization.** High-resolution imaging and spectroscopy rule out blends; RV observations measure *K*; and, for the most favorable targets, transit spectroscopy measures the wavelength-dependent radius *R*p(λ), the input to atmospheric retrieval.

> **Theorem: Information complementarity of transits and radial velocities.** Let Θ = {P, t₀, k, b, a/R★, u₁, u₂} be the photometric parameter set and Φ = {P, t₀, K, e, ω, γ} the spectroscopic set. Then (i) k constrains R_p given R_★; (ii) K constrains M_p sin i given M_★; (iii) for a transiting planet, b < 1+k implies sin i ≈ 1, so the joint posterior on (R_p, M_p) yields the bulk density ρ_p = 3M_p/(4πR_p³) with no remaining geometric degeneracy. *Proof sketch.* The transit duration ratio τ/T₁₄ determines b; with b known, a/R_★ follows from T₁₄; Kepler's third law then fixes M_★ + M_p, and the RV semi-amplitude K gives M_p sin i directly. ∎

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Transit Light-Curve Physics and Limb Darkening

Beyond the zeroth-order depth, the detailed morphology of a transit light curve encodes the stellar density. For a circular orbit, the mean stellar density follows directly from observables:

ρ_★ = (3π/GP²) · (a/R_★)³,

so that a/R_★ derived from the transit duration, together with an independent stellar radius (from Gaia parallax and spectroscopy), yields the planet radius with percent-level precision in the best cases. This *photometric density* constraint is powerful enough to identify false positives: a transit implying a stellar density inconsistent with the spectroscopic classification is a hallmark of a blended eclipsing binary.

In practice, light curves are fit with the Mandel & Agol analytic model evaluated inside a Bayesian sampler, with limb-darkening coefficients either fixed to theoretical values from model atmospheres or fit with uninformative priors. Correlated noise — stellar granulation, spot modulation — is modeled simultaneously with Gaussian processes to avoid biased depth estimates. ### 4.2 The Kepler and TESS Pipelines: Systematics at Scale

*Kepler's* Science Operations Center processed 95 megapixels of CCD data per cadence, selecting roughly 5.4×10⁶ pixels for ~156,000 long-cadence (29.4 min) targets plus 512 short-cadence (58.85 s) targets [4]. Achieving 20 ppm photometry required confronting systematics far larger than the signals sought: differential velocity aberration moved stellar images across pixels by up to a pixel over a quarter; thermal relaxations after data downlinks produced exponential flux ramps; and reaction-wheel friction heated the focal plane. The PDC module's Bayesian cotrending against an ensemble-derived set of cotrending basis vectors (CBVs) removed these common-mode signals while a prior on the cotrending coefficients protected genuine astrophysical variability from overfitting.

The TPS module then applied a wavelet-based adaptive matched filter, computing the Multiple Event Statistic over a duration grid spanning 1.5–15 hours and a period grid extending to hundreds of days, with detection thresholds set by bootstrap estimation of the false-alarm probability. Data Validation subsequently ran seventeen diagnostic tests per candidate — including the difference-image centroid test that localized the transit source to sub-pixel precision — before human vetting and catalog disposition.

*TESS* inherited this architecture in the SPOC pipeline but faced new systematics: a 13.7-day highly elliptical orbit producing strong scattered-light ramps from Earth and Moon, and momentum dumps every few days. The Quick-Look Pipeline (QLP) extended the search to the ~10 million stars in the full-frame images. The strategic payoff of *TESS* was not raw yield but *characterizability*: its stars are 10–100× brighter than *Kepler*'s, placing hundreds of sub-Neptunes within reach of RV mass measurement and JWST spectroscopy [3].

### 4.3 Box Least Squares: Theory and Practice

The Box Least Squares algorithm of Kovács, Zucker & Mazeh (2002) [2] remains the workhorse of community transit searches because it is the natural matched filter for the approximately box-shaped signature of a transit. The model assumes the folded light curve alternates between two levels — out-of-transit *H* and in-transit *L* — with the transit occupying a small fractional duration *q*. For weights w_i (inverse-variance) and folded magnitudes x̃_i binned into *M* bins, the algorithm evaluates, for every candidate in-transit bin interval [i₁, i₂]:

- r = Σ_{in} w̃_i — the fractional weight in transit,
- s = Σ_{in} w̃_i x̃_i — the weighted flux deficit.

The *signal residue* SR = s/√[r(1−r)] is the detection statistic; its spectrum over trial periods is normalized to the *Signal Detection Efficiency* SDE = (SR − ⟨SR⟩)/σ_SR. The key analytic result is that detectability is governed by a single *effective signal-to-noise ratio* α = (δ/σ)√n_t, where δ is the transit depth, σ the per-point noise, and n_t the number of in-transit points: when α ≳ 6, detection is essentially guaranteed; below it, the false-alarm rate climbs steeply.

```python
import numpy as np

def bls_search(t, f, periods, q_grid=np.linspace(0.01, 0.1, 10), nbins=200):
    """Minimal Box Least Squares periodogram (Kovacs, Zucker & Mazeh 2002)."""
    w = np.ones_like(f)
    w /= w.sum()
    f = f - np.average(f, weights=w)          # zero-mean signal
    best = []
    for P in periods:
        phase = ((t % P) / P * nbins).astype(int) % nbins
        bw = np.bincount(phase, weights=w, minlength=nbins)
        bs = np.bincount(phase, weights=w * f, minlength=nbins)
        sr_max, q_best, i0_best = -np.inf, 0.0, 0
        for q in q_grid:
            m = max(1, int(round(q * nbins)))
            r = np.convolve(bw, np.ones(m), mode='same')
            s = np.convolve(bs, np.ones(m), mode='same')
            sr = s / np.sqrt(r * (1 - r) + 1e-12)
            j = int(np.argmax(sr))
            if sr[j] > sr_max:
                sr_max, q_best, i0_best = sr[j], q, j
        best.append((P, sr_max, q_best, i0_best / nbins * P))
    srs = np.array([b[1] for b in best])
    sde = (srs - srs.mean()) / srs.std()      # Signal Detection Efficiency
    return best, sde
```

BLS's principal failure modes are well understood: sensitivity collapses for duty cycles *q* outside the searched grid, for transit-timing variations that smear the folded signal, and in the presence of red noise, which inflates SDE and demands bootstrap recalibration of significance.

> **Theorem: BLS as a generalized likelihood-ratio test.** For white Gaussian noise and a strictly periodic two-level signal with known fractional duration, maximizing the BLS signal residue SR over (P, t₀, q) is equivalent to maximizing the log-likelihood ratio of the transit hypothesis against the null. Hence no other statistic using the same binned data can achieve higher detection power at fixed false-alarm rate (Neyman–Pearson). *Consequence.* SDE thresholds calibrated by bootstrap on the actual light curve yield valid frequentist p-values even under non-Gaussian noise, provided the noise is stationary over the baseline. ∎

### 4.4 Radial Velocity: From Spectra to Masses

The RV observable is extracted by cross-correlating thousands of stellar absorption lines against a binary mask or template, compressing the Doppler information of an entire echelle spectrum into a single cross-correlation function whose centroid shifts by Δλ/λ = v_r/c. Modern stabilized spectrographs (HARPS, ESPRESSO, EXPRES, NEID) reach instrumental precisions below 30 cm s⁻¹, but the *astrophysical* noise floor — granulation (~1 m s⁻¹), supergranulation, and rotating active regions (~2–5 m s⁻¹ for Sun-like stars) — dominates for all but the quietest stars.

The standard analysis fits a Keplerian model plus a Gaussian-process activity term with a quasi-periodic covariance kernel

k(t, t′) = η₁² exp[−(t−t′)²/(2η₂²) − sin²(π(t−t′)/η₃)/(2η₄²)],

where η₃ encodes the stellar rotation period and η₂ the active-region lifetime. For transiting planets the period and epoch are fixed by photometry, collapsing the RV fit to essentially a one-parameter amplitude measurement — the strategy that delivered masses for hundreds of *Kepler* and *TESS* planets and exposed the radius gap near 1.8 R⊕ as a compositional transition between rocky super-Earths and volatile-rich sub-Neptunes.

### 4.5 JWST Transmission Spectroscopy and Atmospheric Retrieval

Transmission spectroscopy measures the wavelength-dependent transit depth δ(λ) = [R_p(λ)/R_★]². Molecular absorption at wavelength λ raises the effective planetary radius by several scale heights H = kT/(μg); the characteristic feature amplitude is ΔD ≃ 2R_pH/R_★² — typically 100–500 ppm for hot Jupiters, an order of magnitude larger than the signals *Kepler* was built to find, but spread across narrow molecular bands demanding space-based spectroscopy.

JWST's instrument suite transformed the field: NIRSpec/PRISM (0.6–5.3 µm, R~100), NIRSpec/G395H (2.9–5.2 µm, R~2700), NIRISS/SOSS (0.6–2.8 µm), and MIRI/LRS (5–12 µm). The Transiting Exoplanet Early Release Science program targeted WASP-39 b (T_eq ≃ 1200 K, Saturn mass, Jupiter radius) and obtained a 0.5–5.5 µm spectrum in which Na (19σ), H₂O (33σ), CO₂ (28σ), and CO (7σ) were robustly detected; the non-detection of CH₄ combined with strong CO₂ favored super-solar metallicity [5]. A 4.05 µm feature, unanticipated by equilibrium chemistry, was best explained by SO₂ at 2.7σ — a photochemical product formed by successive oxidation of sulfur radicals liberated when H₂S is photodissociated [6]. Follow-up photochemical modeling demonstrated that the SO₂ abundance exceeds equilibrium predictions by roughly ten orders of magnitude, constituting the first unambiguous detection of photochemistry in an exoplanet atmosphere [6].

Atmospheric retrieval inverts the spectrum via Bayesian nested sampling over parameterized temperature–pressure profiles, molecular abundances, and cloud/haze properties. The central degeneracies — between metallicity and cloud-top pressure, and between the temperature gradient and mean molecular weight — are broken only by broad wavelength coverage, which is precisely what JWST delivers. Information-content studies of the four WASP-39 b instrument modes show that the minimal warranted model complexity is mode-dependent: non-isothermal terminator profiles and non-grey clouds are required by the data, and omitting SO₂ opacity biases the retrieved C/O ratio [6].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The Kepler DR25 catalog established the empirical foundation: roughly 2,300 planet candidates with measured completeness and reliability, enabling occurrence-rate calculations corrected for the geometric transit probability and pipeline detection efficiency. Three results stand as empirical theorems of the field:

1. **The radius gap.** The planet radius distribution is bimodal with a deficit near 1.8 R⊕ (Fulton et al. 2017), interpreted as the dividing line between stripped rocky cores and planets retaining H/He envelopes — a prediction of photoevaporation and core-powered mass-loss models confirmed by the data.
2. **The mass–radius relation.** Combining transit radii with RV masses yields ρ_p; the resulting mass–radius diagram shows distinct compositional tracks — rocky, water-rich, Neptunian, Jovian — validating interior structure models.
3. **Atmospheric metallicity trend.** JWST retrievals (WASP-39 b at super-solar metallicity, WASP-96 b showing a clear H₂O-dominated spectrum) extend the solar-system mass–metallicity correlation to exoplanets, constraining formation pathways.

Transit-timing variations provide an independent mass probe in multi-planet systems, yielding masses without any spectroscopy — .

## 6 Limitations

The transit–RV–spectroscopy chain has sharp, well-characterized limits. *Geometric bias*: only ~0.5% of Earth analogs transit, so occurrence rates rest on large completeness corrections. *Period sensitivity*: BLS and TPS lose sensitivity beyond ~1-year periods where fewer than three transits occur in a *Kepler*-length baseline; single-transit events (common in *TESS*'s 27-day sectors) yield only weak period constraints [3]. *Astrophysical false positives*: background eclipsing binaries diluted in the photometric aperture mimic planetary transits, requiring centroid vetting, high-resolution imaging, and statistical validation frameworks. *Stellar activity*: RV jitter of 1–3 m s⁻¹ for Sun-like stars sets a practical floor near a few Earth masses at short periods and rules out true Earth-analog mass measurement with current facilities. *Atmospheric degeneracies*: high-altitude clouds and hazes mute transmission features entirely (as in GJ 1214 b), and retrieval solutions remain model-dependent, sensitive to the assumed temperature profile and cloud parameterization. *Limb-darkening systematics*: fixing limb-darkening coefficients to theory can bias R_p/R_★ at the percent level for high-impact-parameter transits.

## 7 Conclusion

Exoplanet detection via transit photometry and radial velocity has evolved from discovery into precision measurement science. The *Kepler* and *TESS* pipelines demonstrated that systematics correction at the 20-ppm level is achievable at scale [1][3][4]; the Box Least Squares formalism provided the statistically optimal detection statistic for periodic box-shaped signals [2]; Keplerian RV analysis converted radii into densities; and JWST transmission spectroscopy is now converting densities into compositions, with photochemical tracers like SO₂ opening an entirely new diagnostic dimension [5][6]. The remaining grand challenge — the mass and atmosphere of a true Earth analog — requires the conjunction of all three techniques at their physical limits: micro-magnitude photometry from PLATO, cm s⁻¹ velocimetry from next-generation spectrographs, and spectroscopy of temperate terrestrial planets with future flagship observatories. The mathematical machinery reviewed here — matched filtering, Bayesian cotrending, Keplerian inference, and atmospheric retrieval — will underpin that effort unchanged in form, differing only in the severity of the noise.
