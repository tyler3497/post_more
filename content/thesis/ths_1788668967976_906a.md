# Generalized Consensus without a Leader: EPaxos, Fast Paxos, Mencius, and Dependency-Based Commit for Geo-Replicated State Machines

## Abstract

Leader-based replication pays a structural tax: every command is serialized through a single coordinator, which becomes a throughput bottleneck, a latency detour for distant clients, and a single point of stall during failover. This thesis analyzes four protocols that weaken or remove that tax: *Fast Paxos*, which lets proposers bypass the leader but charges larger quorums and a collision-recovery slow path; *EPaxos*, which replaces the total order with a dependency graph and commits non-interfering commands in one round trip from any replica; *Mencius*, which partitions the instance space across rotating coordinators but pays a skip-message tax when replicas idle; and *Generalized Paxos*, which makes command commutativity a first-class citizen of consensus itself. We develop a unified framework — crash-fault asynchronous model, quorum intersection algebra, message-delay accounting — and derive latency and fault-tolerance bounds for each protocol, with correctness sketches, a WAN comparison, and open problems in reconfiguration, dependency chains, and optimal fast-path quorums.

## 1. Introduction

State machine replication (SMR) is the canonical recipe for fault-tolerant services: replicate a deterministic state machine across $2f+1$ servers, order client commands identically at every replica, and tolerate $f$ crash failures [4]. Lamport's Paxos [2] showed *safe* consensus is achievable asynchronously, and its refinement into *Multi-Paxos* — a stable leader driving an unbounded stream of Phase 2 instances — became the workhorse of systems from Chubby to ZooKeeper-derived designs [4].

Yet the stable leader is also the protocol's structural weakness, with three costs. **Throughput:** the leader does $O(n)$ work per command while followers idle. **Latency:** a Singapore client whose leader sits in Virginia pays a full intercontinental round trip before ordering begins. **Availability:** leader failure triggers an election and recovery sweep during which nothing commits.

The natural question — *can we order commands without anyone in charge?* — produced a family of leaderless and multi-leader protocols. This thesis studies four landmarks. **Fast Paxos** [2] lets proposers contact acceptors directly, learning values in two message delays at the price of larger quorums and collision recovery. **EPaxos** [1] abandons the leader: every replica proposes, and agreement is reached on a *dependency graph* from which each replica derives a consistent execution order. **Mencius** [3] partitions the instance space across rotating coordinators, paying with *skip messages* when servers idle. **Generalized Paxos** makes command commutativity a first-class citizen of consensus itself.

We proceed: framework (Section 3), per-protocol deep dive (Section 4), safety sketches and WAN comparison (Section 5), open problems (Section 6).

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Paxos and Multi-Paxos

Classic Paxos decides a single value in two phases. Phase 1: a proposer sends *prepare* requests with ballot $b$; acceptors promise to reject lower ballots and return any previously accepted value. Phase 2: the proposer sends *accept* requests carrying the highest observed value (or its own); a value is *chosen* once a quorum accepts it at one ballot. Safety rests on one algebraic fact:

> **Lemma (Quorum Intersection):** With majorities of $2f+1$ acceptors, any two quorums intersect, and the promise chain guarantees at most one choosable value per ballot sequence.

A value is *learned* in three message delays; a stable leader that has completed Phase 1 reduces Multi-Paxos to two delays per instance — all through the leader [9]. The election and Phase 1 sweep on failover is the availability gap leaderless protocols close.

### 2.2 Raft and the Understandability Counterpoint

Raft [4] doubles down on strong leadership: randomized timeouts elect one leader replicating a totally-ordered log. Ongaro and Ousterhout's user study showed Raft is substantially more understandable than Paxos, making it the industrial default. Yet the Raft paper concedes the ceiling: *"Egalitarian Paxos (EPaxos) can achieve higher performance under some conditions with a leaderless approach..."* [4]. Understandability favors leaders; latency and load balance favor their removal.

### 2.3 Quorum Engineering and the EPaxos Correctness Saga

The quorum is the load-bearing abstraction here. Flexible Paxos [7] showed Phase 1 and Phase 2 quorums need only *cross-phase* intersection, and WPaxos [8] applied flexible quorums to the WAN by migrating object ownership across zones.

Leaderless protocols are hard to get right. Sutra [5] showed the original EPaxos contained subtle recovery-path safety violations — a cautionary tale about dependency graphs meeting ballot-based recovery. The *Making Democracy Work* line [6] fixes and simplifies EPaxos into EPaxos*, the first provably correct protocol matching Lamport's optimal bound $n \ge \max\{2e+f+1,\, 2f+1\}$ [6]. We treat EPaxos* as canonical below.

## 3. Methodology / Formal Framework

We analyze all four protocols inside one formal setting so that their trade-offs are directly comparable.

### 3.1 System Model

We assume an asynchronous message-passing system of $n = 2f+1$ processes with *crash* (non-Byzantine) failures, at most $f$ of which may crash. Messages may be lost, duplicated, or reordered but never corrupted. Safety holds in all executions; liveness requires a sufficiently long synchronous period [9]. Each process has stable storage for protocol state it must not forget.

The service is a deterministic state machine replicated at all processes. Clients submit *commands*; the protocol guarantees [1]: **nontriviality** (only proposed commands execute), **stability** (no command executes twice with different sequence numbers), **consistency** (no two correct processes execute in orders violating the agreed order), and **execution linearizability** (execution respects real-time precedence of non-overlapping commands).

### 3.2 Commutativity and Interference

Two commands *interfere* if different execution orders can produce different states or outputs; otherwise they *commute*. Writes to the same key interfere; reads commute with everything; writes to disjoint keys commute. An interference predicate $I(c_1, c_2)$ partitions the command space, and dependency-based protocols order only interfering pairs — their whole latency advantage.

### 3.3 Quorum Algebra

Let $\mathcal{C}$ be the family of *classic* (slow-path) quorums and $\mathcal{F}$ the family of *fast* quorums. All protocols in this thesis satisfy variants of the following intersection requirements:

| Requirement | Statement | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | $\forall C_1, C_2 \in \mathcal{C}:\ C_1 \cap C_2 \neq \emptyset$ | Paxos, Raft |
| Q2 | $\forall F_1, F_2 \in \mathcal{F},\, \forall C \in \mathcal{C}:\ F_1 \cap F_2 \cap C \neq \emptyset$ | Fast Paxos |
| Q3 | $\forall F_1, F_2 \in \mathcal{F}:\ \|F_1 \cap F_2\| \ge f+1$ | Fast Paxos |
| Q4 | $\forall F \in \mathcal{F},\, \forall C \in \mathcal{C}:\ F \cap C \neq \emptyset$ | EPaxos |

With $n = 2f+1$ and classic quorums of size $f+1$, Q2–Q3 force fast quorums of $2f+1 - \lfloor f/2 \rfloor$: $3$ of $3$ for $f=1$, $4$ of $5$ for $f=2$, $6$ of $7$ for $f=3$ [2]. EPaxos's optimized fast quorum is smaller — $F + \lfloor (F+1)/2 \rfloor$ for $n = 2F+1$ (e.g., $3$ of $5$ when $F=2$) — because it agrees on *dependency sets* and its TryPreAccept recovery gathers extra evidence [1][6].

### 3.4 Message-Delay Accounting

We measure commit latency in *message delays* — one delay is a single one-way traversal; a round trip is two. We distinguish the *proposer-to-learner* path (client-visible) from *replica-to-replica* agreement, and parameterize WAN analysis by the RTT matrix between sites.

```tla
------------------------------- MODULE QuorumCore -------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Acceptors, F  \* F = max tolerated failures, |Acceptors| = 2*F+1
ClassicQuorum(Q) == Q \subseteq Acceptors /\ Cardinality(Q) = F + 1
FastQuorum(Q)    == Q \subseteq Acceptors /\ Cardinality(Q) = 2*F + 1 - (F \div 2)
\* Q2: any two fast quorums and any classic quorum share an acceptor
Q2 == \A Q1, Q2 \in {Q \in SUBSET Acceptors : FastQuorum(Q)} :
        \A C \in {C \in SUBSET Acceptors : ClassicQuorum(C)} :
          Q1 \cap Q2 \cap C # {}
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Leader Bottleneck in Multi-Paxos and Raft

In Multi-Paxos and Raft, the leader sits on every command's critical path: client $\to$ leader $\to$ quorum $\to$ leader $\to$ client — $2$ delays colocated, $2 + 2d_1$ a WAN hop away, with every command contending for the leader's bandwidth. Three pathologies follow. **Throughput collapse:** the leader does $O(n)$ work per command while followers idle. **Failover stalls:** elections plus log reconciliation freeze commits for several WAN RTTs. **Load imbalance:** all clients hammer one site.

Raft accepts these costs: one leader and one log is far easier to reason about [4]. The question below is how much latency and throughput that simplicity costs — and whether leaderless designs repay their complexity.

### 4.2 Fast Paxos: Two-Delay Rounds and the Collision Tax

Fast Paxos [2] observes that the leader's Phase 2 role is merely to *select* the value; proposers can send values directly to acceptors. In a *fast round*, proposers broadcast directly and acceptors vote for the first value seen. A unanimous fast quorum $Q \in \mathcal{F}$ chooses $v$ in **two message delays** — the minimum for consensus in this model.

The price is twofold. First, **larger quorums**: Q2–Q3 demand $2f+1 - \lfloor f/2 \rfloor$ acceptors — 4 of 5 instead of 3 — so the fast path tolerates only $\lfloor f/2 \rfloor$ failures. Second, **collisions**: concurrent $v_1 \neq v_2$ may split votes so no fast quorum is unanimous. Nothing is chosen, and the coordinator must run a *classic* round — a *collision recovery* of at least two extra delays, with the leader back on the critical path.

> **Lemma (Fast Paxos Safety):** A value chosen in a fast round cannot be contradicted by any later round. *Proof sketch.* If $v$ received votes from fast quorum $F$, any later ballot's classic quorum $C$ intersects $F$ (Q4), so the coordinator observes a vote for $v$; and any two fast quorums share $\ge f+1$ acceptors (Q3), so a competing $v' \neq v$ would need $f+1$ acceptors to vote twice — impossible, since acceptors vote once per round. ∎

The practical consequence: Fast Paxos shines when a single proposer dominates and collapses under contention, where *every* concurrent proposal triggers collision recovery. It removes the leader from the *message path* but not from the *protocol* — the coordinator remains the arbiter of every disagreement.

### 4.3 EPaxos: Dependency Graphs and the One-Round Fast Path

EPaxos [1] abandons the total order — and with it, the leader — entirely. Each replica keeps its own *instance space*, and any replica may lead a client command. Instead of agreeing on a position in a global log, replicas agree on the command's *dependencies*: previously seen commands that interfere with it. Execution order is then derived deterministically at every replica from the resulting dependency graph.

**The commit path.** To propose $c$, replica $r$ computes $deps(c)$ — interfering commands in $r$'s log — and broadcasts $\langle \text{PreAccept}, c, deps(c) \rangle$ to a fast-path quorum of $F + \lfloor (F+1)/2 \rfloor$ replicas, each replying with its own view $deps_q(c)$. If every reply matches, the set is *stable*: $r$ broadcasts $\langle \text{Commit}, c, deps(c) \rangle$ and $c$ commits in **one round trip** from the nearest replica. Otherwise $r$ runs a Paxos *Accept* phase with a classic quorum of $F+1$ over the union of reported dependencies, then commits — four delays, only under genuine interference or replica lag.

**Execution from the graph.** Committed commands and dependencies form a graph, executed in reverse topological order over *strongly connected components* (SCCs): within an SCC, ascending sequence number (ties by replica id); across SCCs, dependencies first. All correct replicas eventually commit identical dependency sets (Section 5), hence identical graphs and identical execution — *execution consistency* with no leader assigning log positions.

**Recovery.** If a command leader crashes mid-protocol, any replica may *Prepare* the instance at a higher ballot. The subtlety that broke the original protocol [5]: recovery must reconstruct a dependency set *consistent* with what the fast path may have committed. The optimized recovery uses *TryPreAccept* to gather dependency evidence from a quorum before proposing [1][6]; finding no trace of a command, it finalizes the instance with a no-op, unblocking the graph.

EPaxos combines three properties no prior protocol had: optimal WAN commit latency (one round trip, common case), uniform load balancing, and graceful degradation. Its five-region EC2 evaluation demonstrated these against a production Multi-Paxos baseline [1].

```python
def epaxos_propose(replica, cmd, fast_quorum):
    """One-round fast path with dependency agreement. Pseudocode."""
    deps = {c for c in replica.log if interfere(c, cmd)}
    replies = broadcast_and_collect(
        fast_quorum, ("PreAccept", cmd, deps))
    if all(r.deps == deps for r in replies):
        broadcast(("Commit", cmd, deps))      # FAST PATH: 1 round trip
        return "committed-fast"
    merged = deps | union(r.deps for r in replies)
    paxos_accept_phase(cmd, merged, classic_quorum(replica))  # SLOW PATH
    broadcast(("Commit", cmd, merged))
    return "committed-slow"

def execute_graph(committed):

    sccs = strongly_connected_components(committed)  # Tarjan
    for scc in reversed(topological_order(sccs)):
```

### 4.4 Mencius: Rotating Coordinators and the Skip-Message Tax

Mencius [3] keeps coordinators but rotates them: the instance space is partitioned round-robin — server $i$ owns instances $i, i+n, i+2n, \dots$ and coordinates exactly those. A server proposing a client command uses its *own* next instance, running the standard two-delay Paxos commit with itself as leader. Statically scheduled leadership never contends.

The catch is **total order**: instance $k+1$ cannot execute until instance $k$ commits, even if $k$ belongs to another server. Under light or skewed load, an idle server's uncommitted instances would stall everyone. Mencius's answer is the **skip message**: a server that learns others have committed beyond its own next slot proposes no-ops for its outstanding instances. Skips are small and batchable, but pure overhead — work done solely to simulate a leader's continuous proposal stream.

The deeper cost is **correlated slowness**: every server's instances gate global execution, so latency is set by the *slowest* server. A straggler in a distant datacenter delays execution everywhere, whereas EPaxos confines a slow replica's damage to its own dependency neighborhood. Mao's evaluation found Mencius's commit latency usually no worse than Paxos and often better, while noting that network variance effects on both are complex [3]: rotation helps the median and hurts the tail.

| Dimension | Multi-Paxos / Raft | Fast Paxos | EPaxos | Mencius |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coordinator | Single leader | Recovery only | None | Rotating (static) |
| Commit, common case | 2 delays via leader | 2 delays from proposer | 1 round trip | 2 delays |
| Fast quorum ($n{=}2f{+}1$) | $f{+}1$ | $2f{+}1{-}\lfloor f/2 \rfloor$ | $F{+}\lfloor(F{+}1)/2\rfloor$ | $f{+}1$ |
| Contention cost | Leader serialization | Collision recovery | Slow path on interference | Skip messages when idle |
| Slowest-link sensitivity | Leader only | Coordinator on collision | Dep. neighborhood only | Global (slowest server) |
| Load balance | None | Partial | Uniform | Uniform |

### 4.5 Commutativity as a First-Class Citizen: Generalized Consensus

All three protocols exploit the same insight: *total order is stronger than necessary*. Generalized consensus makes it explicit. In Lamport's Generalized Paxos, the decided value is a *command structure* (c-struct) — a partially ordered set where interfering commands are ordered and commuting ones are not; any linear extension is an acceptable execution [2]. EPaxos's dependency graph is the practical realization of a c-struct, computed incrementally rather than decided wholesale.

This reframing dissolves an apparent paradox: EPaxos commits commands *without ordering them*, yet replicas execute consistently. The resolution is that consensus is reached on the *partial order* (the dependency sets), and the total execution order is a deterministic function of it — computable locally by every replica. The lesson is sharp: **identify the weakest order your application needs, and pay consensus costs only for that order**.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Analytic WAN Latency Comparison

Consider five sites — Virginia, Oregon, Ireland, Singapore, Sydney. The table reports median client-visible commit latency for a Singapore client, the hardest case for leader-centric designs.

| Protocol | Critical path (Singapore client) | Median |
|---|---|---|
| Multi-Paxos, leader Virginia | SIN→VA→quorum→SIN | ~300 ms |
| Multi-Paxos, leader Singapore | local quorum round | ~150 ms |
| Fast Paxos (no collision) | SIN→quorum→SIN (2 delays) | ~190 ms |
| Fast Paxos (collision) | + coordinator recovery round | ~380 ms |
| EPaxos fast path | SIN→fast quorum→SIN, 1 round trip | ~170 ms |
| EPaxos slow path | + Accept phase (2 delays) | ~330 ms |
| Mencius (all servers live) | 2 delays from local coordinator | ~170 ms |
| Mencius (one straggler) | gated by slowest server | ~300+ ms |

In sum: EPaxos's fast path matches every competitor's best case — clients propose at the nearest replica, so there is no leader-placement problem [1][8]. Fast Paxos's collision cost is the table's largest penalty: under contention it is worse than Multi-Paxos, which is why practical Fast Paxos deployments are rare. Mencius's headline number is excellent but fragile: it assumes uniform server health, precisely what WANs do not provide [3].

### 5.2 Published Measurements

The EPaxos paper's EC2 evaluation is the landmark empirical result for this family: across five regions, EPaxos committed in a single wide-area round trip in the common case, needed only a simple majority of non-faulty replicas, used a linear number of messages per command, and took at most two rounds in any case [1]. Throughput scaled with replica count — the uniform load balancing leader-based designs cannot achieve. Mencius's OSDI evaluation: commit latency usually no worse than Paxos, often better, with the variance caveats above [3].

### 5.3 Correctness Sketches

> **Theorem (EPaxos Stability):** If $c$ commits with dependency set $D$ at any correct replica, every correct replica eventually commits $c$ with exactly $D$.
> *Proof sketch.* Fast-path commit requires unanimous agreement on $D$ across a fast quorum $F$. Any recovery quorum $C$ intersects $F$ (Q4), so recovery observes a vote for $D$; the TryPreAccept evidence rule forces recovery to propose a consistent $D$, and ballot ordering fixes the committed set [1][6]. ∎

> **Theorem (EPaxos Execution Consistency):** All correct replicas execute committed commands in orders respecting every dependency edge.
> *Proof sketch.* By Stability, all replicas build the identical graph; SCC decomposition with the (sequence number, replica id) tiebreak is deterministic, so every replica derives the same execution order, and topological order respects all edges. ∎

> **Theorem (Mencius Safety):** Mencius inherits Paxos safety per instance; skip messages are ordinary no-ops and cannot violate consistency.
> *Proof sketch.* Each instance runs an independent Coordinated Paxos with its owner as coordinator — per-instance safety is exactly Paxos safety [9] — and execution follows instance order — a total order over per-instance decisions. ∎

The common thread: every argument bottoms out in *quorum intersection*. Leaderless protocols do not escape it; they re-target it from *values* to *dependency sets* (EPaxos) or from *one log* to *many statically-owned logs* (Mencius).

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**Reconfiguration remains the hardest unsolved problem.** Adding or removing a replica in Multi-Paxos is routine; in EPaxos, every instance space and in-flight dependency set references the membership, and no coordinator exists to serialize the transition. Implementations either punt or use bounded, raft-like configuration commands.

**Long dependency chains.** A command interfering with a long chain of uncommitted commands cannot execute until the chain resolves; under adversarial workloads, execution latency is unbounded even when commit latency is not. Bounding chain length without reintroducing a leader is an active design question.

**Contention collapse.** Fast Paxos degrades to repeated collision recovery under concurrent proposers; EPaxos to the slow path under high interference; Mencius to skip-message storms under skew. A protocol that adapts its commit path to observed contention without manual tuning does not exist.

**Optimal fast-path quorums.** Lamport's bound $n \ge \max\{2e+f+1,\, 2f+1\}$ trades fast-path tolerance $e$ against total $f$; EPaxos* is the first provably correct protocol to match it [6]. Whether the bound can be met with *simpler* recovery, and whether analogous bounds hold for Byzantine or crash-recovery models, remain open.

**Verification gap.** Paxos has machine-checked proofs [9]; EPaxos-class protocols have TLA+ specifications of their core [6] but rarely verify both together. Given the history of subtle bugs [5], closing this gap is the most practically important open problem in the area.

## 7. Conclusion

The leader is a convenience, not a necessity. Fast Paxos lets proposers reach acceptors directly, at the cost of larger quorums and a collision-recovery slow path that keeps the coordinator in the loop. Mencius statically rotates leadership, at the cost of skip messages and global sensitivity to the slowest server. EPaxos replaces the total order with a dependency graph, committing non-interfering commands in one round trip from any replica — the closest any crash-tolerant protocol has come to the latency floor — at the cost of recovery complexity that took a decade to tame [1][5][6].

The right choice follows the workload: low contention favors Fast Paxos; uniform WAN load favors EPaxos; predictable per-site streams favor Mencius; operational simplicity still favors Raft [4]. What unites the leaderless designs is one idea with the force of a paradigm shift: *order only what interferes, and agree only on that*. Generalized consensus named it; EPaxos built it; the open problems of Section 6 are the bill coming due for its complexity. Paying it — verified, reconfigurable, contention-adaptive leaderless consensus — is the central challenge for the next decade of replication research.
