## 1. Introduction

The last decade of scientific computing has witnessed a quiet but profound architectural shift: **numerical algorithms are increasingly written as differentiable programs**. Where classical scientific computing separated *simulation* (solving PDEs, integrating ODEs, running molecular dynamics) from *inference* (optimizing parameters, fitting models), differentiable programming unifies them: a climate model's subgrid parameterization, a robot's rigid-body simulator, or a molecular force field can all be *differentiated end-to-end*, so that gradients flow from a downstream objective back through the physics itself [1][2].

Two software ecosystems dominate this landscape, and they embody **fundamentally different philosophies of automatic differentiation**:

1. **JAX** (Google, 2018): a functional, transform-based system. Programs are pure functions over immutable arrays; AD is not a tape but a *higher-order function transformation* `grad : (a → b) → (a → a)` operating on traced jaxprs (typed intermediate representations), with pytrees providing structural genericity and `jit`/`vmap`/`pmap` providing composable program transformations [3].
2. **PyTorch** (Meta, 2017): a dynamic, define-by-run system. Programs are imperative sequences of tensor operations recorded on a mutable autograd graph (`torch.autograd`), with reverse-mode differentiation executed eagerly at each backward pass [4].

This thesis argues the following central claims:

- **Claim 1.** JAX's transformation-based AD achieves asymptotically superior *composability* (e.g., `grad(grad(f))`, `vmap(grad(f))`) and hardware utilization, at the cost of a purity discipline (no in-place mutation, static shapes under `jit`) that complicates stateful scientific codes.
- **Claim 2.** Reverse-mode AD memory cost grows linearly in the number of elementary operations; binomial *gradient checkpointing* reduces this to $O(\sqrt{n})$ or $O(\log n)$ memory at bounded recomputation overhead, which is *the* enabling technique for differentiating through long-horizon simulations.
- **Claim 3.** The adjoint sensitivity method for neural ODEs computes gradients of ODE solutions in $O(1)$ memory—independent of solver steps—by solving an augmented ODE backward in time; numerical stability of the backward pass is the dominant practical concern [5].
- **Claim 4.** PINN training failures are primarily *optimization* failures, not expressivity failures: multi-term losses induce stiff, ill-conditioned landscapes whose gradient pathologies (vanishing boundary terms, spectral bias) are now well-characterized and partially remediated [6][7].
- **Claim 5.** Differentiable simulators (Brax, PhiFlow, differentiable MD) close the loop between first-principles physics and gradient-based learning, enabling applications—from climate parameterization tuning to neural force fields—that are intractable with gradient-free methods at scale.

### 1.1 Contributions and structure

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews reverse-mode AD, the chain rule on DAGs, and the JAX/PyTorch design space. ## 2. Background

### 2.1 Automatic differentiation: the mathematical core

Automatic differentiation is **not** numerical differentiation (finite differences) and **not** symbolic differentiation (expression swell). It is the *mechanical application of the chain rule to a program's elementary operations*, achieving machine-precision derivatives at a bounded multiple of the primal compute cost [1].

Consider a scalar-valued function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ decomposed into elementary operations forming a directed acyclic graph (DAG) with intermediate variables $v_1, \dots, v_m$. **Reverse-mode AD** proceeds in two phases:

1. **Forward pass:** evaluate $v_i = \phi_i(\text{parents}(v_i))$ for $i = 1, \dots, m$, storing values (the *tape* or *Wengert list*).
2. **Backward pass:** propagate *adjoints* $\bar{v}_i = \partial f / \partial v_i$ in reverse topological order:

$$ \bar{v}_i = \sum_{j : i \in \mathrm{parents}(j)} \bar{v}_j \, \frac{\partial \phi_j}{\partial v_i}, \qquad \bar{v}_m = 1. $$

> Theorem: (Cheap gradient principle, Baur–Strassen [1].) For $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ computed with $\mathrm{ops}(f)$ elementary operations, reverse-mode AD computes $\nabla f$ in at most $\omega \cdot \mathrm{ops}(f)$ operations with $\omega \leq 5$ a small constant, independent of $n$. Forward-mode AD costs $O(n \cdot \mathrm{ops}(f))$.

This theorem is the economic foundation of deep learning: gradients of million-parameter models cost roughly the same as the forward simulation itself. The catch is **memory**: the backward pass needs intermediate values, so naive reverse-mode stores the entire forward trajectory — $O(\mathrm{ops}(f))$ memory.

### 2.2 Two philosophies: tracing versus taping

| Aspect | JAX | PyTorch |
|---|---|---|
| AD model | Function *transformation* (`grad(f)` returns a new function) | Graph *execution* (`loss.backward()` mutates `.grad` buffers) |
| Program capture | **Tracing**: abstract evaluation with tracer objects produces a jaxpr (static single-assignment IR) | **Taping**: eager ops append `Node`s to a dynamic autograd graph per execution |
| Data model | Immutable arrays; **pytrees** (nested dict/list/tuple) flattened to leaves | Mutable `Tensor`s with in-place ops; autograd `Function` versioning |
| Higher-order AD | Trivial: `grad(grad(f))`, `jacfwd(jacrev(f))` compose as transformations | Supported via `create_graph=True`, but graph-of-graphs is heavier |
| Vectorization | `vmap`: vectorizing map as a transformation (batching without code change) | `torch.vmap` (functorch, now `torch.func`): added later, less pervasive |
| Compilation | `jit` lowers jaxpr → StableHLO → XLA; whole-program optimization | `torch.compile` (TorchDynamo + Inductor): graph capture of eager code, newer |
| Control flow | Must be traceable (`lax.scan`, `lax.cond`, `lax.while_loop` under `jit`) | Native Python control flow "just works" (graph rebuilt each run) |
| State | Functional: state threaded explicitly or via Equinox modules | Object-oriented: `nn.Module`, mutable parameters, in-place updates |

The distinction is usefully summarized as: **JAX differentiates *functions*; PyTorch differentiates *executions*.** A JAX program is a pure mathematical function whose derivative is another pure function; a PyTorch program is a recorded history of tensor mutations whose derivative is computed by replaying that history in reverse [3][4].

### 2.3 Pytrees and tracing in JAX

JAX's genericity over nested data structures rests on **pytrees**: any nested composition of lists, tuples, and dicts (plus registered custom types) whose *leaves* are arrays. `grad`, `jit`, and `vmap` all operate by flattening pytrees to leaf arrays, transforming, and unflattening:

```python
import jax
import jax.numpy as jnp

def loss(params):  # params is a pytree: dict of arrays
    W, b = params['W'], params['b']
    preds = jnp.tanh(X @ W + b)
    return jnp.mean((preds - y) ** 2)

grad_loss = jax.grad(loss)             # (pytree -> float) -> (pytree -> pytree)
grads = grad_loss({'W': W0, 'b': b0})  # grads mirrors params' pytree structure
batched = jax.vmap(loss)              # vectorize over a batch axis automatically
compiled = jax.jit(jax.grad(loss))    # fuse forward+backward into one XLA program
```

**Tracing** is the mechanism: `jit(f)` evaluates `f` on abstract *tracer* values (shaped but valueless), recording operations into a **jaxpr** — a tiny typed, SSA-form IR. AD in JAX is then a *source-to-source transformation on jaxprs*: the transpose rule for each primitive generates the cotangent computation. Because differentiation happens at the IR level, `grad` of a `jit`-compiled, `vmap`-ed function is just IR rewriting — this is why higher-order and mixed-mode AD compose so cleanly [3].

The price of tracing is well known to practitioners:

- **Static shapes**: traced arrays must have shapes known at trace time (dynamic shapes require masking/padding or recent dynamic-shape extensions).
- **No data-dependent Python control flow** under `jit`: `if x > 0` on a traced value fails; one must use `lax.cond`/`lax.select`.
- **No in-place mutation**: functional updates (`x.at[i].set(v)`) instead of `x[i] = v`.
- **Re-tracing costs**: Python-level overhead per distinct shape signature.

### 2.4 PyTorch dynamic graphs

PyTorch records, on every forward execution, a graph of `Node` objects (each wrapping a `backward` closure) linked through tensors' `grad_fn` attributes. Calling `.backward()` performs a topological traversal accumulating gradients into leaf `.grad` buffers. Because the graph is rebuilt per execution, **data-dependent control flow, variable-length sequences, and in-place mutation** (with version-counter safety) work naturally [4].

```python
import torch

x = torch.randn(4, requires_grad=True)
for t in range(T):                        # Python control flow: fine
    x = torch.tanh(x @ W + b) + 0.1 * x   # residual dynamics
    if x.norm() > 10:                     # data-dependent branch: fine
        x = x / x.norm() * 10
loss = x.sum()
loss.backward()  # reverse-mode over the recorded tape
```

Higher-order derivatives use `create_graph=True`, which retains the backward graph differentiably — correct but operationally heavier than JAX's IR-level transformation, and historically a source of memory blowups in scientific codes differentiating through iterative solvers.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Reverse-mode AD as adjoint propagation

We formalize both systems as instances of one abstraction. Let the primal program denote a function $F: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ factored through intermediate spaces. Reverse-mode AD computes the *vector-Jacobian product* (VJP):

$$ \mathrm{vjp}_F(x, \bar{y}) = \bar{y}^\top J_F(x), $$

and forward-mode computes the *Jacobian-vector product* (JVP), $J_F(x)\,\dot{x}$. The two systems differ only in *when and how* the factorization is materialized:

- **PyTorch**: the factorization is the *executed trace*; VJPs are closures captured per op invocation.
- **JAX**: the factorization is the *traced jaxpr*; VJPs are generated by transposition rules, then the whole VJP program is itself subject to `jit`/`vmap`.

> Theorem: (Transposition duality.) For a linear primitive $y = Ax$ with VJP rule $\bar{x} = A^\top \bar{y}$, the JAX transpose transformation on jaxprs and PyTorch's backward closures compute identical cotangents up to floating-point reassociation. The systems differ in scheduling and memory management, not in the mathematics of the chain rule.

### 3.2 Memory analysis and gradient checkpointing

Naive reverse-mode AD stores all $n$ intermediate values: memory $M = O(n)$, time $T = O(n)$. For long-horizon simulations (e.g., $10^5$ MD timesteps, $10^3$-step PDE rollouts), this is infeasible. **Gradient checkpointing** (also *rematerialization*) trades recomputation for memory: store only a subset of *checkpoints* and recompute forward segments during the backward pass [8].

> Theorem: (Binomial checkpointing, Griewank–Walther [8].) For a chain of $n$ steps with $c$ checkpoints, the optimal (binomial) schedule achieves memory $O(c)$ with bounded recomputation overhead; with $c = O(\log n)$ checkpoints, total time is $O(n \log n)$ and memory $O(\log n)$. With $c = \sqrt{n}$, memory is $O(\sqrt{n})$ at $O(1)$ amortized recomputation per step.

In practice:

- **PyTorch**: `torch.utils.checkpoint` wraps segments; recomputation happens under `torch.no_grad()` with RNG state stashing. Pitfalls: in-place ops and randomness inside checkpointed regions.
- **JAX**: `jax.checkpoint` (formerly `jax.remat`) is a *transformation* on the jaxpr — it rewrites the program so the backward pass recomputes. Because it operates at the IR level, it composes with `jit`/`vmap`/`grad` arbitrarily, and policy-based rematerialization (`saveable`/`offloadable` predicates) gives fine-grained control.

A useful mental model is the **memory–compute Pareto frontier**:

| Strategy | Memory | Time multiplier | Use case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Store-all | $O(n)$ | $1\times$ | Short horizons, debugging |
| $\sqrt{n}$ checkpoints | $O(\sqrt{n})$ | $\approx 2\times$ | Medium rollouts ($10^3$–$10^4$ steps) |
| Binomial ($\log n$) | $O(\log n)$ | $O(\log n)$ | Very long horizons |
| **Adjoint method** | $O(1)$ | $\approx 2$–$3\times$ (backward solve) | **ODE-constrained** problems only |

### 3.3 The adjoint sensitivity method for neural ODEs

Neural ODEs [5] parameterize a vector field with a neural network and define the model as the *solution* of an initial value problem:

$$ \frac{d\mathbf{h}(t)}{dt} = f(\mathbf{h}(t), t, \theta), \qquad \mathbf{h}(t_0) = \mathbf{h}_0, $$

with scalar loss $L(\mathbf{h}(t_1))$ on the terminal state. Differentiating *through the solver's internal steps* (discretize-then-optimize) costs $O(\text{steps})$ memory. The **adjoint sensitivity method** (optimize-then-discretize) instead defines the *adjoint state* $\mathbf{a}(t) = \partial L / \partial \mathbf{h}(t)$ and derives:

$$ \frac{d\mathbf{a}(t)}{dt} = -\mathbf{a}(t)^\top \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{h}}, \qquad \mathbf{a}(t_1) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}(t_1)}, $$

$$ \frac{dL}{d\theta} = -\int_{t_1}^{t_0} \mathbf{a}(t)^\top \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta} \, dt. $$

All three quantities — $\mathbf{h}(t)$, $\mathbf{a}(t)$, $dL/d\theta$ — are obtained by solving **one augmented ODE backward in time** from $t_1$ to $t_0$, requiring $O(1)$ memory regardless of the number of solver steps. The VJPs $\mathbf{a}^\top \partial f / \partial \mathbf{h}$ and $\mathbf{a}^\top \partial f / \partial \theta$ are exactly reverse-mode AD applied to a *single evaluation* of $f$ — the memory savings come from never materializing the solver's step history [5].

**Stability caveat** (the central practical issue): the backward ODE integrates $-\mathbf{a}^\top \partial f/\partial\mathbf{h}$, whose dynamics can be *stiff or unstable* even when the forward ODE is benign — particularly for dissipative systems run backward in time. Practical remedies include:

1. **Interpolated checkpointing (ANODE-style)**: store $\mathbf{h}(t)$ at coarse checkpoints and interpolate during the backward pass, bounding error accumulation.
2. **Symplectic/reversible solvers** for Hamiltonian structure.
3. **Discretize-then-optimize with binomial checkpointing** when the vector field is cheap — often more robust than the continuous adjoint.

Both JAX (`diffrax`) and PyTorch (`torchdiffeq`) implement adaptive-step Dormand–Prince solvers with adjoint support; `diffrax`'s design is notable for making the solver itself a pure function amenable to `jit`/`vmap`/`grad` composition [5].

### 3.4 PINN formulation

Physics-informed neural networks [6] solve PDEs by minimizing a composite loss over a neural ansatz $u_\theta(x, t)$:

$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \lambda_r \mathcal{L}_r + \lambda_b \mathcal{L}_b + \lambda_d \mathcal{L}_d, $$

where, for a PDE $\mathcal{N}[u] = 0$ on $\Omega$ with boundary operator $\mathcal{B}[u] = g$ on $\partial\Omega$,

- $\mathcal{L}_r = \frac{1}{N_r}\sum_i \|\mathcal{N}[u_\theta](x_i^r)\|^2$ (PDE residual, via AD through the network),
- $\mathcal{L}_b = \frac{1}{N_b}\sum_i \|\mathcal{B}[u_\theta](x_i^b) - g(x_i^b)\|^2$ (boundary/initial conditions),
- $\mathcal{L}_d$ is an optional data-misfit term.

The PDE residual requires *higher-order* AD through the network (e.g., $u_{xx}$ for diffusion), which is where JAX's composable `grad(grad(f))` and PyTorch's `create_graph=True` both get exercised heavily — and where their performance characteristics diverge most.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 JAX vs PyTorch autograd: a systems comparison

**Expressiveness of transformations.** JAX's `grad` is closed under composition in a way PyTorch's `backward()` is not. Consider per-example gradients, ubiquitous in scientific ML (per-particle forces, per-trajectory sensitivities):

```python
# JAX: per-example gradients by composing vmap and grad — no manual batching code
per_example_grads = jax.vmap(jax.grad(single_example_loss))(batch_params)
```

The PyTorch equivalent requires `torch.func.grad` + `torch.func.vmap` (the functorch lineage) or explicit looping — functional transforms retrofitted onto an imperative system. Conversely, PyTorch handles **dynamic, data-dependent** scientific codes (adaptive mesh refinement loops, event-driven MD with neighbor-list rebuilds conditioned on particle displacement) with zero refactoring, while JAX demands restructuring into `lax.while_loop`/`lax.scan` with static-shape discipline.

**Compilation and hardware.** `jax.jit` lowers the *entire* differentiated program to XLA/StableHLO, enabling cross-operation fusion (e.g., fusing the forward residual, its VJP, and the optimizer update into minimal kernels). `torch.compile` (TorchDynamo tracing + Inductor codegen) achieves similar fusion but must *guard and recompile* on dynamic shapes — a real cost in scientific codes with varying grid resolutions. Benchmarks on PDE-residual workloads (second-order AD through MLPs) consistently show JAX ahead by 1.5–3× on TPUs and roughly parity-to-better on GPUs, with the gap widening as the transformation stack deepens (`vmap(grad(jit(f)))`) [3].

**Mutation and state.** Scientific simulators are stateful: particle positions updated in place, grid buffers reused. PyTorch's in-place ops (`add_`, `copy_`) and `nn.Module` state dicts match this idiom. JAX requires functional state threading — libraries like Equinox (modules as pytrees) reduce the friction, but porting a legacy Fortran-style MD integrator to JAX is a genuine rewrite, whereas PyTorch can differentiate it almost line-by-line.

**Verdict.** For *greenfield* scientific ML with heavy higher-order AD, vectorized ensembles, and TPU targets, JAX's transform architecture is the stronger foundation. For *brownfield* differentiation of existing imperative simulators, interactive research, and dynamic control flow, PyTorch's define-by-run remains more productive. The field is converging: `torch.func` brings transforms to PyTorch; `torch.compile` brings whole-program compilation.

### 4.2 Neural ODEs and the adjoint method in practice

The adjoint method's $O(1)$ memory is theoretically optimal for ODE-constrained optimization, but three practical issues dominate:

1. **Backward stability.** As noted in §3.3, integrating the adjoint ODE backward through a dissipative forward system amplifies errors. For the learned vector fields typical in neural ODEs — which are *not* guaranteed contractive — the continuous adjoint can diverge where discretize-then-optimize with checkpointing succeeds. Empirical studies report that interpolated-adjoint variants recover most of the memory benefit with bounded error [5].
2. **Adaptive step counts.** `dopri5` with tight tolerances may take thousands of steps on stiff learned dynamics; the backward pass takes a *different* number of steps, so wall-clock is unpredictable. JAX's `diffrax` exposes step-count as a traced quantity, enabling `vmap` over ODE solves with per-example step counts — difficult in `torchdiffeq`.
3. **Stiffness.** Learned vector fields often become stiff during training (large Lipschitz constants), forcing implicit solvers. Differentiating through implicit solves needs implicit-function-theorem adjoints (differentiate the *fixed point*, not the Newton iterations) — supported by both `diffrax` (via `ImplicitAdjoint`) and recent `torchdiffeq` extensions.

A canonical application is **continuous normalizing flows** and **latent ODEs** for irregular time series, but the scientific-ML payoff is larger: neural ODEs serve as *differentiable surrogates* for expensive physics (e.g., learning a coarse-grained MD force field as a vector field, then differentiating through it for parameter inference).

### 4.3 PINN loss landscapes: why training fails and how to fix it

PINNs are notorious for failing on problems where classical solvers succeed — and the failure is now understood as **optimization pathology**, not lack of expressivity [7]:

- **Competing gradient scales.** $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_r$, $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_b$, $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_d$ routinely differ by orders of magnitude. The residual term dominates early; the network satisfies the PDE in the interior while ignoring boundary conditions — converging to a *wrong* solution with low total loss. Remedies: **adaptive loss weighting** (learning-rate annealing per term [7], NTK-based weighting), and curriculum sampling of collocation points.
- **Spectral bias.** MLPs learn low frequencies first; PDE solutions with shocks, boundary layers, or high-frequency modes (turbulence!) are learned last or never. Fourier feature embeddings and sinusoidal activations (SIREN) partially mitigate this — at the cost of harder optimization.
- **Stiffness and ill-conditioning.** The Hessian of $\mathcal{L}_r$ for second-order PDEs has condition numbers scaling with the fourth power of frequency content; first-order optimizers (Adam) stall. The modern recipe is **Adam warmup followed by L-BFGS** (exploiting the smooth, deterministic loss), which is standard in PINN libraries in both JAX and PyTorch.
- **Causality violation.** For time-dependent PDEs, PINNs minimize residuals at all times simultaneously, violating temporal causality; *causal training* (growing the time window as early-time residuals fall) restores it and dramatically improves wave-equation results.

> Theorem: (Informal, from NTK analysis of PINNs [7].) Under gradient flow, the convergence rate of each loss component is governed by the eigenvalues of its neural tangent kernel; components whose NTK spectra decay faster train slower. Disparate NTK spectra across $\mathcal{L}_r, \mathcal{L}_b$ imply that no single learning rate trains all terms efficiently — adaptive weighting is *necessary*, not optional.

Notably, **higher-order AD cost** makes PINNs a stress test for autograd systems: computing $u_{xx}$ for $10^5$ collocation points via nested `grad` is precisely where JAX's IR-level transformation (forward-over-reverse, `jacfwd(jacrev(f))`, giving better constant factors for Hessians) outperforms naive nested reverse-mode — a concrete, measurable consequence of the design philosophies in §2.

### 4.4 Differentiable simulators: Brax, PhiFlow, and differentiable MD

**Differentiable simulators** embed physics engines inside the AD framework so that gradients flow through *contact dynamics, fluid solves, and particle interactions*:

- **Brax** (Google) is a differentiable rigid-body physics engine written in JAX: spring-mass contacts, articulated bodies, and locomotion environments, all `jit`-compiled and `vmap`-ed over thousands of parallel environments. Differentiating through Brax enables *analytic policy gradients*: backpropagating through the physics rather than relying on score-function estimators, with reported order-of-magnitude sample-efficiency gains on locomotion tasks [9]. The JAX substrate is load-bearing here — `vmap` over environments and `grad` through contact dynamics compose only because the engine is a pure function.
- **PhiFlow** (Thuerey et al.) is a differentiable PDE framework (PyTorch, JAX, and TensorFlow backends) for fluid dynamics and differentiable physics research, exposing staggered grids, semi-Lagrangian advection, and pressure solves as differentiable operators. It underpins work on learned turbulence closures and differentiable fluid control [10].
- **Differentiable molecular dynamics**: frameworks such as JAX-MD (pure-function MD in JAX: neighbor lists, Lennard-Jones/Buckingham potentials, NVT integrators as transformations) differentiate through entire MD trajectories, enabling *differentiable coarse-graining* (learning CG potentials by matching fine-grained statistics via reparameterized gradients) and force-field fitting directly against experimental observables.

The shared pattern is **end-to-end differentiability through time integration**, which is exactly where §3.2's checkpointing theory and §3.3's adjoint methods become operational necessities: a $10^6$-step MD trajectory cannot be taped naively in either framework.

### 4.5 Applications: climate modeling and molecular dynamics

**Climate modeling.** General circulation models (GCMs) contain *parameterizations* — heuristic closures for subgrid processes (clouds, convection, turbulence) with dozens of tunable parameters traditionally fit by hand or via expensive ensemble Kalman methods. Differentiable programming offers a direct path: reimplement the dynamical core and parameterizations in JAX (e.g., the emerging generation of JAX-based GCMs), then **optimize parameterization parameters by gradient descent against observational or high-resolution reference data**. Because a climate rollout spans $10^4$–$10^6$ timesteps, this is feasible only with checkpointing/binomial schedules (§3.2) or adjoint formulations. Early results show learned closures correcting systematic biases (e.g., precipitation patterns) beyond the reach of manual tuning — though *stability of the coupled learned-physics system* over century-scale rollouts remains the central open challenge (a learned closure that is accurate for 10 days may blow up at 10 years).

**Molecular dynamics.** Classical force fields (AMBER, CHARMM) have hundreds of parameters fit to quantum-chemical data; **machine-learned interatomic potentials** (neural network potentials, message-passing architectures like NequIP/MACE) are trained on DFT energies and forces — where the *forces are themselves gradients* ($\mathbf{F} = -\nabla_{\mathbf{R}} E$), so training requires differentiating the network w.r.t. inputs (forward-over-reverse AD through message passing, a JAX-strong workload). Differentiable MD then closes the loop: simulate with the learned potential, differentiate *through the trajectory* to fit against dynamical observables (diffusion coefficients, radial distribution functions), correcting the *distribution shift* between static DFT training data and the dynamical ensemble the potential actually generates. This "differentiable simulation for force-field refinement" is among the most compelling demonstrations of the paradigm: gradients through $10^5$ integration steps, made tractable by checkpointing, yielding potentials stable over nanosecond simulations where naive fits fail within picoseconds.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Complexity summary

| Operation | Time | Memory (naive) | Memory (checkpointed/adjoint) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reverse-mode AD, $n$ ops | $\Theta(n)$ | $\Theta(n)$ | $\Theta(\sqrt{n})$ ($2\times$ time) |
| Hessian via nested reverse | $\Theta(n \cdot p)$ ($p$ params) | $\Theta(n \cdot p)$ | — |
| Hessian via forward-over-reverse | $\Theta(n \cdot p)$ time, better constants | $\Theta(n)$ | JAX `jacfwd(jacrev(f))` |
| Neural ODE gradient, $S$ steps | $\Theta(S)$ | $\Theta(S)$ (discretize-then-optimize) | **$\Theta(1)$** (adjoint) |
| PINN residual, $N$ points, 2nd order | $\Theta(N \cdot w^2)$ ($w$ width) | $\Theta(N \cdot w)$ | — |
| Differentiable MD, $T$ steps, $N$ atoms | $\Theta(T \cdot N \log N)$ | $\Theta(T \cdot N)$ | $\Theta(\sqrt{T} \cdot N)$ |

### 5.2 Reported empirical scalings (literature)

1. **Neural ODEs** [5]: on MNIST-scale dynamics, the adjoint method reduces memory from $O(S)$ to $O(1)$ with a 2–3× wall-clock overhead versus backpropagating through the solver; adaptive-step solves use 10–100× fewer function evaluations than fixed-step residuals of comparable accuracy.
2. **PINNs** [6][7]: adaptive-weighting schemes (learning-rate annealing) reduce relative $L^2$ error on stiff problems (e.g., Allen–Cahn, Navier–Stokes at moderate Re) by 1–2 orders of magnitude versus fixed weights; Adam+L-BFGS hybrid optimization is the consistent best practice across benchmarks.
3. **Brax** [9]: `vmap`-parallel rollouts achieve near-linear scaling to thousands of environments on a single accelerator; analytic policy gradients through differentiable contacts improve sample efficiency by roughly an order of magnitude over PPO on locomotion benchmarks.
4. **JAX vs PyTorch on higher-order AD**: forward-over-reverse Hessian computation (`jacfwd(jacrev(f))`) in JAX is consistently reported 2–5× faster than nested `create_graph=True` reverse mode in PyTorch for MLP-based physics residuals, with the gap growing in parameter count — a direct consequence of transformation-level versus tape-level AD.
5. **Differentiable climate closures**: JAX-GCM studies report that gradient-tuned subgrid parameterizations reduce short-horizon forecast error measurably versus hand-tuned baselines; long-horizon stability (the "climate drift" problem) remains partially unsolved.

### 5.3 Formal analysis: when does the adjoint win?

> Theorem: (Adjoint vs. checkpointing selection criterion.) For an ODE solve with $S$ steps, per-step cost $c$, and state dimension $d$: discretize-then-optimize with $\sqrt{S}$ checkpoints costs $\approx 2Sc$ time and $O(\sqrt{S}\,d)$ memory; the continuous adjoint costs $\approx (2$–$3)Sc$ time (forward + backward solves, each adaptive) and $O(d)$ memory. The adjoint strictly dominates when $\sqrt{S}\,d$ exceeds device memory; checkpointing dominates when the backward ODE is stiff/unstable or when exact gradients of the *discretized* objective are required (e.g., for bilevel optimization through the solver).

A similar criterion governs **PINN vs. classical solver** selection: PINNs are competitive for high-dimensional, parametric, or inverse problems (where mesh-based methods suffer the curse of dimensionality), and uncompetitive for low-dimensional forward problems where spectral/finite-element solvers achieve machine precision in milliseconds — a comparison the PINN literature sometimes elides.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Tracing rigidity (JAX).** Static shapes, no data-dependent control flow under `jit`, and functional-state discipline remain genuine obstacles for legacy scientific codes; `lax` control flow is expressive but alien to Fortran/C++-trained practitioners, and debugging traced programs ("tracer leaks," concretization errors) has a steep learning curve.
2. **Eager overhead and graph brittleness (PyTorch).** Per-op dispatch overhead taxes fine-grained scientific kernels; `torch.compile` mitigates this but introduces guard-based recompilation cliffs on dynamic shapes common in AMR and particle codes.
3. **Adjoint instability.** The continuous adjoint method can diverge on dissipative or stiff learned dynamics; it is not a drop-in replacement for checkpointed discretize-then-optimize, and diagnosing backward-solve divergence is difficult.
4. **PINN reliability.** Despite progress on loss weighting and causality, PINNs still fail unpredictably on stiff, multiscale, and high-Reynolds-number flows; error bounds are essentially nonexistent, unlike classical solvers with *a priori* estimates. They should not be presented as replacements for mature PDE solvers in safety-critical forward simulation.
5. **Differentiable-simulator bias.** Gradients through contact-rich or chaotic physics are noisy and can be *misleading* (chaotic divergence makes long-horizon gradients explode; contact discontinuities make them biased). Truncated backpropagation through time and stochastic smoothing are workarounds, not cures.
6. **Climate-scale cost.** End-to-end differentiation through GCM rollouts at operational resolution remains beyond current hardware; published successes use coarse resolutions or short windows, and century-scale stability of learned closures is unproven.
7. **Reproducibility.** Adaptive-step solvers, XLA fusion nondeterminism, and chaotic gradient blowup make exact reproduction of differentiable-simulation results difficult; the field needs standardized benchmarks that do not yet exist.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Differentiable programming has earned its place as the computational backbone of scientific machine learning, and its two great implementations — **JAX's transform-based functional autograd** and **PyTorch's dynamic define-by-run AD** — are better understood as points in a design space than as rivals. JAX's tracing, pytrees, and composable `grad`/`jit`/`vmap` transformations deliver unmatched composition of higher-order derivatives and hardware-efficient whole-program compilation, at the cost of a purity discipline that strains legacy scientific codes. PyTorch's imperative tape differentiates existing simulators almost line-by-line and handles dynamic control flow naturally, at the cost of heavier higher-order AD and later-arriving compilation.

The mathematical core — reverse-mode AD as adjoint propagation, with memory tamed by checkpointing theory and, for ODE-constrained problems, by the adjoint sensitivity method — is framework-independent. Its payoff is visible in PINNs (whose failures are now legible as optimization pathologies with known remedies), in differentiable simulators like Brax and PhiFlow (which turn physics engines into learnable layers), and in frontier applications: gradient-tuned climate parameterizations and force fields refined by differentiating through molecular dynamics trajectories.

The open problems are substantial: stability of learned closures over climatic timescales, reliable gradients through chaos and contact, error certification for neural PDE solvers, and benchmarks the field can agree on. But the trajectory is clear — *the simulator is becoming a differentiable layer*, and scientific computing will not go back.
