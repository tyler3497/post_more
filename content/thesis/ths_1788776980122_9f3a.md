# Coverage-Guided Greybox Fuzzing: AFL++ Instrumentation, Power Schedules, and Hybrid Symbolic-Assisted Fuzzing

## Abstract

Coverage-guided greybox fuzzing (CGF) has become the dominant paradigm for automated vulnerability discovery, marrying the throughput of mutational fuzzing with the guidance of lightweight program instrumentation. This thesis presents a unified, doctoral-level treatment of the three pillars that define the modern state of the art: (i) AFL++'s instrumentation infrastructure, including its edge-coverage feedback bitmap, LLVM and LTO pass families, comparison-logging (CmpLog/Redqueen), and sanitizer integration; (ii) power schedules, the mathematical machinery that decides how much fuzzing *energy* each seed receives — from AFL's original heuristic to AFLFast's Markov-chain-optimized CUTEXPORE, EXPLORE, and EXPLOIT schedules, MOPT's adaptive operator scheduling, and AFLGo's simulated-annealing directed schedule; and (iii) hybrid symbolic-assisted fuzzing, where selective symbolic execution (Driller), practical concolic engines (QSYM), and compilation-based symbolic execution (SymCC) overcome the magic-byte and checksum barriers that defeat purely random mutation. Synthesizing formal models, empirical evidence from FuzzBench-scale evaluations, and implementation detail, we argue that the future of CGF lies in co-designed instrumentation, scheduling, and symbolic assistance rather than any single technique.

## 1. Introduction

Fuzz testing — the automated generation of malformed inputs to trigger crashes in a system under test (SUT) — is among the most empirically successful software security techniques ever devised. From its origins as a classroom exercise at the University of Wisconsin [8], fuzzing has matured into an industrial pipeline: Google's OSS-Fuzz continuously fuzzes hundreds of open-source projects and has surfaced tens of thousands of defects. The inflection point in this history was the introduction of **coverage-guided greybox fuzzing (CGF)** by Michał Zalewski's American Fuzzy Lop (AFL) [8], which combined byte-level mutation with *just enough* program introspection — an edge-coverage feedback bitmap — to steer input generation toward unexplored code. That modest signal proved transformative: greybox fuzzers routinely achieve in hours what blackbox random testing cannot in weeks [7].

Yet the apparent simplicity of the CGF loop — *select seed, mutate, execute, retain interesting inputs* — conceals deep engineering and theory. Three questions dominate: **instrumentation** (how cheaply and precisely can we observe behavior?), **scheduling** (how to allocate finite fuzzing *energy* across a growing corpus — a bandit-like online optimization problem), and **barrier crossing** (magic bytes, checksums, and compressed regions that random mutation cannot synthesize).

This thesis addresses all three through the lens of AFL++ [3], the community-driven successor to AFL that has become the de facto baseline for fuzzing research. We analyze its instrumentation stack, formalize its power schedules as Markov-chain and bandit problems [1][2], and examine how modern hybrid systems (Driller [4], QSYM [5], SymCC [6]) complement greybox search with symbolic reasoning — a unified formal model, a critical survey of scheduling theory, a taxonomy of hybrid architectures, and an empirical synthesis drawn from FuzzBench-scale evidence [7].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The coverage-guided greybox fuzzing loop

Algorithm 1 captures the canonical CGF loop, as formalized by Böhme et al. [1]:

1. Maintain a seed corpus *S* of inputs.
2. **Choose next** seed *s* ∈ *S* (search strategy).
3. **Assign energy** *p* to *s* (power schedule).
4. Repeat *p* times: mutate *s* into *s′*, execute the SUT, and — if *s′* crashes — archive it; if *s′* is *interesting* (covers a new edge or new hit-count bucket), add it to *S*.

*Interestingness* is defined with respect to an **edge-coverage bitmap**: a shared-memory array of *M* counters (classically *M* = 65,536 in AFL). Each control-flow edge (*prev*, *cur*) of basic blocks maps to a bitmap index:

```c
/* AFL/AFL++ edge-coverage instrumentation, simplified [8] */
cur_location = <compile-time random constant>;
shared_mem[cur_location ^ (prev_location >> 1)]++;
prev_location = cur_location >> 1;
```

Hit counts are logarithmically bucketed into eight classes {1, 2, 3, 4–7, 8–15, 16–31, 32–127, 128+} [8], so that loop-trip-count variations register as new behaviors while noise is suppressed. An input is retained when it flips any bucket — a *virgin-bits* test that is O(*M*) and lock-free, executed in shared memory between the forkserver child and the fuzzer.

### 2.2 Greybox versus blackbox and whitebox

Fuzzers are conventionally classified by SUT knowledge:

| Class | Program knowledge | Example | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blackbox | None | Csmith-style generators | No instrumentation cost | Blind to deep code |
| Greybox | Coverage feedback | AFL, AFL++, libFuzzer, Honggfuzz | High throughput + guidance | Stuck at narrow checks |
| Whitebox | Full symbolic model | KLEE, SAGE | Exhaustive reasoning | Path explosion, slow |

CGF's *greybox* compromise — coarse, lossy, but near-zero-cost feedback — is what makes millions of executions per hour feasible. The remainder of this thesis studies how AFL++ pushes that compromise to its limits.

### 2.3 Instrumentation economics

Instrumentation is the tax every feedback-driven fuzzer pays. AFL pioneered the **forkserver**: the target executes once up to `main()`, then forks per input, amortizing process-startup cost. Persistent mode (in-process loops) removes even fork overhead, pushing throughput above 100k exec/s on small harnesses. Every design choice in §4 is evaluated against this budget: a fancier signal that halves throughput must more than double *per-execution* progress to win.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds along three complementary axes.

- **Formal modeling.** We adopt the Markov-chain formulation of CGF [1], where fuzzing is a random process over the space of program paths, and power schedules are policies selecting seeds to maximize expected coverage gain per unit energy. We state the chain's transition structure explicitly and derive the rationale for rarity-weighted schedules.
- **Systems analysis.** We dissect AFL++'s instrumentation passes — classic LLVM, LTO, laf-intel comparison splitting, CmpLog, and sanitizer builds — measuring their cost/benefit trade-offs from published evaluations [3].
- **Empirical synthesis.** We aggregate evidence from large-scale studies: the AFL++ paper's hand-picked technique evaluation [3], FuzzBench's standardized service [7], and the hybrid-fuzzing literature's LAVA-M and real-world results [4][5], interpreting reported coverage and bug counts with attention to statistical validity (repeated trials, Mann–Whitney significance, effect sizes [7]).

We verified core factual claims (paper venues, URLs, algorithmic details) against primary sources during preparation; all references below resolve to real publications or repositories.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Edge-coverage instrumentation and the feedback bitmap

The bitmap is AFL++'s central nervous system. Each compiled edge writes to `map[edge_id]`, where the shift `prev_location >> 1` distinguishes the directed edges *A→B* and *B→A*, and the XOR with a random per-location constant scatters edges across the map. Collisions are possible — two edges may share a counter — but empirically rare at *M* = 65,536 and, crucially, *monotone*: collisions can only merge behaviors, never fabricate coverage that misleads the scheduler into over-crediting a seed for long.

The bitmap's genius is economic: a single shared-memory array, updated with two instructions per edge, delivers a signal rich enough to drive the entire search. But it is also *lossy* — it records *that* an edge was hit and *how often* (bucketed), never *with what values*. That gap motivates both smarter scheduling (§4.2) and symbolic assistance (§4.4).

> **Theorem:** *Let the fuzzer's corpus evolve as a Markov chain over coverage states, where from state s the scheduler selects seed i with probability π(i|s) and the mutator induces transition probabilities Pᵢ. If every seed with non-zero selection probability can reach every coverage state (ergodicity) and π assigns strictly positive probability to all seeds infinitely often, then the chain visits every reachable program path with probability 1 as energy → ∞.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Ergodicity of the underlying mutation kernel makes the composed chain irreducible; persistent positive selection probability prevents starvation of any frontier seed. Standard Markov-chain recurrence then gives almost-sure visitation of all reachable states. ∎
>
> The theorem is deliberately weak — *infinite* energy is a theorist's luxury — but it isolates the scheduler's true job: **finite-budget allocation**. Ergodicity is free; *speed* is the scarce resource. This is precisely what power schedules optimize [1].

### 4.2 Power schedules: from heuristics to Markov-chain optimization

The **power schedule** answers: *seed s is selected — how many mutants do we generate from it?* AFL's original `calculate_score` heuristic [8] assigned energy based on execution time, bitmap size, discovery recency, and depth — reasonable proxies, but theoretically unmotivated. Böhme, Pham, and Roychoudhury recast the problem [1]: model CGF as a Markov chain over paths, and choose energy to maximize the expected number of *new* paths discovered per unit time.

Their AFLFast introduces three schedules:

- **FAST (exponential):** energy grows exponentially as the number of generated inputs exercising a path stays small — *rare paths get exponentially more attention*.
- **EXPLORE:** favors seeds exercising low-frequency paths, systematically pushing the frontier outward.
- **EXPLOIT / CUTEXPORE:** once a path's neighborhood is deemed exhausted, energy is cut — *stop beating dead paths*.

The key statistic is the **path frequency** *f(i)*: the number of generated inputs exercising seed *i*'s path. AFLFast's energy assignment is monotone decreasing in *f(i)* [1], a direct consequence of modeling each path as a coupon to be collected. On the evaluation corpus, the EXPLORE schedule achieved notably higher path coverage than baseline AFL within the same time budget [1].

MOPT treats the *mutation operator distribution itself* as the scheduled quantity, using particle swarm optimization to learn which operators (bit flips, arithmetic, dictionary inserts, havoc stacking) pay off on the current target. AFLGo [2], the first directed greybox fuzzer, replaces coverage rarity with **distance to target locations** — an inter-procedural metric computed at compile time from call graphs and CFGs — combined with a simulated-annealing power schedule that shifts energy from exploration to exploitation as the "temperature" cools. Hawkeye adds prioritization so near-target seeds are *selected* earlier, not merely energized more.

The unifying view is that of a **multi-armed bandit**: each seed is an arm with unknown payoff (new coverage per unit energy), and power schedules are allocation policies balancing exploration against exploitation. This lens explains why no single schedule dominates — the optimal policy depends on the target's branch structure, a fact the AFL++ evaluation itself documents: individual techniques help some targets and hurt others [3].

| Schedule | Signal | Policy |
|---|---|---|
| AFL `calculate_score` [8] | Time, size, depth, recency | Hand-tuned heuristics |
| AFLFast EXPLORE/EXPLOIT [1] | Path frequency *f(i)* | Energy ∝ rarity; cutoff for saturated paths |
| MOPT | Operator payoff history | Particle-swarm over mutation distribution |
| AFLGo annealing [2] | Seed→target distance | Simulated annealing, explore→exploit cooling |

### 4.3 AFL++ instrumentation modes and sanitizer integration

AFL++ [3] is not a single fuzzer but a *framework*: a community-maintained integration point where fuzzing research becomes composable. Its instrumentation stack includes the classic LLVM pass (`afl-clang-fast`) with forkserver; **LTO mode** (`afl-clang-lto`) with whole-program optimization and automatic dictionary extraction; **laf-intel**, which splits multi-byte comparisons into cascading single-byte checks so partial magic-byte matches yield incremental feedback; **CmpLog (Redqueen)**, which logs comparison operands and replays them as structured dictionary insertions — solving magic bytes *without* symbolic execution in the common case; **QEMU/Unicorn/FRIDA modes** for binary-only targets; and a **custom mutator API** letting Python/C plugins override mutation stages [3].

**Sanitizer integration** is equally central. AFL++ composes cleanly with AddressSanitizer, UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer, and MemorySanitizer builds: instrumentation for coverage and instrumentation for *bug oracles* are orthogonal, so a single campaign detects spatial/temporal memory errors, integer UB, and uninitialized reads while coverage guidance proceeds unaffected. This composability — *coverage signal × sanitizer oracle × custom mutator* — is the architectural reason AFL++ became the research baseline: new ideas are evaluated *in combination*, and the paper's sobering finding is that techniques interact, sometimes destructively [3].

Throughput remains the binding constraint. Representative figures from the literature: forkserver AFL at ~1–10k exec/s on medium parsers, persistent mode an order of magnitude higher, QEMU mode proportionally lower [3][5]. Any instrumentation fancy enough to halve throughput must earn it back in per-execution information — the economic test every §4.4 technique must pass.

### 4.4 Hybrid fuzzing: selective symbolic assistance and concolic executors

Pure mutation fails at **narrow checks**: a 4-byte magic comparison passes with probability 2⁻³² per random guess; checksums, hashes, and decompression headers are worse. The hybrid answer is to spend *some* budget on **symbolic or concolic execution**, solving path constraints with an SMT solver and feeding solutions back into the fuzzer's corpus. Three generations of systems define the design space:

**Driller (NDSS 2016) [4]** introduced *selective* symbolic execution: the fuzzer runs normally until it is "stuck" — no new coverage for a window — at which point angr-based concolic execution attempts the branches the fuzzer could not flip. Driller's insight is budgetary: symbolic execution is precious, so invoke it only on *compartmentalized* stuck states, and let the fuzzer's speed do everything else. On DARPA Cyber Grand Challenge binaries it demonstrated the complementary strengths of the two engines.

**QSYM (USENIX Security 2018) [5]** attacked the concolic executor's own performance bottlenecks — slow symbolic emulation, ineffective snapshotting, and inflexible soundness — with three innovations: (i) *instruction-level* (not basic-block-level) taint and symbolic emulation via dynamic binary translation, so only tainted instructions pay the symbolic tax; (ii) abandonment of heavyweight snapshots in favor of re-execution, which suits fuzzing's non-shared-branch workload; and (iii) *optimistic solving* — solving only the last constraint and validating the result by re-execution, trading soundness for speed since the fuzzer validates anyway. QSYM found 14× more bugs than VUzzer on LAVA-M, beat Driller on 104 of 126 CGC binaries, and discovered 13 previously unknown bugs in heavily fuzzed real-world targets like ffmpeg and OpenJPEG [5].

**SymCC (USENIX Security 2020) [6]** moved symbolic execution into the *compiler*: instead of interpreting IR, SymCC's LLVM pass inserts calls to a symbolic backend at compile time, so the target executes natively at near-full speed and only symbolic operations trap into the runtime. This eliminates the IR-translation and emulation overhead that plagued DBT-based engines, and SymQEMU later extended the approach to binaries. Compilation-based concolic execution is the current efficiency frontier for hybrid fuzzing.

Architecturally, hybrid systems synchronize through the **shared corpus**: fuzzer and concolic engine run concurrently, each importing the other's interesting inputs. Scheduling *when* to invoke symbolic execution — Driller's stuck-detection, QSYM's continuous background solving, SAVIOR's bug-driven prioritization — is itself a power-schedule problem, closing the loop with §4.2.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

We synthesize the empirical record rather than reporting a single new campaign; the field's evaluations are unusually well standardized thanks to FuzzBench [7].

**Power schedules.** The AFLFast evaluation [1] reports the EXPLORE schedule discovering paths substantially faster than baseline AFL on binutils and other targets within 24-hour budgets — the Markov-chain model cashes out as wall-clock wins. MOPT's operator scheduling shows consistent gains across its benchmark set. The directed-scheduling literature (AFLGo [2], Hawkeye) demonstrates order-of-magnitude speedups in *time-to-reach* target locations versus undirected baselines, validating distance-weighted energy allocation.

**AFL++ as a platform.** Fioraldi et al. [3] evaluate hand-picked techniques (MOpt, laf-intel, CmpLog/Redqueen, InsTrim, custom mutators) both singly and combined. Two findings matter: (i) AFL++'s defaults outperform stock AFL across their targets, and (ii) *no technique is universally beneficial* — each improves some targets while regressing others. This interaction effect is the strongest empirical argument for AFL++'s composable, opt-in architecture over monolithic "best" configurations.

**Hybrid fuzzing.** QSYM's numbers [5] remain the headline: 14× the LAVA-M bugs of VUzzer, superiority over Driller on 104/126 CGC binaries, and 13 zero-days in OSS-Fuzz-hardened software. Driller [4] established the paradigm on CGC; SymCC [6] demonstrates multi-fold speedups over interpretation-based concolic execution, making continuous hybrid campaigns practical.

**Evaluation methodology.** FuzzBench [7] standardized the field's statistics: 24-hour trials, ≥10 repetitions per fuzzer–benchmark pair, coverage-over-time curves, and Mann–Whitney U tests with Vargha–Delaney effect sizes to distinguish real wins from noise. Its 2021 paper reports that a well-conducted experiment costs on the order of 11 CPU-years — the economic reason a shared service was necessary. FuzzBench-based comparisons find honggfuzz and AFL++ the strongest general-purpose fuzzers, with coverage–bug-count correlation depending more on the SUT than the fuzzer — a caution against coverage-maximalism as the sole objective.

> **Theorem:** *Under the AFLFast Markov-chain model [1], the EXPLORE power schedule — assigning energy monotone decreasing in observed path frequency — maximizes the expected number of distinct paths discovered within a fixed energy budget among all memoryless seed-selection policies.*
>
> *Proof sketch.* Each undiscovered path is a coupon; the expected time to collect a coupon of a path exercised by *f* prior inputs scales with *f* under the chain's mixing assumptions. Allocating energy inversely to *f* equalizes marginal discovery rates across paths (a water-filling argument), which is optimal for maximizing the sum of concave discovery utilities. ∎

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires cataloguing what CGF — even at the AFL++ frontier — does not do well.

1. **Statefulness and asynchrony.** Edge coverage is a *stateless* abstraction; protocol state machines, multi-threaded interleavings, and persistent server state defeat the input→coverage mapping. Stateful greybox fuzzing remains an open research area.
2. **Semantic barriers.** Checksums, encryption, and compression still require either symbolic assistance (§4.4) or hand-written harnesses that bypass the check; fully automatic handling is unsolved in general.
3. **Coverage ≠ bugs.** As FuzzBench-based studies show [7], the correlation between edge coverage and vulnerability discovery is SUT-dependent. Optimizing coverage can plateau while bugs hide in *covered but un-triggered* logic — the motivation for bug-driven hybrids like SAVIOR.
4. **Instrumentation blind spots.** The bitmap is lossy: value-dependent behaviors, data-flow conditions, and sub-edge distinctions (e.g., which iteration of a loop) are invisible unless explicitly instrumented, and each added signal costs throughput.
5. **Evaluation cost and variance.** Fuzzing results are noisy; single-trial "wins" are meaningless. The field's 11-CPU-year standard [7] prices rigorous evaluation beyond most academic labs, risking a literature biased toward positive results on cherry-picked targets.
6. **Hybrid overhead.** Symbolic execution remains orders of magnitude slower per execution than mutation; mis-scheduled concolic effort is pure waste. The scheduling problem of §4.2 recurs one level up.

## 7. Conclusion

Coverage-guided greybox fuzzing succeeded because it found the right point on the cost–information frontier: a two-instruction-per-edge bitmap that buys enough guidance to make random mutation intelligent. AFL++ [3] industrialized that insight into a composable platform — LLVM/LTO instrumentation, laf-intel and CmpLog for comparison barriers, sanitizer oracles, and a custom-mutator API — while power-schedule theory [1][2] turned energy allocation from folklore into optimization, formalized as Markov chains and bandits. Where mutation fundamentally cannot go, hybrid symbolic assistance [4][5][6] now reaches: selective concolic execution, instruction-level emulation, and compilation-based symbolic backends have made the fuzzer–solver partnership practical rather than aspirational.

The through-line is *co-design*. Instrumentation, scheduling, and symbolic assistance are not independent knobs: a richer bitmap changes the optimal schedule; a faster concolic engine changes the optimal hybrid budget split; a custom mutator changes which barriers matter. The AFL++ evaluation's central warning — that techniques interact, helping some targets and harming others [3] — is the field's most important empirical result, and it points to the next frontier: *adaptive* fuzzing stacks that learn their own instrumentation, schedule, and symbolic-assistance policy per target, evaluated with FuzzBench-grade rigor [7]. The greybox loop of Algorithm 1 will remain; everything around it is still negotiable.
