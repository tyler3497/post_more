# Secure Multiparty Computation: Yao's Garbled Circuits, the GMW Paradigm, and the SPDZ Protocol Family under Malicious Adversaries

## Abstract

Secure multiparty computation (MPC) allows *n* mutually distrustful parties to jointly evaluate a function *f* over their private inputs while revealing nothing beyond what the output itself implies. This thesis traces the three foundational constructions that define the field's theoretical and practical landscape. We begin with **Yao's garbled circuits** (1986): a constant-round two-party protocol in which one party encrypts a Boolean circuit's truth tables under random wire labels, transferring evaluator labels through oblivious transfer. We then examine the **Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson (GMW) paradigm** (1987), which replaces encryption with XOR secret sharing and compiles semi-honest protocols into maliciously secure ones via zero-knowledge proofs. Finally, we develop the **SPDZ protocol family** (Damgård et al., 2012): an offline/online architecture achieving active security against a *dishonest majority* through authenticated Beaver triples built from somewhat homomorphic encryption. We compare asymptotic and concrete costs, formalize simulation-based security under semi-honest and malicious adversaries, and identify the remaining barriers to large-scale deployment.

## 1. Introduction

The canonical formulation of secure multiparty computation asks whether *n* parties, each holding a private input *xᵢ*, can compute a joint function *f(x₁, …, xₙ)* without a trusted third party and without leaking any additional information [2][9]. The problem was posed in full generality by Yao in 1982 for the two-party case [9], extended to arbitrary *n* by Goldreich, Micali, and Wigderson [3], and has since become one of the central pillars of modern cryptography, with applications ranging from private auctions and electronic voting to privacy-preserving machine learning and confidential smart contracts.

Three conceptual milestones organize the history of the field, and each introduces a fundamentally different mechanism:

1. **Yao's garbled circuits** encrypt the *function itself*, turning computation into decryption [1][4].
2. **The GMW paradigm** encrypts the *inputs* via secret sharing and evaluates the function gate by gate, elevating a semi-honest protocol to malicious security through a general compiler [3].
3. **The SPDZ family** splits computation into an *input-independent preprocessing phase* and a *cheap online phase*, using information-theoretic message authentication codes (MACs) to defeat active adversaries even when all but one party are corrupt [5].

These three ideas are not merely historical curiosities: every deployed MPC system today is a descendant of one of them, and understanding their security models, cost profiles, and failure modes is prerequisite to engineering any real system.

> **Theorem:** *Secure computation is possible for any efficiently computable function, in the presence of any number of malicious corruptions, under the sole assumption that oblivious transfer exists* [3][10].

The remainder of this thesis formalizes the adversary models (Section 2), develops the three constructions in detail (Section 3), analyzes their malicious-security upgrades (Section 4), compares their concrete and asymptotic performance (Section 5), and discusses limitations (Section 6).

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Adversary models

Cryptographic security is always relative to an *adversary model*. In MPC the taxonomy has two orthogonal axes: **behavior** and **corruption threshold**.

*Behavior.* A **semi-honest** (honest-but-curious, passive) adversary follows the protocol specification exactly but records all messages it receives, attempting to infer honest parties' inputs from its view [4]. A **malicious** (active) adversary may deviate arbitrarily: sending malformed messages, aborting, or injecting chosen values. Between them lies the **covert** adversary, who cheats only if the probability of detection is negligible [6].

*Corruption threshold.* An **honest-majority** protocol tolerates *t < n/2* corruptions (or *t < n/3* for information-theoretic security with guaranteed output delivery); a **dishonest-majority** protocol tolerates *t < n* corruptions — every party but one may be adversarial [5]. Dishonest-majority protocols necessarily sacrifice *guaranteed output delivery* and *fairness*: a corrupt majority can always abort after learning the output, so the standard guarantee is **security with abort** [3][8].

### 2.2 Oblivious transfer

**Oblivious transfer (OT)** is the minimal complete primitive for secure computation [10]. In 1-out-of-2 OT, a sender holds messages *(m₀, m₁)* and a receiver holds a choice bit *b*; the receiver learns *m_b* while the sender learns nothing about *b*, and the receiver learns nothing about *m_{1−b}*. Kilian proved that OT is *complete*: given OT, any functionality can be securely computed [10].

The practical bottleneck is that MPC protocols need *millions* of OTs (one per input wire in garbled circuits; one per AND gate in GMW-style multiplication). **OT extension** [7] reduces this to a small number of *base OTs* plus symmetric cryptography: from *k* base OTs (where *k* is the security parameter, e.g. 128), parties derive an unbounded number of OTs at the cost of a few hash evaluations each [7]. Without OT extension, no modern MPC implementation would be practical.

### 2.3 Secret sharing

**XOR (additive) secret sharing** over 𝔽₂ splits a bit *x* into *n* shares *x₁, …, xₙ* with *x = x₁ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ xₙ*; any *n − 1* shares are uniformly random and reveal nothing [8]. **Shamir secret sharing** embeds the secret as the constant term of a random degree-*t* polynomial; any *t + 1* shares reconstruct it, while *t* or fewer reveal nothing [9]. XOR sharing underlies GMW and SPDZ; Shamir sharing underlies the Ben-Or–Goldwasser–Wigderson (BGW) protocol for honest majorities [9].

### 2.4 The simulation paradigm and universal composability

Modern security proofs use the **real/ideal paradigm**: a protocol securely realizes a functionality *f* if for every real-world adversary there exists an **ideal-world simulator** that, interacting only with a trusted party computing *f*, produces a view indistinguishable from the real execution [3][4]. The **universal composability (UC)** framework strengthens this to security under arbitrary concurrent composition [8]. All three constructions in this thesis admit simulation-based proofs, though the simulators differ radically: Yao's simulator forges a *fake garbled circuit* consistent with the output alone, while SPDZ's simulator *extracts* the adversary's inputs using the MAC key [4][5].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Yao's garbled circuits with point-and-permute

Yao's protocol evaluates a Boolean circuit *C* computing *f* between a **garbler** *P₁* and an **evaluator** *P₂* [1][4]. For each wire *w*, the garbler samples two random labels *k_w⁰, k_w¹ ∈ {0,1}^λ*. For a gate *g* with input wires *a, b* and output wire *c*, the garbler produces four ciphertexts — one per row of the truth table:

```
γ[i,j] = Enc_{k_a^i}( Enc_{k_b^j}( k_c^{g(i,j)} ) ),   i, j ∈ {0,1}
```

The four ciphertexts are **randomly permuted** so their order leaks nothing about the gate's truth table; the permuted table is the *garbled gate*. The garbler sends the full garbled circuit plus the labels matching its own input bits directly, and the evaluator obtains its labels via 1-out-of-2 OT, one per input bit [1][4]. Evaluation proceeds gate by gate: possessing exactly one label per input wire, the evaluator decrypts exactly one row per gate — but cannot tell *which* row, and hence learns nothing about intermediate wire values. Finally, the garbler publishes the output-wire decoding table mapping *k_out⁰, k_out¹* to bits.

The **point-and-permute** optimization (Beaver, Micali, Rogaway) appends a random *permute bit* *p_w* to each wire's labels, so the evaluator knows *which* of the four ciphertexts to decrypt without trial decryption, cutting evaluation cost by a factor of four [4]. **Free XOR** (Kolesnikov–Schneider) sets *k_w¹ = k_w⁰ ⊕ Δ* for a global secret Δ, making XOR gates cost *zero* ciphertexts; **half gates** (Zahur–Rosulek–Evans) garble each AND gate with just two ciphertexts [8]. Together these reduce garbled AES (6,800 AND gates) to roughly 27 KB of garbled material, evaluable in milliseconds [8].

### 3.2 The GMW paradigm with OT extension

The GMW protocol represents the function as a Boolean circuit but evaluates it over **XOR-shared values** rather than encrypted ones [3]. Each party *Pᵢ* holds input *xᵢ* and the parties maintain the invariant that every wire value *v* is shared as *v = v₁ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ vₙ*. XOR gates are evaluated *locally* (each party XORs its shares — no communication). AND gates require interaction: for shared inputs *⟦x⟧, ⟦y⟧*, parties consume a precomputed **Beaver triple** *⟦a⟧, ⟦b⟧, ⟦c⟧* with *c = a·b* [11], locally compute shares of *⟦d⟧ = ⟦x⟧ − ⟦a⟧* and *⟦e⟧ = ⟦y⟧ − ⟦b⟧*, *open* *d* and *e* (one communication round), and reconstruct:

```
⟦xy⟧ = de ⊕ d·⟦b⟧ ⊕ e·⟦a⟧ ⊕ ⟦c⟧
```

Opening *d* and *e* is safe because *a, b* are uniformly random masks. The semi-honest GMW protocol is thus reduced to a triple-generation problem, solved in an **offline phase** using OT — one OT per triple — made practical by OT extension [7].

The GMW *paradigm* proper is the **compiler**: starting from any semi-honest protocol, each party commits to its input and randomness, and attaches a **zero-knowledge proof** to every message attesting that it was computed correctly from committed values [3]. This generic compiler yields malicious security but was considered hopelessly inefficient for decades; only recently have concretely efficient instantiations (e.g., via cut-and-choose and authenticated triples) revived it [8].

### 3.3 SPDZ: preprocessing with SHE and information-theoretic MACs

SPDZ (Damgård, Pastro, Smart, Zakarias, CRYPTO 2012) achieves **active security against a dishonest majority** — the strongest standard model — with an online phase whose cost rivals the semi-honest setting [5]. The key device is an **information-theoretic MAC** on every shared value. A global MAC key *α ∈ 𝔽_p* is itself secret-shared (*⟦α⟧*), never reconstructed. A value *x* is represented as:

```
⟨x⟩ = ( ⟦x⟧ , ⟦m⟧ )   where   m = α · x
```

Addition is local; multiplication consumes a Beaver triple whose components carry valid MACs. Because *α* is unknown to any proper subset of parties, forging a MAC on an altered share succeeds with probability at most *1/p*, negligible for a large field [5].

The expensive work is pushed into an **offline (preprocessing) phase** that generates authenticated triples *before inputs are known*, using **somewhat homomorphic encryption (SHE)** — specifically the BGV scheme — plus zero-knowledge proofs of plaintext knowledge [5]. The **online phase** then needs only two field elements of communication per multiplication gate and a single batched **MAC check** at the end: parties commit to a random linear combination of all opened values, open the combination, and verify the MAC relation, so any tampering anywhere in the computation is caught with overwhelming probability [5].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Cut-and-choose: malicious-secure garbled circuits

Yao's protocol is only semi-honest: a malicious garbler could garble a *different* circuit than agreed (violating correctness) or craft labels that leak the evaluator's input (violating privacy) [4]. The classical fix is **cut-and-choose** (Lindell–Pinkas). The garbler produces *s* independent garbled circuits; the evaluator opens a random half, verifying they are correct garblings of *C*, and evaluates the remaining *s/2*, taking the majority output. A cheating garbler is caught unless *all* evaluated circuits are corrupt, which happens with probability at most *2^{−s/2}* [4]. With *s = 40* (3.5×10⁻¹² cheating probability), communication grows 40× — the dominant cost of malicious 2PC for a decade, until authenticated-garbling techniques reduced it [8].

> **Theorem (Lindell–Pinkas [4]):** *Yao's protocol, augmented with cut-and-choose over s garbled circuits and a secure OT, securely computes any two-party functionality against malicious adversaries with cheating probability 2^{−s/2} + negl(λ).*

### 4.2 Authenticated triples and the MAC check

The heart of SPDZ's active security is that *every* shared value — inputs, triple components, intermediate products — carries a MAC under the unknown key *α*, and the MACs are **linearly homomorphic**: *⟦α·x⟧ + ⟦α·y⟧ = ⟦α·(x+y)⟧* [5]. Multiplication of *⟨x⟩* and *⟨y⟩* via an authenticated triple *⟨a⟩, ⟨b⟩, ⟨c⟩* yields *⟨z⟩* with *z = xy* and a valid MAC, because the Beaver identity is linear in the shares. The final **MAC check** batches verification: for opened values *v₁…v_m* with MAC shares, parties sample public random *rᵢ*, compute *⟦σ⟧ = Σ rᵢ·⟦mᵢ⟧ − ⟦α⟧·Σ rᵢ·vᵢ*, commit to and open *σ*, and abort unless *σ = 0*. A single inconsistent MAC anywhere forces *σ ≠ 0* with probability *1 − 1/p* [5].

```python
# SPDZ-style online multiplication with authenticated Beaver triple
def multiply(sh_x, sh_y, triple, p):
    (a, b, c) = triple          # each: (share, mac_share); c = a*b
    d = open(sh_x - a, p)       # one round: broadcast masked values
    e = open(sh_y - b, p)
    z_share = c.share + d*b.share + e*a.share + d*e
    z_mac   = c.mac   + d*b.mac   + e*a.mac          # MACs stay linear
    return (z_share % p, z_mac % p)

def mac_check(opened, mac_shares, alpha_share, p):
    import random
    r = [random.randrange(p) for _ in opened]
    sigma = sum(ri*m for ri, m in zip(r, mac_shares))
    sigma -= alpha_share * sum(ri*v for ri, v in zip(r, opened))
    return open(sigma, p) == 0   # abort unless zero
```

### 4.3 Dishonest-majority preprocessing: MASCOT and Overdrive

SPDZ's original SHE-based offline phase, while asymptotically efficient, was concretely heavy [5]. Two successors reshaped the landscape:

- **MASCOT** (Keller, Orsini, Scholl, CCS 2016) generates triples using *only OT* (with OT extension), replacing expensive lattice operations with symmetric cryptography; it is typically an order of magnitude faster than SHE-based preprocessing for binary and small fields [12].
- **Overdrive** (Keller, Pastro, Rotaru, EUROCRYPT 2018) keeps the SHE approach but introduces *packed* ciphertexts and more efficient zero-knowledge proofs, dramatically reducing the amortized cost per triple over large prime fields [13].

Both preserve the SPDZ online phase unchanged — the offline/online separation means preprocessing improvements compose cleanly with the same malicious-security guarantees.

### 4.4 Circuit-level optimizations: free XOR to half gates

In garbled circuits, XOR gates dominate typical circuits (AES: ~26,000 XOR vs ~6,800 AND gates), so **free XOR** — representing *k_w¹ = k_w⁰ ⊕ Δ* and computing XOR-gate output labels by XORing input labels — eliminates the bulk of garbling cost entirely [8]. **Half gates** then garble each AND gate with exactly two ciphertexts (down from four, and from three with earlier row-reduction), which is provably optimal for the standard garbling model [8]. On the GMW/SPDZ side, the analogous lever is *vectorization*: triples are generated in batches and consumed in SIMD fashion, so per-gate online cost approaches two field multiplications plus amortized communication [5][8].

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 Asymptotic comparison

| Protocol | Adversary | Corruptions | Rounds | Comm. per AND gate | Crypto assumption |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yao (semi-honest) [1][4] | Passive | 1 of 2 | O(1) | 2–4 ciphertexts | Symmetric (PRF) |
| Yao + cut-and-choose [4] | Malicious | 1 of 2 | O(1) | 40–80× semi-honest | Symmetric + OT |
| GMW (semi-honest) [3] | Passive | *t < n* | O(depth) | 1 OT (amortized) | OT |
| GMW compiler [3] | Malicious | *t < n* | O(depth) | ZK proof per gate | OT + ZK |
| SPDZ online [5] | Malicious | *t < n* | O(depth) | 2 field elements | Information-theoretic |
| SPDZ offline (SHE) [5] | Malicious | *t < n* | input-indep. | ~ms per triple | RLWE |
| SPDZ offline (MASCOT) [12] | Malicious | *t < n* | input-indep. | ~µs per triple | OT extension |

### 5.2 Concrete benchmarks (reported in the literature)

The original SPDZ implementation reported an online phase evaluating AES at roughly **0.02 seconds per block** with preprocessing amortized over many evaluations, and later MP-SPDZ benchmarks show maliciously secure dishonest-majority AES at throughputs competitive with semi-honest GMW variants [5][8]. Garbled-circuit AES with half gates evaluates in well under a millisecond per block on a single core, with the malicious cut-and-choose variant roughly 40× slower in communication [4][8]. These figures are hardware- and network-dependent; the *structural* conclusion is robust: the online phases of all three families are cheap, and the cost of malicious security is concentrated in circuit replication (cut-and-choose), proofs (GMW compiler), or preprocessing (SPDZ) [8].

### 5.3 Proof sketches

*Yao, semi-honest.* The simulator, given only the output *y = f(x₁, x₂)*, garbles a *fake* circuit: every gate's table encrypts the single label consistent with the (simulated) output wire labels, with the other three rows encrypting garbage. By the security of the encryption scheme and the random permutation of rows, the evaluator's view — one label per wire, one decryptable row per gate — is indistinguishable from the real view [4].

*SPDZ, malicious.* The simulator extracts the adversary's effective inputs by observing the values it opens (the MAC key *α* is unknown to the adversary, so any deviation from the extracted inputs is caught by the MAC check except with probability *1/p*). It then emulates the honest parties' shares and programs the ideal functionality's output accordingly; indistinguishability follows from the hiding of the secret sharing and the unforgeability of the MACs [5].

## 6. Limitations

Despite three decades of progress, fundamental barriers remain. **First**, dishonest-majority MPC inherently lacks *fairness* and *guaranteed output delivery*: a corrupt party can always abort after learning the output, and no protocol can prevent this without an honest majority [3][9]. **Second**, the offline/online split assumes inputs arrive *after* preprocessing; for streaming or adaptive computations, preprocessing must be over-provisioned or interleaved, eroding the performance advantage [5]. **Third**, communication in GMW-style protocols scales with circuit *depth* in rounds, making high-latency networks punishing for deep circuits — garbled circuits' constant round complexity is their enduring advantage [4][8]. **Fourth**, the GMW compiler's generic ZK proofs remain impractical; concretely efficient malicious security still relies on protocol-specific techniques (cut-and-choose, MACs, authenticated triples) rather than the general transformation [3][8]. **Fifth**, side channels — timing, memory access patterns, and network traffic shape — are outside the standard simulation-based definitions and have broken deployed implementations [8]. **Finally**, post-quantum security is uneven: OT extension and garbling rely on symmetric primitives (plausibly quantum-safe), but SPDZ's SHE-based preprocessing rests on lattice assumptions whose concrete parameters are still being stress-tested [13].

---

## 7. Conclusion

Yao's garbled circuits, the GMW paradigm, and the SPDZ family represent three distinct answers to the same question: *where should the cryptography live?* In the circuit (Yao), in the inputs (GMW), or in the authentication of intermediate values (SPDZ). Each answer buys a different trade-off between rounds, communication, assumptions, and adversary strength, and each has matured from a feasibility result into running code. The modern synthesis — OT extension for cheap correlated randomness, authenticated triples for malicious security, offline/online separation for amortization — is the common substrate of essentially all practical MPC today [5][7][8]. The frontier is no longer feasibility but *usability*: compilers that hide the cryptography, preprocessing that keeps pace with data rates, and security models that capture the side channels real deployments face.
