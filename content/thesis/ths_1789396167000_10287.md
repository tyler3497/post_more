# Predictive Coding in the Cortex: Hierarchical Bayesian Inference, Precision-Weighted Prediction Errors, and the Free-Energy Principle

## Abstract
Predictive coding proposes that the brain is a hierarchical inference machine: each cortical level predicts the activity of the level below, and only residual prediction errors ascend to revise beliefs. This thesis develops the theory from Helmholtz's unconscious inference to variational Bayesian inference, where perception and learning both reduce to minimizing variational free energy — an upper bound on sensory surprise. We derive the predictive-coding updates from a hierarchical Gaussian generative model under Laplace and mean-field approximations, showing how precision (inverse variance) weighting implements attention as gain control on error units. We map the message-passing scheme onto cortical laminae via the Bastos canonical microcircuit — superficial pyramidal cells as error units, deep cells as expectation units — extend the framework to action through active inference, review evidence including the mismatch negativity and repetition suppression, and examine recent proofs linking predictive coding to backpropagation. We close with open problems: error-neuron identity, the dark-room problem, and falsifiability.
## 1 Intro

In 1867, Hermann von Helmholtz proposed that perception is *unconscious inference*: the visual system does not passively register retinal images but actively infers their most probable causes in the world [1]. The sensory evidence is impoverished — the retina delivers a two-dimensional, noisy projection — yet perception is rich, stable, and three-dimensional. Something must fill the gap between sensation and experience, and Helmholtz argued that this something is inference guided by prior knowledge.

Predictive coding formalizes Helmholtz's insight as a computational architecture. Rather than transmitting the full sensory signal up the cortical hierarchy, each cortical level predicts the activity of the level below it; only the *difference* between prediction and reality — the prediction error — is passed upward, where it revises beliefs at the next level [4]. Beliefs at each level are thus posterior expectations about the causes of sensations, and the whole hierarchy converges, through recurrent message passing, toward an approximate Bayesian posterior.

Two developments elevated predictive coding from an elegant idea to a candidate grand theory. First, Friston showed that both perceptual inference and synaptic learning follow from minimizing a single objective: variational *free energy*, borrowed from statistical physics, which bounds the surprise (negative log-evidence) of sensory data under a generative model [1][2]. Under empirical Bayes, hierarchical models allow each level to supply context-sensitive priors to the level below, and free-energy minimization unifies perception, learning, and — via active inference — action. Second, Bastos and colleagues demonstrated a striking correspondence between the message-passing equations of predictive coding and the canonical laminar microcircuit of the cortical column: superficial pyramidal cells carry forward prediction errors, deep pyramidal cells carry backward predictions [10].

The sections ahead develop this story in order: background and variational foundations, derivation of the predictive-coding equations with a working simulation, deep dives into message passing, the canonical microcircuit, the free-energy principle, and the backpropagation connection, followed by empirical evidence, limitations, and conclusions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Rao and Ballard's predictive coding model

The modern era of predictive coding began with Rao and Ballard's 1999 model of the visual cortex [4]. They built a hierarchical network in which feedback connections from higher to lower visual areas carry *predictions* of lower-level neural activity, while feedforward connections carry the *residual errors* between predictions and actual activity. Trained on natural image patches, the network developed simple-cell-like receptive fields at the lowest level — oriented, localized filters resembling those of V1 neurons.

The model's most striking success was explanatory rather than predictive: a subset of model neurons — those carrying residual errors — exhibited *endstopping* and other extra-classical receptive-field effects [4]. Endstopping, the suppression of a V1 complex cell's response as an optimally oriented bar extends beyond its classical receptive field, had long puzzled physiologists. In the predictive-coding account, when the surround of a bar is predictable from natural image statistics (collinear continuation), higher levels predict it away, and the error units fall silent. What looked like a mysterious nonlinearity became the signature of a system that only broadcasts what it cannot explain.

> **Theorem (Efficient hierarchical encoding):** Under a linear Gaussian generative model, the posterior over hidden causes can be estimated by a hierarchy in which each level transmits only its prediction error to the level above, achieving redundancy reduction in the sense of Barlow's efficient-coding hypothesis. The feedforward signal at level *l* is $\epsilon^{(l)} = \mu^{(l)} - g(\mu^{(l+1)})$, where $g$ is the top-down generative function [4].

### 2.2 Variational inference and the Bayesian brain

Perception-as-inference is formally a problem of computing the posterior $p(\theta \mid s)$ over hidden causes $\theta$ given sensations $s$. Exact Bayesian inference is intractable in any realistic model, so the brain must approximate. Variational inference replaces the true posterior with a tractable *recognition density* $q(\theta)$ and minimizes the Kullback–Leibler divergence $D_{KL}(q \parallel p)$ between them. The celebrated trick is that this divergence can be minimized without ever evaluating the intractable evidence $p(s)$, by minimizing instead the *variational free energy*:

$$F[q] = \mathbb{E}_q[\ln q(\theta) - \ln p(s, \theta)] = D_{KL}(q(\theta) \parallel p(\theta)) - \mathbb{E}_q[\ln p(s \mid \theta)]$$

Free energy decomposes into **complexity** minus **accuracy** and upper-bounds *surprise*, $-\ln p(s)$ [2]; minimizing it maximizes model evidence while penalizing complex beliefs. Under the Laplace approximation, the free-energy gradients yield exactly the predictive-coding updates: error units driven by precision-weighted mismatches, state units descending the free-energy gradient [3].

### 2.3 Anatomical priors: forward versus backward connections

Any process theory of the cortex must respect its anatomy. Cortical areas are arranged hierarchically, and the connections between them show a systematic laminar asymmetry, documented extensively since Felleman and Van Essen and refined by Markov and colleagues:

| Property | Forward (ascending) connections | Backward (descending) connections |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Superficial pyramidal cells (layers 2/3) | Deep pyramidal cells (layers 5/6) |
| Target | Layer 4 (granular) of higher area | Layers 1 and 6 (supra/infragranular), avoiding layer 4 |
| Function (PC account) | Convey prediction errors upward | Convey predictions downward |
| Spectral signature | Gamma-band synchronization | Beta-band synchronization |

Only superficial pyramidal cells send forward projections, so prediction errors *must* originate superficially and predictions *must* originate in deep layers [9][10]. Mumford (1992) anticipated this: descending "templates" fitted to lower-level data, with residuals sent upward [10].

### 2.4 Precision and attention

A subtle but essential ingredient is *precision*: the inverse variance (confidence) of a prediction error. In the update equations, prediction errors are multiplied by their precision, $\Pi^{(l)}$, before they influence beliefs:

$$\dot{\mu}^{(l)} = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial \mu^{(l)}} = \Pi^{(l)} \epsilon^{(l)} + \text{(top-down terms)}$$

Precision acts as a gain control. Feldman and Friston argued that *selective attention is precisely the optimization of precision*: attending to a stimulus increases the gain on its prediction errors, amplifying their influence on belief updating [9]. Neuromodulators such as acetylcholine and dopamine are cast as broadcasting precision estimates — for instance, cholinergic input appears to disinhibit superficial layers, boosting ascending prediction errors [10].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The hierarchical Gaussian generative model

We formalize the brain's world-model as a hierarchical generative model over hidden states $x^{(l)}$ at levels $l = 1, \dots, L$:

$$s = g^{(1)}(x^{(1)}) + \omega^{(1)}, \quad x^{(l)} = g^{(l+1)}(x^{(l+1)}) + \omega^{(l+1)}$$

where $s$ denotes sensory input, $g^{(l)}$ are (possibly nonlinear) generative functions mapping higher-level causes to lower-level states, and $\omega^{(l)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, (\Pi^{(l)})^{-1})$ are Gaussian fluctuations with precision $\Pi^{(l)}$. Empirical Bayes enters because the prior on each level's states is supplied dynamically by the level above — priors are *constructed* by the hierarchy itself, in a context-sensitive fashion [1].

### 3.2 From free energy to predictive coding

We adopt a mean-field recognition density $q(x) = \prod_l q(x^{(l)})$ with Gaussian factors $q(x^{(l)}) = \mathcal{N}(\mu^{(l)}, \Sigma^{(l)})$ — the Laplace approximation. Substituting into the free-energy functional and differentiating yields the now-standard predictive-coding dynamics [3][9]:

$$\epsilon^{(l)} = \mu^{(l)} - g^{(l+1)}(\mu^{(l+1)}) \quad \text{(prediction error at level } l\text{)}$$

$$\dot{\mu}^{(l)} = -\kappa \left( \Pi^{(l)} \epsilon^{(l)} - \left(\frac{\partial g^{(l)}}{\partial \mu^{(l)}}\right)^\top \Pi^{(l-1)} \epsilon^{(l-1)} - \text{prior terms} \right)$$

Read the second equation as neural dynamics: the rate of change of the expectation $\mu^{(l)}$ equals precision-weighted prediction error from the *same* level, minus precision-weighted prediction error from the level *below* backpropagated through the generative weights, minus a leak toward the prior. This is *generalized Bayesian filtering*, also called predictive coding [9]. Learning follows the same principle on a slower timescale: synaptic weights change by gradient descent on the time-averaged free energy, which reduces to Hebbian-like plasticity between error units and state units [3].

### 3.3 Simulation: a two-level predictive-coding network

The following Python implementation demonstrates the core loop — a two-level hierarchy inferring the causes of a noisy sensory signal by minimizing prediction error. Beliefs $\mu$ are updated by gradient descent on free energy; prediction errors $\epsilon$ are computed exactly as in the theory.

```python
import numpy as np

def predictive_coding_inference(s, W, Pi1, Pi2, prior, n_iter=200, dt=0.05):
    """Hierarchical predictive coding: infer hidden causes of sensation s.
    Level 2 (high) generates level 1 (low) via W; level 1 generates s.
    Returns posterior expectations mu1, mu2 and final prediction errors.
    """
    mu1 = np.zeros(W.shape[1])   # low-level state expectations
    mu2 = prior.copy()            # high-level state expectations
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        # --- prediction errors (bottom-up messages) ---
        eps1 = mu1 - W @ mu2          # level-1 error: state vs top-down prediction
        eps2 = mu2 - prior            # level-2 error: state vs prior
        sens_err = s - mu1            # sensory error: input vs low-level prediction
        # --- belief updates: gradient descent on variational free energy ---
        dmu1 = -Pi1 @ eps1 + sens_err      # driven by own error + sensory error
        dmu2 = -Pi2 @ eps2 + W.T @ (Pi1 @ eps1)  # driven by own error, corrected by lower error
        mu1 += dt * dmu1
        mu2 += dt * dmu2
    return mu1, mu2, eps1, eps2

# Demo: recover a hidden cause from noisy observations
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
W = rng.normal(size=(8, 4))          # generative weights (top-down)
true_cause = np.array([1.0, -0.5, 0.3, 0.8])
s = W @ true_cause + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=8)   # noisy sensation
mu1, mu2, e1, e2 = predictive_coding_inference(
    s, W, Pi1=np.eye(8) * 2.0, Pi2=np.eye(4), prior=np.zeros(4))
print("true cause :", true_cause)
print("inferred   :", np.round(mu2, 3))
print("residual prediction error norm:", round(float(np.linalg.norm(e1)), 4))
```

Increasing `Pi1` (sensory precision) makes the network trust the data over the prior — the computational analogue of attention. The same loop, run with the weights as the optimized variables, performs learning.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Hierarchical message passing and the anatomy of inference

The update equations of Section 3.2 define a precise *message-passing* architecture. Bottom-up messages are prediction errors $\Pi^{(l)}\epsilon^{(l)}$; top-down messages are predictions $g^{(l+1)}(\mu^{(l+1)})$; lateral messages within a level implement competition among alternative explanations (explaining away).

> **Theorem (Empirical Bayes in the cortical hierarchy):** If each level's prior is the posterior expectation of the level above, then free-energy minimization at every level jointly maximizes a lower bound on the model evidence of the whole hierarchy, and the resulting scheme is formally identical to hierarchical Kalman filtering under linear-Gaussian assumptions [3].

### 4.2 The canonical microcircuit for predictive coding

Bastos et al.

- **Superficial pyramidal cells (layers 2/3)** encode *prediction errors*. They receive backward predictions (targeting superficial layers, avoiding layer 4) and compare them against ascending input; their axons are the *only* source of forward connections, so errors ascend the hierarchy through them.
- **Deep pyramidal cells (layers 5/6)** encode *posterior expectations*. They integrate errors over time and send backward predictions to lower areas, and also drive subcortical structures.
- **Inhibitory interneurons** mediate the subtraction: predictions inhibit the error units, implementing the $\mu^{(l)} - g(\mu^{(l+1)})$ comparison locally, while precision corresponds to the *gain* (excitability) of the superficial error units, under neuromodulatory control [9][10].

The mapping assigns every equation term to an identified cell population — celebrated and contested in equal measure (Section 6).

### 4.3 The free-energy principle: complexity, accuracy, and self-organization

The free-energy principle generalizes predictive coding from cortex to *any* self-organizing system possessing a Markov blanket — a statistical boundary separating internal from external states [2].

A persisting system must occupy a limited repertoire of states (low sensory entropy, i.e., low average *surprise* $-\ln p(s)$); since the evidence $p(s)$ is intractable but free energy $F \geq -\ln p(s)$ is computable, any such system must minimize free energy [2].

Because $F = \text{complexity} - \text{accuracy}$, minimizing free energy trades off fit against parsimony — the system explains its sensations as accurately as possible *without* adopting unnecessarily complex beliefs. Perception minimizes $F$ by changing beliefs; learning minimizes $F$ by changing the model (synaptic parameters); and — the radical step — *action* minimizes $F$ by changing the world so that sensations conform to predictions.

> **Theorem (Complete class):** Under the free-energy principle, perception, learning, attention, and action are not distinct faculties but different means of minimizing the same functional — changing beliefs, changing parameters, changing precision, or changing sensory sampling, respectively [2].

### 4.4 Predictive coding meets backpropagation

The most consequential recent development is mathematical, not biological: a series of results has shown that predictive-coding networks (*PCNs*) trained by *inference learning* — running the $\mu$ dynamics to equilibrium, then updating weights — approximate, and in special cases exactly replicate, backpropagation [6][7][8].

The key findings:

1. **Whittington and Bogacz (2017)** showed that PCNs approximate backprop weight updates on multilayer perceptrons under a *fixed-prediction assumption*: if top-down predictions are clamped to their feedforward values during inference, the equilibrium errors equal backpropagated gradients [8].
2. **Millidge, Tschantz, and Buckley (2020)** generalized the approximation to *arbitrary computation graphs*, constructing predictive-coding equivalents of CNNs, RNNs, and LSTMs that match backprop performance on standard benchmarks using only local, mostly Hebbian plasticity [6].
3. **Song, Lukasiewicz, Xu, and Bogacz (2020)** introduced **Z-IL** (zero-divergence inference learning), which performs *exact* backprop on MLPs by updating weights after the first nonzero inference step at each layer [7][8].
4. **Salvatori et al. (2021)** extended Z-IL to exact backprop on *any* computational graph, including CNNs, RNNs, and transformers, with weight updates diverging from backprop by exactly zero in experiments [7].

These results are double-edged. On one hand, they dissolve the classic objection that the brain cannot do backprop: a biologically plausible, local learning rule can compute identical updates. On the other hand, a careful analysis showed that the fixed-prediction assumption makes the algorithm *algorithmically equivalent* to a direct implementation of backprop — raising the uncomfortable question of whether predictive coding with that assumption is any more biologically plausible than backprop itself [8].

### 4.5 Precision engineering: attention, neuromodulation, and psychopathology

Precision deserves its own deep dive because it is the theory's bridge from statistics to psychiatry.

Friston and colleagues have argued that many psychiatric syndromes are *disorders of precision* [9]:

| Syndrome | Proposed precision pathology | Predicted phenomenology |
|---|---|---|
| Schizophrenia (positive symptoms) | Failure to attenuate sensory precision; overly precise prediction errors | Hallucinations as false inference driven by noisy sensory evidence |
| Schizophrenia (negative symptoms) | Reduced precision of high-level priors | Loss of goal-directed, context-sensitive behavior |
| Autism | Imbalance favoring sensory precision over prior precision | Hypersensitivity, resistance to contextual modulation |
| Parkinson's disease | Reduced proprioceptive precision | Akinesia: predictions of movement cannot overcome sensory evidence of stillness |

In active inference, movement requires *attenuating* proprioceptive prediction errors — otherwise evidence that the limb is stationary would veto the prediction that it moves — with dopamine cast as encoding proprioceptive precision, linking dopaminergic dysfunction to motor and cognitive symptoms alike [9].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 The mismatch negativity: prediction error made audible

The **mismatch negativity (MMN)** is an event-related potential peaking 150–250 ms after a deviant sound in a stream of standards, and it is widely interpreted as the brain's prediction-error signal [5].

It reproduces deviant responses scaling with rarity, responses to unexpected repeats in alternating sequences, *omission responses* when an expected sound fails to occur — impossible under adaptation accounts — and NMDA-receptor dependence matching pharmacology [5].

Crucially, a new magnetoencephalography experiment reported in the same paper confirmed the model's key prediction: the MMN reflects *active cortical prediction* rather than passive synaptic habituation [5].

### 5.2 Repetition suppression and expectation suppression

When a stimulus repeats, cortical responses diminish — *repetition suppression*. Predictive coding explains this as successful prediction: after the first presentation, higher levels predict the stimulus, and error units fall silent [4]. The sharper prediction is *expectation suppression*: predictable-but-unrepeated stimuli also evoke diminished responses, and *omitted* expected stimuli drive error units to fire — confirmed in V1 and across the visual hierarchy [10].

### 5.3 Extra-classical receptive fields revisited

Beyond endstopping, predictive-coding models account for contrast-dependent surround suppression, texture pop-out, and cross-orientation suppression in V1 [4].

### 5.4 Laminar and spectral fingerprints

The canonical microcircuit makes fine-grained anatomical predictions that are now testable with laminar probes and dynamic causal modeling [10]:

Superficial layers dominate feedforward **gamma**, deep layers dominate feedback **beta** — both observed in macaque visual cortex [10].

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires confronting the theory's weaknesses, several of which are serious.

**The error-neuron identity problem.** The theory's most specific anatomical claim — that superficial pyramidal cells *are* prediction-error units — remains contested. Detailed critiques note ambiguities: are error neurons silenced when predictions succeed (hence hard to detect), or do they signal the *precision-weighted* error (hence modulated by attention in ways that confound the signature)? Population measures like fMRI BOLD conflate error and expectation signals, since both change with predictability, making many "confirmations" ambiguous [10].

**Falsifiability.** Critics argue predictive coding has become *too* flexible: with enough levels, nonlinearities, and precision parameters, it can accommodate almost any finding. The 2015 Frontiers debate explicitly asked whether the theory is "articulated enough to be testable," concluding that progress requires committing to specific microcircuit implementations with risky, quantitative predictions [10].

**The dark-room problem and anatomical loose ends.** If the brain minimizes surprise, why avoid dark rooms? Resolutions invoke *expected* free energy under future policies, including information-seeking drives [2][8]. Meanwhile, dense lateral, cortico-thalamic, and pulvinar pathways — plus interneuron diversity — exceed the canonical microcircuit's simple subtraction scheme [10].

**The backprop equivalence dilemma.** As noted in Section 4.4, the exact-equivalence results rely on the fixed-prediction assumption, which critics show renders predictive coding algorithmically indistinguishable from backprop — undermining the claim to greater biological plausibility [8]. The theory must therefore stand on its *unconstrained* dynamics, whose empirical learning advantages over backprop remain to be demonstrated at scale.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Predictive coding offers the most complete current answer to Helmholtz's challenge: perception as hierarchical Bayesian inference, implemented by reciprocal message passing between predictions and precision-weighted prediction errors, with both inference and learning derived from minimizing variational free energy [1][2][3]. Its explanatory reach is genuinely remarkable — from endstopping in V1 [4] to the mismatch negativity [5], from attention as precision optimization to computational accounts of schizophrenia [9], and from the laminar microcircuit [10] to exact equivalences with backpropagation [6][7].

Yet the theory stands at a crossroads: its flexibility now threatens its scientific status, and the next decade must convert qualitative successes into *risky quantitative predictions* about laminar dynamics and pharmacology [6][7][8]. If predictive-coding networks can match backprop at scale while retaining local plasticity, the theory earns its place not only as a description of the brain but as a blueprint for intelligent machines.
