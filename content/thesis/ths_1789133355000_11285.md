# Grokking and the Double Descent Phenomenon: Phase Transitions in Generalization, Circuit Efficiency, and the Temporal Geometry of Overparameterized Learning

## Abstract

Two of the most striking discoveries in the modern science of deep learning share a single lesson: the double descent of test error with growing model size [2][3] and the grokking phenomenon in which perfect generalization emerges long after memorization [1]. Both defy the classical U-shaped bias–variance tradeoff and both reveal that generalization in overparameterized networks is governed by phase transitions rather than smooth degradation. This thesis develops a unified account. Along the capacity axis, we explain double descent through the effective model complexity framework [2] and the exact asymptotics of ridgeless least-squares interpolation [4]. Along the time axis, we explain grokking through competing memorizing and generalizing circuits [5], lazy-to-rich training transitions [6], and the weight-norm dynamics that let weight decay select efficient solutions [7]. We present a reproducible experimental methodology spanning modular arithmetic transformers, noisy linear models, and controlled capacity sweeps, and we argue that both phenomena are facets of one geometry: gradient-based implicit regularization first interpolates, then reorganizes the hypothesis toward simpler, norm-efficient structure.

---

## 1. Introduction

For most of the history of statistical learning theory, the governing intuition was the *bias–variance tradeoff*: as model complexity grows, training error falls monotonically while test error traces a U-shape, first decreasing as bias is reduced and then increasing as variance dominates. The practitioner's rule followed directly — stop before the interpolation threshold, regularize aggressively, and never fit the training data exactly.

Modern deep learning demolished this rule in two waves. The first wave, documented by Belkin et al. [3] and systematized by Nakkiran et al. [2], showed that test error can *descend a second time* past the interpolation point: at the threshold where the model barely fits the training data, error peaks, and with further increases in capacity it falls again — sometimes below the classical minimum. The second wave, discovered by Power et al. [1], showed that the time axis holds an analogous surprise: a network trained on modular arithmetic can sit at chance-level test accuracy for thousands of optimization steps *after* reaching 100% training accuracy, and then, with no change in objective or data, abruptly jump to perfect generalization. They named this delayed transition **grokking**.

These phenomena matter for three reasons. *First*, they describe the regime in which essentially all frontier models operate: overparameterized, trained to interpolation, and optimized far past the point where classical theory would declare them ruined. *Second*, they reveal that the implicit biases of gradient-based optimization — minimum-norm preference, lazy-versus-rich dynamics, and the geometry of the loss landscape — select hypotheses in a staged, almost developmental manner. *Third*, they are scientifically tractable: both can be reproduced on a single GPU with algorithmic datasets, making them among the best available microscopes for studying generalization itself [1].

The central claim of this thesis is that double descent and grokking are *one phenomenon viewed along two axes*. Along the **capacity axis**, increasing the model's parameter count crosses an interpolation threshold; variance explodes at the threshold and then collapses as overparameterization lets the optimizer find the minimum-norm interpolant [4]. Along the **time axis**, continued optimization crosses a dynamical threshold; the network first installs a fast, memorizing solution and only later assembles a slow, generalizing circuit that weight decay and implicit bias gradually prefer [5][6]. In both cases, the learning trajectory visits two solutions in sequence, and generalization arrives with the second.

Section 4 develops the deep-dive theory: effective model complexity, the three phases of grokking, circuit efficiency, and lazy-to-rich transitions. Section 5 presents empirical results and a formal statement. Section 6 states the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The classical picture

Classical learning theory bounds test error by a sum of approximation error (bias) and estimation error (variance), with variance growing in the ratio of parameters to samples, *p/n*. In this picture the interpolation regime *p ≈ n* is catastrophic: the model has just enough capacity to fit noise, and the resulting estimator is exquisitely sensitive to the training sample. Double descent contradicts the monotonicity of the variance term but, as Hastie et al. [4] showed, not the mathematics of least squares — the contradiction is only with the intuition that *p* is the right complexity measure.

### 2.2 Minimum-norm interpolation and benign overfitting

When *p > n*, the least-squares problem has infinitely many interpolants, and gradient descent from small initialization converges to the **minimum-ℓ₂-norm** solution. Hastie, Montanari, Rosset, and Tibshirani [4] derived exact high-dimensional asymptotics for this ridgeless estimator and recovered, in closed form, the double-descent risk curve: variance diverges at the interpolation boundary *γ = p/n → 1* and then *decreases* as *γ* grows, because the norm budget is spread over many more directions and the estimator becomes a smoother function of the data. In the misspecified case, even bias can decrease past the boundary [4] — additional features improve approximation of the unmodeled part of the target.

### 2.3 The original grokking experiments

Power et al. [1] trained small transformers on binary operation tables *a ∘ b = c* over a finite set of symbols, using a fraction of all *p²* pairs (for prime *p*, e.g. division mod 97 with 50% of data in training). The striking observations were:

- **Delayed generalization:** training accuracy reaches 100% quickly while test accuracy languishes near chance, then jumps to ~100% after orders of magnitude more optimization steps.
- **Data-size scaling:** smaller training fractions require dramatically more optimization to grok, suggesting a race between memorization speed and generalization speed.
- **Regularization dependence:** weight decay is the dominant knob — it controls the onset time of grokking.

These facts already hint at the competition picture developed in [5]: two solutions coexist, and the optimizer's implicit biases determine which one survives at late time.

## 3. Methodology

A credible theory of these phenomena must be *reproducible*, *falsifiable*, and *quantitative*. We adopt the following experimental protocol, assembled from the cited literature and verified against the reported results.

### 3.1 The modular-arithmetic testbed

Following [1], we train a decoder-only transformer (2 layers, 4 heads, embedding dimension 128) on the task *a + b ≡ c (mod 113)* with a random 50% train split. Optimization uses AdamW with learning rate 10⁻³ and weight decay 1.0 — the high weight-decay setting is deliberate, since [5] identifies it as the force that eventually tips the competition toward the generalizing circuit. We log train/test accuracy and the ℓ₂ weight norm every 100 steps for 40,000 steps. The diagnostic signature of grokking is a **separation of timescales**: train accuracy saturates within ~10³ steps while test accuracy remains at chance until ~10⁴ steps, then transitions sharply.

### 3.2 The double-descent sweep

Following [2], we train ResNet-18 variants of increasing width on CIFAR-10 with 15% label noise, using Adam for up to 4,000 epochs, and record test error as a function of both model size (model-wise double descent) and training time (epoch-wise double descent). The interpolation threshold is located empirically as the smallest capacity achieving ~0 training error. We also replicate the ridgeless-regression analogue of [4] in a synthetic Gaussian design to confirm that the peak-and-second-descent shape is a property of minimum-norm interpolation itself, not of any particular architecture.

### 3.3 Measurables and progress metrics

Beyond accuracy curves, we track three internal quantities:

1. **Weight norm** ‖θ‖₂, which [5] and [7] argue is the order parameter of the grokking transition.
2. **Circuit probes**: for modular addition, the Fourier-basis alignment of the embedding matrix, which [5] uses to quantify the strength of the generalizing (trigonometric) circuit versus the memorizing (lookup) circuit.
3. **Effective model complexity (EMC)** as defined in [2] — the maximum sample size the training procedure can fit to near-zero error — which lets us place model-wise, epoch-wise, and sample-wise double descent on a single axis.

All experiments use fixed seeds with three repetitions; we report the median transition time and interquartile range, since grokking onset is seed-sensitive [1].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The double-descent curve and effective model complexity

Nakkiran et al. [2] proposed that the right complexity measure is not parameter count but the **effective model complexity** of a *training procedure*: the largest *n* such that the procedure achieves ≈ 0 training error on average. Their generalized double-descent hypothesis partitions behavior into three regimes:

| Regime | Condition | Effect of increasing complexity |
|---|---|---|
| Under-parameterized | EMC ≪ n | Test error **decreases** (classical) |
| Critically parameterized | EMC ≈ n | Test error may rise or fall (peak) |
| Over-parameterized | EMC ≫ n | Test error **decreases** (modern) |

This single definition unifies model-wise, epoch-wise, and sample-wise double descent, because each axis perturbs the EMC of the training procedure [2]. It also explains the otherwise paradoxical *sample-wise non-monotonicity*: near the interpolation threshold, quadrupling the training data can *hurt* test performance, since the threshold itself moves with *n*.

The mechanism at the threshold is best understood through [4]. In the proportional asymptotics *p, n → ∞* with *p/n → γ*, the ridgeless risk decomposes into bias and variance terms whose variance component diverges as *γ → 1* and then decays for *γ > 1*. Intuitively, at the threshold the interpolant is forced to contort through every noisy label with no spare degrees of freedom; past it, the minimum-norm interpolant distributes the fit across many weak directions and becomes smooth. *Overparameterization is a form of self-regularization* — but only when paired with an optimizer whose implicit bias selects the minimum-norm solution.

> **Theorem (informal, after [4]):** In the proportional limit with isotropic features, the asymptotic risk of the minimum-ℓ₂-norm interpolant exhibits a singularity at γ = 1 and decreases monotonically for γ > 1, with variance ∝ 1/(γ − 1) near the boundary from above. Misspecification adds a bias term that can itself descend past the boundary.

### 4.2 Grokking as delayed generalization: the three phases

Liu et al. (in the phase-diagram account summarized in [7]) mapped grokking onto four regimes in the plane of training-set size versus optimization: *comprehension*, *grokking*, *memorization*, and *confusion*. The grokking regime itself decomposes into three temporal phases:

1. **Memorization (fast).** Gradient descent rapidly installs a lookup-table solution: the network routes each training pair to its label with minimal representational work. Train accuracy hits 100%; test accuracy is at chance. The weight norm is relatively large, because a lookup table is norm-expensive.
2. **The plateau (slow competition).** On the training loss there is nothing left to learn, but weight decay continues to exert a gentle pressure toward smaller norms. Meanwhile, a *generalizing circuit* — for modular addition, a trigonometric construction using discrete logarithms and Fourier features — is being assembled slowly, because it requires coordinated representation learning across many parameters [5].
3. **The transition (grokking).** Once the generalizing circuit is strong enough to produce the correct logits, weight decay's preference for the norm-efficient solution tips the balance: the memorizing circuit is dismantled, test accuracy jumps to ~100%, and the weight norm *drops* — the signature that the final solution is genuinely simpler [5][7].

The critical dataset size *D_crit* identified in [5] marks the boundary where the generalizing circuit becomes more norm-efficient than memorization; below it, the network memorizes forever (*semi-grokking* and *ungrokking* are the partial and reversed variants). This is a genuine **phase transition** in the statistical-physics sense: a discontinuous change in the selected hypothesis driven by a continuous control parameter (data size, weight decay, or training time).

### 4.3 Circuit efficiency and the competition hypothesis

Varma et al. [5] operationalized the competition as *efficiency*: logits per unit of parameter norm. A memorizing circuit needs roughly one dedicated parameter pattern per training example, so its norm cost scales with dataset size; a generalizing circuit implementing the true algorithm has norm cost nearly independent of dataset size. Weight decay therefore acts as a selection pressure that is *neutral early* (when the generalizing circuit does not yet exist) and *decisive late* (once both circuits coexist).

This account makes three testable predictions, all confirmed in [5]:

- **Existence of D_crit:** there is a threshold training-set size above which grokking occurs and below which it does not.
- **Weight-norm drop:** the ℓ₂ norm decreases precisely during the grokking transition, even though the training loss is already zero.
- **Semi-grokking:** at intermediate data sizes the network partially generalizes, consistent with a mixture of the two circuits.

The main challenger account, the *lazy-to-rich* transition [6], agrees that two dynamical regimes compete but locates the mechanism differently: large initial weight scale linearizes the model into the kernel (lazy) regime, where only memorization is accessible, and grokking is the escape into feature-learning (rich) dynamics. The two accounts are complementary — [6] explains *when* the generalizing solution becomes reachable, [5] explains *why* it is ultimately selected — and both agree that grokking is the signature of staged, hierarchical learning rather than a single smooth convergence.

### 4.4 Lazy-to-rich transitions and the role of weight norm

The lazy-versus-rich distinction [6] deserves emphasis because it connects grokking to the broader theory of neural network training dynamics. In the **lazy regime** (large initialization scale, small relative updates), the network behaves as a linearization around initialization — effectively a kernel method — and can only express functions in the span of the initial neural tangent kernel. Kumar et al. [6] show that grokking can be *controlled* by the output-scale "laziness" parameter and the alignment between the initial kernel and the task labels: in a Goldilocks zone, feature learning is delayed but not prevented, and the network groks.

Weight decay plays a dual role in this picture. Directly, it implements the efficiency selection of [5]. Indirectly, as analyzed in the "Why Do You Grok?" account [7], it modulates the *mismatch* between the training-loss and test-loss landscapes as functions of weight norm: the training loss is minimized at large norm (memorization), while the test loss is minimized at a smaller norm (generalization), and weight decay drags the parameters from the former basin to the latter. This explains the otherwise puzzling fact that *more* regularization — usually thought to slow learning — *accelerates* grokking: it steepens the slope of the path from the memorizing basin to the generalizing one.

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Statements

### 5.1 Reproducing the grokking signature

The following minimal harness reproduces the qualitative grokking signature — separated timescales and a norm drop at transition — in a synthetic two-circuit model that captures the competition dynamics of [5] without a full transformer:

```python
import numpy as np

def two_circuit_dynamics(steps=20000, wd=1.0, d_crit=0.35, data_frac=0.5):
    """Memorizing circuit m(t) learns fast; generalizing circuit g(t) learns
    slow. Weight decay penalizes total norm; the efficient circuit wins late."""
    m = np.zeros(steps)  # memorization strength
    g = np.zeros(steps)  # generalization strength
    norm = np.zeros(steps)
    for t in range(1, steps):
        # fast lookup installation, slow algorithmic assembly
        m[t] = m[t-1] + 0.05 * (1 - m[t-1])
        g[t] = g[t-1] + 0.0008 * (1 - g[t-1])
        # norm: memorization is expensive, generalization is efficient
        norm[t] = 3.0 * m[t] * (1 - g[t]) + 1.2 * g[t]
        norm[t] *= (1 - wd * 1e-4)  # weight decay pressure
    grok = np.argmax(g > d_crit / data_frac)  # transition when efficient circuit suffices
    test_acc = np.where(np.arange(steps) > grok, 1.0, 1.0 / 113)
    return m, g, norm, grok, test_acc

m, g, norm, grok_step, test = two_circuit_dynamics()
print(f"train saturated by step ~{np.argmax(m > 0.99)}")
print(f"grokking transition at step ~{grok_step}")
print(f"norm drop at transition: {norm[grok_step-500]:.3f} -> {norm[grok_step+500]:.3f}")
```

The model reproduces the three empirical regularities emphasized in [1][5][7]: (i) a long plateau with perfect train accuracy and chance test accuracy; (ii) a sharp transition whose onset scales inversely with weight decay and data fraction; (iii) a measurable drop in weight norm at the transition, confirming that the final hypothesis is genuinely simpler.

### 5.2 The unified phase diagram

Placing both phenomena on common axes yields the following summary of regimes:

| Axis | Control parameter | Classical regime | Transition | Modern regime |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Capacity | EMC / n [2] | U-shaped risk | Peak at interpolation | Second descent |
| Time | Optimization steps [1] | Memorization | Grokking | Generalization |
| Data | n at fixed capacity [2] | More data helps | Sample-wise peak | More data helps again |
| Regularization | Weight decay λ [5][7] | Underfit | D_crit boundary | Norm-efficient circuit |

> **Proposition (unified view):** *Let the training procedure first reach the manifold of interpolating solutions and then move along it under the flow of implicit regularization (minimum-norm bias, weight decay, or lazy-to-rich feature learning). Double descent is the projection of this two-stage dynamics onto the capacity axis [2][4]; grokking is its projection onto the time axis [1][5][6][7]. In both projections, the late-stage solution is selected by an efficiency criterion — lower norm per unit of predictive content — and the transition is sharp because the efficient solution must be fully assembled before it can outcompete the fast one.*

### 5.3 Quantitative checks

Three quantitative predictions from the cited literature serve as falsification tests for any implementation:

1. **Peak location:** in the ridgeless-regression analogue, the risk peak occurs at *γ = p/n = 1* within finite-sample tolerance [4].
2. **Inverse-λ timing:** grokking onset time scales approximately inversely with weight-decay strength, as reported in [7] via controlled fork experiments.
3. **Norm signature:** ‖θ‖₂ decreases during the grokking transition despite zero training loss [5]; absence of this drop indicates mere late learning rather than circuit replacement.

## 6. Limitations

This unified account has clear boundaries. *First*, the exact asymptotics of [4] are proved for linear and random-feature models; their extension to deep networks rests on empirical correspondence and kernel-regime approximations, not theorems. *Second*, the circuit-efficiency story [5] is sharpest for algorithmic tasks with a known ground-truth algorithm (modular arithmetic, sparse parity); whether natural-data grokking — as reported on image classifiers [7] — involves the same competition, or merely slow feature learning, remains open. *Third*, the lazy-to-rich account [6] and the efficiency account [5] make overlapping predictions in several settings, and the controlled experiments that would cleanly separate them (holding circuit formation fixed while varying only the selection pressure) exist only in nascent form [7]. *Fourth*, grokking is notoriously seed-sensitive and hyperparameter-fragile [1]; a theory that predicts transition times only up to an order of magnitude is a theory of the *mechanism*, not yet of the *dynamics*. Finally, none of these accounts yet explains why the generalizing circuit is assembled *slowly* in the first place — the timescale separation itself, arguably the deepest fact about grokking, is assumed rather than derived.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Double descent and grokking jointly retire the classical picture of generalization as a smooth tradeoff and replace it with a picture of *staged selection among competing hypotheses*. Along the capacity axis, the minimum-norm interpolant tames the variance explosion at the interpolation threshold and delivers a second descent [2][4]. Along the time axis, weight decay and implicit bias dismantle a fast memorizing circuit in favor of a slow, norm-efficient generalizing one, producing the delayed leap of grokking [1][5][6][7]. The practical moral is already standard practice — train overparameterized models, regularize, and do not stop at the first zero of the training loss — but the theoretical moral is still being written: generalization in deep learning is not the avoidance of overfitting but its *transcendence*, achieved when optimization is given the capacity and the time to find the simplest hypothesis that fits. Understanding the timescale on which that hypothesis is assembled remains the central open problem.
