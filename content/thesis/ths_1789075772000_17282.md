# BBR: Model-Based Congestion Control at Kleinrock's Optimal Operating Point

![Figure 1: Kleinrock's optimal operating point — the knee of the throughput–delay curve where BBR targets max bandwidth at min RTT.](ths_1789075772000_17282-0.webp)

## Abstract

This thesis presents a comprehensive treatment of BBR (Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time), a model-based congestion control algorithm that departs from four decades of loss-driven TCP design to operate networks at Kleinrock's optimal operating point: maximum bandwidth delivered at minimum round-trip time. We derive the measurement-theoretic foundations of BBR's two-signal estimation — max-filtered delivery-rate estimation of bottleneck bandwidth and min-filtered estimation of round-trip propagation time — and reconstruct the full state machine of BBRv1 (Startup, Drain, ProbeBW, ProbeRTT), analyzing its pacing-gain cycles, congestion-window gating, and convergence dynamics. We then examine the BBRv2 redesign (ProbeCruise/ProbeRefill/ProbeUp/ProbeDown sub-states, inflight_hi/inflight_lo/inflight bounds, DCTCP-style ECN and loss-aware capacity estimation) and BBRv3 deployment fixes that addressed fairness, coexistence, and shallow-buffer behavior. Placing BBR in the bufferbloat context of Gettys and Nichols, we compare its delay-bounded operation against CUBIC and Reno loss-based probing, survey published measurement studies of RTT-unfairness, shallow-buffer degradation, and BBR–CUBIC coexistence, and discuss deployment evidence from Google's global backbone and YouTube. We conclude with limitations: estimation under variable-rate bottlenecks, latecomer unfairness, and open research directions in hybrid signal-based control.

## 1 Introduction

Since Jacobson's 1988 congestion collapse work, Internet congestion control has been almost uniformly *loss-based*: senders probe for available capacity by increasing their sending rate until packets are dropped, then back off. This design couples the congestion signal to a binary event — loss — that conflates buffer overflow, random wireless errors, shallow-buffer drops, and genuine persistent congestion into a single bit of information [1]. As NIC speeds grew from megabits to gigabits per second and memory costs collapsed, the assumption that "loss equals congestion" became increasingly untenable. The most visible consequence is *bufferbloat*: loss-based senders systematically fill bloated bottleneck buffers, inflating round-trip delays to seconds, as documented by Gettys and Nichols [4].

BBR, developed at Google by Cardwell, Cheng, Gunn, Yeganeh, and Jacobson, inverts this logic. Instead of reacting to loss, BBR *measures* the network path's two fundamental parameters — the bottleneck bandwidth (BtlBw) and the round-trip propagation time (RTprop) — and paces packets so that exactly the bandwidth-delay product (BDP = BtlBw × RTprop) is in flight [1]. This places the connection at what Kleinrock identified in 1979 as the *optimal operating point*: the knee of the throughput–delay curve, where throughput is maximized while delay is minimized, and where adding any additional inflight data increases delay without increasing throughput [1].

> **Theorem (Kleinrock's optimal operating point):** For a network path with bottleneck service rate μ and propagation delay D, the power of the network — defined as throughput divided by delay — is maximized when the sender's offered load satisfies λ = μ and exactly one bandwidth-delay product of data is in flight. Any load λ > μ adds queueing delay without raising throughput; any λ < μ leaves capacity idle.

BBR is the first widely deployed algorithm to target this point explicitly. It has been rolled out across Google's backbone, YouTube, and GCP, standardized as RFC 9438 [2], refined through BBRv2's loss/ECN-aware redesign [3][5], and further hardened in BBRv3 [7]. This thesis reconstructs BBR's estimation theory, state machine, and evolution; surveys the empirical record on fairness and coexistence; and situates the algorithm within the broader bufferbloat and congestion-control literature.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Loss-based congestion control and its discontents

Classic TCP congestion control (Tahoe, Reno, NewReno, CUBIC) is built on the AIMD (additive-increase, multiplicative-decrease) paradigm: the sender grows its congestion window until it observes loss, then cuts the window. This works acceptably when bottleneck buffers are sized near one BDP and losses reliably indicate congestion. It fails in three regimes documented in the BBR literature [1][3]:

- **Deep buffers (bufferbloat):** With bloated buffers, AIMD fills the buffer before loss occurs. CUBIC, for example, keeps buffers 70–100% full, adding seconds of queueing delay to cellular and Wi-Fi paths [1]. Delay-sensitive traffic sharing the queue suffers collateral damage.
- **Shallow buffers:** With small buffers, loss-based control misreads isolated drops as congestion, collapsing throughput even when capacity is plentiful. Sustaining 10 Gbps over a 100 ms RTT path with CUBIC requires a loss rate below 3×10⁻⁸ — more than 40 seconds between losses — which is infeasible in practice [3].
- **Random loss:** Wireless links drop packets for non-congestion reasons; loss-driven senders cannot distinguish these from congestion and needlessly throttle [1][3].

### 2.2 Kleinrock's power metric and the optimal operating point

Kleinrock's 1979 analysis defines *power* = throughput / delay and shows it is maximized at the knee of the load–delay curve. Figure 1 illustrates the operating regimes: to the left of the knee the network is *bandwidth-limited* (underutilized), at the knee it is *optimally loaded* (exactly BDP in flight), and to the right it is *buffer-limited* (queueing delay rises while throughput stays flat). Loss-based controllers operate to the *right* of the knee by design: they must overflow the buffer to detect it. BBR's core insight is that both coordinates of the knee — BtlBw (vertical throughput dimension) and RTprop (horizontal delay dimension) — are *measurable* without overflowing anything [1].

### 2.3 The bufferbloat context

Gettys and Nichols's 2011–2012 analyses showed that excessively large, unmanaged buffers had accumulated throughout the access network — in home routers, cable modems, and cellular base stations — because memory had become cheap and vendors reflexively prevented all loss [4]. The consequence: TCP's congestion-avoidance machinery, designed around loss as a timely signal, was defeated; standing queues of seconds became normal. The bufferbloat community's remedies (FQ-CoDel, PIE, BQL, TCP small queues) attack the problem from the queue-management side. BBR attacks it from the *sender* side: by refusing to put more than ~1 BDP in flight, a BBR sender keeps even a bloated bottleneck's queue empty regardless of the buffer's size [1].

### 2.4 CUBIC and Reno as baselines

CUBIC [3, RFC 8312] remains the Linux default and the natural comparison point. Its window grows as a cubic function of time since last loss, converging to the pre-loss window and then probing beyond it. Reno halves its window per loss epoch. Both are *window-driven* and *loss-driven*: their sending rate is an emergent property of window dynamics, not an explicit control decision. BBR instead *paces* packets at an explicit rate (pacing_rate = pacing_gain × BtlBw) and uses the congestion window only as a safety cap (cwnd = cwnd_gain × BDP), reversing the control hierarchy [1][2].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis follows a measurement-theoretic methodology: we reconstruct BBR from its two estimated quantities and show how every control decision derives from them.

### 3.1 The two-signal model

BBR maintains exactly two path estimates [1][2]:

| Estimate | Symbol | Filter | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bottleneck bandwidth | BtlBw | max filter, 10-RTT window | Sustained delivery rate of the bottleneck |
| Round-trip propagation time | RTprop | min filter, 10-second window | Physical propagation delay of the path |

From these, BBR derives the bandwidth-delay product BDP = BtlBw × RTprop, the exact volume of data that keeps the bottleneck busy without queueing.

### 3.2 Delivery-rate estimation

BBR estimates bandwidth not from the sending rate but from the *delivery rate*: when an ACK arrives acknowledging Δdelivered bytes over Δt, the sample is Δdelivered/Δt. This is a *rate sample*, and the max filter over a 10-RTT window yields BtlBw — the maximum sustained delivery rate, i.e., the bottleneck's service rate [1][2]. Delivery-rate estimation is robust to ACK compression and reordering because it measures what the network actually delivered rather than what the sender attempted to inject. Figure 3 illustrates the filter windows: the max filter rides the envelope of rate samples, while the min filter tracks the floor of RTT samples.

```python
# BBRv1 estimation core (simplified)
def on_ack(ack, rtt_sample, delivered, interval):
    rate_sample = delivered / interval          # delivery-rate sample
    btlbw = max_filter(rate_sample, window=10)  # max over 10 RTTs
    rtprop = min_filter(rtt_sample, window=10.0)  # min over 10 seconds
    bdp = btlbw * rtprop
    pacing_rate = pacing_gain * btlbw
    cwnd = cwnd_gain * bdp
    return pacing_rate, cwnd
```

### 3.3 Gain-driven control

Rather than AIMD, BBR applies *gains* to its estimates: pacing_rate = pacing_gain × BtlBw and cwnd = cwnd_gain × BDP. By cycling gains above and below 1, BBR probes for more bandwidth (gain > 1) and drains queues (gain < 1) while keeping long-term averages near the optimal point [1][2]. This gain machinery is the actuator of the entire state machine described in §4.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 BBRv1 state machine: Startup, Drain, ProbeBW, ProbeRTT

BBRv1's control loop is a four-state machine [1][2], depicted in Figure 2.

![Figure 2: BBR state machine — Startup, Drain, ProbeBW (with cyclic pacing gains), and ProbeRTT.](ths_1789075772000_17282-1.webp)

**Startup.** On connection start, BBR grows exponentially like slow start but with pacing: pacing_gain = 2/ln(2) ≈ 2.89, doubling inflight each RTT. It exits when the delivery-rate estimate plateaus — three consecutive rounds in which BtlBw fails to grow by at least 25% — indicating the bottleneck is saturated [1][2]. Unlike slow start, Startup exits *before* massive loss because the bandwidth estimate, not packet drops, is the exit signal.

**Drain.** After Startup overshoots, BBR drains the self-inflicted queue with pacing_gain = ln(2)/2 ≈ 0.35 until inflight falls back to one BDP [1][2]. Drain is the mirror of Startup: it removes exactly the excess queue the exponential phase created.

**ProbeBW.** The steady state. BBR cycles pacing_gain through the sequence **[1.25, 0.75, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1]**, each phase lasting one RTprop [1][2]. The 1.25 phase probes for additional bandwidth (gently exceeding the estimated BtlBw); the 0.75 phase drains any queue the probe created; the six phases of 1.0 cruise at the estimated rate. Simultaneously, cwnd_gain cycles so the window cap tracks ~2 BDP during probing. This cycle is BBRv1's answer to AIMD: periodic, bounded probing instead of unbounded growth-to-loss.

**ProbeRTT.** If RTprop has not been re-measured (i.e., no lower RTT sample observed) for 10 seconds, BBR suspects the path's propagation delay may have *decreased* while the min filter is stuck on a stale, queue-inflated value. It enters ProbeRTT: cwnd collapses to 4 packets for at least one RTT (200 ms), draining the bottleneck queue so a true propagation-delay sample can be taken, then returns to the previous state [1][2]. When multiple BBR flows share a bottleneck, their ProbeRTT episodes *self-synchronize* — large flows draining the queue let all flows see the true RTprop simultaneously — which Cardwell et al. identify as the key mechanism behind BBR's intra-protocol fairness [1].

### 4.2 Estimation dynamics: filters, timescales, and robustness

The two filters operate on deliberately asymmetric timescales. The **max filter** over 10 RTTs adapts quickly: when capacity increases, the delivery rate rises within a few rounds and BtlBw follows [1]. The **min filter** over 10 seconds adapts slowly: propagation delay is a physical constant of the path (barring route changes), so a long window rejects queueing noise, while ProbeRTT provides a periodic ground-truth refresh. This asymmetry is load-bearing: if RTprop were filtered over a short window, transient queueing would corrupt the BDP computation and BBR would chronically over-inject.

Key robustness properties of delivery-rate estimation [1][2]:

1. *ACK aggregation immunity:* rate samples computed over the ACK's coverage interval are unaffected by ACK compression.
2. *Application-limited awareness:* when the application has nothing to send, samples are marked app-limited and excluded from the max filter, preventing underestimation.
3. *Inflight too high / too low detection:* comparing achieved rate against pacing rate reveals whether the bottleneck or the sender is the constraint.

### 4.3 BBRv2: loss, ECN, and the ProbeBW redesign

BBRv1's weaknesses, documented in measurement studies [6][7] and acknowledged by its authors, motivated BBRv2 [3][5]:

- **Coexistence:** BBRv1 is *congestion-signal agnostic* — it ignores loss and ECN — so against CUBIC/Reno it can take a disproportionate share, since loss-based flows back off on drops that BBRv1 shrugs off [6].
- **Shallow-buffer loss:** BBRv1's 1.25× probing with up to 2-BDP inflight causes high retransmit rates when bottleneck buffers are smaller than ~1.5 BDP [6].
- **ProbeRTT throughput dips:** collapsing to 4 packets every 10 seconds costs measurable throughput.

BBRv2 keeps the estimation core but rebuilds ProbeBW into four sub-states with explicit *inflight bounds* [3]:

| Sub-state | Behavior | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **ProbeCruise** | pacing_gain = 1.0; tracks inflight_lo, bw_lo | Steady cruising; reacts to loss/ECN by lowering short-term bounds |
| **ProbeRefill** | refill pipe to 1 BDP for 1 RTT | Prepare for probing without early loss |
| **ProbeUp** | exponentially grow sending rate | Probe for more bandwidth, cautiously |
| **ProbeDown** | drain queue to inflight_lo | Remove probe-induced queue |

The crucial additions are the bound variables: **inflight_hi** (long-term upper bound on safe inflight, reduced on loss), **inflight_lo** and **bw_lo** (short-term lower bounds capturing current conditions, e.g., when cross-traffic claims capacity) [3]. BBRv2 also reacts to **packet loss** (tolerating a few percent while probing but treating persistent loss as a congestion signal) and **DCTCP-style ECN marks** (with a 50% marking-rate threshold informing inflight_hi) [3][5]. The IETF 105 evaluation reported: high throughput under random loss, bounded queueing delay under deep buffers, *much lower loss rates* for queues below 1.5 BDP, vastly reduced ProbeRTT throughput dips, and improved coexistence with Reno/CUBIC [5].

### 4.4 BBRv3 and deployment hardening

BBRv3, presented at IETF 117 (2023), is an evolutionary hardening of BBRv2 rather than a redesign: bug fixes to the state machine, refined startup and probing behavior, and tuning validated against public-Internet deployment [7]. Google's deployment path is instructive: BBRv1 shipped in the Linux kernel (4.9) and across Google's internal WAN, YouTube, and GCP; BBRv2 followed in kernel 5.x-era trees and QUIC stacks; BBRv3 consolidated fixes from large-scale measurement. A 2024 Applied Networking Research Workshop study of BBRv3 in the public Internet ("a boon or a bane?") found near-equitable bandwidth sharing with CUBIC in most scenarios, though with prolonged convergence times in deep-buffer cases [7].

### 4.5 Fairness theory: intra- and inter-protocol dynamics

BBR's fairness rests on two mechanisms. *Intra-protocol* (BBR vs. BBR): ProbeBW gain cycling makes larger flows yield to smaller ones — the 1.25× probe of a small flow succeeds against a large flow cruising at 1.0×, ratcheting shares toward equality over a few cycles — while synchronized ProbeRTT episodes correct RTprop overestimates [1]. *Inter-protocol* (BBR vs. CUBIC/Reno): BBRv1's signal-agnosticism is the failure mode; BBRv2/v3's loss and ECN awareness restores approximate fairness by making BBR back off under the same signals that restrain loss-based flows [3][5][7]. Measurements confirm the trajectory: BBRv1 monopolizing ~80% of a shared bottleneck in shallow buffers [7], BBRv2/v3 converging to near-equal shares with Jain's fairness indices approaching 1 in most tested configurations [7].

## 5 Empirical Evaluation

Published measurement studies report consistent quantitative patterns [1][5][6][7]:

- **Throughput under random loss:** BBR sustains near-bottleneck throughput at loss rates that collapse CUBIC. The canonical comparison: over a 100 ms RTT path at 1% random loss, CUBIC's Mathis-model bound caps throughput at ~3 Mbps regardless of link capacity, while BBR holds its BtlBw estimate and maintains full utilization [3].
- **Queueing delay:** In the original ACM Queue experiments, CUBIC filled available buffers and cycled between 70% and 100% full, while post-startup BBR ran with *essentially zero queue* at identical throughput — RTTs at the propagation floor rather than seconds above it [1].
- **Startup speed:** BBR's paced exponential Startup reaches full utilization without the massive loss bursts of slow start, which matters on high-BDP paths (e.g., intercontinental 10 Gbps) where slow start's overshoot is catastrophic [1].
- **Fairness (BBR vs. BBR):** Multiple BBR flows on a 100 Mbps / 10 ms bottleneck converge to fair shares within a few ProbeBW cycles; the triangular ProbeRTT dips in throughput traces self-synchronize across flows [1]. RTT-unfairness persists: a flow with higher RTprop targets a proportionally larger BDP and probes on a longer timescale, capturing more than its share — a bias confirmed in NS-3 studies [6], though recent work shows buffer sizing, not RTT ratio alone, governs the outcome.
- **Fairness (BBR vs. CUBIC):** BBRv1 takes the lion's share in shallow buffers (~80% in one multi-RTT study) but yields ground as buffers deepen [7]. BBRv2 and BBRv3 achieve approximately equitable splits across shallow and deep buffers, with Jain's indices near 1 in most scenarios; residual unfairness appears as slow convergence in deep buffers where early starters hold disproportionate shares [7].
- **Wi-Fi and AQM interaction:** Recent Wi-Fi 6 studies show BBRv3 coexisting with CUBIC under FQ-CoDel and CAKE with balanced ~5 Mbps shares and contained cwnd (20–30 KB), while FIFO (pfifo) amplifies asymmetry into persistent unfairness — evidence that AQM remains complementary to sender-side model-based control [6].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Variable-rate bottlenecks.** BBR's max-filtered BtlBw assumes a roughly constant bottleneck rate over 10 RTTs. Cellular schedulers and Wi-Fi aggregation produce rapidly varying capacity; BBR can chase a stale estimate, oscillating between under- and over-injection. BBRv2's bw_lo short-term bound mitigates but does not eliminate this [3].
2. **Latecomer disadvantage.** A flow starting into an already-queued bottleneck measures an inflated RTprop (propagation + standing queue) and computes an inflated BDP, over-injecting persistently until a synchronized ProbeRTT corrects it — a convergence process that can take many seconds [1][6].
3. **RTT unfairness.** Flows with heterogeneous RTTs do not share fairly: higher-RTT flows target larger inflight and probe over longer timescales. Buffer sizing modulates but does not remove the effect [6].
4. **ProbeRTT cost and synchronization.** The 10-second, 4-packet ProbeRTT dip costs throughput (much reduced in BBRv2+ [5]) and its self-synchronization, while aiding fairness, couples flows' dynamics in ways that complicate analysis.
5. **Shallow-buffer probing loss.** Even BBRv2's cautious ProbeUp can overflow sub-BDP buffers; the fundamental tension between probing for unknown capacity and avoiding loss has no clean resolution [3][6].
6. **Policing and non-cooperative bottlenecks.** Rate policers that drop above a configured rate look like congestion to loss/ECN-aware BBRv2+, causing underutilization; conversely, BBRv1's agnosticism can be *too* aggressive against policed or AQM-managed bottlenecks.
7. **Security and gaming.** Model-based pacing is manipulable: ACK-splitting or optimistic ACKs inflate delivery-rate samples, and BBR's trust in measurements makes it more exposed than loss-based schemes to receiver misbehavior — an under-explored attack surface.

## 7 Conclusion

BBR reframes congestion control as *estimation* rather than *reaction*: measure the bottleneck bandwidth and propagation delay, compute the bandwidth-delay product, and pace exactly that much data into the network. In doing so it operationalizes Kleinrock's optimal operating point — maximum throughput at minimum delay — that loss-based control can only approach from the wrong side, by overflowing buffers [1][2]. The algorithm's evolution from BBRv1's pure model-based probing through BBRv2's loss/ECN-aware bounds [3][5] to BBRv3's deployment hardening [7] traces a clear arc: each generation trades a little of the original's elegant signal-agnosticism for coexistence with the loss-based Internet it must share. The empirical record supports the core claims — full throughput under random loss and shallow buffers, near-zero queueing delay under deep buffers — while documenting real fairness limitations (RTT bias, latecomer effects, slow deep-buffer convergence) that remain active research problems [6][7]. As bufferbloat-era access networks give way to 5G, Wi-Fi 7, and terabit backbones, the question BBR poses endures: why should a sender ever need to *cause* congestion in order to *detect* it?
