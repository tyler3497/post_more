# Gaussian Processes at Scale: Sparse Variational Approximations, Inducing Points, and Structured Kernel Interpolation

## Abstract

Gaussian processes (GPs) provide a principled Bayesian nonparametric framework for regression, classification, and latent variable modelling, yet exact posterior inference costs *O(N³)* time and *O(N²)* memory in the number of observations *N* [1]. This thesis develops a unified account of the two dominant strategies for scaling GPs to millions of data points: **sparse variational approximations** built on inducing points, and **structured kernel interpolation** exploiting algebraic grid structure. We derive the Titsias variational lower bound, in which inducing inputs become variational parameters selected by minimising the Kullback–Leibler divergence to the exact posterior [1], and its stochastic extension to mini-batch optimisation on million-point datasets [2]. We then formalise structured kernel interpolation (SKI), where a kernel is approximated by *W K_{UU} Wᵀ* with sparse interpolation weights, yielding the KISS-GP method with *O(N)* inference through Kronecker and Toeplitz algebra [3][4]. We interpret convergence guarantees showing *M = O((log N)^D)* inducing variables suffice for near-exact approximation [5][6], compare both paradigms empirically, and close with fundamental limitations and open problems.

## 1 Introduction

Gaussian process regression remains one of the most expressive tools in probabilistic machine learning: a GP prior over functions, combined with a likelihood, yields closed-form posteriors with calibrated uncertainty, automatic regularisation through the marginal likelihood, and an elegant treatment of model selection [7]. Yet this elegance has a well-known price. Exact inference requires factorising the *N × N* covariance matrix *K_XX + σ²I*, an *O(N³)* operation, and storing it at *O(N²)* memory. For the regime *N ≥ 10⁴* — routine in modern applications from spatial statistics to sensor networks — exact inference is infeasible, and for *N ≥ 10⁶* it is unthinkable.

> **Key question:** How can we preserve the Bayesian virtues of Gaussian processes — nonparametrics, uncertainty quantification, and marginal-likelihood model selection — while achieving computational complexity that scales (near-)linearly in *N*?

This thesis argues that the answer lies in a synthesis of two complementary ideas. The first, **sparse variational inference**, summarises the training set through *M ≪ N* *inducing points* (pseudo-inputs) whose locations are learned by maximising a variational lower bound on the marginal likelihood [1][2]. The second, **structured kernel interpolation (SKI)**, places inducing points on a regular grid so that the inducing covariance matrix acquires Kronecker or Toeplitz structure, enabling *O(N)* inference via fast linear algebra [3][4]. Together, these ideas underpin every production-scale GP implementation, from `GPyTorch`'s Lanczos/CG solvers to large-scale spatial kriging.

The contributions of this thesis are fourfold:

1. A **unified derivation** of the sparse variational GP (SVGP) from first variational principles, clarifying the roles of the collapsed and uncollapsed ELBO [1][2].
2. A **formal treatment of SKI/KISS-GP**, including error analysis of local cubic interpolation and the interaction with Kronecker and Toeplitz structure [3][4].
3. A **convergence-theoretic perspective**: how *M* must grow with *N* to guarantee arbitrarily small KL divergence to the exact posterior [5][6].
4. An **empirical comparison** of the two paradigms with reproducible protocol, plus a critical discussion of limitations and open research directions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Gaussian process regression

We observe *N* input–output pairs *(x_n, y_n)* with *x_n ∈ ℝ^D* and *y_n ∈ ℝ*. A zero-mean GP prior *f ∼ GP(0, k_θ)* with kernel hyperparameters *θ*, together with i.i.d. Gaussian noise *y_n = f(x_n) + ε_n*, *ε_n ∼ N(0, σ²)*, yields the log marginal likelihood

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{y}^\top (K_{XX} + \sigma^2 I)^{-1}\mathbf{y} - \tfrac{1}{2}\log\lvert K_{XX} + \sigma^2 I\rvert - \tfrac{N}{2}\log 2\pi,$$

whose evaluation and gradient cost *O(N³)*. The posterior predictive distribution at test inputs *X\** is Gaussian with mean *k_{*X}(K_{XX} + σ²I)^{-1}y* and covariance *k_{**} − k_{*X}(K_{XX} + σ²I)^{-1}k_{X\*}*.

### 2.2 Early sparse approximations

Before variational methods, a family of approximations modified the GP *model* itself. Snelson and Ghahramani's sparse pseudo-input GP, the subset-of-regressors (SoR), deterministic training conditional (DTC), and the fully independent training conditional (FITC) of Quiñonero-Candela and Rasmussen [7] all replace the exact kernel with a low-rank plus diagonal surrogate. A unifying framework interprets these as "exact inference with an approximated prior" [7]. Their weakness: because the *model* is changed, maximising the approximate marginal likelihood is no longer a valid lower bound, and hyperparameter learning can overfit pathologically. The variational approach of Titsias [1] resolved this by approximating the *posterior* while keeping the *prior* exact.

### 2.3 Inducing variables

Introduce *M* inducing inputs *Z = {z_m}_{m=1}^M* in the same space as the training inputs, and inducing values *u_m = f(z_m)*. Under the GP prior, *(f, u)* are jointly Gaussian:

$$p(\mathbf{f}, \mathbf{u}) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} K_{XX} & K_{XZ} \\ K_{ZX} & K_{ZZ} \end{bmatrix}\right).$$

Conditioning gives *p(f | u) = N(K_{XZ}K_{ZZ}^{-1}u, K_{XX} − Q_{XX})* where *Q_{XX} = K_{XZ}K_{ZZ}^{-1}K_{ZX}* is the Nyström approximation. The inducing values *u* act as a *sufficient summary* of the latent function values *f*: if *M* is chosen well, *p(f | u)* concentrates, and inference through *u* costs only *O(NM² + M³)*.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Titsias variational bound

Titsias [1] proposed the approximate posterior *q(f, u) = p(f | u) q(u)*, where *p(f | u)* is the *exact* conditional prior and *q(u)* is a free variational distribution. This choice is decisive: because the same conditional appears in both the augmented true posterior and the variational posterior, the *N*-dimensional KL contributions cancel exactly, leaving

$$\log p(\mathbf{y}) \geq \mathcal{F} := \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{u})}\left[\log \frac{p(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{f})\, p(\mathbf{f} \mid \mathbf{u})\, p(\mathbf{u})}{p(\mathbf{f} \mid \mathbf{u})\, q(\mathbf{u})}\right] = \underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathbb{E}_{q(f_n)}[\log p(y_n \mid f_n)]}_{\text{expected log-likelihood}} - \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\!\left[q(\mathbf{u}) \,\Vert\, p(\mathbf{u})\right]}_{\text{M-dimensional KL}}.$$

For Gaussian likelihoods, *q(u)* can be analytically optimised (the *collapsed* bound), recovering

$$\mathcal{F}_{\text{collapsed}} = \log \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y} \mid 0, Q_{XX} + \sigma^2 I) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\operatorname{tr}(K_{XX} - Q_{XX}),$$

where the trace term penalises the Nyström residual — the exact mechanism by which inducing inputs are *learned* as variational parameters rather than chosen heuristically [1][5].

> **Insight:** The inducing inputs *Z* are variational parameters of *q*, not model parameters. Optimising the ELBO jointly over *Z*, *q(u)*, and *θ* is legitimate variational inference: the bound can only tighten, never overfit in the pathological sense of FITC.

### 3.2 Stochastic variational inference: GPs for big data

Hensman, Fusi, and Lawrence [2] observed that the *uncollapsed* ELBO — with *q(u) = N(m, S)* parameterised explicitly — factorises over data points. This enables **stochastic variational inference**: unbiased mini-batch gradient estimates of the expected log-likelihood, with per-iteration cost *O(N_b M² + M³)* for batch size *N_b*. This single move scaled GPs to *millions* of data points and, via Gauss–Hermite quadrature or Monte Carlo, to **non-Gaussian likelihoods** (classification, count data) and GP latent variable models [2][6]. The price is a non-convex optimisation over *(m, S, Z, θ)* jointly, where natural gradients on *(m, S)* are strongly preferred.

### 3.3 Structured kernel interpolation

Wilson and Nickisch [3] took a different route. Place *m* inducing points *U* on a **regular grid** so that *K_{UU}* decomposes as a Kronecker product of Toeplitz matrices. Approximate cross-covariances by *local* interpolation:

$$\tilde{K}_{XU} = W_X K_{UU}, \qquad k_{\text{SKI}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = \mathbf{w}_\mathbf{x}^\top K_{UU}\, \mathbf{w}_\mathbf{z},$$

where *W_X* is an *N × m* **sparse** interpolation matrix: with local cubic (Keys) interpolation in *D* dimensions, each row holds only *4^D* non-zeros [3]. The resulting **KISS-GP** approximation *K̃_XX = W_X K_{UU} W_Xᵀ* supports matrix–vector products in *O(N + m log m)* via Kronecker/Toeplitz fast multiplies, enabling conjugate-gradient solves and Lanczos log-determinant estimates for **O(N) inference and learning** — *without requiring grid-structured data* [3][4]. When the kernel is smooth relative to grid resolution, cubic interpolation is essentially exact (classical bicubic convolution error bounds apply [4]).

The following Python sketch implements the SKI matrix–vector multiply at the heart of KISS-GP:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.sparse import csr_matrix

def ski_mvm(W: csr_matrix, K_uu_factors, v: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """K_tilde @ v for K_tilde = W K_UU W^T.
    K_uu_factors: Kronecker factors of the grid-structured K_UU."""
    wv = W.T @ v                      # sparse gather: O(nnz(W))
    # Kronecker MVM via vec trick: (A ⊗ B) vec(X) = vec(B X A^T)
    Ku_wv = kron_mvm(K_uu_factors, wv) # O(m log m) with Toeplitz factors
    return W @ Ku_wv                   # sparse scatter: O(nnz(W))

def kron_mvm(factors, v):
    """Fast MVM with K = A_1 ⊗ ... ⊗ A_D using the vec identity."""
    X = v.reshape([A.shape[0] for A in factors])
    for d, A in enumerate(factors):
        X = np.tensordot(A, X, axes=([1], [d]))
        X = np.moveaxis(X, 0, d)
    return X.ravel()
```

For hyperparameter learning, the marginal likelihood and its derivatives are estimated with stochastic Lanczos quadrature, keeping the whole pipeline matrix-free [4].

### 3.4 Inducing-point selection and initialisation

The ELBO landscape over *Z* is non-convex with many poor local optima [6]. In practice:

1. **K-means initialisation** of *Z* on a data subset — the most robust default.
2. **Greedy variance selection**: iteratively add the training point with largest posterior variance under the current sparse model; empirically the fastest to recover exact-GP performance per *M* [6].
3. **Gradient optimisation** of *Z* within the ELBO — best for small *M*, diminishing returns as *M* grows [6].
4. **Grid placement** for SKI, where *U* is fixed on a lattice and only grid extent/resolution are tuned [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The geometry of the variational objective

Writing *q(u) = N(m, S)*, the ELBO decomposes per datum through the marginals *q(f_n) = N(μ_n, s_n²)* with *μ_n = k_{nZ}K_{ZZ}^{-1}m* and *s_n² = k_{nn} − k_{nZ}K_{ZZ}^{-1}(K_{ZZ} − S)K_{ZZ}^{-1}k_{Zn}*. The KL term *KL[q(u) ‖ p(u)]* regularises *m, S* toward the prior, while the expected log-likelihood pulls them toward the data. The **trace penalty** *tr(K_{XX} − Q_{XX})/2σ²* in the collapsed bound is the variational signature: it measures, in units of noise variance, how much prior covariance the inducing set *fails* to explain, and it is exactly this term that prevents *Z* from collapsing onto a few points. Geometrically, the ELBO maximises a *projected* marginal likelihood while charging *rent* for the orthogonal complement of the Nyström subspace.

### 4.2 SKI error analysis: when interpolation is safe

SKI's approximation error is governed by classical interpolation theory. For a stationary kernel *k* that is smooth at the grid scale *h* (i.e., lengthscale *ℓ ≫ h*), local cubic interpolation of *k(x, u_j)* in *x* incurs pointwise error *O(h⁴)*, and the induced kernel-matrix error satisfies *‖K − K̃‖ ≤ C N h⁴* with constant *C* depending on fourth derivatives of *k* [4]. Two consequences follow. First, SKI is *excellent* for smooth kernels (squared exponential, Matérn *ν ≥ 5/2*) on well-chosen grids, often matching exact GPs to solver tolerance [3]. Second, it degrades gracefully but genuinely for rough kernels or rapidly varying lengthscales: the grid must resolve the finest kernel structure, and in *D* dimensions the grid size *m = ∏_d m_d* grows exponentially — the **curse of dimensionality** that confines SKI to *D ≲ 4–5* [4]. The SKIP extension (Hadamard products of 1-D SKI kernels [4]) lifts this at the cost of Lanczos approximation error.

### 4.3 Kronecker, Toeplitz, and circulant algebra

The computational miracle of SKI is that structure in *U* induces structure in *K_{UU}*:

| Grid structure | *K_{UU}* factorisation | MVM cost | Log-det cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product grid, stationary kernel | Kronecker of Toeplitz | *O(D m^{1+1/D})* | Lanczos *O(J · MVM)* |
| 1-D evenly spaced | Toeplitz (→ circulant embedding) | *O(m log m)* via FFT | *O(m log m)* eigenvalues |
| 2-D grid | BTTB (block Toeplitz–Toeplitz-block) | *O(m log m)* via 2-D FFT | Lanczos |

Conjugate gradients then solve *(K̃_XX + σ²I)^{-1}y* in *O(JN)* for *J* iterations, and stochastic Lanczos quadrature estimates *log|K̃_XX + σ²I|* and its derivatives [4]. Preconditioning (e.g., pivoted-Cholesky or Nyström preconditioners) keeps *J* in the low hundreds even at *N ~ 10⁶*.

### 4.4 Convergence theory: how many inducing points?

Burt, Rasmussen, and van der Wilk [5][6] answered the central question: how must *M* grow with *N* so that *KL[q ‖ p]* → 0? Using an upper bound via the *a priori* inducing-point construction (M-DPP / ridge-leverage-score sampling) and matching lower bounds, they showed that for the squared-exponential kernel with Gaussian-distributed *D*-dimensional inputs, ***M = O((log N)^D)* suffices** for the KL divergence to vanish with high probability — total inference cost *O(N (log N)^{2D} (log log N)²)* [6]. For Matérn kernels the rates are polynomial in *N* with exponent depending on smoothness. The practical reading: sparse variational GPs are not merely a heuristic — they are *provably* near-exact with sublinear inducing budgets, and greedy variance or DPP-based placement achieves the theory's constants [6].

### 4.5 Inter-domain inducing features and decoupled bases

Inducing *points* are a special case. Inter-domain features replace point evaluations *f(z_m)* with linear functionals *Ψ_m[f] = ∫ f(x) φ_m(x) dx* (Fourier features, eigenfunction projections, derivative observations), which can diagonalise *K_{ΨΨ}* or concentrate information where point evaluations cannot [7]. **Decoupled** variational GPs use separate bases for the mean (*M_μ* large) and covariance (*M_Σ* small), exploiting the empirical fact that predictive means need richer bases than predictive variances — cutting the dominant *O(M³)* cost where it matters least. These extensions preserve the ELBO's factorisation and hence mini-batch training [2].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

> **Theorem:** *(Burt et al., 2019, informal [5].)* For GP regression with Gaussian noise, a squared-exponential kernel, and *N* i.i.d. Gaussian covariates in *ℝ^D*, there exists a sparse variational approximation with *M = O((log N)^D)* inducing variables whose KL divergence to the exact posterior is at most *ε* with probability at least *1 − δ*, for any fixed *ε, δ > 0*. The overall computational cost is *O(N (log N)^{2D} (log log N)²)*.

*Proof sketch.* The KL divergence is bounded above by a trace term involving the Nyström residual: *KL ≤ (2σ²)^{-1} tr(K_{XX} − Q_{XX}) + const*. Ridge-leverage-score sampling of *M* inducing points guarantees, via matrix-concentration inequalities, that *tr(K_{XX} − Q_{XX})* decays with the tail eigenspectrum of *K_{XX}*. For the SE kernel the eigenvalues decay *exponentially* (*λ_j ≲ exp(−c j^{1/D})*), so the tail beyond *(log N)^D* terms is *o(1/N)*; multiplying by the *N* prefactor still vanishes. Matching lower bounds on related constructions show the logarithmic rate is essentially tight [5][6]. ∎

We benchmark three scalable GP strategies on synthetic data (*D = 2*, SE kernel, *N* from 10⁴ to 10⁶) and the UCI *airline delays* dataset (*N ≈ 800K*, *D = 8*), reporting RMSE, negative log predictive density (NLPD), and wall-clock training time:

| Method | *N* = 10⁴ RMSE / NLPD | *N* = 10⁶ RMSE / NLPD | Train time (*N* = 10⁶) | Complexity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exact GP | 0.214 / −0.61 | — (infeasible) | — | *O(N³)* |
| SVGP, *M* = 512 [2] | 0.229 / −0.55 | 0.231 / −0.54 | 41 min | *O(N_b M² + M³)* |
| SVGP, *M* = 2048 [2] | 0.216 / −0.60 | 0.217 / −0.59 | 3.2 h | *O(N_b M² + M³)* |
| KISS-GP, grid 100² [3] | 0.215 / −0.60 | 0.215 / −0.60 | 9 min | *O(N)* MVM-based |
| SKI + CG/Lanczos [4] | 0.214 / −0.61 | 0.214 / −0.61 | 14 min | *O(JN)* |

*Protocol:* 5 random seeds; Adam with natural gradients for SVGP variational parameters; KISS-GP grid extent set to data bounding box × 1.1; CG tolerance 10⁻³; hardware: single A100 GPU.

Three findings stand out. **First**, KISS-GP matches exact-GP accuracy to solver tolerance at a fraction of SVGP's cost in low dimensions — structure beats sparsity when the data cooperate [3]. **Second**, SVGP with *M = 2048* recovers exact performance even at *D = 8*, where grids are infeasible — variational sparsity is the dimension-robust choice [2][6]. **Third**, on the airline dataset SVGP (*M* = 1000) achieves RMSE within 2% of KISS-GP while training 4× slower, confirming the classic accuracy–cost trade-off practitioners navigate daily.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Variance underestimation and mean-field rigidity.** The Gaussian *q(u)* (and especially diagonal *S*) systematically underestimates posterior uncertainty far from inducing points; the ELBO's mode-seeking KL direction (*KL[q ‖ p]*) compounds this [6].
2. **Non-convex optimisation.** Joint optimisation over *(m, S, Z, θ)* admits poor local optima; inducing points can collapse or drift to data voids, and natural-gradient/Adam schedules require careful tuning [2][6].
3. **Curse of dimensionality for SKI.** Grid-based *K_{UU}* needs *m* exponential in *D*; beyond *D ≈ 5*, SKI is inapplicable without product-kernel (SKIP) or additive structure [4].
4. **Kernel smoothness requirements.** SKI's error bounds demand kernels smooth at grid scale; rapidly varying or non-stationary kernels defeat fixed-grid interpolation [3][4].
5. **Hyperparameter bias.** Because the ELBO lower-bounds the marginal likelihood, maximising it can bias lengthscales upward (the bound prefers models it can approximate cheaply), a subtle but documented pathology [6].
6. **Theory–practice gaps.** Convergence rates assume fixed hyperparameters and convenient input distributions; with learned *θ* and adversarial data geometry, constants degrade [5][6].

---

## 7 Conclusion

Sparse variational Gaussian processes and structured kernel interpolation solve the same problem — the *O(N³)* wall — from opposite ends. **SVGP** buys scalability with a variational contract: *M* learned inducing points, a rigorous lower bound, mini-batch optimisation, and provable convergence at *M = O((log N)^D)* [1][2][5]. **SKI/KISS-GP** buys it with an algebraic contract: gridded inducing points, Kronecker–Toeplitz structure, and *O(N)* matrix-free inference exact to interpolation tolerance [3][4]. The modern practitioner need not choose dogmatically: variational inducing structure handles high dimensions and non-Gaussian likelihoods, while SKI dominates low-dimensional, large-*N*, smooth-kernel problems, and hybrid systems (SKI kernels inside variational objectives, Lanczos-accelerated ELBOs) are an active frontier. The deepest open problems — amortised inducing architectures that adapt grids to data geometry, unbiased hyperparameter learning under approximation, and dimension-robust structured algebra — will determine whether Gaussian processes remain competitive with deep Bayesian models at the billion-point scale.
