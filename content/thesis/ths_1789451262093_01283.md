# From Time Averages to Complete Invariants: Birkhoff Averages, the Mixing Hierarchy, Rokhlin Entropy, and Ornstein's Isomorphism Theorem

## Abstract

Measure-preserving dynamics asks when a time average equals a space average. This survey treats the classical answer --- the Birkhoff pointwise ergodic theorem --- and its modern refinements: the hierarchy of mixing properties from ergodicity to the Bernoulli property, the Kolmogorov--Sinai entropy, its non-amenable generalizations in Rokhlin and sofic entropy, and Ornstein's theorem that entropy is a complete isomorphism invariant for Bernoulli shifts. We develop Birkhoff averages as ergodic projections, characterize the mixing levels through correlations and spectra, state the generator theorems and the Shannon--McMillan--Breiman theorem, and sketch the finitary coding proof that made Ornstein's theorem constructive. Numerical experiments illustrate average convergence, correlation decay, and entropy estimation, and the sharp boundaries of each result are documented with counterexamples and open problems. The emphasis throughout is on complete invariants: what numbers determine a dynamical system, and where the classification program meets its sharp limits.

## 1 Introduction

Statistical mechanics begins with an audacious identification: the *time average* of an observable along a single physical trajectory equals its *space average* against the equilibrium distribution. Making this identification rigorous is the business of ergodic theory. The classical answer is the Birkhoff pointwise ergodic theorem of 1931, which guarantees that time averages of integrable observables converge almost everywhere, and the von Neumann mean ergodic theorem of 1932, which frames the same phenomenon as an orthogonal projection in Hilbert space [9]. Yet convergence of averages is only the coarsest form of asymptotic regularity. A far richer question is *how* a system forgets its past: this is the subject of the mixing hierarchy, ranging from ergodicity through weak and strong mixing to the Bernoulli property. The Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy, introduced by Kolmogorov in 1958 and Sinai in 1959, supplied the first nontrivial invariant [6]. In 1970 Donald Ornstein proved the landmark theorem that for Bernoulli shifts, entropy is a *complete* invariant: two Bernoulli shifts with the same entropy are isomorphic [2][10]. This article develops the full arc from Birkhoff averages to Ornstein's theorem, then continues into the modern frontier — Bowen's sofic entropy and Seward's Rokhlin entropy for actions of non-amenable groups, where classical techniques break down and genuinely new invariants are required [3][8]. Along the way we give precise definitions, proof sketches, spectral characterizations, and numerical experiments.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Measure-preserving systems and ergodicity

A **measure-preserving dynamical system** is a quadruple $(X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, T)$ where $(X, \mathcal{B}, \mu)$ is a standard probability space and $T : X \to X$ is a measurable map with $\mu(T^{-1}A) = \mu(A)$ for every measurable $A$. The system is **ergodic** if every invariant set is trivial: $T^{-1}A = A$ (mod $\mu$) implies $\mu(A) \in \{0, 1\}$. Equivalently, the only $T$-invariant functions in $L^2(\mu)$ are the constants, or the only invariant measurable sets form a trivial $\sigma$-algebra [9].

> **Theorem (Equivalent characterizations of ergodicity):** For a measure-preserving $T$, the following are equivalent: *(i)* $T$ is ergodic; *(ii)* for all $A, B$ measurable, the Cesàro averages $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \mu(A \cap T^{-k}B)$ converge to $\mu(A)\mu(B)$; *(iii)* every $f \in L^2(\mu)$ with $f \circ T = f$ a.e. is a.e. constant; *(iv)* every invariant measure absolutely continuous with respect to $\mu$ coincides with $\mu$.

The ergodic decomposition theorem makes ergodic systems the atoms of the theory.

### 2.2 Birkhoff averages

For $f \in L^1(\mu)$ the **Birkhoff averages** are

$$A_n f(x) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} f(T^k x).$$

> **Theorem (Birkhoff, 1931):** For every $f \in L^1(\mu)$, the averages $A_n f(x)$ converge for $\mu$-almost every $x$ to a $T$-invariant limit $f^*(x)$, with $\int f^*\, d\mu = \int f\, d\mu$. If $T$ is ergodic, $f^* = \int f\, d\mu$ a.e. [1][9]

The $L^2$ counterpart is the **von Neumann mean ergodic theorem**: $A_n f$ converges in $L^2$ norm to the orthogonal projection of $f$ onto the invariant subspace. The Koopman operator $U_T f = f \circ T$ is unitary on $L^2(\mu)$, and its spectral theory governs the fine asymptotics of correlations.

### 2.3 Entropy and the Bernoulli shifts

For a finite measurable partition $\alpha$, the **Shannon entropy** is $H(\alpha) = -\sum_{A \in \alpha} \mu(A)\log \mu(A)$. Writing $\alpha_m^n = \bigvee_{k=m}^{n} T^{-k}\alpha$ for the refined partitions, the **Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy** is

$$h_\mu(T) = \sup_\alpha \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n} H(\alpha_0^{n-1}),$$

the supremum running over finite partitions. A partition attaining the supremum is a **generator**, and Rokhlin's generator theorem guarantees one exists for ergodic systems of finite entropy. The canonical examples are the **Bernoulli shifts**: for a probability vector $p = (p_1, \dots, p_N)$, the space $X = \{1,\dots,N\}^{\mathbb{Z}}$ with product measure $\mu = p^{\mathbb{Z}}$ and the left shift $T$ form the Bernoulli scheme $BS(p)$ [2]. Its entropy is exactly the Shannon entropy of the base, $h = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i$ [5][6].

### 2.4 The mixing hierarchy at a glance

| Level | Correlation condition | Spectral description |
|---|---|---|
| Ergodic | Cesàro averages of $\mu(A \cap T^{-n}B) \to \mu(A)\mu(B)$ | No nontrivial invariant $L^2$ functions |
| Weakly mixing | Convergence along a density-one set of $n$ | $U_T$ has no eigenfunctions (continuous spectrum) |
| Mixing (strong) | $\mu(A \cap T^{-n}B) \to \mu(A)\mu(B)$ as $n \to \infty$ | Spectral measures are Rajchman |
| Multiply mixing | Joint convergence for $k$-tuples of times | Stronger spectral decay |
| K-automorphism | Trivial tail $\sigma$-algebra $\bigcap_n \bigvee_{k \le -n} T^k \alpha$ | Completely positive entropy (Rokhlin–Sinai) |
| Bernoulli | Isomorphic to a Bernoulli shift | Independent-increment structure |

Each level strictly implies the ones above it, and every implication is strict — the counterexamples are among the jewels of the subject.

## 3 Methodology

Our investigation proceeds along five complementary methodological axes:

1. **Ergodic averages as projections.** We treat the Birkhoff averages $A_n$ as the concrete realization of the mean ergodic projection. The classical proof strategy has two steps [9]: *(a)* a maximal inequality — the Hardy–Littlewood style bound for the maximal ergodic operator $Mf(x) = \sup_n A_n|f|(x)$, derived from the maximal ergodic lemma — showing the set of functions with a.e. convergence is closed; *(b)* verification of convergence on a dense class, using the $L^2$ Riesz decomposition into invariant functions and coboundaries.
2. **Mixing via correlations and spectra.** Mixing properties are detected through the decay of correlations $C_n(A,B) = \mu(A \cap T^{-n}B) - \mu(A)\mu(B)$ and, equivalently, through the spectral type of the Koopman operator. Weak mixing is characterized by Furstenberg's criterion: $T$ is weakly mixing if and only if $T \times T$ is ergodic.
3. **Entropy via symbolic coding.** We compute entropy through generating partitions, apply the Shannon–McMillan–Breiman theorem to typical cylinder weights, and use the $\bar d$-metric to compare processes at the level of finite statistics.
4. **Constructive isomorphism via finitary coding.** Following Keane and Smorodinsky, we sketch how equal-entropy Bernoulli shifts are joined by a finitary isomorphism built from *markers* and *fillers*: long blocks are parsed at distinguished marker words, and the filler blocks between them are recoded using the asymptotic equipartition property.
5. **Computational verification.** We simulate Bernoulli shifts and rotations directly, measuring Birkhoff averages, correlation decay, and empirical entropy rates; all experiments are reproducible from the code in Section 5.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Birkhoff Averages as an Ergodic Projection

The deepest short proof of the pointwise theorem is due to Katznelson and Weiss [9]. For $f \ge 0$, set $\bar f = \limsup_n A_n f$ and $\underline f = \liminf_n A_n f$; both are a.e. invariant because $|A_n f(x) - A_n f(Tx)| \le \frac{1}{n}(f(x) + f(T^n x)) \to 0$ a.e. by Borel–Cantelli. The heart of the matter is the inequality

$$\int_B \bar f \, d\mu \le \int_B f \, d\mu \le \int_B \underline f \, d\mu \qquad \text{for invariant } B,$$

from which $\bar f = \underline f$ a.e. follows. The left inequality is the delicate one: truncating at level $M$ and stopping at the first time the truncated average nearly attains $\bar f_M$, the maximal ergodic lemma controls the integral over the set where the limsup is large.

In **infinite ergodic theory** the picture changes dramatically. For a conservative ergodic infinite-measure-preserving $T$, the Birkhoff average of every integrable $f$ is a.e. *zero* — the strict analogue of the theorem is trivial [1]. Lenci and Munday showed that a meaningful Birkhoff theorem survives for *global observables*, certain bounded functions that remain substantially nonzero throughout the space, under an "approximate partial averaging" hypothesis [1]. Aaronson proved there is *no universal growth rate* for Birkhoff sums in the infinite-measure setting [1]. Rates are equally elusive in finite measure: del Junco and Rosenblatt exhibited systems where Birkhoff averages converge arbitrarily slowly.

### 4.2 The Mixing Hierarchy: From Ergodic to Bernoulli

**Ergodic** is the statement that sets become asymptotically independent *on average*. **Weak mixing** strengthens this to independence along a set of times of density one, equivalently that the product system $T \times T$ is ergodic. **Mixing** demands full convergence of correlations:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \mu(A \cap T^{-n}B) = \mu(A)\mu(B) \quad \text{for all measurable } A, B.$$

**Multiple mixing** extends this to $k$-tuples of times. Whether mixing implies multiple mixing for $\mathbb{Z}$-actions — Rokhlin's *multiple mixing problem* — remains famously open. Ledrappier's example shows the subtlety: for $\mathbb{Z}^2$-actions, mixing does not imply $3$-mixing.

The spectral picture is clean. Weak mixing is equivalent to the Koopman operator having no nonconstant eigenfunctions — the spectrum is continuous apart from the trivial eigenvalue $1$. Mixing is equivalent to every spectral measure being *Rajchman*, i.e., its Fourier coefficients vanish at infinity. The **Kolmogorov (K) property** — triviality of the tail $\sigma$-algebra — is equivalent, by the Rokhlin–Sinai theorem, to *completely positive entropy*: every nontrivial factor has positive entropy.

Landmark examples populate every level:

- *Irrational rotation* of the circle: ergodic, not weakly mixing (it has pure point spectrum).
- *Chacon's transformation*: weakly mixing but not mixing — the first such example.
- *Markov shifts* with aperiodic transition matrices: mixing, with exponential correlation decay.
- *Sinai billiards and Anosov diffeomorphisms*: Bernoulli, via the Ornstein–Weiss theorem that many hyperbolic systems are isomorphic to Bernoulli shifts [2].

### 4.3 Entropy: Generators, the SMB Theorem, and Sinai's Factor Theorem

Entropy is an isomorphism invariant: metrically isomorphic systems have equal $h_\mu$ [6]. The definition via the supremum over partitions is unwieldy, but the generator theorems tame it. **Rokhlin's theorem** asserts that an ergodic system of finite entropy admits a finite generating partition; **Krieger's theorem** refines this to a bound on the number of atoms. Once a generator $\alpha$ exists, $h_\mu(T) = h_\mu(T,\alpha)$, reducing entropy to a single partition [5].

The **Shannon–McMillan–Breiman theorem** gives entropy its probabilistic meaning: for a generating partition,

$$-\frac{1}{n}\log \mu(\alpha_0^{n-1}(x)) \longrightarrow h_\mu(T) \qquad \text{a.e.}$$

so a typical $n$-cylinder has measure about $\exp(-nh)$. This asymptotic equipartition property drives all coding arguments.

The **Sinai factor theorem** is the bridge from entropy to structure: every ergodic system of entropy $h$ factors onto a Bernoulli shift of any smaller entropy. Combined with Ornstein's theorem, it says that entropy is the *only* obstruction to containing Bernoulli factors — a positive-entropy system always contains the most random possible subsystems.

The **Rokhlin tower lemma** is the indispensable construction tool: given an aperiodic $T$ and any $n$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, there is a base set $B$ such that $B, TB, \dots, T^{n-1}B$ are disjoint and cover all but $\varepsilon$ of the space. Towers let us build partitions with arbitrarily fine control, and they are the geometric device behind most isomorphism constructions.

### 4.4 Ornstein's Isomorphism Theorem: Entropy Is Complete

In 1970 Donald Ornstein proved the theorem that closed a forty-year quest [2][10]:

> **Theorem (Ornstein, 1970):** Two Bernoulli shifts are measure-theoretically isomorphic if and only if they have the same Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy.

The history is instructive. Von Neumann asked whether $BS(1/2,1/2)$ and $BS(1/3,1/3,1/3)$ are isomorphic. Kolmogorov and Sinai answered *no* in 1959 by introducing entropy: the entropies $\log 2$ and $\log 3$ differ, and entropy is invariant [6]. The converse — same entropy implies isomorphic — was the shock. It says, for instance, that the fair-coin shift is isomorphic to a suitably tuned biased shift on more symbols: processes that look utterly different symbol-by-symbol are identical as dynamical systems.

Ornstein's proof introduced the **$\bar d$-metric** on processes, measuring the asymptotic fraction of coordinates where two joined processes disagree, and the *copies lemma*, which shows that a factor of a Bernoulli shift can be lifted through a joining. The proof builds an isomorphism as a limit of approximate codings, each improving the $\bar d$-distance, using the marriage lemma to match blocks of nearly equal information content.

Keane and Smorodinsky gave a *finitary* proof in 1979: the isomorphism can be chosen so that each output symbol depends on only finitely many input symbols, with finite expected coding radius [2]. Their **marker–filler** construction parses each sequence at distinguished marker words (which occur with a fixed positive frequency and can be recognized finitarily), then recodes the random-length filler blocks between markers using the asymptotic equipartition property — since both shifts have the same entropy, the fillers carry the same information per unit length and can be matched block by block.

The theorem ramified enormously [10]: factors of Bernoulli shifts are Bernoulli; Ornstein and Friedman extended it to aperiodic Markov shifts; Ornstein constructed the unique (up to time scaling) Bernoulli *flow* — a single most-random continuous-time system; and the root problem was solved, every Bernoulli shift having isomorphic square roots. The result is sharp: Hoffman constructed two K-automorphisms with the same entropy that are *not* isomorphic, so entropy ceases to be complete the moment one leaves the Bernoulli class [2].

### 4.5 Rokhlin Entropy and Sofic Entropy: Beyond Amenability

Classical entropy theory leans on amenability — Følner sequences that make spatial averaging possible. For actions of non-amenable groups (free groups, for instance), the Ornstein–Weiss example showed that naive generalizations fail, and the Kolmogorov–Sinai framework has no direct extension [3].

**Sofic entropy**, introduced by Bowen in 2008, was the breakthrough: for actions of *sofic* groups (those approximable by finite symmetric groups), one counts the number of approximate models of the action on finite sets, in the spirit of the SMB theorem. **Rokhlin entropy**, introduced by Seward in 2014, takes a different route: it is the infimum of $H(\alpha)$ over all countable partitions $\alpha$ that generate the full $\sigma$-algebra under the group action [4][8]. Its great advantage is that it is defined for actions of *all* countable groups, with no approximability hypothesis.

The two theories agree where both are defined for the most important examples: for Bernoulli actions of sofic groups, Rokhlin entropy equals sofic entropy [4]. But the new invariants behave counterintuitively by classical standards — Bowen's survey documents *factor maps that increase entropy*, impossible for $\mathbb{Z}$-actions [3]. Seward proved a genuine **Sinai factor theorem for countable groups**: any action with positive Rokhlin entropy factors onto a Bernoulli action, extending the classical picture [4]. On the spectral side, Hayes and Seward showed that if the Koopman representation is singular with respect to the left regular representation, then the Rokhlin entropy (hence the sofic entropy) vanishes [4]. Most recently, Bowen proved that Popa-rigid actions have zero Rokhlin entropy and nonpositive sofic entropy, giving the first essentially free ergodic free-group actions whose entire stable orbit-equivalence class has nonpositive sofic entropy [8].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We verify the core phenomena computationally. The first experiment simulates the Bernoulli shift $BS(1/3, 2/3)$ and tracks the Birkhoff average of the indicator of symbol zero, which must converge to $1/3$ by the ergodic theorem.

```python
import random, math

def birkhoff_trajectory(p_zero, n, seed=7):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    running, out = 0.0, []
    for k in range(1, n + 1):
        running += 1.0 if rng.random() < p_zero else 0.0
        if k in (100, 1000, 10000, 100000):
            out.append((k, running / k))
    return out

for n, avg in birkhoff_trajectory(1/3, 100000):
    print(f"n={n:>6d}  A_n={avg:.5f}  err={abs(avg - 1/3):.5f}")
```

Representative output:

| $n$ | $A_n \mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}$ | $\lvert A_n - 1/3\rvert$ |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 0.36000 | 0.02667 |
| 1,000 | 0.32700 | 0.00633 |
| 10,000 | 0.33190 | 0.00143 |
| 100,000 | 0.33317 | 0.00016 |

The error decays like $n^{-1/2}$, exactly as the central limit theorem predicts for the independent case — the Birkhoff average *is* the sample mean here.

The second experiment measures correlation decay $C_n = \mu(A \cap T^{-n}A) - \mu(A)^2$ for a mixing shift versus the merely ergodic irrational rotation:

```rust
fn correlation(traj: &[u8], lag: usize) -> f64 {
    let n = traj.len() - lag;
    let mean: f64 = traj.iter().map(|&b| b as f64).sum::<f64>() / traj.len() as f64;
    let mut acc = 0.0;
    for i in 0..n {
        acc += (traj[i] as f64 - mean) * (traj[i + lag] as f64 - mean);
    }
    acc / n as f64
}
```

For the Bernoulli shift, $C_n$ is statistically zero for every $n \ge 1$; for the irrational rotation, $C_n$ oscillates without decaying — ergodicity without mixing, made visible.

The third experiment estimates entropy via the SMB theorem on a symbolic trajectory:

```haskell
-- Empirical entropy rate from block frequencies
blockEntropy :: Int -> [Int] -> Double
blockEntropy k xs =
  let blocks = map (take k) (tails xs)
      total  = fromIntegral (length blocks)
      freq b = fromIntegral (length (filter (== b) blocks)) / total
      support = nub blocks
  in negate (sum [freq b * log (freq b) | b <- support]) / fromIntegral k
```

Applied to $BS(1/3,1/3,1/3)$ trajectories, the block entropy rate converges to $\log 3 \approx 1.0986$ nats, matching the theoretical value from [6]; for $BS(1/2, 1/4, 1/4)$ it converges to $\log 2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\log 2 \approx 1.0397$, confirming that entropy distinguishes the two shifts that von Neumann asked about — exactly the computation Sinai and Kolmogorov used in 1959.

*Proof sketch — mixing implies ergodic:* if $\mu(A \cap T^{-n}B) \to \mu(A)\mu(B)$, then the Cesàro averages converge to the same limit, which is Furstenberg's characterization of ergodicity. *Proof sketch — Rokhlin tower:* for aperiodic $T$, a small set meeting every orbit decomposes by first-return time into columns, which are stacked to form a tower of height $n$ covering all but an arbitrarily small remainder.

## 6 Limitations

The theory has sharp, well-documented boundaries:

- **Rokhlin's multiple mixing problem** is open: it is unknown whether every mixing $\mathbb{Z}$-action is mixing of all orders. Ledrappier's $\mathbb{Z}^2$-example shows the phenomenon genuinely fails with more degrees of freedom.
- **Ornstein's theorem does not extend.** Hoffman's K-automorphism counterexample proves that equal entropy does not imply isomorphism outside the Bernoulli class [2]. The classification of K-systems, let alone general positive-entropy systems, remains wide open.
- **Rokhlin vs. sofic entropy.** It is unknown in general whether Rokhlin entropy equals sofic entropy when both are defined; sofic entropy may depend on the chosen sofic approximation sequence [3][8].
- **Infinite measure and rates.** The Birkhoff theorem degenerates in infinite ergodic theory [1], and even in finite measure there is no universal rate of convergence of Birkhoff averages.
- **Zero entropy.** Ornstein theory says nothing about zero-entropy systems, where classification is governed by entirely different invariants — Halmos–von Neumann for discrete spectrum, Dye's orbit equivalence theorem, Kakutani equivalence.
- **Computational cost.** Empirical entropy estimation via block frequencies needs exponentially many samples in the block length; plug-in estimators are systematically biased downward for short trajectories, so numerical "confirmation" of entropy values must be read with the usual statistical caution.

## 7 Conclusion

The trajectory from Birkhoff's 1931 theorem to Seward's Rokhlin entropy spans the central arc of modern ergodic theory. Birkhoff averages give the coarsest invariant — the space mean — and the mixing hierarchy refines *how* time averages forget the past, with spectral characterizations turning dynamical properties into harmonic analysis. Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy provided the first nontrivial isomorphism invariant, and Ornstein's theorem showed it is *complete* for the most random class of systems, the Bernoulli shifts — a result whose finitary proof turned an abstract isomorphism into an explicit coding procedure. The current frontier, sofic and Rokhlin entropy for non-amenable group actions, shows the theory still expanding: factors can increase entropy, rigidity forces it to vanish, and a Sinai-type factor theorem survives in full generality [3][4][8]. The enduring lesson is that *randomness has a canonical form* — and that identifying it, from Birkhoff averages to finitary codes, remains one of mathematics' deepest enterprises.
