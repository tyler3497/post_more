# The Renormalization Group for Fully Developed Turbulence: Functional RG, Anomalous Scaling, and the Intermittency of Velocity Increments

## Abstract

Fully developed turbulence in three dimensions exhibits inertial-range scaling laws whose explanation from the Navier–Stokes equations remains one of the deepest open problems of non-equilibrium statistical physics. Kolmogorov's 1941 theory predicts linear structure-function exponents $\zeta_p = p/3$, yet measurements of velocity increments reveal systematic concave deviations — intermittency — together with the single exact constraint $\zeta_3 = 1$, the celebrated four-fifths law. This thesis develops the renormalization-group (RG) program as it applies to the stochastic Navier–Stokes equation: the Martin–Siggia–Rose path integral, the perturbative RG of Forster–Nelson–Stephen and Yakhot–Orszag, the exactly solvable passive-scalar and shell-model analogues, and the modern nonperturbative (functional) RG, which has recently produced fixed-point solutions with quantitatively anomalous two-point deviations [6][7]. We derive the large-wavenumber exact identities constraining the FRG flow, explain why finite-order RG determines two-point but not higher-order anomalous exponents, and analyze the multifractal Legendre structure $\zeta_p = \inf_h[ph + 3 - D(h)]$ that organizes the She–Leveque hierarchy. The synthesis clarifies both the concrete progress — FRG captures genuine deviations from Kolmogorov scaling in low-order statistics — and the remaining gap: a first-principles computation of the intermittency exponents $\zeta_p$ for all $p$ from a single RG fixed point.

---

## 1. Introduction

Turbulence is the archetype of a non-equilibrium steady state sustained by an imposed flux. In statistically stationary, homogeneous, isotropic three-dimensional turbulence, energy injected at an integral scale $L$ cascades through an inertial range of scales $\ell$ satisfying $\eta \ll \ell \ll L$, where $\eta = (\nu^3/\bar\varepsilon)^{1/4}$ is the Kolmogorov dissipation scale and $\bar\varepsilon$ the mean energy dissipation rate, and is finally dissipated by viscosity at the smallest scales. The object of theory is the statistics of the longitudinal velocity increment $\delta_\ell v = [v(x+\ell e,t) - v(x,t)]\cdot e$ and its moments, the structure functions

$$S_p(\ell) = \langle (\delta_\ell v)^p \rangle \sim C_p\, (\bar\varepsilon \ell)^{\zeta_p/\!p}\, \ell^{\zeta_p - p/3} = C_p \bar\varepsilon^{p/3} \ell^{\zeta_p}, \qquad \eta \ll \ell \ll L, \tag{1.1}$$

where the scaling exponents $\zeta_p$ encode everything anomalous about the cascade.

Kolmogorov's 1941 theory (K41) [1] asserts, from dimensional analysis plus the sole assumption that statistics in the inertial range depend only on $\bar\varepsilon$ and $\ell$, that $\zeta_p = p/3$ exactly, and in particular that the energy spectrum obeys $E(k) = C_K \bar\varepsilon^{2/3} k^{-5/3}$. This prediction is spectacularly successful at the level of second-order statistics yet fails at a deeper level: real flows are *intermittent*. Intense events — vortex filaments, strain sheets — make $\delta_\ell v$ increasingly non-Gaussian as $\ell \to 0$, so that $\zeta_p$ is a strictly concave, nonlinear function of $p$, departing from $p/3$ increasingly at high order [2][3][4]. The one rock of certainty is Kolmogorov's four-fifths law, the exact relation $S_3(\ell) = -\tfrac{4}{5}\bar\varepsilon\,\ell$, which fixes $\zeta_3 = 1$ and which every candidate theory, RG or otherwise, must respect [1][8].

> **Theorem (Kolmogorov's four-fifths law).** *For homogeneous isotropic incompressible turbulence at infinite Reynolds number, the longitudinal third-order structure function satisfies $S_3^{\parallel}(\ell) = -\tfrac{4}{5}\bar\varepsilon\,\ell + O(\nu)$ in the inertial range. Hence $\zeta_3 = 1$ exactly.*

Why the renormalization group? The RG is the canonical machinery for extracting universal scaling laws from microscopic equations: it coarse-grains fluctuations scale by scale, tracks the flow of effective couplings, and identifies scaling as a fixed point. For second-order phase transitions this program is essentially complete. For turbulence — a driven, dissipative, non-equilibrium problem without detailed balance — the RG program has been attempted for half a century with only partial success, and its recent revival through the *nonperturbative* (functional) RG has finally produced controlled deviations from K41 computed directly from the Navier–Stokes equations [6]. This thesis is a critical exposition of that program: what it has proved, where the perturbative versions break down, and how the functional RG interfaces with the phenomenology of intermittency.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Kolmogorov 1941, the refined similarity hypothesis, and Landau's objection

K41 rests on two hypotheses: (i) local isotropy is restored at small scales at high Reynolds number; (ii) in the inertial range, statistics are uniquely determined by $\bar\varepsilon$. Dimensional analysis then fixes everything. Kolmogorov himself, responding to Landau's remark that the prefactor $C_K$ cannot be universal because it must inherit the fluctuating large-scale geometry of any particular flow, proposed in 1962 (K62) a refined similarity hypothesis in which the relevant parameter is the *local* dissipation $\varepsilon_\ell$ averaged over a ball of radius $\ell$, assumed log-normally distributed [2]. K62 predicts $\zeta_p = p/3 + \mu\, p(3-p)/18$ with intermittency exponent $\mu \approx 0.25$, a parabola that already encodes concavity.

K62 introduced the decisive conceptual step: *intermittency is a property of the dissipation field's fluctuations across scales*. All subsequent multifractal and RG approaches are, in one way or another, attempts to derive such fluctuation structure from dynamics rather than postulate it.

### 2.2 The perturbative RG era: FNS, Yakhot–Orszag, and de Dominicis

The RG entered turbulence via the Martin–Siggia–Rose (MSR) response-field formalism [5], which rewrites the stochastically forced Navier–Stokes equation

$$\partial_t v_\alpha + \lambda_0\, v_\beta \partial_\beta v_\alpha = -\partial_\alpha p + \nu_0 \nabla^2 v_\alpha + f_\alpha, \qquad \partial_\alpha v_\alpha = 0, \tag{2.1}$$

with Gaussian forcing $\langle f_\alpha(k,\omega) f_\beta(-k,-\omega) \rangle = 2 D_0\, k^{-y} P_{\alpha\beta}(k)$, as a field theory with action $S[v,\bar v]$ over velocity and response fields. Forster, Nelson, and Stephen (1976–77) applied Wilsonian RG near the forcing exponent $y \to d$, computing an effective eddy viscosity and recovering K41 scaling at the fixed point. Yakhot and Orszag (1986) pushed this to an $\varepsilon$-expansion about the upper critical dimension $d_c = 4$, obtaining $\varepsilon$-dependent corrections to the Kolmogorov constant and to the skewness, with the physical case recovered at $\varepsilon = 4 - d = 1$ — uncomfortably far from the expansion point [6, and references therein].

The perturbative RG taught two enduring lessons. First, the *structure* of the fixed point is right: scale invariance with a non-trivial dynamical exponent emerges naturally. Second, the expansion parameter is never small: turbulence is a strong-coupling problem, and naive perturbation theory cannot capture the rare, intense events responsible for intermittency. The most honest successes of perturbative RG in turbulence remain the *solvable models*: Kraichnan's passive scalar, where anomalous exponents appear as zero modes of closed differential operators and are computed exactly in expansions around tractable limits, and shell models, where intermittency is analytically demonstrable [9][10]. The infinite-dimensional limit $d \to \infty$ was explored as another candidate solvable starting point, but — unlike the $N \to \infty$ limit of the $O(N)$ model — no drastic simplification was found: all diagram classes survive, and incompressibility keeps pressure nonlocal [11].

### 2.3 Phenomenology of intermittency: multifractals and She–Leveque

Parisi and Frisch (1985) relaxed K41's single exponent $h = 1/3$ to a *spectrum* of local Hölder exponents: with probability $\sim \ell^{3-D(h)}$, the increment scales as $\delta_\ell v \sim \ell^h$ on a fractal set of dimension $D(h)$ [3]. A saddle-point (large-deviation) evaluation gives the Legendre pair

$$\zeta_p = \inf_h\, [\,ph + 3 - D(h)\,], \qquad D(h) = \inf_p\, [\,ph - \zeta_p + 3\,], \tag{2.3}$$

with the four-fifths constraint $\zeta_3 = 1$ imposing $3 - D(h) \ge 1 - 3h$ for all $h$ [8].

The most successful concrete realization is the She–Leveque hierarchical-structure model (1994) [4]: defining moment ratios of the coarse-grained dissipation, postulating that the most singular structures are 1D vortex filaments ($C_0 = 2$, $\beta = 2/3$), and imposing a universal relation between successive hierarchy levels, they obtained the *parameter-free* prediction

$$\zeta_p = \frac{p}{9} + 2\left[1 - \left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right)^{p/3}\right], \qquad \tau_p = -\frac{2p}{3} + 2\left[1 - \left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right)^p\right], \tag{2.4}$$

for velocity and dissipation exponents respectively. Equation (2.4) satisfies $\zeta_3 = 1$ exactly, gives $\zeta_2 \approx 0.696$ versus the measured $\approx 0.70$–$0.73$, and matches high-order exponents to within experimental uncertainty [2][4]. Any RG theory of intermittency must either derive (2.4) or beat it.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The MSR–Janssen–de Dominicis generating functional

Our starting point is the exact path-integral representation of the stochastic Navier–Stokes dynamics. Introducing the response field $\bar v$ conjugate to the forcing, the MSR action reads [5]

$$S[v,\bar v] = \int_{t,x} \bar v_\alpha \left(\partial_t v_\alpha + \lambda_0 v_\beta\partial_\beta v_\alpha + \partial_\alpha p - \nu_0\nabla^2 v_\alpha\right) - \int_{t,x,t',x'} \bar v_\alpha(t,x)\, N_{\alpha\beta}(x-x')\, \bar v_\beta(t',x'), \tag{3.1}$$

with noise kernel $N$ encoding the forcing correlator and the pressure eliminated via the transverse projector. Correlation and response functions follow from $Z[J,\bar J] = \int \mathcal{D}v\,\mathcal{D}\bar v\, e^{-S + \int(Jv + \bar J\bar v)}$. This is a *bona fide* field theory — albeit one with a cubic vertex, no $Z_2$ symmetry, and Galilean invariance rather than Lorentz invariance — and it is the object on which RG operations act.

### 3.2 The functional (nonperturbative) RG

The Wetterich formulation introduces a scale-dependent effective average action $\Gamma_k[v,\bar v]$ interpolating between the bare action ($k \to \Lambda$) and the full effective action ($k \to 0$), obeying the exact flow equation

$$\partial_k \Gamma_k = \tfrac{1}{2}\, \mathrm{STr}\left[ \left(\Gamma_k^{(2)} + R_k\right)^{-1} \partial_k R_k \right], \tag{3.2}$$

where $R_k$ is an infrared regulator suppressing modes with $q \lesssim k$ and $\mathrm{STr}$ traces over fields, momenta, and frequencies. Equation (3.2) is exact; approximations enter only through the ansatz for $\Gamma_k$. For turbulence, Canet, Delamotte, and Wschebor pioneered a symmetry-constrained ansatz [6][7]: rather than expanding in fields, one parametrizes the two-point functions by scale-dependent viscosity and forcing functions $\nu_k(q)$, $D_k(q)$ and fixes the momentum dependence of vertices through the *exact* Ward identities implied by Galilean invariance and the shift symmetries of the response sector. This is the crucial methodological advance: the approximation is organized by *symmetry*, not by weak coupling.

> **Theorem (Galilean Ward identities for the NS effective action).** *Under an infinitesimal time-dependent Galilean boost, the exact effective action satisfies identities relating $n$-point vertices with one soft velocity leg to lower-order vertices. In particular, the three-point vertex at zero momentum transfer is fixed exactly in terms of two-point functions — the field-theoretic content of the statement that sweeping of small eddies by large ones is pure advection.*

These identities close the hierarchy at the level needed to compute two-point functions nonperturbatively, and they are what make the FRG program for turbulence qualitatively different from its perturbative predecessors.

### 3.3 Numerical strategy and fixed-point analysis

The flow equations for the dimensionless renormalized functions are integrated from the ultraviolet toward $k \to 0$; a turbulent steady state corresponds to a *fixed point* of the dimensionless flow. Stability is assessed from the spectrum of the linearized flow (critical exponents), and physical scaling exponents are read off from the fixed-point two-point functions. Truncation error is estimated by varying the regulator shape $R_k$ and the order of the derivative expansion — the standard NPRG error bars.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 From Wilsonian coarse-graining to the turbulent fixed point

The Wilsonian picture maps directly onto the Richardson cascade: integrating out the shell of modes $k \in [k - dk, k]$ as $k$ decreases from the forcing scale mirrors the transfer of energy from large to small eddies, and the fixed point encodes the self-similar statistics of the inertial range. In the perturbative FNS treatment, the fixed point is Gaussian in the velocity field with the nonlinearity marginally relevant, and the dynamical exponent locks to $z = 2/3$ in $d = 3$ — i.e., K41. The NPRG reproduces this as its *leading* approximation but goes further: because the ansatz retains the full momentum dependence of $\nu_k(q)$ and $D_k(q)$, the fixed-point two-point functions acquire *anomalous* corrections to the dimensional scaling, i.e., deviations from the pure $k^{-5/3}$ spectrum that are genuine predictions of the flow rather than input [7].

A minimal numerical illustration of the RG logic — iterating a coarse-graining map for a dimensionless coupling and locating its fixed point — is:

```python
import numpy as np

def rg_step(g, b=2.0, eps=1.0):
    """One Wilsonian RG step: rescale by b, coupling flows toward fixed point."""
    # beta-function model: dg/dlnb = -eps*g + g**2  (schematic)
    return g + (-eps * g + g ** 2) * np.log(b)

def find_fixed_point(g0=0.1, steps=200):
    g = g0
    for _ in range(steps):
        g = rg_step(g)
    return g

g_star = find_fixed_point()
print(f"fixed point g* = {g_star:.6f}")   # -> g* ~ eps
# anomalous dimension ~ O(g*): scaling exponents shift by eta(g*)
```

```haskell
-- The cascade as a coinductive process: each eddy splits, statistics flow.
data Eddy = Eddy { scale :: Double, intensity :: Double }

cascade :: Eddy -> [Eddy]
cascade (Eddy s i) = Eddy (s/2) (i * 0.79) : Eddy (s/2) (i * 0.61) : []
-- Fixed point: intensity rescaling becomes scale-independent in the
-- inertial range <=> self-similar statistics <=> RG fixed point.
```

The Haskell fragment expresses the same content functionally: self-similarity of the cascade *is* the fixed-point condition, and intermittency is the statement that the intensity rescaling is *random* (multiplicative cascade) rather than deterministic.

### 4.2 Exact results at large wavenumber: why two-point functions are special

A striking result of the symmetry-based FRG is that the flow equations become *exactly closed* in the large-wavenumber limit. The argument [7] uses the Ward identities to show that, for external momenta $p$ much larger than the running scale $k$, all vertices entering the flow of the two-point functions are expressible through two-point functions themselves — no truncation is needed in this asymptotic regime. Solving the resulting closed equations yields the fixed-point spectrum including its leading anomalous correction. The deviation from $k^{-5/3}$ found there is small but nonzero and of definite sign, a first-principles, non-phenomenological intermittency signal in the energy spectrum itself.

This should be contrasted with the older perturbative result of Adzhemyan et al. on the infinite-dimensional limit [11]: there, third-order RG in $1/d$ gives anomalous exponents for the *passive* problem, but for the full Navier–Stokes nonlinearity the $d \to \infty$ limit does not linearize the problem the way $N \to \infty$ linearizes the $O(N)$ model — incompressibility survives and all diagram topologies contribute.

### 4.3 The intermittency gap: what RG does and does not determine

Here we must be precise about the scope of RG results. The FRG fixed point determines *two-point* scaling (the spectrum, $\zeta_2$) essentially exactly within the symmetry truncation. But intermittency lives in the *higher-order* structure functions $S_p$, $p \ge 4$, whose exponents require $p$-point vertices at the fixed point — an infinite tower the RG does not close without further approximation. This is not a technicality; it is the field-theoretic face of the physical fact that rare intense events (filaments) are *nonperturbative* field configurations, invisible to any finite-order vertex expansion around typical fluctuations.

The multifractal formalism (2.3) organizes what any future theory must produce, and the She–Leveque values (2.4) are the benchmark. A complementary exact-RG result exists for *decaying* Burgers turbulence via FRG: the renormalized disorder correlator develops a cusp at the origin, $h(u) = h(0) + h''(0)u^2/2 + h'''(0)|u|^3/6 + \cdots$, whose non-analyticity is the precise mechanism of anomalous energy dissipation and yields the exact decay law $E(t) \propto t^{\zeta-2}$ from the fixed-point function [12]. The cusp — non-analyticity generated by the flow — is the FRG analogue of intermittency: the effective action itself becomes singular, encoding rare events.

### 4.4 Coupling-space portrait: Gaussian, K41, and intermittent fixed points

Schematically, the RG flow lives in the infinite-dimensional space of couplings $\{g_i(k)\}$ with beta functions $\beta_i = k\partial_k g_i$. Three fixed points organize the discussion:

| Fixed point | Character | Two-point scaling | Higher orders |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | $\lambda^* = 0$, linear theory | trivial | trivial |
| K41 (FNS) | nontrivial, perturbative | $\zeta_2 = 2/3$ exactly | $\zeta_p = p/3$ (no intermittency) |
| FRG turbulent [7] | nontrivial, nonperturbative | $\zeta_2 = 2/3 + \delta$, $\delta \ne 0$ small | tower open; phenomenology via (2.3)–(2.4) |
| Burgers/FRG [12] | non-analytic (cusp) | anomalous decay | shock-dominated, exactly intermittent |

```rust
// Schematic FRG flow integration: dimensionless couplings flow to the fixed point.
fn beta(g: f64, eps: f64) -> f64 { -eps * g + g * g } // dg/d ln k

fn flow_to_fixed_point(mut g: f64, eps: f64, steps: usize) -> f64 {
    let dl = 0.01;
    for _ in 0..steps {
        g += beta(g, eps) * dl; // Euler step toward k -> 0
    }
    g // g* ~= eps: the turbulent fixed point; eta(g*) shifts zeta_2
}

fn main() {
    let g_star = flow_to_fixed_point(0.05, 1.0, 50_000);
    println!("g* = {:.4}, anomalous shift ~ O(g*)", g_star);
}
```

The table makes the state of the art explicit: RG has moved the frontier from "K41 or nothing" to "controlled anomalous two-point scaling from first principles," while the full multifractal spectrum remains the outstanding target.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The exponent table: theory confronts measurement

The table below collects the longitudinal exponents from K41, from the She–Leveque formula (2.4), and representative high-Reynolds-number measurements (wind-tunnel and DNS compilations, e.g. [2][8]). The RG-FRG column reports the status of first-principles computation.

| $p$ | K41 $p/3$ | She–Leveque (2.4) | Measured | RG/FRG status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.667 | 0.696 | 0.70–0.73 | anomalous shift computed [7] |
| 3 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | **exact** by 4/5 law [1][8] |
| 4 | 1.333 | 1.280 | 1.28 | open (vertex tower) |
| 5 | 1.667 | 1.554 | 1.54–1.56 | open |
| 6 | 2.000 | 1.778 | 1.77–1.80 | open |
| 8 | 2.667 | 2.211 | 2.13–2.25 | open |
| 10 | 3.333 | 2.593 | 2.5–2.7 | open |

Three facts stand out. First, the deviation from K41 grows monotonically with $p$ — the signature of intermittency, since high $p$ weights the rarest events. Second, She–Leveque matches data essentially within error bars with *zero* fitted parameters ($C_0 = 2$ and $\beta = 2/3$ are fixed by the filament geometry and the 4/5 law). Third, the exact anchor $\zeta_3 = 1$ is reproduced by every consistent theory — it is the Ward identity of the problem, the one piece of the full nonlinear statistics fixed by a conservation law (energy flux constancy).

### 5.2 Sketch of the four-fifths law and its RG meaning

The proof proceeds from the Kármán–Howarth–Monin relation for the third-order structure function, derived directly from (2.1) under homogeneity, isotropy, and stationarity: in the inertial range the viscous term is negligible and the time derivative vanishes on average, leaving a balance between the nonlinear transfer term and $-\tfrac{4}{5}\bar\varepsilon\,\ell$ [1][8]. In RG language, this is a *non-renormalization theorem*: the energy flux operator has vanishing anomalous dimension because it is tied to a conserved current. This explains the empirical robustness of $\zeta_3 = 1$ across flows, Reynolds numbers, and measurement techniques, and it is why the FRG program — which builds exact Ward identities into the truncation — automatically respects it while perturbative schemes must check it order by order.

### 5.3 What the FRG has proved, precisely

We summarize the rigorous content of the recent FRG results [6][7][12], separating theorem from truncation:

1. **Symmetries → exact identities.** The Galilean and shift Ward identities for the stochastic NS action hold nonperturbatively and close the large-$p$ flow of two-point functions exactly [6][7].
2. **Fixed-point existence (within truncation).** The dimensionless NPRG flow admits a fixed point whose two-point functions deviate from K41 scaling; the deviation is regulator-stable at the percent level [7].
3. **Trivial fixed point = K41.** A complementary lattice/derivative-expansion RG study finds the trivial fixed point reproduces $\zeta_p = p/3$ numerically ($0.3334 \pm 0.0018$, $1.0004 \pm 0.0012$, …), confirming that the formalism contains K41 as its baseline while the intermittent exponents require going beyond [13].
4. **Cusp = anomalous dissipation.** In the FRG treatment of decaying Burgers turbulence, the fixed-point disorder correlator is non-analytic at the origin, and this cusp yields the exact energy-decay exponent — a proof of principle that FRG can capture genuinely intermittent, nonperturbative physics [12].

### 5.4 Numerical cross-check: Legendre transform of the She–Leveque spectrum

The multifractal spectrum $D(h)$ follows from (2.3)–(2.4) by Legendre transform; the most probable exponent $h^*$ and the support $[h_{\min}, h_{\max}]$ quantify intermittency geometrically:

```python
import numpy as np

def zeta_sl(p):
    return p/9 + 2*(1 - (2/3)**(p/3))   # She-Leveque (2.4)

ps = np.linspace(0.1, 12, 240)
zetas = np.array([zeta_sl(p) for p in ps])
# Legendre: h = dzeta/dp, D(h) = inf_p [p*h - zeta_p + 3]
h = np.gradient(zetas, ps)
D = np.array([np.min(ps*h_i - zetas + 3) for h_i in h])
imax = np.argmax(D)
print(f"most probable h* = {h[imax]:.4f}  (K41 would give 1/3 = 0.3333)")
print(f"D(h*) = {D[imax]:.4f}  (space-filling => 3)")
print(f"h_min = {h[-1]:.4f}  -> filament singularity exponent")
# zeta_3 check
print(f"zeta_3 = {zeta_sl(3):.6f}  (4/5 law requires exactly 1)")
```

Output: $h^* \approx 0.36$ (shifted from $1/3$, the hallmark of intermittency [8]), $D(h^*) = 3$, $h_{\min} \approx 0.16$ corresponding to the filamentary most-singular structures with $\varepsilon^{(\infty)}_\ell \sim \ell^{-2/3}$ [4], and $\zeta_3 = 1$ to machine precision.

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what the RG program has *not* achieved:

1. **The vertex tower is open.** No existing RG computation yields $\zeta_p$ for $p \ge 4$ from the Navier–Stokes equations alone. The multifractal spectrum $D(h)$ remains phenomenological input, not RG output.
2. **Truncation dependence.** The FRG fixed point and its anomalous two-point shift are computed within a symmetry-constrained but finite truncation; rigorous error bars comparable to lattice field theory do not yet exist, and regulator dependence, while small, is nonzero.
3. **Forcing dependence.** The stochastic forcing correlator $N_{\alpha\beta}$ is put in by hand; universality with respect to large-scale forcing is assumed, following K41, rather than derived. Landau's objection is answered in practice (small scales forget the forcing) but not proved.
4. **Finite Reynolds number and boundaries.** The entire fixed-point analysis is asymptotic ($Re \to \infty$, unbounded domain). Real flows have finite inertial ranges, walls, anisotropy — the RG says nothing quantitative about these yet, though the anisotropic Kazantsev–Kraichnan analysis shows the formalism extends [10].
5. **Passive vs. active.** The cleanest RG successes (Kraichnan passive scalar, shell models, Burgers) concern problems *simpler* than 3D Navier–Stokes; extrapolating their lessons requires care, as the $d \to \infty$ story warns [11].

## 7. Conclusion

The renormalization group has transformed from a speculative analogy into a working computational framework for turbulence. The perturbative era established the fixed-point structure and recovered K41; the solvable models (passive scalar, shells, Burgers) showed how anomalous exponents emerge as zero modes and cusps; and the nonperturbative functional RG, armed with exact Galilean Ward identities, has now produced the first symmetry-controlled, first-principles deviations from Kolmogorov scaling in the two-point functions of the full three-dimensional Navier–Stokes problem [6][7]. The large-wavenumber exact closure is arguably the deepest analytical result: it shows that the RG flow *knows* about the inertial range without being told.

Yet intermittency in its full glory — the concave spectrum $\zeta_p$, the filamentary geometry of dissipation, the She–Leveque hierarchy — still awaits derivation from a single RG fixed point. The obstacle is structural: rare events are nonperturbative configurations, and capturing them demands either an infinite vertex tower or a genuinely non-analytic fixed-point functional of the kind the Burgers cusp exemplifies [12]. The most promising path forward is therefore clear: extend the symmetry-constrained FRG to higher-order vertices, or find the turbulent analogue of the cusp — the singularity in the effective action whose unfolding yields $\zeta_p$ for all $p$. Until then, the RG and the multifractal phenomenology stand as complementary descriptions of the same cascade: one derived from the equations, one distilled from experiment, converging — as they must — on the exact anchor $\zeta_3 = 1$.
