# Fully Homomorphic Encryption: RLWE Foundations, BGV and CKKS Constructions, and the Bootstrapping Blueprint

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) enables arbitrary computation on encrypted data without ever decrypting it, resolving a problem posed in 1978 and open for more than three decades. This thesis presents a self-contained technical treatment of the modern lattice-based theory of FHE. We begin with the learning-with-errors (LWE) problem of Regev [1] and its algebraic refinement, ring-LWE (RLWE), due to Lyubashevsky, Peikert, and Regev [2], which converts worst-case ideal-lattice hardness into efficient average-case cryptography over cyclotomic rings. We develop the Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan (BGV) scheme [4], emphasizing modulus switching and key switching as the core noise-management disciplines that make leveled FHE possible without bootstrapping. We then present the Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) scheme [5] for approximate arithmetic over the reals and complex numbers, including canonical-embedding encoding, rescaling, and its distinctive approximate bootstrapping [8]. Finally, we reconstruct Gentry's bootstrapping blueprint [3] that lifts any bootstrappable somewhat-homomorphic scheme to full homomorphism, and evaluate empirical performance from the Gentry–Halevi implementation through contemporary libraries such as HElib [6] and RNS-CKKS [7].

## 1. Introduction

In 1978, Rivest, Adleman, and Dertouzos asked whether one could *compute on encrypted data*: evaluate a function $f$ on ciphertexts so that decryption yields $f(m)$. For thirty years this "holy grail" of cryptography resisted every attack — partial results such as RSA (multiplicative), Paillier (additive), and Boneh–Goh–Nissim (one multiplication) fell short of universality.

The conceptual core of every lattice-based FHE scheme is **noise**. Encryption embeds a small random error into an algebraic object; homomorphic addition and multiplication cause that error to grow, and decryption succeeds only while the accumulated noise stays below a threshold tied to the modulus. A *somewhat* homomorphic encryption (SHE) scheme tolerates circuits of bounded depth.

Modern practice, however, runs largely on *leveled* schemes that postpone or avoid bootstrapping. The BGV scheme [4] introduced **modulus switching**, shrinking noise relative to a shrinking modulus as computation descends through levels. The CKKS scheme [5] traded exactness for efficiency, defining homomorphic arithmetic over *approximate* numbers with a native rescaling operation. These constructions share one foundation: RLWE [2].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Lattices and Worst-Case Hardness

A lattice $\mathcal{L} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is a discrete additive subgroup, typically $\mathcal{L} = B\mathbb{Z}^n$ for a basis $B$. The classical hard problems are the *shortest vector problem* (SVP: find a nonzero $v \in \mathcal{L}$ minimizing $\|v\|$) and the *closest vector problem* (CVP: given $t$, find the nearest lattice point).

> **Theorem (Ajtai):** If one can solve random instances of certain lattice problems efficiently, then one can solve *every* instance of the approximate shortest independent vectors problem (SIVP) efficiently.

The quantum-era significance is well known: no efficient quantum algorithm is known for approximate SVP, making lattice schemes leading candidates for post-quantum cryptography.

### 2.2 Learning with Errors

Oded Regev's 2005 paper introduced the learning-with-errors problem and used it to build public-key encryption with worst-case hardness [1]. Let $q$ be a modulus, $n$ a dimension, and $\chi$ an error distribution over $\mathbb{Z}_q$ concentrated near zero (typically a discrete Gaussian of width $\alpha q$). Fix a secret $s \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n$. The **search-LWE** problem is:

> **Definition (LWE):** Given polynomially many samples $(a_i, b_i)$ with $a_i \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^n$ uniform and $b_i = \langle a_i, s \rangle + e_i \bmod q$, $e_i \leftarrow \chi$, recover $s$.

The decision variant asks to distinguish such samples from uniform pairs. Regev proved that solving LWE (even on average, even quantumly) implies solving worst-case GapSVP and SIVP — a remarkable reduction from a random-looking algebraic problem to the geometry of arbitrary lattices [1].

Regev's cryptosystem itself is a direct template for all that follows: the public key is a matrix of LWE samples $b = As + e$; encryption of a bit $m$ picks a random subset-sum of the samples and adds $m\lfloor q/2 \rfloor$; decryption computes the noisy inner product with $s$ and rounds.

### 2.3 Ring-LWE and Cyclotomic Rings

Plain LWE carries a quadratic overhead: keys and ciphertexts are matrices/vectors of dimension $n$. Lyubashevsky, Peikert, and Regev removed this penalty by exploiting algebraic structure, defining **ring-LWE** over the ring of integers of a number field [2]. In practice, the ambient ring is

$$R = \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^n + 1), \qquad R_q = R / qR,$$

with $n$ a power of two (a $2n$-th cyclotomic). RLWE samples are pairs $(a, b = as + e) \in R_q^2$ with $a$ uniform and $e$ drawn coefficient-wise from a small Gaussian. Multiplication in $R_q$ is negacyclic convolution, computable in $O(n \log n)$ via the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) — the same arithmetic engine powering modern libraries [6][7].

> **Theorem (Lyubashevsky–Peikert–Regev):** The RLWE distribution over cyclotomic rings is pseudorandom assuming the hardness of worst-case problems on *ideal lattices* for polynomial-time quantum algorithms [2].

Two technical points matter enormously for implementations. First, the *canonical embedding* $\sigma: R \to \mathbb{C}^{n/2}$ maps the ring into a complex vector space with good geometry; CKKS encoding lives here [5]. Second, the noise analysis must track the *expansion factor* of multiplication in $R$ — the geometry of the ring amplifies errors by roughly $\sqrt{n}$, which propagates into every parameter choice.

| Problem | Ambient object | Hardness basis | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| LWE [1] | $\mathbb{Z}_q^n$ vectors | Worst-case SVP/SIVP | $\tilde{O}(n^2)$ per sample |
| RLWE [2] | Cyclotomic ring $R_q$ | Worst-case ideal-lattice SVP | $\tilde{O}(n)$ per sample via NTT |
| Ideal-lattice SVP | Ideals in $\mathcal{O}_K$ | Worst case (quantum reduction) | — |
| SSSP (sparse subset sum) | Subset-sum instance | Average case (Gentry's squashing) [3] | — |

### 2.4 The Noise Budget: A Unifying View

Every scheme in this thesis encrypts $m$ as $c = \mathrm{Enc}(m)$ such that $\langle c, s \rangle = \text{signal} + \text{noise} \pmod q$, with correct decryption iff $|\text{noise}| < q/(2t)$ for some plaintext modulus or scaling factor. Homomorphic addition adds noise; homomorphic multiplication *multiplies* it (roughly squaring the magnitude in the worst case).

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is constructive: we build a generic RLWE-based somewhat-homomorphic scheme, then introduce the noise-management operators — modulus switching, key switching, rescaling — as disciplined transformations of that scheme's ciphertexts, and finally assemble them into the BGV and CKKS cryptosystems.

### 3.1 A Canonical RLWE Encryption Scheme

Let $R = \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^n+1)$, $q$ a ciphertext modulus, and $\chi$ a narrow error distribution. The secret key is $s \leftarrow \chi$ (ternary or Gaussian; smallness of $s$ is itself a noise-management choice). The public key is an RLWE pair $(a, b = -(as + e))$ — equivalently $(a, b)$ with $b + as \approx 0$.

To encrypt a plaintext polynomial $m \in R$ (with small coefficients), sample $u, e_0, e_1 \leftarrow \chi$ and output

$$c_0 = bu + e_0 + m \cdot \Delta, \qquad c_1 = au + e_1,$$

where $\Delta = \lfloor q/t \rfloor$ scales a plaintext modulus $t$ into the ciphertext space. Decryption computes $c_0 + c_1 s = m\Delta + e'$ and rounds away the error:

$$m = \left\lfloor \frac{c_0 + c_1 s}{\Delta} \right\rceil \bmod t.$$

Correctness holds iff $\|e'\|_\infty < \Delta/2$. Homomorphic addition is component-wise and adds errors: $e'_{\text{add}} = e'_1 + e'_2$. Homomorphic multiplication is tensoring:

$$(c_0 + c_1 s)(d_0 + d_1 s) = c_0 d_0 + (c_0 d_1 + c_1 d_0) s + c_1 d_1 s^2,$$

which yields a degree-2 ciphertext $(e_0, e_1, e_2)$ decryptable under $(1, s, s^2)$ — and an error term whose magnitude is roughly the *product* of the inputs' errors times a ring-expansion factor. A tiny Python sketch of the noise dynamics (over integers, for intuition) is:

```python
import random, math

q = 2**40            # ciphertext modulus
t = 2**8            # plaintext modulus
Delta = q // t      # scaling factor

def enc(m, s, a):
    u  = random.randint(-1, 1)
    e0 = random.randint(-2, 2); e1 = random.randint(-2, 2)
    e  = random.randint(-2, 2)
    b  = (-(a*s) + e) % q
    return ((b*u + e0 + m*Delta) % q, (a*u + e1) % q)

def dec(ct, s):
    c0, c1 = ct
    v = (c0 + c1*s) % q
    v = v - q if v > q//2 else v          # center mod q
    return round(v / Delta) % t

def noise_estimate(ct, s, m):
    c0, c1 = ct
    v = (c0 + c1*s) % q
    v = v - q if v > q//2 else v
    return abs(v - m*Delta)               # must stay < Delta/2

s, a = random.randint(-1,1), random.randint(0, q-1)
ct1, ct2 = enc(5, s, a), enc(7, s, a)
ct_add = ((ct1[0]+ct2[0]) % q, (ct1[1]+ct2[1]) % q)
print("dec(5+7) =", dec(ct_add, s), "| noise:", noise_estimate(ct_add, s, 12))
```

Addition doubles the noise; each multiplication roughly multiplies it by the plaintext magnitude and the secret's norm. The thesis of noise management is: *never let the operator see noise grow unchecked*.

### 3.2 Design Choices Across Schemes

BGV and CKKS differ in one foundational decision — what "signal" means:

- **BGV [4]:** exact arithmetic modulo a plaintext modulus $t$. Ciphertexts carry $(m \cdot \Delta + e)$; modulus switching shrinks $q$ (and proportionally the noise) as levels are consumed.
- **CKKS [5]:** *approximate* arithmetic over $\mathbb{C}^{n/2}$. Ciphertexts carry $(m + e)$ directly, with the message itself scaled by a large factor $\Delta$; the rescaling operation divides out the scale after each multiplication, treating the encryption noise as just another rounding error.

Both need key switching (relinearization) to collapse the degree-2 ciphertext produced by multiplication back to a linear one under the original secret.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Modulus Switching: BGV's Central Innovation

Brakerski, Gentry, and Vaikuntanathan's breakthrough was a noise-management technique so effective it made leveled FHE practical *without* bootstrapping [4]. Suppose a ciphertext $c$ at modulus $q$ decrypts as $\langle c, s \rangle = m\Delta + e \pmod q$ with $\|e\| < B$. Define a smaller modulus $q' < q$ and the switched ciphertext

$$c' = \left\lfloor \frac{q'}{q} \, c \right\rceil \bmod q',$$

where rounding is coefficient-wise. Then $\langle c', s \rangle = m \cdot \frac{q'}{q}\Delta + e' \pmod{q'}$ with $\|e'\| \approx \frac{q'}{q} B + \text{(rounding error)}$. The noise shrinks *proportionally* to the modulus — the ratio $\|e\|/q$, which is what actually matters for correctness, is essentially preserved, but the *absolute* noise drops, restoring headroom for further multiplications at the new modulus.

> **Theorem (BGV modulus switching):** Switching a ciphertext from modulus $q$ to $q'$ reduces the noise magnitude by the factor $q'/q$ (up to an additive rounding term bounded by $\|s\|_1 / 2$), preserving correctness while freeing noise budget for subsequent levels [4].

The scheme is therefore organized as a *ladder* of moduli $q_L > q_{L-1} > \dots > q_0$. Fresh ciphertexts live at the top; after each multiplication (and relinearization), the evaluator switches down one level. A depth-$L$ circuit consumes $L$ levels.

### 4.2 Key Switching and Relinearization

Homomorphic multiplication of $(c_0, c_1)$ and $(d_0, d_1)$ produces the degree-2 ciphertext $(e_0, e_1, e_2)$ decryptable under $(1, s, s^2)$. Left unchecked, ciphertext dimension grows exponentially with multiplicative depth.

Concretely, the evaluation key contains $\mathrm{Enc}_s(s^2 \cdot B^i)$ for a gadget decomposition base $B$ (powers of two or a CRT/RNS decomposition). Relinearization writes $e_2$ in base $B$, $\sum_i e_{2,i} B^i$, and replaces the quadratic term by $\sum_i e_{2,i} \cdot \mathrm{Enc}_s(s^2 B^i)$, yielding a fresh linear ciphertext.

> **Theorem (Key-switching correctness):** Relinearization maps a degree-2 ciphertext with noise $B$ to a linear ciphertext with noise $B + O(\ell \cdot B_{\text{ks}})$, where $B_{\text{ks}}$ is the noise in the evaluation key, and preserves the encrypted message exactly [4].

### 4.3 The BGV Cryptosystem, Assembled

BGV is parameterized by a chain of moduli and supports exact arithmetic modulo $t$ (with $t$ often 2 for Boolean circuits, or a prime for integer arithmetic). The full pipeline per multiplication is:

1. **Tensor:** $(c_0, c_1) \otimes (d_0, d_1) \to (e_0, e_1, e_2)$, noise $\approx B_1 B_2 \cdot \mathrm{poly}(n)$.
2. **Relinearize:** key-switch $(e_0, e_1, e_2)$ to $(f_0, f_1)$, adding decomposition noise.
3. **Modulus-switch:** move from $q_i$ to $q_{i-1}$, scaling noise down by $q_{i-1}/q_i$.

Security rests on RLWE at each level's modulus [2]; the distinguishing advantage compounds only negligibly across levels. Bootstrapping is *optional* — used only as an optimization for very deep circuits, where its amortized per-gate cost can beat extending the modulus chain [4]. This "leveled without bootstrapping" paradigm, implemented in HElib [6], underwrites most practical exact-arithmetic FHE deployments today.

### 4.4 CKKS: Approximate Arithmetic and Rescaling

Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song observed that most real-world computation — statistics, machine learning, signal processing — already tolerates rounding error, so the cryptosystem might as well too [5]. A vector $z \in \mathbb{C}^{n/2}$ is encoded as $m = \lfloor \Delta \cdot \sigma^{-1}(z) \rceil \in R$ via the inverse canonical embedding and encrypted so that $c_0 + c_1 s \approx m$.

Homomorphic multiplication of $(m_1 + e_1)$ and $(m_2 + e_2)$ yields $m_1 m_2 \Delta^2$ — the scale *squares*. The **rescaling** operation

$$\mathrm{RS}(c) = \left\lfloor \frac{1}{\Delta} c \right\rceil \bmod \frac{q}{\Delta}$$

divides out one factor of $\Delta$, simultaneously consuming one modulus level and keeping the message precision constant.

> **Theorem (CKKS precision, informal):** For a circuit of multiplicative depth $L$, the relative error of homomorphic evaluation exceeds the error of the same computation in unencrypted fixed-point arithmetic by at most $O(L)$ bits of precision loss; rescaling is what keeps the loss linear rather than exponential in $L$ [5].

CKKS supports SIMD-style *packing*: one ciphertext encrypts $n/2$ complex slots, and Galois automorphisms $x \mapsto x^{2k+1}$ of the cyclotomic ring implement slot rotations, enabling vectorized encrypted computation. The full RNS variant replaces the single large modulus by a product of word-sized primes, making every operation NTT-friendly and unlocking hardware acceleration [7].

### 4.5 Bootstrapping: Gentry's Blueprint and Its Modern Form

A scheme is **bootstrappable** if it can homomorphically evaluate its own (squashed) decryption circuit plus one additional gate. Gentry's blueprint [3]:

1. Start from a somewhat-homomorphic scheme $E$.
2. *Squash* the decryption circuit (via the sparse-subset-sum trick: publish a large set of field elements whose sparse subset sums to the secret-key-dependent value) so it fits within $E$'s capacity.
3. Publish an encryption of the secret key under its own public key (the *circular security* assumption).
4. To refresh a noisy ciphertext $c$, homomorphically evaluate $\mathrm{Dec}_{\overline{sk}}(c)$ using the encrypted secret key, obtaining an encryption of the same plaintext with *fresh, small* noise.
5. Recurse: any circuit is evaluated by refreshing whenever noise approaches the threshold.

Gentry proved that any bootstrappable SHE scheme yields a *fully* homomorphic scheme — the first in history [3]. The original implementation effort (Gentry–Halevi, 2011) reported roughly 30 minutes per basic bit operation: a proof of possibility, not of practicality.

Modern bootstrapping looks very different. For BGV/BFV, bootstrapping digit-extracts the message via homomorphic modular reduction. For CKKS, bootstrapping must homomorphically evaluate the *modular reduction* $x \mapsto x \bmod q$ on an approximate value — a non-polynomial function — via polynomial approximations of the sine function (exploiting $\sin(2\pi x/q)$-based identities), Chebyshev/minimax approximations, and the RNS-friendly CoeffToSlot/SlotToCoeff transforms [8].

## 5. Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### 5.1 Performance Across Eras

| System | Year | Scheme | Reported metric |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gentry–Halevi implementation [3] | 2011 | Gentry's ideal-lattice FHE | ~30 min per basic bit operation |
| BGV leveled evaluation [4] | 2012 | BGV (no bootstrapping) | $\tilde{O}(\lambda L^3)$ per gate, quasi-linear in $\lambda$ |
| HElib bootstrapping [6] | 2014– | BGV with Smart–Vercauteren packing | Amortized seconds per gate on packed ciphertexts |
| CKKS approximate arithmetic [5] | 2017 | CKKS (leveled) | Logistic regression on encrypted data in minutes |
| RNS-CKKS + bootstrapping [7][8] | 2018–2021 | RNS-CKKS | Bootstrap in seconds; >90-bit precision at depth ~11 [8] |

The trajectory is unambiguous: six orders of magnitude of improvement separate Gentry's first implementation from contemporary RNS-CKKS, driven by modulus switching (which eliminated the need for bootstrapping in leveled settings), NTT-based ring arithmetic, RNS decomposition, and packing.

### 5.2 Security Reductions and Parameter Selection

The security story rests on two pillars. First, the *worst-case to average-case* reductions: Regev's quantum reduction from GapSVP/SIVP to LWE [1], and the LPR reduction from worst-case ideal-lattice problems to RLWE [2]. Second, *concrete cryptanalysis*: the Homomorphic Encryption Standard (2018, updated 2021) tabulates RLWE parameter sets targeting 128-bit security against the best known primal and dual lattice attacks (BKZ with sieving).

> **Theorem (FHE from bootstrappable SHE):** Let $E$ be a public-key encryption scheme that is somewhat homomorphic and can homomorphically evaluate its own (squashed) decryption circuit plus one NAND gate. Then, under a circular-security assumption on the encrypted secret key, there exists a fully homomorphic encryption scheme [3].

*Proof sketch.* Publish $\overline{sk} = \mathrm{Enc}_{pk}(sk)$. Given a noisy ciphertext $c$ encrypting $m$, compute $c' = \mathrm{Eval}_{pk}(\mathrm{Dec}, \overline{sk}, c)$. By homomorphism, $c'$ encrypts $\mathrm{Dec}_{sk}(c) = m$, and because $\mathrm{Dec}$ is evaluated *fresh*, the noise in $c'$ depends only on the decryption circuit's depth — a fixed constant — not on the history of $c$. Evaluating any circuit then proceeds gate by gate, refreshing whenever the noise budget is nearly exhausted. ∎

A subtlety worth stating plainly: bootstrapping's circular-security assumption (encrypting the secret key under its own public key) is not implied by standard RLWE; it is an additional, widely-believed but unproven assumption. Leveled schemes avoid it entirely by fixing depth in advance [4].

### 5.3 What the Reductions Do Not Cover

The reductions guarantee security against key recovery, not against *misuse*. All mainstream FHE schemes are IND-CPA secure only; homomorphism is fundamentally incompatible with IND-CCA2 (an adversary can maul the challenge ciphertext and query the decryption oracle).

## 6. Limitations

1. **Bootstrapping remains the bottleneck.** Even state-of-the-art CKKS bootstrapping consumes roughly 10–12 multiplicative levels and seconds of compute; deep circuits that bootstrap frequently remain far slower than plaintext computation [8].
2. **Parameter rigidity.** Leveled schemes must fix the circuit depth before key generation. Underestimate $L$ and evaluation fails mid-circuit; overestimate it and every operation pays for unused levels in key size and latency [4].
3. **No verifiability or access control.** FHE provides confidentiality of inputs, not integrity of computation: a malicious server can return garbage, and the client cannot detect it without additional machinery (SNARKs, MACs).
4. **Plaintext-space constraints.** BGV/BFV compute modulo $t$ — comparisons, branching, and division require expensive workarounds (digit decomposition, polynomial approximations). CKKS handles real arithmetic natively but only *approximately*, with precision bounded by parameter choices [5].
5. **Key management at scale.** Evaluation keys (relinearization, rotation, bootstrapping keys) are large — often hundreds of megabytes — and must be transmitted to the evaluator, complicating deployment.
6. **Assumption fragility.** Ideal-lattice and RLWE hardness, while extensively studied, lack the decades of cryptanalytic battle-testing that factoring enjoys; the sparse-subset-sum assumption in Gentry's original squashing step was the weakest link of the first generation [3].
7. **Side channels.** Timing and power analysis of NTT-heavy implementations, and the CKKS decryption-leakage issue noted above, demand constant-time, noise-flooded implementations for production use [6].

---

## 7. Conclusion

Fully homomorphic encryption traveled from a thirty-year-old impossibility conjecture to deployable libraries in a single decade. The intellectual spine of that journey is the RLWE problem [2]: worst-case ideal-lattice hardness distilled into efficient ring arithmetic.
