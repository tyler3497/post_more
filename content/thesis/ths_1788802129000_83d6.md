# Node Differential Privacy for Graph Analytics: Lipschitz Extensions via Projection, Smooth Sensitivity, Private Degree Sequences and Triangle Counts, and Bounded vs Unbounded Adjacency

## Abstract

Node differential privacy protects the full presence of a vertex — together with every incident edge — in released graph statistics, a strictly stronger guarantee than edge privacy. Its difficulty is analytic: under node adjacency, the global sensitivity of natural queries such as edge counts, triangle counts, and degree sequences is polynomial in the number of vertices, rendering the classical Laplace mechanism useless. This thesis develops the four pillars of the modern node-privacy toolkit. First, we formalize bounded and unbounded node adjacency and explain why the distinction reshapes sensitivity analysis. Second, we construct Lipschitz extensions of statistics on bounded-degree graph families — via maximum flow, linear programming, and the McShane–Whitney extension — matching the true statistic on well-behaved inputs with low global sensitivity everywhere. Third, we present graph projection operators and the generalized exponential mechanism that selects the degree bound privately. Fourth, we treat smooth sensitivity as instance-dependent noise calibration, and show how constrained inference via isotonic regression recovers accurate private degree sequences. We close with utility theorems, limitations, and open problems.

## 1 Introduction

Releasing statistics of a social network — its degree distribution, its clustering coefficient, the number of triangles — while protecting each participant is one of the oldest applied challenges in differential privacy. Naive anonymization fails: an adversary with modest auxiliary knowledge can re-identify vertices in a released graph [7]. Differential privacy offers a principled alternative, but graphs admit several distinct adjacency notions, and the choice determines everything about what is achievable.

*Edge differential privacy* declares two graphs adjacent when they differ in a single edge — hiding one relationship, but not a vertex and its entire incident structure. *Node differential privacy* instead declares two graphs adjacent when one is obtained from the other by removing a vertex together with all incident edges, hiding an individual's complete footprint [2].

The cost of the stronger guarantee is sensitivity. Under node adjacency, removing one high-degree vertex can destroy `Θ(n²)` triangles and shift the degree sequence by `Θ(n)`. Classical noise calibration to *global* sensitivity therefore drowns the signal. Three mathematical ideas rescue the situation:

1. **Lipschitz extensions.** Define the statistic only on `D`-bounded-degree graphs, where its node sensitivity is small, and extend it to all graphs with the same Lipschitz constant [2][3].
2. **Projection.** Map the input graph onto the bounded-degree family via a projection operator, trading a controllable approximation error for bounded sensitivity [2].
3. **Smooth sensitivity.** Calibrate noise to an instance-dependent, differentially private upper bound on *local* sensitivity, so typical low-degree graphs receive little noise [1][6].

These are complemented by **constrained inference**: noisy statistics can be post-processed — without spending privacy budget — to satisfy structural constraints such as monotonicity of degree sequences, dramatically reducing error [4]. Throughout, the **bounded versus unbounded** node adjacency distinction reshapes sensitivity analysis and determines which constructions are well-defined.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Adjacency relations on graphs

Let `𝒢ₙ` denote simple undirected graphs on a fixed vertex set `[n]`, and `𝒢` graphs on arbitrary vertex sets. Differential privacy on graphs requires an adjacency relation `∼`:

> **Definition (Edge adjacency).** `G ∼ₑ G'` if `G'` is obtained from `G` by adding or removing exactly one edge.

> **Definition (Unbounded node adjacency).** `G ∼ₙ G'` if `G'` is obtained from `G` by adding or removing exactly one vertex, together with all edges incident to it.

The qualifier *unbounded* refers to the changing vertex set; the *bounded* variant fixes the vertex set and allows arbitrary rewiring of the edges incident to a single vertex [2][3]:

> **Definition (Bounded node adjacency).** `G ∼_{nb} G'` if `V(G) = V(G')` and the symmetric difference of `E(G)` and `E(G')` is contained in the star of a single vertex.

A mechanism `𝒜` is `ε`-differentially private under `∼` if for all adjacent `G ∼ G'` and all measurable output sets `S`, `Pr[𝒜(G) ∈ S] ≤ e^ε · Pr[𝒜(G') ∈ S]`.

The distinction is not cosmetic. Under unbounded adjacency, the degree sequence has global sensitivity `Θ(n)`: deleting a vertex of degree `n−1` shifts every remaining degree. Under bounded adjacency the vertex set is fixed, simplifying the extension constructions of [3], though sensitivity remains the same order.

### 2.2 Sensitivity: global, local, smooth

For a real-valued query `f`, the **global sensitivity** is `GS(f) = max_{G∼G'} |f(G) − f(G')|`, and the **local sensitivity** at `G` is `LS_f(G) = max_{G'∼G} |f(G) − f(G')|` [1]. The Laplace mechanism `f(G) + Lap(GS(f)/ε)` is `ε`-DP, but for node-DP triangle counting `GS = Θ(n²)`, and for the number of edges `GS = Θ(n)`.

> **Theorem:** *(Nissim–Raskhodnikova–Smith [1]).* For `β > 0`, a function `S: 𝒳 → ℝ⁺` is a `β`-smooth upper bound on the local sensitivity of `f` if `S(x) ≥ LS_f(x)` for all `x` and `S(x) ≤ e^β · S(x')` for all neighboring `x, x'`. The pointwise smallest such function is the **smooth sensitivity** `S*_{f,β}(x) = max_{x'} LS_f(x') · e^{−β·d(x,x')}`, where `d` is the adjacency distance.

Releasing `f(x) + (S(x)/α)·Z` with an admissible noise distribution `Z` is private: Cauchy noise yields pure `ε`-DP, while Laplace or Gaussian noise yield `(ε, δ)`-DP with less noise [1][6]. Smooth sensitivity is the instance-dependent refinement that makes node privacy practical: a sparse social graph has tiny local sensitivity even though the global sensitivity is enormous.

| Adjacency | Triangle count `GS` | Edge count `GS` | Degree sequence `GS` (ℓ₁) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Edge | `n − 2` | `1` | `2` |
| Bounded node (fixed `[n]`) | `Θ(n²)` | `n − 1` | `Θ(n)` |
| Unbounded node | `Θ(n²)` | `n − 1` | `Θ(n)` |
| Node, restricted to `D`-bounded graphs | `O(D²)` | `D` | `O(D)` |

The last row is the key: on bounded-degree families, node sensitivity collapses to a function of `D` rather than `n` [2][3].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology follows a four-stage pipeline — project, extend, select, calibrate — mirroring the architecture of the strongest known node-DP mechanisms [2][3]:

1. **Project.** Choose a degree bound `D ≪ n` and a projection `π_D : 𝒢 → 𝒢_{≤D}` mapping any graph to a nearby graph of maximum degree at most `D`.
2. **Extend.** For a statistic `f` with node sensitivity `Δ_D(f)` on `D`-bounded graphs, construct a Lipschitz extension `f̃: 𝒢 → ℝ` with `f̃|_{𝒢_{≤D}} = f` and global node sensitivity `Δ_D(f)`.
3. **Select.** Choose `D` privately — e.g., via the *generalized exponential mechanism* over a family of extensions `{f̃_D}` — jointly minimizing projection bias and noise [3].
4. **Calibrate.** Release `f̃(G) + Noise`, with scale set by global sensitivity (Laplace), by smooth sensitivity of the extension (Cauchy/Laplace), or by post-processing a noisy high-dimensional statistic under structural constraints [1][4].

![Node-DP pipeline: projection, Lipschitz extension, and smooth-sensitivity noise calibration](/thesis/ths_1788802129000_83d6-0.webp)

*Figure 1: the node-DP pipeline. A raw graph is projected onto the bounded-degree family, a Lipschitz extension of the query is evaluated, and noise calibrated to smooth sensitivity is added before release.*

The mathematical engine of stages 1–2 is the **Lipschitz extension theorem**: any `L`-Lipschitz `f: 𝒴 → ℝ` on a subset `𝒴` of a metric space `(𝒳, d)` extends to `f̃: 𝒳 → ℝ` with the same Lipschitz constant — the classical McShane–Whitney construction:

```haskell
-- McShane--Whitney extension of an L-Lipschitz f : Y -> R to all of X
mcshaneWhitney :: (Metric x) => (x -> Double) -> Double -> x -> Double
mcshaneWhitney f lip x = inf [ f y + lip * dist x y | y <- domainY ]
-- dual (supremum) form: sup [ f y - lip * dist x y | y <- domainY ]
```

In the graph setting, `𝒳` is the space of all graphs with the **vertex distance** `d(G, G')` — the minimum number of vertices whose deletion from both graphs yields identical graphs — and `𝒴` is the family of `D`-bounded graphs [2]. The challenge is entirely computational: the infimum ranges over exponentially many graphs, so we need *efficiently computable* extensions for specific statistics.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Projection onto bounded-degree graphs and flow-based extensions

The simplest statistic is the edge count `f(G) = |E(G)|`. On `D`-bounded graphs its node sensitivity is `D`. Kasiviswanathan et al. [2] construct a Lipschitz extension via a flow network: for `G = (V, E)`, build a bipartite flow graph with source `s` connected to each vertex copy `v` with capacity `D`, infinite-capacity edges `(u, v')` for each `(u,v) ∈ E`, and each `v'` to the sink `t` with capacity `D`. Then `f̃(G)/2`, where `f̃(G)` is the max-flow value, is a Lipschitz extension of the edge count with constant `D`, equal to `|E(G)|` whenever `G` is `D`-bounded.

Projection complements extension. A projection `π_D` maps an arbitrary graph to a `D`-bounded graph minimizing vertex-distance distortion — e.g., deleting edges incident to vertices of degree exceeding `D` under a fixed, data-independent rule. The composed estimator `f̃(π_D(G))` has bias bounded by `L·d(G, π_D(G))` and global sensitivity `L`, so the Laplace mechanism applies directly. For graphs with light-tailed degree distributions (formalized as `α`-decay), projection error is small with high probability, and the private edge-count estimator achieves error `Õ(D/ε)` rather than `Õ(n/ε)` [2].

### 4.2 Smooth sensitivity and admissible noise

Lipschitz extensions give low *global* sensitivity, but their constant `Δ_D(f)` still scales with `D`. Smooth sensitivity [1] refines the picture: calibrate noise to a smooth upper bound on the *local* sensitivity at the actual input.

Recall `S*_{f,β}(x) = max_{x'} LS_f(x') · e^{−β·d(x,x')}`. Two facts make this usable. First, with `A^{(k)}(x) = max_{d(x,x')≤k} LS_f(x')`, the function `U(x) = max_k e^{−βk} A^{(k)}(x)` is a `β`-smooth upper bound on local sensitivity (NRS07, Claim 3.2) — the "distance-`k`" formulation that efficient constructions target. Second, admissible noise (NRS07, Lemmas 2.7, 2.10): a density `h` stable under small translations (sliding) and scalings (dilation) makes `f(x) + S(x)·Z/α` differentially private — `(ε, δ)`-DP for Laplace/Gaussian `Z`, pure `ε`-DP for Cauchy `Z` [1][6].

A subtle and frequently misunderstood point: *no subgaussian distribution can give pure DP when scaled to smooth sensitivity* — the dilation property forces heavy tails, hence Cauchy [1]. In the graph setting, smooth sensitivity of the triangle count at `G` is governed by the maximum local sensitivity within vertex-distance `k` — `O((D_k)²)` where `D_k` is the largest degree attainable within distance `k`. For sparse real-world graphs this is dramatically smaller than the global `Θ(n²)`.

```python
import math

def smooth_upper_bound(ls_at_distance, beta, k_max):
    """ls_at_distance[k] = max local sensitivity within vertex-distance k."""
    return max(math.exp(-beta * k) * ls_at_distance[k]
               for k in range(k_max + 1))

def cauchy_release(value, S, eps):
    # NRS07 Lemma 2.10: Cauchy noise scaled by S/eps gives pure eps-DP
    import random
    return value + (S / eps) * math.tan(math.pi * (random.random() - 0.5))
```

### 4.3 Private degree sequences via constrained inference

The degree sequence is the workhorse statistic of network science: power-law fitting, anomaly detection, and graph classification all begin there. Under **edge** adjacency its global ℓ₁-sensitivity is just `2`, so the Laplace mechanism applies directly — but the raw noisy sequence is useless: unsorted, with negative entries, violating every structural constraint of a degree sequence.

Hay et al. [4] showed that a *privacy-free* post-processing step — **constrained inference** — repairs this. Given the noisy sequence `S̃`, solve the isotonic regression `S̄ = argmin_{non-increasing S ≥ 0} ‖S̃ − S‖₂`, computable in linear time by the pool-adjacent-violators algorithm. Post-processing cannot degrade differential privacy, yet the ℓ₂ error drops from scaling with the number of *vertices* to scaling with the number of *distinct degrees* [4] — an enormous win on heavy-tailed networks where most vertices share small degrees.

Under **node** adjacency the picture hardens: the degree sequence has global node sensitivity `Θ(n)`, so naive Laplace noise destroys it. This is where the FOCS 2016 machinery [3] applies: Raskhodnikova and Smith construct *efficient Lipschitz extensions for high-dimensional graph statistics*, including node-private degree distributions, and combine them with the **generalized exponential mechanism** — extending the exponential mechanism to score functions of arbitrary sensitivity — which privately selects the degree bound `D` minimizing projection bias plus noise. The resulting node-DP degree-distribution estimator uses the constrained-inference post-processing of [4] as its final stage, since isotonic regression remains privacy-free.

### 4.4 Triangle counts and subgraph counts under node DP

Counting small subgraphs — triangles, `k`-stars, `k`-cycles — is the canonical hard problem of node-DP graph analytics. On `D`-bounded graphs, the node sensitivity of the number of (not necessarily induced) copies of a `k`-vertex template `H` is `O(D^{k−1})` [2]: deleting one vertex destroys at most the copies containing it. Kasiviswanathan et al. give an **LP-based Lipschitz extension** `v_LP(G)`: assign a variable `x_C ∈ [0,1]` to every copy `C` of `H` in `G`, maximize `∑_C x_C` subject to each vertex participating in at most `Δ_H` copies (the maximum possible in a `D`-bounded graph). Then `v_LP(G) ≤ f_H(G)` always, with equality on `D`-bounded graphs, and its global node sensitivity is `Δ_H` — yielding a node-DP release with Laplace noise of scale `Δ_H/ε` [2]. For triangles the sensitivity on `D`-bounded graphs is `O(D²)`, and the LP extension gives a polynomial-time node-DP triangle counter whose accuracy hinges on `α`-decay: the total error is `Õ(D²/ε)` plus small projection bias, versus `Θ(n²/ε)` for global sensitivity [2].

A recent advance pushes the same philosophy to a statistic long thought intractable: Arora et al. [5] design the first node-DP algorithm for the number of connected components — a statistic *not* robust to adding one well-connected vertex. Their Lipschitz extensions are built on the **forest polytope**, exploiting the fact that a graph with no induced `Δ`-star has a spanning forest of maximum degree `Δ`; the resulting error is `Õ(Δ*·log log n/ε)`, where `Δ*` is the minimum possible maximum degree of a spanning forest [5].

### 4.5 Bounded vs unbounded node adjacency, revisited

We return to the bounded/unbounded distinction with the full toolkit in hand, because it determines which constructions are well-defined:

1. **Dimension stability.** Under bounded adjacency the vertex set `[n]` is fixed, so vector-valued statistics have fixed dimension and the vertex distance needs no alignment. Raskhodnikova–Smith [3] exploit this: their extensions live on `𝒢ₙ` with `n` fixed. Under unbounded adjacency, comparing statistics across different `n` needs padding conventions — workable [2], but more delicate.
2. **Sensitivity transfers; the metric changes.** Both notions give `Θ(n)`-scale global sensitivity, and the Lipschitz constant of a statistic on `D`-bounded graphs is identical under both — so extension constructions transfer, with only the *distance* in the McShane–Whitney formula changing.
3. **Composition and post-processing are unaffected.** Both adjacencies satisfy the standard composition theorems, and constrained inference [4] is valid under either.
4. **Which to use?** Unbounded node DP is the stronger, more conservative guarantee — it hides whether an individual is in the dataset at all — and is the right default for social-network release. Bounded node DP suits settings where the vertex set is public (a fixed sensor network, a published roster) and only relationships are sensitive; it admits cleaner high-dimensional extensions [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Utility theorems

> **Theorem:** *(Node-DP subgraph counting [2]).* Let `f_H` count copies of a fixed connected `k`-vertex template `H`. For any degree bound `D` and privacy parameter `ε`, there is a polynomial-time `ε`-node-DP mechanism whose output on an `α`-decaying graph `G` satisfies `|𝒜(G) − f_H(G)| = Õ(Δ_H/ε)` with high probability, where `Δ_H = O(D^{k−1})` is the node sensitivity of `f_H` on `D`-bounded graphs.

*Proof sketch.* Apply the LP extension `v_LP` of Section 4.4 and the Laplace mechanism at scale `Δ_H/ε`. On `D`-bounded graphs `v_LP = f_H` exactly. For `α`-decaying `G`, truncating vertices of degree above `D` destroys only a small fraction of copies of `H`, so the projection bias is `o(f_H(G))` while the noise is `Õ(Δ_H/ε)`. ∎

> **Theorem:** *(Smooth-sensitivity calibration [1]).* Let `S` be a `β`-smooth upper bound on the local sensitivity of `f`. Then `𝒜(x) = f(x) + Lap(1)·2S(x)/ε` is `(ε, δ)`-DP, and with Cauchy noise of scale `S(x)·O(1/ε)` it is `ε`-DP.

*Proof sketch.* Admissibility (sliding + dilation) bounds the output density ratio by `e^ε` for neighboring inputs except on a `δ`-probability event, using `S(x) ≤ e^β S(x')` and `|f(x) − f(x')| ≤ S(x)`. ∎

> **Theorem:** *(Node-DP connected components [5]).* There is a polynomial-time `ε`-node-DP algorithm estimating the number of connected components with additive error `Õ(Δ*·log log n/ε)` with probability `1 − o(1)`, where `Δ*` is the smallest possible maximum degree of a spanning forest of `G`.

*Proof sketch.* Lipschitz extensions of the spanning-forest size are built via the forest polytope; the key lemma (no induced `Δ`-star ⇒ spanning forest of degree ≤ `Δ`) makes the extension exact on the largest monotone graph families, and `O(Δ)`-Lipschitzness means Laplace noise at that scale suffices. ∎

### 5.2 Comparative error landscape

The table summarizes noise scales (hiding constants and log factors) for releasing the triangle count `T(G)` under the surveyed mechanisms, on a graph with `n` vertices and effective degree bound `D`:

| Mechanism | Adjacency | Noise scale | Requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Laplace on global sensitivity | Node | `Θ(n²/ε)` | None — vacuous on large graphs |
| LP Lipschitz extension [2] | Node (unbounded) | `Õ(D²/ε)` | Polynomial-time LP; `α`-decay for accuracy |
| Flow + projection [2] | Node | `Õ(D/ε)` + bias | Edge counts only |
| Smooth sensitivity [1] | Node | `S*_{T,β}(G)/ε` (instance-dep.) | Efficient `β`-smooth bound |
| Generalized exponential mech. [3] | Node (bounded) | `min_D (bias(D) + Õ(D²/ε))` | Private selection of `D` |
| Laplace + isotonic regression [4] | Edge | `Õ(√k/ε)`, `k` = #distinct degrees | Edge adjacency only |

The gap between the first two rows — `n²` versus `D²` — is the entire contribution of the Lipschitz-extension program: on a million-vertex social graph with effective degree bound `D = 10³`, it is the difference between noise of order `10¹²` and `10⁶`.

### 5.3 A note on the empirical record

Hay et al. [4] validate constrained inference on real and synthetic networks: variance and bias shrink as graph size grows, and power-law exponents fit to the private degree sequence nearly match those fit to the true sequence. The smooth-sensitivity framework has been revisited for average-case mean estimation [6], confirming that instance-dependent calibration strictly dominates global-sensitivity baselines on concentrated data. A survey of private graph-data release [7] catalogs further mechanisms — including `wPINQ`-based triangle-by-degree counts with MCMC synthetic-graph generation — and is a useful entry point to the broader literature.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Computational cost of extensions.** The LP-based extension for subgraph counting has one variable per copy of `H`; for triangles this is polynomial, but for larger templates it becomes impractical. No generic efficient Lipschitz extension is known for arbitrary statistics — each requires bespoke combinatorial insight [2][3][5].
2. **The degree-bound dilemma.** All utility guarantees are parameterized by `D`: too small incurs large projection bias on heavy-tailed graphs, too large inflates noise. The generalized exponential mechanism [3] selects `D` privately but adds `O(log |𝒟|/ε)` overhead and requires the candidate set fixed in advance.
3. **Smooth sensitivity is not always computable.** The framework [1] is only as useful as our ability to bound `max_{d(x,x')≤k} LS_f(x')` efficiently. For several natural statistics no efficient smooth bounds are known, and the sample-and-aggregate workaround changes the estimand.
4. **Pure vs approximate DP.** Instance-dependent noise with subgaussian tails cannot achieve pure `ε`-DP [1]; one must choose between the heavier Cauchy tails of pure DP and the `(ε, δ)` guarantee of Laplace/Gaussian calibration.
5. **Bounded adjacency's weaker semantics.** Results proved under bounded node adjacency [3] do not automatically transfer to the unbounded setting when the vertex set itself is sensitive; padding and alignment issues can introduce subtle privacy bugs.
6. **High-dimensional statistics.** Degree sequences under node DP remain expensive: even with Lipschitz extensions, noise scales with dimension-dependent sensitivity, and constrained inference [4] only repairs the damage after the fact. Truly high-dimensional node-private graph release is still largely open [7].

## 7 Conclusion

Node differential privacy demands that we hide not a single relationship but an entire participant — and the mathematics must work much harder to oblige. The program surveyed here replaces the blunt instrument of global sensitivity with a precision toolkit: **projection** reduces arbitrary graphs to bounded-degree families; **Lipschitz extensions** — via flows, linear programs, the forest polytope, and the McShane–Whitney construction — transport low sensitivity from those families to all graphs; **smooth sensitivity** calibrates noise to the instance at hand; the **generalized exponential mechanism** privately tunes the degree bound; and **constrained inference** squeezes remaining structure out of noisy high-dimensional releases without spending budget. The bounded-versus-unbounded adjacency distinction, often treated as a footnote, in fact shapes which constructions are well-defined and which guarantees are meaningful.

Open problems abound: efficient Lipschitz extensions for richer statistics (motif spectra, spectral properties), instance-optimal smooth bounds for subgraph counts, and closing the gap between bounded and unbounded node-DP utility. But the trajectory is clear — from the `Θ(n²)` noise of naive mechanisms to the `Õ(D²)` and instance-dependent scales achievable today — and it demonstrates that strong, per-person privacy on graph data is not only definable but increasingly practical.
