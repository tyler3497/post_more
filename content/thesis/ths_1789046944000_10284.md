# Indistinguishability Obfuscation: From Virtual-Black-Box Impossibility through GGH13 Multilinear Maps to the Functional-Encryption Construction

## Abstract

Program obfuscation asks whether code can be made unintelligible while preserving its input–output behavior. Barak et al. showed that the strongest formalization — virtual-black-box (VBB) obfuscation — is impossible for general circuits, a result that appeared to close the field [2]. The candidate construction of Garg, Gentry, Halevi, Raykova, Sahai, and Waters revived it through a weaker but still extraordinarily powerful notion: *indistinguishability obfuscation* (iO), which demands only that obfuscations of any two functionally equivalent, equal-size circuits be computationally indistinguishable [1]. This thesis reconstructs the intellectual arc from impossibility to construction. We formalize VBB and its unobfuscatable counterexample families; present multilinear jigsaw puzzles and the GGH13 graded-encoding framework that made candidate iO possible [3]; detail the NC¹-to-P/poly bootstrap via fully homomorphic encryption and the derivation of functional encryption for all circuits from iO [1]; survey the cryptanalytic response — zeroizing and annihilation attacks [4][7] — and the weak-map and graph-induced defenses that followed [5][6][8]; and conclude with the equivalence loop closed by compact functional encryption and the first iO constructions from well-founded assumptions [9][10].

## 1 Introduction

Few questions in cryptography are as intuitively compelling as *program obfuscation*: can we transform a program into one that computes the same function but reveals nothing about its internal structure — its algorithms, its hardcoded secrets, its design choices? A general-purpose obfuscator would yield signatures without trusted setup, deniable encryption, and traitor tracing with optimal parameters [1][12].

The modern formal study began with Barak et al. [2]. Their strongest definition, *virtual-black-box* (VBB) obfuscation, requires that anything learnable from the obfuscated program be learnable by a simulator with only oracle access to its input–output behavior — the code teaches nothing beyond what running it teaches. And they showed it cannot exist: *unconditionally*, for all polynomial-size circuits [2].

> **Theorem (VBB impossibility, informal [2]).** There exists no efficient obfuscator satisfying the virtual-black-box property for all polynomial-size circuits.

For over a decade this impossibility cast a long shadow. Then, in 2013, Garg, Gentry, Halevi, Raykova, Sahai, and Waters proposed a candidate for a *weaker* notion — *indistinguishability obfuscation* (iO), earlier introduced by Barak et al. and shown by Goldwasser and Rothblum to be "best possible" [1]. Where VBB demands that code hide *everything* not inferable from behavior, iO demands only that the obfuscator hide *which* of two equivalent programs it was given: it concedes that some programs are inherently leaky, and asks only that the obfuscation procedure itself leak nothing *additional*.

What followed was one of the most dramatic episodes in modern cryptography: a candidate built on novel *multilinear maps* [3], an explosion of applications, devastating cryptanalysis of the maps' algebraic structure [4][7], defensive redesigns [5][6], and — in 2020 — constructions from standard assumptions [9][10]. This thesis presents that story with full technical precision: the impossibility proof's architecture, the GGHRSW construction in three steps, the cryptanalysis, and the functional-encryption construction that made iO the field's "cryptographic hub."

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Obfuscation notions

Let $\mathcal{C} = \{\mathcal{C}_\lambda\}$ be a class of circuits. An *obfuscator* $\mathcal{O}$ for $\mathcal{C}$ is a (possibly randomized) polynomial-time algorithm such that for every $C \in \mathcal{C}$: (1) **functionality preservation** — $\mathcal{O}(C)$ computes the same function as $C$; (2) **polynomial slowdown** — $|\mathcal{O}(C)| \le \mathrm{poly}(|C|, \lambda)$. The security properties distinguish the notions:

| Notion | Security requirement | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Virtual black-box (VBB) [2] | $\forall$ PPT $A$, $\exists$ PPT simulator $S$: $A(\mathcal{O}(C)) \approx_c S^C(1^\lambda)$ | **Impossible** for general circuits |
| Indistinguishability (iO) [1] | $C_0 \equiv C_1$, $\|C_0\|=\|C_1\| \Rightarrow \mathcal{O}(C_0) \approx_c \mathcal{O}(C_1)$ | Candidate constructions; 2020 from standard assumptions [9][10] |
| Best-possible obfuscation [11] | Hides at least as much as *any* efficient obfuscator | Equivalent to iO (efficiency-respecting sense) |
| Differing-inputs / extractability | Stronger variants for specific applications | Separated from iO; subtle definitional issues |

Goldwasser and Rothblum showed iO is *best possible*: indistinguishability hides everything any efficient procedure could hide, and anything iO fails to hide is unhideable by definition [11] — reframing the VBB impossibility as a calibration of what obfuscation can mean, not its death.

### 2.2 The VBB impossibility

Barak et al. construct an *unobfuscatable function family*: two programs whose code jointly reveals a secret bit, though oracle access to either alone reveals nothing [2] — combining a "secret tester" with an encrypted payload:

```python
# Sketch of the BGI+01 unobfuscatable ensemble (conceptual)
def C_alpha_beta(x):          # program family 1
    if x == alpha:            # alpha: random n-bit secret
        return beta           # beta: random secret bit
    return 0

def D_alpha(ciphertext):      # program family 2
    # Homomorphically evaluates C_alpha_beta on encrypted input, then decrypts:
    # leaks beta only from *both* programs' code jointly
    return decrypt(sk, hom_eval(C_alpha_beta, ciphertext))
```

An adversary holding *both* programs' code extracts $\beta$ by feeding an encryption of $\alpha$ through the homomorphic evaluation in $D_\alpha$. But any simulator with only *oracle* access to $C_{\alpha,\beta}$ cannot find $\alpha$ (hidden among $2^n$ possibilities), hence cannot extract $\beta$. No single simulator handles the joint distribution of the two programs' code — so no VBB obfuscator exists for the class containing them [2]. The proof is unconditional: it defeats the *definition itself*, not any computational assumption.

The lesson: VBB demands too much. Programs *inherently* encoding unhideable structure cannot all be sanitized. iO sidesteps the trap by never promising to hide what is unhideable — only that the *choice of representation* among equivalent programs leaks nothing.

### 2.3 Functional encryption, informally

*Functional encryption* (FE) generalizes public-key encryption: ciphertexts encrypt inputs $x$, and keys $SK_C$ are issued for circuits $C$; decrypting $CT_x$ with $SK_C$ reveals $C(x)$ and nothing else about $x$ [1]. As we shall see, iO and FE for general circuits are deeply intertwined: GGHRSW showed iO (+ PKE + NIZK) yields FE for all circuits [1], while compact FE was later shown to *imply* iO — closing an equivalence loop [10].

## 3 Methodology

The GGHRSW candidate proceeds in three staged steps, each reducing a harder obfuscation problem to a more tractable one [1].

### Step 1: iO for NC¹ from Multilinear Jigsaw Puzzles

The first step builds a candidate iO for *NC¹ circuits* — polynomial size, $O(\log n)$ depth — on a new algebraic assumption. The key move is Barrington's theorem: every NC¹ circuit converts into an equivalent *matrix branching program* of polynomial length [11] — matrix pairs $(A_{i,0}, A_{i,1})_{i=1}^m$ whose ordered product, selected by input bits, determines the output.

The construction *randomizes* the branching program — sandwiching each matrix pair between random invertible matrices and scaling by random scalars — so the matrices reveal nothing beyond the program's length and behavior. This randomization is protected by *Multilinear Jigsaw Puzzles* (MJP), a simplified variant of multilinear maps (§4.2): matrices are encoded at graded levels, evaluation multiplies encodings level-by-level, and only the top-level result is zero-tested [1][3].

```haskell
-- Matrix branching program evaluation (conceptual)
type Matrix = [[Integer]]
type BP = ([Matrix], [Matrix])
evalBP :: BP -> (Int -> Int -> Int) -> [Bool] -> Bool
evalBP (zeros, ones) sel x = product == identity
  where
    mats    = [ if x !! sel i j then ones !! i else zeros !! i | (i,j) <- steps ]
    product = foldl1 matMul mats
```

### Step 2: Bootstrapping NC¹ to P/poly via FHE

The second step is a general *bootstrapping theorem*: iO for NC¹ plus FHE whose decryption lies in NC¹ yields iO for all polynomial-size circuits [1]. To obfuscate an arbitrary circuit $C$: generate $(pk, sk)$, encrypt the circuit description $ct \leftarrow \mathsf{Enc}(pk, C)$, and obfuscate (with the NC¹ obfuscator) the circuit $D_{sk}$ which on input $x$ homomorphically evaluates the universal circuit on $ct$ and decrypts.

Since FHE decryption is in NC¹, $D_{sk}$ is an NC¹ circuit [1]. The only component depending on $C$ is $ct$, whose semantic security hides $C$ entirely; $D_{sk}$ depends on $C$ only through its *size*. Two equivalent equal-size circuits thus yield indistinguishable obfuscations — exactly the iO guarantee. This bootstrapping pattern, reducing general obfuscation to obfuscation of a weak class, became one of the most reused techniques in the area.

### Step 3: From iO to functional encryption for all circuits

The third step constructs *functional encryption for all circuits* from iO for circuits, PKE, and NIZK [1], with *succinct ciphertexts* (sublinear in circuit size) — unlocking reusable garbled circuits and deniable encryption [12]. The design obfuscates a program decrypting with one of two embedded keys depending on a hidden mode bit; the iO guarantee lets the security proof *switch modes* between hybrids undetectably, the two programs being functionally equivalent. NIZK proofs bind ciphertexts to claimed plaintexts, preventing malleability. The proof proceeds through hybrids, each adjacent pair differing by an obfuscation of equivalent circuits (iO security) or encryptions of equal-length messages (PKE security) [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Branching programs and why NC¹ suffices

Barrington's theorem: bounded-width, polynomial-length branching programs recognize exactly the languages in NC¹ [11]. GGHRSW exploits this twice — to restrict the algebraic machinery to a manageable class, and because matrix branching programs admit Kilian-style *randomization* hiding matrix entries information-theoretically while preserving their product [1].

```rust
// Graded-encoding API sketch (GGH13-style), conceptual
struct GradedEncoding { value: RingElem, level: LevelSet }

impl GradedEncoding {
    fn add(a: &Self, b: &Self) -> Option<Self> {   // levels must match
        (a.level == b.level).then(|| Self { value: a.value + b.value, level: a.level })
    }
    fn mul(a: &Self, b: &Self) -> Option<Self> {   // levels must be disjoint
        a.level.disjoint(&b.level).then(|| Self { value: a.value * b.value,
                                                level: a.level.union(&b.level) })
    }
    fn is_zero_top(e: &Self) -> Option<bool> {     // only at top level [kappa]
        (e.level == LevelSet::top()).then(|| e.value.is_small_multiple_of_zt())
    }
}
```

The randomized branching program is encoded *level by level*: matrix $i$'s encodings sit at singleton level $\{i\}$, so the map's level discipline *forces* the evaluator to multiply matrices in order — any reordering produces an encoding that cannot reach the top level and be zero-tested. The algebraic structure thus enforces the program's *control flow*.

### 4.2 Multilinear maps and graded encodings

A (leveled) *multilinear map* generalizes bilinear pairings: groups $\mathbb{G}_1, \dots, \mathbb{G}_\kappa$ with an efficiently computable map $e: \mathbb{G}_1 \times \cdots \times \mathbb{G}_\kappa \to \mathbb{G}_T$ linear in each argument. Garg, Gentry, and Halevi gave the first *candidate* from ideal lattices — a *graded encoding scheme*: elements encoded relative to level sets $S \subseteq [\kappa]$; same-level encodings add, disjoint-level encodings multiply, and only top-level encodings admit a zero-test via a published parameter $p_{zt}$ [3].

In GGH13, a level-$S$ encoding looks like $c = (e + g \cdot r)/\prod_{i \in S} z_i$ over a quotient ring $R_q$ ($g$ generates the plaintext ideal, $z_i$ secret denominators); the zero-test parameter $p_{zt} = [h z^\kappa / g]_q$ makes $u \cdot p_{zt}$ "small" exactly when $u$ encodes zero [3][5]. The test leaks more than a bit — its *output* is a ring element correlated with secret randomness — but in 2013 it was the only available substrate, and GGHRSW built on a simplified "jigsaw" abstraction [1].

### 4.3 Cryptanalysis: zeroizing and annihilation attacks

The cryptanalytic response began with *zeroizing attacks* exploiting low-level encodings of zero to recover secret parameters [7]; Cheon et al. extended these into a broad cryptanalysis of branching-program obfuscations over GGH13 — breaking all existing candidates at recommended parameters via an NTRU solver and matrix zeroizing, and showing the lattice dimension must scale as $\tilde{\Theta}(\kappa \cdot \lambda)$ [7].

But the most conceptually significant attack was the *annihilation attack* of Miles, Sahai, and Zhandry [4]:

> **Theorem (Annihilation cryptanalysis [4], informal).** There exist simple, functionally equivalent programs whose GGH13-based obfuscations are efficiently distinguishable in polynomial time, with *no* low-level encodings of zero required.

The attack evaluates the obfuscated program on inputs where the branching program outputs zero, obtaining top-level encodings of zero, and applies a constructed *annihilating polynomial* — nonlinear, vanishing on honest evaluation values — that, through the zero-test's leaked ring elements, yields a linear-algebraic distinguisher between the two programs [4][5]. Because it needs only *top-level* zeros, which any correct obfuscation must provide, it struck at the construction's heart rather than at a parameter choice. The attack timeline:

| Year | Attack / defense | Target | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2015 | Zeroizing attacks on GGH13 | Low-level zero encodings | [7] |
| 2016 | Annihilation attacks | GGH13-based iO candidates | [4] |
| 2016–17 | NTRU-based BP cryptanalysis | All BP obfuscations at recommended params | [7] |
| 2016 | Weak multilinear map model + secure candidate | All *known* poly-time attacks | [5] |
| 2018 | GGH15 zeroizing-resistance proofs | Graph-induced maps | [8] |

### 4.4 Defenses: weak models and graph-induced maps

Two defensive paradigms emerged. First, a *weak multilinear map model* capturing exactly the extra information leaked by zero-tests in all known attacks, with a modified iO candidate provably secure *in that model* — the first candidate covering every known polynomial-time attack on GGH13 [5], under a new assumption on the hardness of computing annihilating polynomials.

Second, Gentry, Gorbunov, and Halevi proposed *graph-induced* multilinear maps (GGH15), a fundamentally different substrate: encodings are associated with *paths* in a directed graph, and multiplication is allowed only along composable edges [6]. Because the graph can be designed so that no low-level zero-encodings are ever published, large classes of zeroizing attacks are structurally impossible; follow-up work formalized a zeroizing-attack model for this setting and proved security against it [8].

```tla
                                     level |-> p.level \cup q.level]}

    e.level = Levels => e.val \in {0}

```

### 4.5 Closing the loop: compact FE implies iO

For years the implication ran one way: iO (+ OWF) gives FE [1]. The converse — *compact* FE (ciphertext size sublinear in circuit size) implies iO — completed an equivalence: *iO for P/poly exists iff compact FE for P/poly exists* [10]. This reoriented the search toward building FE with ever-better compactness from ever-weaker assumptions.

The culmination came in 2020, when Jain, Lin, and Sahai constructed iO from *well-founded* assumptions — subexponential LWE, a Boolean PRG in NC⁰ with sufficient stretch, and related standard objects — via single-ciphertext secret-key FE amplified to compact public-key FE, then the FE-to-iO compiler [9]. These assumptions had survived decades of cryptanalysis; follow-up work simplified them further to classical Boolean Goldreich PRGs [10]. The result effectively ended debate over whether iO exists mathematically — while efficient *implementation* remains the great open problem [12].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We now state the central technical results with proof sketches, and quantify the concrete costs separating candidate iO from deployable iO.

### 5.1 The bootstrapping theorem

> **Theorem (NC¹-to-P/poly bootstrap [1]).** Assume iO for NC¹ circuits and FHE with decryption in NC¹. Then iO exists for all polynomial-size circuits.

> *Proof sketch.* Given $C$, sample $(pk, sk) \leftarrow \mathsf{FHE.KeyGen}$, compute $ct \leftarrow \mathsf{Enc}(pk, C)$, and output $(\,ct,\; \mathcal{O}_{NC^1}(D_{sk})\,)$ where $D_{sk}(x) = \mathsf{FHE.Dec}(sk, \mathsf{Eval}(U, ct, x))$. For $C_0 \equiv C_1$, $|C_0| = |C_1|$: replace $ct_0$ by $ct_1$ (FHE semantic security; $D_{sk}$ is functionally identical in both hybrids, decrypting a homomorphic evaluation of the *same* function). The two obfuscations are then of equivalent NC¹ circuits — indistinguishable by the NC¹-iO guarantee. ∎

The proof uses iO exactly once, on two circuits whose equivalence is *information-theoretic* — a pattern that became the template for dozens of subsequent constructions.

### 5.2 FE security: the mode-switching hybrid

> **Theorem (iO ⇒ FE for all circuits [1]).** Assuming iO for P/poly, CPA-secure PKE, and NIZK, there exists FE for all circuits with succinct ciphertexts.

> *Proof sketch.* The master public key contains an obfuscation of a program with two *modes*: mode 0 decrypts the challenge ciphertext with $sk_0$; mode 1 with $sk_1$. Hybrids: (H₀) real game, $x_0$ under $pk_0$; (H₁) switch the obfuscated program to mode 1 — functionally equivalent on all *valid* inputs (NIZK excludes malformed ciphertexts where modes could diverge), so iO applies; (H₂) replace the $pk_0$-ciphertext with an encryption of $x_1$ — undetectable by PKE security, since the mode-1 program never touches $sk_0$; (H₃) switch back to mode 0 by iO. H₀ ≈ H₃, yet H₃ encrypts $x_1$: FE security follows. ∎

The NIZK component is essential: without it, an adversary could submit a ciphertext on which the two modes *disagree*, breaking the functional-equivalence precondition of iO. This illustrates a recurring theme — iO's power is unleashed only when adversarial input *validity* is enforced by other means.

### 5.3 Concrete costs

For security parameter $\lambda$ and multilinearity degree $\kappa = \mathrm{poly}(\lambda)$:

| Component | Size / cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Branching program length (NC¹ circuit of size $s$) | $O(s^{O(1)})$ matrices | Barrington: width 5, polynomial length [11] |
| GGH13 encoding of one matrix entry | $\tilde{O}(\kappa \lambda^2)$ bits | Lattice dimension $\tilde{\Theta}(\kappa\lambda)$ [7] |
| Obfuscated program size | $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda, s)$, large degree | Megabytes for toy circuits |
| Zero-test per evaluation | One ring multiplication + size check | Leaks annihilator fodder [4] |
| JLS20 construction overhead | Tower of FE amplifications | Purely theoretical |

No general-purpose iO implementation runs on real hardware for nontrivial programs. The gap between *existence* (now on firm foundations [9][10]) and *efficiency* remains the field's central open problem.

## 6 Limitations

**Assumption fragility.** The original candidate rested on new, untested algebraic assumptions — and the cryptanalytic history (§4.3) vindicated the skeptics: every concrete instantiation proposed before 2016 was broken in polynomial time [4][7]. The weak-map model [5] and GGH15 [6][8] restored confidence only *relative to known attacks*, a fundamentally retrospective guarantee.

**The iO definition's blind spots.** iO guarantees nothing about programs that are *inherently* leaky: it cannot hide a hardcoded secret the program's own functionality reveals, and it permits leaking coarse features like program size. Applications must therefore push all security-critical hiding into computational assumptions (as in the mode-switching proof of §5.2), using iO only across functionally equivalent boundaries.

**Efficiency.** Even the well-founded constructions [9][10] compose layers of FE, puncturable PRFs, and lattice trapdoors with large polynomial overheads and subexponential security losses. Obfuscating a single AES circuit under these schemes is far beyond any conceivable hardware.

**Definitional subtleties.** Stronger notions like differing-inputs obfuscation — needed for some applications — were shown problematic or unachievable in full generality, and iO's "best possible" characterization [11] holds only in an efficiency-respecting sense. Extracting more from iO than indistinguishability of equivalents requires extreme care.

---

## 7 Conclusion

From Barak et al.'s impossibility [2] to Jain–Lin–Sahai's well-founded construction [9]: a case study in how cryptography refines its questions. VBB asked for too much and received a proof of impossibility; iO asked for exactly what is achievable and received, after a turbulent decade, a theorem. Along the way the field gained graded encodings [3], the FHE bootstrapping technique [1], punctured programming, graph-induced maps [6], and the iO–FE equivalence [10].

What remains is the engineering chasm: turning an existence theorem into a usable obfuscator. The next breakthrough will likely be a *cheaper* realization — from lattices alone, or a new algebraic idea — rather than a stronger definition. Until then, iO stands as both a triumph of theory and a standing challenge: the most powerful cryptographic object we know how to build, and the least practical one we know how to use.
