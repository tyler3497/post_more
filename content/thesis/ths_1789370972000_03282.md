## 1 Introduction

The defining mystery of the deep learning era is not why neural networks can *fit* data — universal approximation settled that question long ago — but why the solutions they find *generalize*. A contemporary image classifier with a hundred million parameters trained on fifty thousand examples has enough capacity to memorize random labels, and yet, trained on real labels with plain stochastic gradient descent, it achieves test accuracy that would be astonishing to a classical statistician. The resolution now widely accepted is that generalization in deep learning is not a property of the hypothesis class but a property of the *optimizer*: gradient descent, in overparameterized settings, does not reach an arbitrary minimizer but one carrying an implicit regularizer imposed by the dynamics of the algorithm [1][2].

This phenomenon, called *implicit bias* or *implicit regularization*, expresses a simple but profound principle: the trajectory of gradient descent encodes a preference ordering over the level sets of the loss. Two weight vectors may both achieve zero training loss, yet be qualitatively different — one a "sharp" minimizer sitting in a narrow valley, the other a "flat" minimizer in a broad basin — and the algorithm systematically selects the latter. Three research programs have transformed this intuition into mathematics.

The first program, initiated by Soudry et al., studies gradient descent on linearly separable classification with the logistic or exponential loss and proves that the weight vector converges *in direction* to the maximum-margin classifier — precisely the solution of the hard-margin support vector machine, even though the objective contains no margin term [1]. The result extends to homogeneous deep linear networks [2], diagonal and convolutional architectures, and — through the neural tangent kernel — to infinitely wide networks.

The second program, ignited by Cohen et al.'s empirical discovery of the *Edge of Stability* [3], showed that deterministic gradient descent on deep networks violates the textbook stability condition almost immediately: sharpness, defined as the top eigenvalue of the loss Hessian, climbs during training until it reaches the critical value 2/η, where η is the step size, and then *hovers* there — loss oscillating nonmonotonically but trending downward — for the remainder of training. Subsequent work revealed the underlying mechanism: a self-stabilization in which cubic terms in the local Taylor expansion pull the iterate back from instability [5], and showed that Edge-of-Stability dynamics implement an implicit gradient flow that *minimizes sharpness* along the manifold of minimizers [4].

The third program turns the observation into an algorithm. Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) explicitly optimizes a minimax objective that seeks parameters whose entire neighborhood has low loss, approximating the inner maximization with a single gradient-ascent step [6]. SAM has become a standard tool for improving generalization across vision, language, and finetuning tasks.

This thesis synthesizes these three programs into a single narrative: gradient descent's implicit bias is, in an operational sense, a bias toward *stability* — and flatness is its geometric shadow. Along the way we give rigorous definitions, sketch proofs, present empirical signatures, and assess the limits of the theory.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Notation and Setup

We consider supervised learning with training data S = {(x₁, y₁), …, (xₙ, yₙ)} and a parameterized model f(x; w), w ∈ ℝᵈ, trained by minimizing the empirical risk

$$L(w) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(x_i; w), y_i).$$

Gradient descent (GD) iterates wₜ₊₁ = wₜ − η∇L(wₜ), and stochastic gradient descent (SGD) replaces ∇L with a minibatch estimate. We denote the Hessian by H(w) = ∇²L(w) and the *sharpness* at w by λ₁(H(w)), its largest eigenvalue. When the model is overparameterized, the minimizer set M = {w : L(w) = min L} is typically a high-dimensional manifold rather than a point; the question is which w ∈ M the algorithm finds.

### 2.2 The Generalization Paradox of Interpolation

Classical learning theory bounds the generalization gap by complexity measures of the hypothesis class — VC dimension, Rademacher complexity, covering numbers — that grow with parameter count. In the *interpolation regime*, where d ≫ n and the model fits training data exactly, these bounds are vacuous. The paradox is sharpened by the observation that the same architecture trained on random labels memorizes perfectly yet generalizes catastrophically, so the hypothesis class alone cannot distinguish the two cases. The distinguishing factor must lie in the *function* selected by the algorithm, and the algorithm's selection criterion is its implicit bias.

### 2.3 Flat versus Sharp Minima

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber conjectured in the 1990s that "flat" minima — regions where the loss changes little under parameter perturbation — generalize better than "sharp" minima, because a minimum that is robust to parameter noise is also robust to the shift from training to test data.

| Max-margin direction | argmax over unit vectors of minᵢ yᵢ⟨β, xᵢ⟩ | Limit direction of GD on separable linear classification [1] |
| Implicit regularizer R(β) | Effective penalty the algorithm minimizes | Characterizes which interpolant GD selects [2] |
| Edge of Stability | Regime with λ₁(H) ≈ 2/η | Deterministic GD self-regulates sharpness [3] |

The classical convergence theory of gradient descent on smooth objectives rests on a single inequality: if L has Lipschitz gradient with constant L, then the step wₜ₊₁ = wₜ − η∇L(wₜ) monotonically decreases the loss whenever η < 2/L.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Maximum-Margin Implicit Bias on Separable Data

The cleanest theorem in the implicit-bias literature concerns a setting classical enough to be a textbook exercise: *unregularized* logistic regression on linearly separable data.

> **Theorem (Soudry et al., 2018):** Let the data be linearly separable and let L be the exponential or logistic loss. Then gradient descent with sufficiently small step size satisfies ‖wₜ‖ → ∞ and
>
> $$ \frac{w_t}{\|w_t\|} \;\longrightarrow\; \hat{w} \;=\; \frac{w^*}{\|w^*\|}, $$
>
> where w* = argmin_w ‖w‖₂ subject to yᵢ⟨w, xᵢ⟩ ≥ 1 for all i — the hard-margin SVM solution. Convergence in direction is slow: the angular error decays only as O(1/log t).

*Proof sketch.* The gradient of the exponential loss is a positive linear combination of the data, ∇L(w) = −Σᵢ αᵢ(w) yᵢxᵢ with αᵢ(w) = exp(−yᵢ⟨w, xᵢ⟩) > 0. As ‖wₜ‖ grows, the weights αᵢ concentrate on the examples with smallest margin yᵢ⟨wₜ, xᵢ⟩ — the *support vectors* — because the exponential tail amplifies the worst-classified points. Writing wₜ = g(t)·ŵ + ρₜ with g(t) → ∞, one shows the residual ρₜ grows only logarithmically while the dominant component aligns with the KKT point of the margin problem, whose stationarity condition reads w* = Σ_{i∈S} λᵢyᵢxᵢ with λᵢ ≥ 0 over the support set S [1]. The extension to the logistic loss uses the same exponential tail; multiclass and "restricted deep network" settings follow by analogous arguments.

Three consequences deserve emphasis.

```python
import numpy as np

def gd_exponential_loss(X, y, eta=0.1, T=20000):
    """Gradient descent on separable data with exponential loss.
    Tracks the angle to the hard-margin SVM direction."""
    w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for t in range(T):
        margins = y * (X @ w)
        # gradient of sum_i exp(-margin_i)
        grad = -(X * (y * np.exp(-margins))[:, None]).sum(axis=0)
        w = w - eta * grad
    return w

# On separable synthetic data, w / ||w|| aligns with the SVM solution;
# the alignment improves only logarithmically in t (Soudry et al.).
```

### 4.3 Progressive Sharpening and the Edge of Stability

Classical optimization theory predicts that gradient descent on a smooth loss with step size η is stable only while λ₁(H(wₜ)) < 2/η.

1. **Progressive sharpening.** From initialization, where sharpness is typically small, λ₁(H(wₜ)) *rises steadily* throughout training, even as the loss falls. The optimizer actively seeks sharper regions.
2. **Edge of Stability.** Once λ₁ reaches 2/η, it does not cross decisively into divergence. Instead it *hovers* just at or above 2/η for the rest of training, while the loss continues to decrease — nonmonotonically over short timescales, reliably over long ones [3].

This is not a quirk of one architecture: it holds across MLPs, CNNs, ResNets, and Transformers, across datasets from CIFAR to language modeling, provided the step size is in a reasonable range.

Why does sharpness rise in the first place?

### 4.4 Self-Stabilization: The Mechanism Beneath the Edge

Damian, Nichani, and Lee showed that the Edge of Stability is not chaotic but is captured quantitatively by a *cubic* Taylor expansion of the loss [5].

> **Theorem (informal; Damian et al., 2023):** Under mild regularity conditions, gradient descent at the Edge of Stability is well-approximated by projected gradient descent under the constraint λ₁(H(w)) ≤ 2/η. The dynamics exhibit *self-stabilization*: cubic terms in the loss expansion counteract linear instability along the top Hessian eigenvector, keeping sharpness pinned near 2/η while the loss decreases on average [5].

A complementary analysis by Arora, Li, and Panigrahi proved that certain variants of gradient descent *provably* enter the Edge of Stability and, once there, execute a deterministic flow on the manifold of minimizers that *minimizes* λ₁(H) — i.e., the EoS phase implements an implicit sharpness-minimization procedure [4].

The oscillation structure has been further characterized through bifurcation theory: as training proceeds, the dynamics undergo a cascade of period-doubling-like transitions in the unstable subspace, a signature of deterministic nonlinear dynamics rather than noise [7].

```rust
struct EoSMonitor {

    fn stability_threshold(&self) -> f64 {

    fn at_edge(&self, top_hessian_eigenvalue: f64) -> bool {

    /// Cohen et al.: sharpness rises (progressive sharpening) until it
    /// reaches 2/eta, then hovers there while loss trends downward [3].

            "edge of stability: nonmonotonic loss, downward trend"
        } else {
```

### 4.5 Sharpness-Aware Minimization: Making the Bias Explicit

If gradient descent implicitly favors flat minima, can we *explicitly* optimize for flatness?

$$\min_w \;\max_{\|\epsilon\|_2 \le \rho}\; L(w + \epsilon).$$

The inner maximization seeks the worst-case loss in a radius-ρ neighborhood; the outer minimization finds parameters whose *neighborhoods* have uniformly low loss.

$$\hat{\epsilon}(w) = \rho\,\frac{\nabla L(w)}{\|\nabla L(w)\|_2},$$

and updates w ← w − η∇L(w + ε̂(w)) — two gradient evaluations per step, the second at the adversarially perturbed point.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Empirical Signatures

The theory above is unusually well-supported by measurement:

- **Directional convergence.** On separable synthetic data, the angle between wₜ/‖wₜ‖ and the SVM solution decays roughly as 1/log t, matching the Soudry et al. rate; training well past zero training error continues to increase the margin [1].
- **Sharpness trajectories.** Across architectures, the curve λ₁(H(wₜ)) rises monotonically until it plateaus at 2/η, after which the loss oscillates with a clear downward envelope — the canonical EoS signature reproduced in dozens of independent studies [3].
- **Self-stabilization predictions.** Damian et al. verify precise quantitative predictions for the loss, sharpness, and deviation from the projected-gradient-descent trajectory on standard benchmarks [5].
- **SAM benchmarks.** SAM improves ResNet and Vision Transformer accuracy on CIFAR and ImageNet by margins that were state-of-the-art at publication, with the m-sharpness metric correlating with test error across hyperparameter sweeps [6].

### 5.2 A Worked Proof Fragment: Stability Implies the 2/η Threshold

For completeness, we derive the threshold that organizes the entire EoS literature. Near a minimizer w* with Hessian H ⪰ 0, write eₜ = wₜ − w*. Linearizing GD:

$$e_{t+1} = e_t - \eta H e_t + o(\|e_t\|) = (I - \eta H)e_t + o(\|e_t\|).$$

Decomposing along the eigenbasis of H with eigenvalues λᵢ ≥ 0, each component evolves as (1 − ηλᵢ)ᵗ. Stability requires |1 − ηλᵢ| < 1 for all i, i.e., ηλᵢ < 2, i.e., λ₁(H) < 2/η. At λ₁(H) = 2/η the top eigendirection is marginally stable (oscillation with constant amplitude); beyond it, the component diverges geometrically — the linear instability that cubic terms must then tame [3][5].

### 5.3 Formal Specification of the SAM Update

The following TLA+ sketch specifies the two-phase SAM step abstractly, making the ascent-then-descent structure amenable to model checking of simple cases:

```tla+
Ascent ==
    \* adversarial perturbation along the normalized gradient
    LET eps == Rho * grad / Norm(grad)
    IN  w' = w + eps /\ UNCHANGED grad

Descent ==
    \* gradient step evaluated at the perturbed point
    w' = w - Eta * GradAt(w) /\ UNCHANGED grad

Next == Ascent \/ Descent
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<w, grad>>
====
```

### 5.4 Synthesis: One Bias, Three Faces

The three programs describe one underlying phenomenon at different resolutions. In separable classification, the implicit bias is exactly characterizable: gradient descent converges in direction to the maximum-margin predictor, the flattest solution in the sense of classification margin [1]. In deep nonlinear training, the optimizer drives itself to the Edge of Stability, where self-stabilization pins sharpness at 2/η and the residual dynamics minimize curvature along the minimizer manifold [3][4][5]. And SAM shows that directly penalizing neighborhood worst-case loss — the operational definition of flatness — improves generalization across the board [6]. Flatness, margin, and stability are not three separate biases; they are three measurements of the same selection pressure.

## 6 Limitations

**The scale-invariance objection.** Dinh et al. observed that for ReLU networks, rescaling incoming and outgoing weights of a node by reciprocal factors leaves the function — and hence generalization — unchanged while arbitrarily changing Hessian-based sharpness. Any claim that "flat minima generalize better" must therefore be qualified: *which* sharpness, under *which* normalization? Scale-aware definitions (ASAM, Fisher-based, or function-space notions) partially address this, but no canonical resolution exists.

**Non-asymptotic gaps.** The Soudry et al. margin result is asymptotic with a 1/log t rate; at practical training horizons and initialization scales, the effective bias is richer and not fully captured by the limit [1]. Similarly, EoS theory describes full-batch GD, while practice uses SGD with momentum, weight decay, and normalization layers that interact with sharpness in incompletely understood ways.

**Sharpness is not sufficient.** Counterexamples exist in which sharper minima generalize as well as flatter ones, and the correlation between sharpness measures and test error, while robust, is imperfect [6]. Flatness is a useful inductive bias, not a law.

**SAM's cost and fragility.** SAM doubles the per-step gradient cost, its theory rests on a first-order approximation of the inner maximization that can fail (hallucinated minimizers at large ρ), and its benefits shrink under some modern training recipes [6][related analysis].

**Scope of architectures.** Most EoS theory assumes smooth losses and deterministic updates; non-smooth activations, adaptive optimizers, and distributed training introduce effects outside the current theorems [4][5].

## 7 Conclusion

The implicit bias of gradient descent is the central explanatory concept of modern deep learning generalization. We have traced its three great arcs: the maximum-margin directional convergence on separable data, which made the bias mathematically precise [1]; the Edge of Stability, which revealed that practical gradient descent operates at the boundary of its own stability and self-organizes toward flatter minima [3][4][5]; and Sharpness-Aware Minimization, which converted the implicit bias into an explicit, effective algorithm [6]. Together with the architectural refinements of Gunasekar et al. [2] and the dynamical-systems refinements of the bifurcation perspective [7], they support a unified thesis: *gradient-based learning is biased toward flat, stable minima, and this bias is increasingly something we can measure, understand, and design.*

The open frontier is control. If the optimizer's implicit regularizer can be characterized as precisely for deep networks as Soudry et al. characterized it for linear classification — and if the Edge-of-Stability dynamics can be steered the way SAM steers the objective — then the next generation of training algorithms will not merely inherit their inductive bias from the dynamics of gradient descent. They will choose it.
