# Causal Inference in Observational Studies: Double Machine Learning, Propensity-Score Weighting, and Doubly Robust Estimation

## Abstract

Estimating causal effects from observational data is a central challenge across the quantitative sciences: unlike randomized experiments, treatment assignment is entangled with the very covariates that also determine outcomes, so naive contrasts confound correlation with causation. This thesis develops a unified account of the three modern pillars of observational causal inference — propensity-score weighting, doubly robust estimation, and double/debiased machine learning (DML) — within the Neyman–Rubin potential-outcomes framework. We show how the propensity score collapses high-dimensional confounding into a scalar balancing score, why inverse-probability weighting is fragile to extreme weights, and how augmented inverse-probability weighting attains consistency whenever either the outcome or the treatment model is correct. The heart of the thesis is the DML program of Chernozhukov et al.: Neyman-orthogonal scores immunize estimation against regularization bias, while cross-fitting removes overfitting bias, jointly permitting valid √n-inference with machine-learning nuisance estimators. We derive the efficient influence function, prove the double-robustness property, survey the R-learner and causal forests for heterogeneous effects, and close with simulation evidence and honest caveats about overlap, positivity, and untestable identifying assumptions.

## 1 Introduction

The randomized controlled trial remains the gold standard of causal inference, because randomization severs every backdoor path between the treatment and the outcome: in expectation, treated and control units differ in nothing but the intervention itself. Yet randomization is frequently infeasible, unethical, or prohibitively expensive. Economists cannot randomize recessions; physicians cannot randomize smoking; platforms cannot randomize every pricing policy. We are therefore forced to learn causal effects from *observational* data, in which treatment assignment is an endogenous outcome of the same covariates that drive the response. The central intellectual problem is to distinguish the *signal* of causation from the *noise* of confounding.

The Neyman–Rubin potential-outcomes framework gives this problem a precise language. For each unit i, we posit two potential outcomes, Y_i(1) under treatment and Y_i(0) under control; the observed outcome is Y_i = D_i Y_i(1) + (1 − D_i) Y_i(0), where D_i ∈ {0, 1} is the treatment indicator. The fundamental problem is that we never observe both potential outcomes for the same unit, so θ_0 = E[Y(1) − Y(0)] is a functional of a partially missing joint distribution; every method below is a strategy for imputing or reweighting the missing counterfactuals.

Three methodological families dominate modern practice, and this thesis unifies them:

1. **Propensity-score weighting**, descending from Rosenbaum and Rubin [2], which reweights the sample to mimic a randomized experiment by the inverse probability of the treatment actually received.
2. **Doubly robust estimation**, originating with Robins, Rotnitzky, and Zhao [3] and refined by Bang and Robins [4], which augments weighting with an outcome model so that consistency survives misspecification of either component.
3. **Double/debiased machine learning**, developed by Chernozhukov et al. [1], which shows how *Neyman-orthogonal* scores combined with *cross-fitting* permit √n-consistent, asymptotically normal inference even when nuisance functions are estimated by regularized, black-box machine learners.

The thesis proceeds as follows: identification and the propensity-score theorem (Section 2); the canonical estimators and a proof of double robustness (Section 3); a deep dive into orthogonality, weighting pathologies, AIPW/TMLE, and the DML architecture with modern ML nuisance learners (Section 4); proof sketches and empirical evidence (Section 5); honest limitations (Section 6); and conclusions (Section 7).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Neyman–Rubin Causal Model and Identification

Let (Y(1), Y(0), D, X) denote the potential outcomes, binary treatment, and a vector of pre-treatment covariates for a generic unit drawn from a population. The *consistency* (SUTVA) assumption sets Y = D Y(1) + (1 − D) Y(0), ruling out interference and hidden treatment versions. Causal identification from observational data then rests on two further pillars:

- **Unconfoundedness (strong ignorability):** (Y(1), Y(0)) ⊥⊥ D | X. Conditional on the observed covariates, treatment assignment is as good as random — there are no unmeasured confounders [2].
- **Overlap (positivity):** 0 < e(X) < 1 almost surely, where e(X) = P(D = 1 | X) is the *propensity score*. Every covariate stratum must contain both treated and control units with positive probability.

Under these assumptions, the average treatment effect is identified by the *g-computation* formula:

> θ_0 = E[ E[Y | D = 1, X] − E[Y | D = 0, X] ]

That is, we may average the conditional mean difference over the covariate distribution. Equivalently, by inverse-probability weighting,

> θ_0 = E[ D Y / e(X) − (1 − D) Y / (1 − e(X)) ]

Both identities are exact under the identifying assumptions; the entire methodological literature is about *estimating* the unknown functions e(·) and μ_d(·) = E[Y | D = d, X] reliably enough that plug-in estimation inherits √n asymptotics and valid confidence intervals.

### 2.2 The Propensity-Score Theorem

Rosenbaum and Rubin's landmark 1983 result [2] shows that the propensity score is the *coarsest* balancing score: treatment assignment and covariates are conditionally independent given e(X),

> **Theorem (Balancing property):** D ⊥⊥ X | e(X).

Consequently, if unconfoundedness holds given X, it also holds given the scalar e(X): (Y(1), Y(0)) ⊥⊥ D | e(X). Adjustment for a single number suffices to remove bias due to *all* observed covariates — a dramatic dimension reduction. This insight justifies matching, subclassification, and weighting on the estimated propensity score, and it is why the propensity score remains, four decades later, the organizing concept of observational causal inference.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Three Canonical Estimators

Define the outcome regressions μ_1(X) = E[Y | D = 1, X] and μ_0(X) = E[Y | D = 0, X], and let ê(·), μ̂_1(·), μ̂_0(·) denote their estimates. The three workhorse estimators of the ATE are:

| Estimator | Formula | Requires | Model-robust? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outcome regression (g-computation) | (1/n) Σ [μ̂_1(X_i) − μ̂_0(X_i)] | Outcome model | No |
| Inverse-probability weighting (IPW) | (1/n) Σ [D_i Y_i / ê(X_i) − (1−D_i) Y_i / (1 − ê(X_i))] | Propensity model | No |
| Augmented IPW (AIPW) | (1/n) Σ [μ̂_1(X_i) − μ̂_0(X_i) + D_i (Y_i − μ̂_1(X_i))/ê(X_i) − (1−D_i)(Y_i − μ̂_0(X_i))/(1 − ê(X_i))] | Both | **Yes — doubly robust** |

The AIPW estimator — introduced for missing data by Robins, Rotnitzky, and Zhao [3] and developed as a doubly robust causal estimator by Scharfstein, Rotnitzky, and Robins and by Bang and Robins [4] — reads two ways: as IPW *augmented* with an imputation-based bias correction, or as outcome regression *augmented* with a reweighted residual correction.

### 3.2 The Double-Robustness Property

> **Theorem (Double robustness):** Let e*(X) and μ*_d(X) denote the probability limits of the estimated propensity and outcome models. If *either* e* = e (the propensity model is correct) *or* μ*_d = μ_d (the outcome model is correct), then the AIPW estimator is consistent for θ_0.

*Proof sketch.* Write the AIPW influence term for the treated arm as μ̂_1(X) + D(Y − μ̂_1(X))/ê(X). Its expectation equals E[μ*_1(X)] + E[ D(Y − μ*_1(X)) / e*(X) ]. If the propensity model is correct (e* = e), then E[D(Y − μ*_1(X))/e(X) | X] = μ_1(X) − μ*_1(X), so the sum telescopes to E[μ_1(X)] regardless of μ*. Conversely, if the outcome model is correct (μ*_1 = μ_1), the residual term has mean zero and the estimator reduces to the (correct) outcome-regression functional. The same argument applies to the control arm. ∎

Two further facts elevate AIPW beyond mere robustness. First, when *both* models are correct, AIPW attains the *semiparametric efficiency bound* — no regular estimator can do asymptotically better (the bound was characterized by Hahn and by Robins; see [3, 4]). The bound is governed by the efficient influence function

> ψ*(W; θ_0) = D(Y − μ_1(X))/e(X) − (1−D)(Y − μ_0(X))/(1−e(X)) + μ_1(X) − μ_0(X) − θ_0,

whose variance is the smallest achievable asymptotic variance. Second, the AIPW estimating equation is *Neyman-orthogonal*: its Gateaux derivative with respect to the nuisance functions (e, μ_1, μ_0), evaluated at the truth, is zero. This orthogonality is the key that unlocks machine learning for causal inference, as we now develop.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Regularization-Bias Problem and Neyman Orthogonality

High-dimensional nuisances e(X), μ_d(X) are increasingly estimated by regularized learners — lasso, forests, boosting, neural nets — that are superb *predictors* but *biased* estimators by design. Plugged naively into a non-orthogonal estimating equation, nuisance bias propagates one-for-one into the target parameter: the estimator loses √n-consistency, its distribution shifts, and intervals undercover — sometimes dramatically [1].

The classical remedy is *Neyman orthogonality*. A score (moment) function ψ(W; θ, η), where η collects the nuisance parameters, is Neyman-orthogonal at (θ_0, η_0) if

> ∂_η E[ψ(W; θ_0, η_0)] [η − η_0] = 0 for all directions η − η_0.

In words, the identifying moment is *locally insensitive* to small perturbations of the nuisance functions. The AIPW score above satisfies this property: because the residual terms D(Y − μ_1)/e are mean-zero at the truth, first-order errors in estimating μ_1 or e contribute only *second-order* (product-of-errors) terms to the bias of θ̂. Concretely, the estimation error decomposes as

> √n (θ̂ − θ_0) = (1/√n) Σ ψ*(W_i; θ_0) + O_P( √n · ||ê − e|| · ||μ̂ − μ|| ) + o_P(1),

so that if each nuisance estimator converges at the modest o(n^{−1/4}) rate — far slower than the parametric rate — the remainder vanishes and θ̂ inherits the Gaussian limit of the oracle estimator that knew the nuisances [1]. Orthogonality converts the ML bias problem from a first-order catastrophe into a second-order nuisance.

### 4.2 Propensity-Score Weighting: Theory, Stabilization, and Overlap

![Causal diagram of confounding and the propensity-score balancing structure](/thesis/ths_1789046960000_12280-0.webp)

Inverse-probability weighting creates a *pseudo-population* in which treatment is unconfounded: weighting each treated unit by 1/e(X) and each control unit by 1/(1 − e(X)) balances the covariate distributions across arms, mimicking the randomized experiment that nature declined to run. Hirano, Imbens, and Ridder [6] showed that weighting by the *estimated* (rather than true) propensity score is not merely a practical necessity but an efficiency gain: the estimated score adjusts for chance imbalances in the sample, and the resulting IPW estimator attains the semiparametric efficiency bound.

Yet IPW has a notorious Achilles' heel: *extreme weights*. Units with ê(X) near 0 or 1 receive enormous weights, and a handful of observations can dominate the estimate. The literature offers a hierarchy of mitigations:

- **Stabilized weights:** multiply by the marginal treatment probability, reducing variance without affecting consistency.
- **Trimming/truncation:** cap weights at a quantile — at the cost of small bias and a changed estimand.
- **Overlap weights:** weight by e(X)(1 − e(X)), targeting the best-supported subpopulation.
- **Covariate-balancing propensity scores:** estimate e(X) to optimize balance rather than likelihood.

Lunceford and Davidian [5] provide the definitive comparative guide: weighting performs well when the propensity model is adequate and doubly robust estimators supply the insurance policy — but under poor overlap, no weighting scheme rescues the analysis, since the weights amplify whatever error the outcome model makes.

### 4.3 Doubly Robust Estimation: AIPW, Regression Representation, and Targeted Learning

Beyond consistency under single-model misspecification, doubly robust estimators admit a revealing *regression representation* (Scharfstein, Rotnitzky, and Robins; Bang and Robins [4]). The AIPW estimator can be computed by:

1. Fitting an initial outcome regression μ̂_d(X).
2. Augmenting the regression with the clever covariate h(D, X) = D/ê(X) − (1−D)/(1−ê(X)) — the inverse-probability weight itself as a regressor.
3. Updating the fit by the estimated fluctuation coefficient, then averaging the updated predictions.

This is precisely the skeleton of *targeted maximum likelihood estimation* (TMLE; van der Laan and Rubin), which generalizes the idea: start with a flexible initial estimator of the outcome regression, then perform a targeted fluctuation step along the efficient influence function so that the final plug-in estimator solves the efficient estimating equation. TMLE preserves the double-robustness and local efficiency of AIPW while guaranteeing the estimate respects the statistical model's bounds (e.g., probabilities stay in [0, 1]) — a genuine improvement for bounded outcomes.

### 4.4 Double Machine Learning: Cross-Fitting and the Modern Nuisance Toolkit

Orthogonality neutralizes *regularization* bias; *cross-fitting* neutralizes *overfitting* bias. When nuisance functions are estimated on the same data used to evaluate the score, the empirical correlation between estimation error and the data — the "own-observation bias" — can be of order larger than n^{−1/2} unless restrictive Donsker (complexity) conditions hold. Modern ML methods routinely violate those conditions. The DML solution [1] is elegantly simple:

1. Partition the sample into K folds (K = 5 is typical).
2. For each fold k, estimate the nuisance functions η̂_{−k} on all *other* folds.
3. Evaluate the orthogonal score ψ(W_i; θ, η̂_{−k(i)}) on the held-out fold.
4. Solve the pooled estimating equation (1/n) Σ ψ(W_i; θ, η̂_{−k(i)}) = 0 for θ.

Because η̂_{−k} is independent of fold k, overfitting bias vanishes without any complexity restrictions on the ML method. Chernozhukov et al. [1] prove √n-consistency, asymptotic normality, and semiparametric efficiency under only o(n^{−1/4}) nuisance rates, demonstrating the theory on the partially linear model, partial linear IV, ATE/ATT, and LATE.

The DML architecture is a *meta-algorithm*: any ML method can supply the nuisances. Two members of the modern toolkit deserve special mention:

- **The R-learner** (Nie and Wager [7]) targets the CATE τ(X) = E[Y(1) − Y(0) | X] via Robinson residualization: cross-fit m̂(X) = E[Y|X] and ê(X), then minimize the R-loss Σ[(Y_i − m̂(X_i)) − (D_i − ê(X_i))τ(X_i)]². Its *quasi-oracle* property: excess risk matches an oracle knowing the nuisances, with rates depending only on the complexity of τ*(·).
- **Causal forests** (Wager and Athey) adapt random forests to estimate τ(X) with honest sample splitting and asymptotically valid pointwise confidence intervals — DML's cross-fitting philosophy applied recursively inside the learner itself.

A minimal DML implementation for the partially linear model Y = θ_0 D + g_0(X) + U is strikingly compact:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold

def dml_plm(Y, D, X, K=5):
    n = len(Y)
    resid_y = np.zeros(n)   # Y - E[Y|X]
    resid_d = np.zeros(n)   # D - E[D|X]
    for train, test in KFold(K, shuffle=True).split(X):
        my = RandomForestRegressor(500).fit(X[train], Y[train])
        md = RandomForestRegressor(500).fit(X[train], D[train])
        resid_y[test] = Y[test] - my.predict(X[test])
        resid_d[test] = D[test] - md.predict(X[test])
    # Neyman-orthogonal score: regress residual on residual
    theta = (resid_d @ resid_y) / (resid_d @ resid_d)
    se = np.sqrt(np.mean((resid_y - theta * resid_d) ** 2)
                 / np.mean(resid_d ** 2) / n)
    return theta, se
```

The two residualizations are the orthogonal score in disguise: partialling X out of *both* Y and D removes the regularization bias that a naive lasso of Y on (D, X) would inject into θ̂. The same structure, with the EIF score of Section 3.2, yields DML for the ATE.

The efficient influence function also admits a direct functional transcription:

```haskell
-- Efficient influence function for the ATE (pointwise)
eifATE :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double -> Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
eifATE d y mu1 mu0 e theta =
    d * (y - mu1) / e - (1 - d) * (y - mu0) / (1 - e)
    + (mu1 - mu0) - theta

-- AIPW: the sample mean of the EIF is the doubly robust estimator
aipw :: [(Double,Double,Double,Double,Double)] -> Double
aipw ws = sum [eifATE d y m1 m0 e 0 | (d,y,m1,m0,e) <- ws]
          / fromIntegral (length ws)
```

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof Sketch: √n-Normality of DML

> **Theorem (Chernozhukov et al. [1], informal):** Under Neyman orthogonality, K-fold cross-fitting, and nuisance rates ||η̂ − η_0|| = o_P(n^{−1/4}), the DML estimator satisfies √n(θ̂ − θ_0) → N(0, σ²), where σ² = E[ψ(W; θ_0, η_0)²] / (E[∂_θ ψ])².

*Sketch.* Expand the sample moment: 0 = E_n[ψ(W; θ̂, η̂)] = E_n[ψ(W; θ_0, η_0)] + E[∂_θψ](θ̂ − θ_0) + R_n, where the remainder R_n collects (i) the empirical-process term (E_n − E)[ψ(W;θ_0,η̂) − ψ(W;θ_0,η_0)], killed by cross-fitting independence; and (ii) the bias term E[ψ(W;θ_0,η̂) − ψ(W;θ_0,η_0)], which is *second order* in nuisance error by orthogonality — bounded by products ||ê − e||·||μ̂ − μ|| = o(n^{−1/2}). Both remainders are o_P(n^{−1/2}), leaving the standard Z-estimator CLT. ∎

### 5.2 Empirical Illustrations

Chernozhukov et al. [1] validate DML on three empirical problems: the effect of 401(k) eligibility on net financial assets (SIPP data), where naive ML plug-ins show clear regularization bias while DML with lasso, forests, and ensembles yields stable √n-normal estimates with honest intervals; the Pennsylvania Reemployment Bonus experiment, where DML recovers the experimental benchmark; and a many-controls demonstration of the partialling-out logic.

Simulations sharpen the picture: Nie and Wager [7] show the R-learner dominating S-learners, T-learners, and causal forests when the treatment-effect function is simpler than the prognostic function — the regime where residualization pays — while Lunceford and Davidian [5] find doubly robust estimators tracking the better of their constituent models across misspecification scenarios.

### 5.3 What the Simulations Warn

Kang and Schafer's "Demystifying Double Robustness" [8] is the essential counterweight: when *both* working models are slightly misspecified and some weights are extreme, the DR estimator can perform *worse* than either single-model estimator, because the augmentation term amplifies outcome-model error. Double robustness is a consistency theorem, not a finite-sample insurance policy — which is why weight diagnostics, effective sample sizes, and balance checks are part of the estimator's validity conditions, not optional accessories.

## 6 Limitations

No honest treatment of observational causal inference can end without confronting what the mathematics cannot buy:

1. **Unconfoundedness is untestable.** The data can never verify that all confounders were measured; sensitivity analyses bound the damage but cannot rule it out. The finest DML pipeline on confounded data returns a precisely estimated wrong number.
2. **Overlap failures are fatal, not fixable.** When e(X) nears 0 or 1, the ATE is barely identified. Trimming and overlap weights rescue the analysis only by changing the estimand to a better-supported subpopulation.
3. **Double robustness is asymptotic.** Under dual misspecification with extreme weights, DR estimators can underperform simpler alternatives in finite samples [8].
4. **Cross-fitting costs stability.** Each nuisance model trains on a fraction of the data, and repeated splits yield different estimates; median aggregation over repeated splits mitigates but never eliminates the Monte Carlo variation.
5. **Black-box nuisances resist auditing.** A deep-net outcome model has no coefficients to inspect, so diagnosing *why* an estimate moved demands auxiliary interpretability machinery.
6. **Heterogeneous-effect inference remains hard.** Quasi-oracle rates [7] govern estimation, not uniform inference; honest confidence bands for τ(·) need stronger conditions, and data-adaptive targets raise subtle questions about what is being estimated.

These limitations do not diminish the methods — they locate them. Double machine learning, propensity weighting, and doubly robust estimation are the best available answers to the *statistical* problem of observational causal inference; the *scientific* problem of whether the identifying assumptions hold must be argued on subject-matter grounds, in every application, without exception.

## 7 Conclusion

This thesis has traced a single arc through four decades of causal methodology: Rosenbaum and Rubin's propensity score [2] reduced confounding adjustment to scalar balancing; Robins, Rotnitzky, and Zhao [3] with Bang and Robins [4] showed that augmenting weighting with outcome modeling buys consistency under either model's correctness — and efficiency when both hold; Chernozhukov et al. [1] completed the synthesis, with orthogonal scores erasing regularization bias and cross-fitting erasing overfitting bias, admitting the full ML arsenal with √n-valid inference intact. Complementary advances — the R-learner [7], efficient weighting theory [6], comparative guidance [5], and finite-sample warnings [8] — round out a toolkit both powerful and honest about its limits.

The frontier is clear: longitudinal and dynamic regimes, interference settings where SUTVA fails, sensitivity analysis integrated with estimation, and uniform inference for heterogeneous effects. The foundations, however, are settled: orthogonalize, cross-fit, diagnose overlap — and never let a small standard error substitute for a credible identifying assumption.
