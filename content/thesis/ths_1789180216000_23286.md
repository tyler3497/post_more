# Verifiable Secret Sharing and Distributed Key Generation: Feldman, Pedersen, and Asynchronous DKG Protocols

## Abstract

Verifiable secret sharing (VSS) and distributed key generation (DKG) eliminate the trusted dealer from threshold cryptography, allowing a set of parties to jointly establish secret-shared keys with no single point of compromise. This thesis traces the evolution of these protocols from Feldman's non-interactive VSS [1] and Pedersen's information-theoretically hiding VSS [2], through Pedersen's first distributed key generation protocol and the bias attack of Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Rabin together with their provably secure repair [3, 4], to modern asynchronous DKG constructions that dispense with synchrony assumptions entirely [5–7]. We formalize the commitment-based verification equations underlying each scheme, analyze the complaint and extraction phases that enforce robustness against rushing adversaries, compare communication complexity and resilience bounds across protocol generations, and validate the core algebraic relations with a reference simulation. We conclude with the principal limitations — trusted commitment-key setup, super-quadratic communication scaling, and the t < n/3 asynchronous resilience barrier — and identify open problems in post-quantum and adaptively secure key generation.

## 1 Introduction

Threshold cryptography distributes trust: a secret key is split into *n* shares such that any *t* of them suffice to sign or decrypt, while fewer reveal nothing. The classical formulation due to Shamir reduces key management to a single, critical ceremony — the dealer who samples the polynomial and distributes the shares. A corrupt dealer can distribute *inconsistent* shares, silently biasing the key or planting a backdoor; a compromised dealer is a single point of failure for the entire system. *Verifiable secret sharing* (VSS), introduced by Chor, Goldwasser, Micali, and Awerbuch and surveyed in [8], equips shareholders with the means to check the consistency of their shares without learning the secret, neutralizing a malicious dealer. *Distributed key generation* (DKG) goes further: it removes the dealer altogether, letting the parties jointly sample a uniformly random secret that no proper subset ever learns.

This thesis reconstructs four decades of progress along this axis. We begin with **Feldman's** non-interactive VSS [1], whose homomorphic commitments made share verification a single exponentiation check, and **Pedersen's** dual-polynomial VSS [2], which traded computational secrecy for information-theoretic hiding. We then examine **Pedersen's DKG** and the devastating *bias attack* discovered by Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Rabin, whose repaired protocol [3, 4] became the blueprint for synchronous threshold key setup. Finally, we survey the **asynchronous** frontier — from Kate and Goldberg's Internet-oriented DKG [7] through the first fully asynchronous construction of Kokoris-Kogias, Malkhi, and Spiegelman [6] to the practical, implemented ADKG of Das et al. [5]. Along the way we formalize the verification equations, compare asymptotic costs, validate the algebra with a reference simulation, and map the limitations that remain open.

## 2 Background

### Secret sharing and the discrete-logarithm setting

Shamir's *(t, n)*-threshold scheme works over a finite field F_q. To share a secret *s*, the dealer samples a random polynomial

*f(x) = s + a₁x + a₂x² + … + a_{t−1}x^{t−1}*

and privately sends each party *Pᵢ* the share *sᵢ = f(i)*. Any *t* shares reconstruct *s* via Lagrange interpolation at *x = 0*:

*s = Σ_{i∈S} sᵢ · λᵢ*,  where  *λᵢ = Π_{j∈S, j≠i} j/(j−i)*.

Fewer than *t* shares reveal nothing about *s* — information-theoretically. VSS and DKG protocols are almost always instantiated in a cyclic group *G* of prime order *q* with generator *g*, where the *discrete logarithm assumption* holds: given *gˣ*, no efficient adversary recovers *x*. All secrecy arguments below reduce to this assumption.

### Pedersen commitments

A **Pedersen commitment** to a value *s* with randomness *r* is *C = gˢhʳ*, where *h* is a second generator whose discrete logarithm base *g* is unknown to all parties [2]. The scheme is *perfectly hiding* — *C* is uniformly random independent of *s* — and *computationally binding*: opening *C* to two distinct values yields log_g *h*, breaking the discrete-log assumption. Commitments are additively homomorphic — *C(s₁,r₁)·C(s₂,r₂) = C(s₁+s₂, r₁+r₂)* — a property VSS exploits to verify linear relations on committed shares.

### The VSS security model

A VSS protocol must withstand two active attacks [8]:

- a dealer distributing *inconsistent* shares during the dealing phase;
- participants submitting *incorrect* shares during reconstruction.

Formally, VSS guarantees three properties:

1. **Correctness** — honest parties reconstruct the dealer's committed secret.
2. **Secrecy** — the adversary's view is independent of the secret (computationally or information-theoretically, depending on the scheme).
3. **Commitment** — the dealing phase fixes a unique secret; the dealer cannot later equivocate.

### Network and adversary models

We distinguish the *synchronous* model — messages arrive within a known bound Δ, and a *broadcast channel* delivers identical values to all parties — from the *asynchronous* model, where the adversary controls delivery scheduling subject only to eventual delivery. The adversary is *static* (chooses corrupt parties upfront) and *rushing* (sees honest messages before choosing its own in each round). Classical bounds: synchronous protocols tolerate *t < n/2* corruptions; asynchronous Byzantine agreement — and hence fully asynchronous DKG — requires *t < n/3*.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *reconstructive* study: rather than proposing a new protocol, we rebuild the major VSS and DKG constructions from their primary sources [1–7], re-derive their verification equations in a uniform notation, and reduce their security claims to explicit hardness assumptions. Our comparative analysis proceeds along four axes — *round complexity*, *communication complexity*, *resilience*, and *setup assumptions* — summarized in §5. To guard against algebraic error, we implemented a reference simulation of the Feldman and Pedersen verification relations over a toy group (ℤ₂₃*, subgroup of order 11), confirming that every share verifies and that reconstruction recovers the secret exactly. Performance figures for modern protocols are drawn from published implementations, principally the evaluation in Das et al. [5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Feldman's Non-Interactive VSS

Feldman's 1987 scheme [1] makes Shamir sharing verifiable with a single broadcast. The dealer holding secret *s* samples *f(x) = s + Σ_{k=1}^{t−1} aₖxᵏ* and broadcasts the *commitment vector*

*Cₖ = g^{aₖ}*  for *k = 0, …, t−1*  (with *a₀ = s*),

while sending each *Pᵢ* its share *sᵢ = f(i)* over a private channel. Any party verifies its share by checking

*g^{sᵢ} = Π_{k=0}^{t−1} Cₖ^{iᵏ}*.

The equality holds because *g^{f(i)} = g^{Σ aₖiᵏ} = Π (g^{aₖ})^{iᵏ}*. Reconstruction is standard Lagrange interpolation, with each revealed share re-checked against the commitments — a corrupt participant cannot submit a bogus share undetected.

Three properties define the scheme's character. First, it is **non-interactive**: dealing requires one broadcast round plus private messages, with no back-and-forth. Second, verification is **homomorphic** — the commitments to coefficients compose exactly as the shares do, which is why the check is a single multi-exponentiation. Third, secrecy is only **computational**: the broadcast value *C₀ = gˢ* is a deterministic function of the secret, so an unbounded adversary could recover *s*; security rests on the discrete-log assumption. Moreover, verifiability is *private*: only the holder of *sᵢ* can evaluate the check, since computing *g^{sᵢ}* requires the share itself. A third party cannot audit another party's share.

> **Theorem 1 (Feldman VSS correctness and secrecy).** If the dealer is honest, every honest party's share satisfies the verification equation, and *t* verified shares reconstruct *s*. If the discrete-log assumption holds in *G*, the adversary's view *(C₀, …, C_{t−1}, {sᵢ}_{i corrupt})* is computationally independent of *s*. [1]

### 4.2 Pedersen's VSS: Information-Theoretic Hiding

Pedersen [2] observed that Feldman's leakage — *C₀ = gˢ* — is inherent to committing to coefficients with a single base. His repair adds a *blinding polynomial* *b(x) = r + Σ bₖxᵏ* of the same degree, sampled uniformly at random, and publishes the dual commitments

*Eₖ = g^{aₖ}h^{bₖ}*  for *k = 0, …, t−1*,

sending each *Pᵢ* the pair *(sᵢ = f(i), tᵢ = b(i))*. Verification becomes

*g^{sᵢ}h^{tᵢ} = Π_{k=0}^{t−1} Eₖ^{iᵏ}*,

which holds by the homomorphism of Pedersen commitments. Because each *Eₖ* is a perfectly hiding commitment, the entire broadcast is *information-theoretically independent* of the secret — even an unbounded adversary learns nothing from the dealing transcript. Binding, however, is now computational: a dealer who knew *log_g h* could open commitments arbitrarily, so the common reference string *(g, h)* must be generated with *unknown* discrete-log relation, typically via hash-to-curve or another nothing-up-my-sleeve procedure.

| | Feldman VSS [1] | Pedersen VSS [2] |
|---|---|---|
| Commitment | *Cₖ = g^{aₖ}* | *Eₖ = g^{aₖ}h^{bₖ}* |
| Share check | *g^{sᵢ} = Π Cₖ^{iᵏ}* | *g^{sᵢ}h^{tᵢ} = Π Eₖ^{iᵏ}* |
| Secrecy | Computational (DL) | Information-theoretic |
| Binding | Perfect | Computational (DL) |
| Setup | Generator *g* only | *(g, h)* with unknown *log_g h* |
| Rounds | 1 (non-interactive) | 1 (non-interactive) |

> **Theorem 2 (Pedersen VSS secrecy).** For uniformly random blinding polynomial *b(x)*, the distribution of *(E₀, …, E_{t−1})* is independent of *s*; the scheme is a perfectly hiding VSS, with binding under the discrete-log assumption. [2]

### 4.3 Pedersen's DKG and the Gennaro–Jarecki–Krawczyk–Rabin Repair

Pedersen also proposed the first **distributed key generation** protocol: *n* parties each act as Feldman dealers of independent random secrets *zᵢ*, and the joint secret becomes *s = Σ zᵢ* with public key *y = gˢ = Π g^{zᵢ}*; party *Pⱼ*'s share is *sⱼ = Σᵢ fᵢ(j)*. Elegant — and broken. Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Rabin showed [3] that a *rushing* adversary, observing all honest commitments before choosing its own contribution, can **bias** the distribution of the resulting public key, steering it into a subset of the key space. Since threshold protocols require the key to be *uniformly* random and *simulatable* — the adversary must learn nothing beyond the public key — the bias is a genuine security failure, not a theoretical quibble.

The GJKR protocol repairs DKG with five phases [3]:

1. **Sharing.** Each *Pᵢ* runs *Pedersen* VSS (not Feldman) as dealer, sharing a random *zᵢ* via polynomials *fᵢ, bᵢ*.
2. **Complaints.** Parties verify received shares; a party whose check fails broadcasts a complaint against the dealer, who must then broadcast the disputed share. Dealers whose broadcast shares fail verification are disqualified.
3. **Extraction.** Each surviving *Pᵢ* broadcasts Feldman commitments *A_{ik} = g^{a_{ik}}* to its *fᵢ* coefficients, exposing the contribution in the exponent while keeping *zᵢ* hidden.
4. **Verification.** Parties check the *A*-values against shares received in phase 1; fresh complaints disqualify cheaters, and the qualified set *QUAL* is fixed.
5. **Key derivation.** The public key is *y = Π_{i∈QUAL} A_{i0} = g^{Σ zᵢ}*; each *Pⱼ* holds *xⱼ = Σ_{i∈QUAL} fᵢ(j)*, a valid *(t, n)*-share of *x = Σ zᵢ*.

The crucial insight is that Pedersen VSS in phase 1 keeps contributions hidden until complaints resolve, while phase 3's extraction forces every contribution into the exponent *before* the adversary can adapt — restoring uniformity. GJKR prove the protocol *simulatable*: the adversary's view can be simulated given only the public key, in the synchronous model with broadcast against a static *t < n/2* adversary [3]. A companion work [4] applies the same machinery to build threshold cryptosystems whose security reduces to the underlying centralized scheme.

> **Theorem 3 (GJKR DKG security, informal).** Under the discrete-log assumption, the GJKR protocol generates a uniformly random key pair *(x, y)* with *t < n/2* static corruptions in the synchronous broadcast model; the joint view of the adversary is simulatable from *y* alone. [3]

### 4.4 Asynchronous DKG: Dispensing with Synchrony

Synchronous DKG assumes a broadcast channel and lock-step rounds — assumptions the open Internet does not provide. But asynchrony collides with a fundamental barrier: the **FLP impossibility** result implies no deterministic protocol achieves Byzantine agreement in fully asynchronous networks with even one fault, and DKG needs agreement (on the contributor set *QUAL*) to terminate consistently. Asynchronous DKG therefore requires *randomized* agreement primitives and settles for *expected* round complexity.

**Kate and Goldberg (2009)** built the first practical Internet-oriented DKG [7], combining an asynchronous-model VSS with a leader-based agreement protocol, and demonstrated that the notorious difficulty of randomized Byzantine agreement could be managed in a real system. Their work reframed DKG as a systems problem: timeouts, leader election, and group membership changes matter as much as the cryptography.

The first *fully* asynchronous DKG came from **Kokoris-Kogias, Malkhi, and Spiegelman** [6]. Their key innovation is *high-threshold asynchronous VSS* (HAVSS): using an asymmetric bivariate polynomial, a dealer shares a secret hidden from any *k* nodes with *f+1 < k ≤ 2f+1*, while an honest party that missed the sharing phase can still recover its share from just *n − 2f* others. Paired with an *Eventually Perfect Common Coin* (EPCC) and an *Eventually Efficient Asynchronous Binary Agreement* (EEABA), the construction yields dual-threshold *(f, 2f+1)* keys — exactly what scalable consensus needs — with expected *O(n)* rounds and *O(κn⁴)* total communication against *t < n/3* static corruptions [6].

**Das, Yurek, Xiang, Miller, Kokoris-Kogias, and Ren (2022)** made ADKG practical [5]. Their protocol runs *n* instances of *asynchronous complete secret sharing* (ACSS), uses reliable broadcast to propose candidate contributor sets, and then runs parallel asynchronous binary agreements to settle on one set — sidestepping the heavyweight machinery of [6] while keeping the optimal *t < n/3* resilience. Crucially, they *implemented* it: key generation completes in 8.5 seconds for *n = 16* and 134 seconds for *n = 64* on cloud infrastructure, the first concrete evidence that asynchronous DKG is deployable rather than merely possible.

> **Theorem 4 (ADKG liveness and safety, informal).** With *t < n/3* static corruptions, the Das et al. protocol terminates with probability 1; all honest parties output shares of a common uniformly random secret, and the adversary learns nothing beyond the public key. [5]

### 4.5 Modern Deployments and Variants

These protocols now underpin the applications their designers targeted: DKG serves as the setup phase for randomness beacons, threshold signatures, and general multiparty computation [5], and the GJKR framework [3, 4] remains the reference design for synchronous threshold key establishment. Ongoing work extends the same template toward adaptive security, transparent setup, and post-quantum hardness — each generation trading the same currencies: rounds, communication, resilience, and setup assumptions.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### Proof sketches

> **Theorem 1′ (Feldman verification soundness).** If *g^{sᵢ} ≠ Π Cₖ^{iᵏ}*, then with overwhelming probability the dealer distributed a share inconsistent with the committed polynomial; an honest verifier rejects. *Proof sketch.* The commitments fix the polynomial *f* in the exponent under the DL assumption; any *sᵢ ≠ f(i)* fails the check except with negligible probability. ∎ [1]

> **Theorem 2′ (Pedersen binding).** Any PPT dealer producing two valid openings *(s, t) ≠ (s′, t′)* of the same commitment vector yields *log_g h*. *Proof sketch.* From *gˢhᵗ = g^{s′}h^{t′}* with *s ≠ s′*, compute *log_g h = (s − s′)/(t′ − t)*. ∎ [2]

> **Theorem 3′ (GJKR simulatability).** The simulator, given the target public key *y*, programs the honest parties' Feldman extraction values to be consistent with *y* while embedding the adversary's contributions verbatim; indistinguishability follows from the hiding of Pedersen VSS. ∎ [3]

### Asymptotic comparison

| Protocol | Rounds | Communication | Resilience | Key property |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Feldman VSS [1] | 1 | *O(n)* bcast + *O(n)* private | *t < n* (secrecy) | Non-interactive, private verification |
| Pedersen VSS [2] | 1 | *O(n)* bcast + *O(n)* private | *t < n* (secrecy) | Information-theoretic hiding |

### Reference simulation

We validated the Feldman and Pedersen verification equations over the toy group ℤ₂₃* (subgroup of order 11, *g = 4*), with secret *s = 7*, threshold *t = 2*, *f(x) = 7 + 3x*, blinding *b(x) = 5 + 2x*, *h = 9*:

```python
p, q, g, h = 23, 11, 4, 9
a, b = [7, 3], [5, 2]                      # f(x), blinding b(x) coeffs
C = [pow(g, ak, p) for ak in a]            # Feldman commitments
E = [(pow(g, a[k], p) * pow(h, b[k], p)) % p for k in range(2)]  # Pedersen

def poly(c, x):
    return (c[0] + c[1] * x) % q

for i in (1, 2, 3):
    si, ti = poly(a, i), poly(b, i)
    assert pow(g, si, p) == (C[0] * pow(C[1], i, p)) % p            # Feldman
    assert (pow(g, si, p) * pow(h, ti, p)) % p == (E[0] * pow(E[1], i, p)) % p
print("all share verifications pass")
```

All three parties' shares verify under both schemes, and Lagrange interpolation over any two Feldman shares recovers *s = 7* exactly. The equations hold bit-for-bit, confirming the algebraic derivations of §4.1–4.2. A production verifier follows the same equation:

```rust
fn verify_share(share: Scalar, comms: &[G], i: Scalar) -> bool {
    let lhs = G::generator() * share;              // g^{s_i}
    let rhs = comms.iter().enumerate()             // Π C_k^{i^k}
        .map(|(k, c)| c * i.pow(k as u64))
        .fold(G::identity(), |acc, t| acc + t);
    lhs == rhs
}
```

## 6 Limitations

- **Feldman** offers only computational secrecy and *private* verifiability; shareholders cannot audit each other's shares, and *C₀ = gˢ* enables offline dictionary attacks on low-entropy secrets [1].
- **Pedersen** demands a trusted common reference string *(g, h)* with unknown discrete-log relation; a compromised setup destroys binding entirely [2].
- **GJKR** requires synchrony, a broadcast channel, and a *static* adversary; adaptive security needs heavier machinery, and complaint rounds add interaction precisely when faults occur [3].
- **Asynchronous DKG** pays steeply: *O(κn⁴)*-class communication, expected (not worst-case) round complexity, the *t < n/3* resilience ceiling, and reliance on PKI plus reliable broadcast [5, 6].
- **Quantum vulnerability.** Every scheme here reduces to the discrete-logarithm assumption; a cryptographically relevant quantum computer breaks Feldman, Pedersen, GJKR, and current ADKG alike. Post-quantum DKG from lattice assumptions remains an open engineering challenge.
- **Setup idealizations.** Analyses assume secure erasure of ephemeral randomness, authenticated confidential channels, and — for Pedersen — honest generation of *h*; real deployments must enforce each.

## 7 Conclusion

From Feldman's single broadcast of exponentiated coefficients [1] to Pedersen's perfectly hiding dual commitments [2], from the discovery and repair of DKG bias [3, 4] to fully asynchronous key generation with optimal resilience [5–7], the trajectory of verifiable secret sharing is the progressive elimination of trust: first the honest dealer, then the synchronous network, then the secrecy of the dealing transcript itself. The verification equations have barely changed — *g^{sᵢ} = Π Cₖ^{iᵏ}* remains the workhorse — but the adversary models they survive have grown steadily stronger. What remains is the hardest trust to remove: the setup. Trusted commitment keys, PKI, and the looming quantum break of the discrete logarithm define the next frontier. Until post-quantum, adaptively secure, truly setup-free DKG matures, practitioners must choose their position on the trade-off surface this thesis has mapped — and choose it with eyes open.
