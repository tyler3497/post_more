# Gravitational-Wave Astronomy: Matched Filtering, Waveform Systematics, and Bayesian Parameter Estimation of Compact Binary Coalescences

## Abstract

The direct detection of gravitational waves has transformed astrophysics into an observational science of strong-field gravity. This thesis develops the complete data-analysis pipeline by which sub-threshold strain perturbations — of order one part in \(10^{21}\) — are extracted from kilometers-scale interferometers, declared statistically significant, and converted into astrophysical measurements. We derive the optimal linear detection statistic under stationary Gaussian noise, the matched filter, and show how template-bank searches over waveform families accumulate signal-to-noise ratio coherently across the inspiral, merger, and ringdown phases of compact binary coalescence. We survey the principal waveform approximants — post-Newtonian expansions, the effective-one-body resummation, phenomenological models, and numerical-relativity surrogates — and quantify the systematic biases their differences imprint on inferred parameters. We then formulate Bayesian parameter estimation via Markov-chain Monte Carlo and nested sampling, as implemented in LALInference and Bilby, and discuss calibration uncertainty, multi-detector coherence, and the multi-messenger exploitation of binary neutron star mergers. The analysis is grounded throughout in the observational record of the LIGO–Virgo network, from GW150914 through GW170817 to GW190521.

## 1 Introduction

Einstein's general theory of relativity predicts that accelerated masses radiate ripples in the fabric of spacetime — gravitational waves — that propagate at the speed of light and carry away energy and angular momentum from their source [1]. The emission from even the most violent astrophysical events is astonishingly feeble by the time it reaches Earth: a binary black hole merger radiating several solar masses of energy in a fraction of a second produces a strain \(h \sim \Delta L/L \sim 10^{-21}\) in a 4-km interferometer arm, a displacement smaller than a thousandth of a proton diameter [1]. Extracting such signals demands not only exquisite instrumentation — the Advanced LIGO and Advanced Virgo detectors are among the most sensitive instruments ever constructed — but also a mathematically optimal data-analysis framework capable of digging signals out of noise that is thousands of times louder.

This thesis treats that framework in its full technical depth. The core insight is that compact binary coalescence (CBC) signals are *predictable*: given the intrinsic parameters of a binary (component masses, spin vectors) and its extrinsic configuration (distance, sky position, orientation, polarization, coalescence time and phase), general relativity specifies the emitted waveform to high precision. Detection therefore becomes a problem of *pattern recognition against a parametrized signal model*: correlate the data stream against a bank of predicted templates, and ask whether the correlation is too large to be a noise fluctuation. Once detected, the same models serve as the likelihood function in a Bayesian inference that maps the full posterior distribution over source parameters — and, through them, to astrophysics, cosmology, and tests of fundamental physics [2][3].

Three interlocking themes organize this work:

1. **Matched filtering and the template-bank search**, the optimal linear detector for known waveform shapes in Gaussian noise and the practical machinery (bank placement, coincidence, vetoes) that makes it robust against non-Gaussian detector glitches;
2. **Waveform approximants and systematics**, the hierarchy of theoretical models — post-Newtonian (PN), effective-one-body (EOB), phenomenological (Phenom), and numerical-relativity (NR) surrogate families — and the faithfulness criteria that determine when model errors threaten parameter-estimation fidelity;
3. **Bayesian parameter estimation**, the stochastic-sampling infrastructure (MCMC, nested sampling) that converts a trigger into posterior credible intervals on masses, spins, and distances, together with its extension into multi-messenger standard-siren cosmology.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Gravitational waves from compact binaries

A gravitational wave is a propagating solution of the linearized Einstein field equations. Far from the source, the transverse-traceless strain admits a multipole expansion dominated by the mass quadrupole:

> **Theorem:** In linearized gravity, the leading-order radiated strain at distance \(r\) from a slowly moving source is
> \(h_{ij}^{TT}(t, r) = \frac{2G}{c^4 r}\,\ddot{I}_{ij}^{TT}(t - r/c)\),
> where \(I_{ij}\) is the reduced mass-quadrupole tensor. Energy is carried away at the rate \(dE/dt = -\frac{G}{5c^5}\langle \dddot{I}_{ij}\dddot{I}_{ij}\rangle\).

For a quasi-circular binary, this quadrupole emission at twice the orbital frequency produces the characteristic *chirp*: frequency and amplitude both rise as the orbit shrinks, culminating in merger and the exponentially damped *ringdown* of the remnant black hole's quasinormal modes. The binary's phase evolution encodes the **chirp mass** \(\mathcal{M} = (m_1 m_2)^{3/5}/(m_1+m_2)^{1/5}\) at leading PN order, while higher-order corrections probe the mass ratio \(q = m_2/m_1 \le 1\) and the spins \(\chi_i = S_i c/(G m_i^2)\).

### 2.2 The interferometric detector

An interferometric gravitational-wave detector measures differential arm-length changes \(\Delta L(t)\) induced by the passing wave's strain \(h(t) = \Delta L(t)/L\). The Advanced LIGO detectors (Hanford, Washington and Livingston, Louisiana, each with 4-km arms) and Advanced Virgo (3-km arms near Pisa) reach strain sensitivities near \(10^{-23}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\) around 100 Hz [1]. The data \(s(t) = n(t) + h(t;\theta)\) consist of instrument noise \(n(t)\) — seismic, thermal, quantum shot noise, and transient *glitches* — plus any astrophysical signal \(h(t;\theta)\) parametrized by source parameters \(\theta\).

### 2.3 Prior art

Matched filtering for gravitational waves was formalized by the early LIGO data-analysis community building on Wiener's optimal-filter theory. The search architecture — noise PSD estimation, template-bank tiling with minimal-match guarantees, multi-detector coincidence, and signal-based vetoes — matured through the initial LIGO science runs and was proven in engineering runs preceding the September 14, 2015 detection of **GW150914**, a binary black hole merger with matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio \(\rho \approx 24\) and false-alarm rate below one per 203,000 years [1]. The companion parameter-estimation analysis [2] established the Bayesian machinery as the community standard, while the binary neutron star merger **GW170817** [3] opened multi-messenger gravitational-wave astronomy.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The matched filter as optimal linear statistic

Assume the noise is stationary and Gaussian with one-sided power spectral density \(S_n(f)\). Define the noise-weighted inner product

\[
(a \mid b) = 4\,\mathrm{Re}\int_0^{\infty} \frac{\tilde{a}^*(f)\,\tilde{b}(f)}{S_n(f)}\,df.
\]

For data \(s = n + h\), the log-likelihood ratio of the signal hypothesis against the null is, up to normalization, the overlap \((s \mid h)\) minus half the template norm. Maximizing over the unknown signal amplitude yields the celebrated detection statistic:

> **Theorem (Wiener/Matched filter):** Under stationary Gaussian noise, the Neyman–Pearson-optimal linear statistic for detecting a known waveform shape \(h\) is the normalized cross-correlation \(\rho = (s \mid h)/\sqrt{(h \mid h)}\). The quantity \(\rho\) is the *matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio*; its expectation under the signal hypothesis is the *optimal SNR* \(\rho_{\mathrm{opt}} = \sqrt{(h \mid h)}\).

Because the coalescence time and phase are unknown a priori, the filter is implemented efficiently in the frequency domain via the inverse Fourier transform of \(\tilde{s}^*(f)\tilde{h}(f)/S_n(f)\), producing the complex SNR time series \(\rho(t_c)\) whose modulus is maximized over \(t_c\) and phase in a single FFT pass.

### 3.2 Template banks and bank placement

The signal shape depends on intrinsic parameters (masses, spins), so a single filter is insufficient: one must tile the parameter space with a **template bank**. Bank placement uses the *mismatch metric* \(g_{ij}\), defined by the fractional loss of SNR between neighboring templates,

\[
1 - \frac{(h(\theta) \mid h(\theta + \Delta\theta))}{\sqrt{(h(\theta)\mid h(\theta))(h(\theta+\Delta\theta)\mid h(\theta+\Delta\theta))}} \approx g_{ij}(\theta)\,\Delta\theta^i \Delta\theta^j.
\]

Templates are laid down — typically on hexagonal lattices or via stochastic placement — so that the worst-case *minimal match* is 0.97, bounding the SNR loss from bank discreteness at 3%. A modern low-mass bank contains \(\mathcal{O}(10^5\)–\(10^6)\) templates, and matched filtering is embarrassingly parallel over templates, which is why GPU-accelerated search pipelines (PyCBC, GstLAL, MBTA) can process the full band in near real time.

### 3.3 From triggers to detections: coincidence and vetoes

Single-detector matched-filter output is polluted by non-Gaussian glitches that can mimic short CBC signals. Robust detection therefore requires:

1. **Coincidence**: triggers in two or more detectors must agree in template parameters and arrive within the light-travel time (\(\lesssim 10\) ms between Hanford and Livingston) plus timing uncertainty.
2. **Signal-based vetoes**, notably the \(\chi^2\) test, which partitions the template into frequency bands and demands that SNR accumulate across bands as the true signal predicts; glitches concentrate power anomalously in narrow bands.
3. **Background estimation** by time slides: artificially shifting one detector's data relative to another destroys true coincidences while preserving glitch rates, yielding an empirical false-alarm-rate mapping for each candidate [8].

Only when a coincident, veto-passing candidate survives with a false-alarm rate below the search's detection threshold (e.g., one per several observing runs) is a detection claimed [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Waveform approximant families: PN, EOB, Phenom, and NR surrogates

No closed-form solution of the two-body problem in full general relativity exists, so waveform modeling is a patchwork of complementary approximations:

| Family | Foundation | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Post-Newtonian (PN)** | Expansion in \(v/c\), e.g. TaylorF2 | Analytic, fast; accurate early inspiral | Diverges near merger; no ringdown |
| **Effective-one-body (EOB)** | Resummed PN mapped to a deformed test-particle problem, e.g. SEOBNRv4 [4] | Accurate inspiral–merger, NR-calibrated | Slow; expensive for long banks |
| **Phenomenological (Phenom)** | Closed-form frequency-domain fits, e.g. IMRPhenomXPHM [5] | Fast, covers precession and higher modes | Fit-region extrapolation risk |
| **NR surrogates** | Interpolants of numerical-relativity simulations, e.g. NRSur7dq4 | Most faithful to full GR | Limited parameter range (mass ratio, spin) |

The EOB program [4] re-sums the PN series into an effective Hamiltonian for the relative motion, whose plunge waveform is then completed with NR-calibrated merger–ringdown attachment. Phenom models [5] instead fit a parametrized closed-form expression directly to hybrid PN/NR datasets, producing the workhorse frequency-domain approximants used in both search banks and rapid parameter estimation. Surrogates interpolate reduced-basis representations of hundreds of full NR simulations and are currently the gold standard for accuracy within their domain of validity.

### 4.2 Faithfulness, effectualness, and the Lindblom criterion

Two distinct questions arise: *can the bank find the signal* (effectualness) and *will parameter estimation be unbiased* (faithfulness)? Given an exact waveform \(h_e\) and a model \(h_m\), the **mismatch** \(\mathcal{M} = 1 - \max_{t_c,\phi_c}(h_e \mid h_m)/\sqrt{(h_e\mid h_e)(h_m\mid h_m)}\) quantifies the fractional SNR loss.

> **Theorem (Lindblom accuracy criterion):** If the model mismatch satisfies \(\mathcal{M} < 1/(2\rho^2)\), then systematic parameter biases from waveform error are subdominant to statistical uncertainty at signal-to-noise ratio \(\rho\). Equivalently, models must become *more* faithful as detectors observe *louder* events.

This criterion drives the relentless improvement of approximants: at design sensitivity, events with \(\rho \sim 100\) will demand mismatches below \(5 \times 10^{-5}\), a regime where today's Phenom and EOB families begin to strain — particularly for precessing, unequal-mass systems with significant higher-mode content.

### 4.3 Bayesian parameter estimation: LALInference and Bilby

Detection asks *is there a signal*; parameter estimation asks *what produced it*. Given data \(d\), Bayes' theorem gives the posterior over parameters \(\theta\),

\[
p(\theta \mid d) = \frac{\mathcal{L}(d \mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{\mathcal{Z}}, \qquad \log\mathcal{L} \propto -\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_k (d_k - h_k(\theta) \mid d_k - h_k(\theta)),
\]

with the noise-weighted inner product evaluated coherently across the detector network \(k\). The 15-dimensional CBC parameter space (two masses, six spin components, distance, inclination, sky position, polarization, time, phase) is explored with stochastic samplers:

- **MCMC** (Markov-chain Monte Carlo), including parallel-tempered ensembles, walks the posterior via detailed-balance proposals;
- **Nested sampling** climbs nested iso-likelihood contours, simultaneously estimating the evidence \(\mathcal{Z}\) — essential for model comparison.

The LALInference library [6] pioneered this machinery for LIGO–Virgo, analyzing every early detection; **Bilby** [7] reimplemented it as a modular, user-friendly Python package that has become the workhorse of the third observing run catalogs. A minimal likelihood evaluation in this style looks like:

```python
import numpy as np

def log_likelihood(theta, data_fd, freqs, psd, waveform_model):
    """Gaussian log-likelihood of CBC parameters theta."""
    hp, hc = waveform_model(theta, freqs)          # model polarizations
    residual = data_fd - project_onto_detectors(hp, hc, theta)
    inner = 4.0 * np.real(np.sum(
        np.conj(residual) * residual / psd)) * (freqs[1] - freqs[0])
    return -0.5 * inner

# Bilby-style prior + sampler wiring (schematic)
priors = dict(mass_1=Uniform(10, 80), mass_2=Uniform(10, 80),
              luminosity_distance=UniformComovingVolume(100, 2000),
              a_1=Uniform(0, 0.99), a_2=Uniform(0, 0.99))
result = bilby.run_sampler(likelihood, priors, sampler='dynesty',
                           nlive=2000, outdir='gw150914_pe')
```

Posterior samples are visualized as *corner plots* — the 2D marginal distributions over chirp mass, mass ratio, effective spin \(\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}\), and luminosity distance that have become the signature figure of every detection paper [2].

### 4.4 Calibration, coherence, and multi-detector networks

The strain data are only as trustworthy as the detector **calibration**: photon-calibrator and electrostatic-drive references map photodiode counts to meters, with residual amplitude and phase uncertainties (typically a few percent in amplitude, a few degrees in phase) that must be *marginalized* over in parameter estimation rather than ignored [2]. Multi-detector coherence adds sky localization: arrival-time triangulation across the network constrains the source to annuli on the sky, with a three-detector network (adding Virgo) shrinking GW170817's localization to \(\sim 28\,\mathrm{deg}^2\) — the key that enabled its electromagnetic counterpart search [3]. The network also breaks the distance–inclination degeneracy: amplitude scales as \(1/d_L\) while the plus/cross polarization ratio encodes inclination, so multiple antenna patterns disentangle the two.

### 4.5 Multi-messenger astronomy and standard sirens

**GW170817** — a binary neutron star inspiral detected at \(\rho \approx 32.4\) with a chirp mass \(\mathcal{M} = 1.188^{+0.004}_{-0.002}\,M_\odot\) — was followed 1.7 s later by the short gamma-ray burst GRB 170817A and, eleven hours later, by the kilonova AT2017gfo [3]. This single event simultaneously (i) confirmed that binary neutron star mergers produce short GRBs and r-process nucleosynthesis sites, (ii) constrained the neutron-star tidal deformability \(\Lambda\) and hence the dense-matter equation of state, and (iii) delivered the first *standard-siren* measurement of the Hubble constant, \(H_0 = 70^{+12}_{-8}\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\), from the gravitational-wave distance combined with the host galaxy redshift — a distance ladder independent of the cosmic distance ladder entirely [3].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

The observational record validates the pipeline end to end. We summarize the landmark events:

| Event | Type | Network SNR | Key inferred parameters | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GW150914 [1][2] | BBH | 24 | \(m_1 = 36^{+5}_{-4}M_\odot\), \(m_2 = 29^{+4}_{-4}M_\odot\), \(d_L = 410^{+160}_{-180}\) Mpc | FAR < 1 / 203,000 yr |
| GW151226 | BBH | 13 | \(m_1 = 14.2^{+8.3}_{-3.7}M_\odot\); notable spin, \(\chi_{\mathrm{eff}} > 0\) | Second BBH |
| GW170817 [3] | BNS | 32.4 | \(\mathcal{M} = 1.188\,M_\odot\), \(d_L = 40^{+8}_{-14}\) Mpc | Multi-messenger |
| GW190521 | IMBH remnant | 14.7 | Total mass \(\sim 150\,M_\odot\); remnant \(\sim 142\,M_\odot\) | Mass-gap challenge |

Three results deserve emphasis as *empirical proofs* of the method:

1. **GW150914 parameter estimation** [2]: independent analyses with EOB (SEOBNR) and Phenom (IMRPhenom) waveform families returned statistically consistent posteriors — median component masses agreeing within their 90% credible intervals — demonstrating that waveform systematics were subdominant at \(\rho \sim 24\), exactly as the Lindblom criterion predicts.
2. **IMR consistency tests**: splitting each signal at a cutoff frequency and inferring the remnant mass and spin separately from the inspiral and post-inspiral segments yields mutually consistent estimates for all catalog events, a direct null test of general relativity in the genuinely strong-field regime [2].
3. **Population inference**: hierarchical Bayesian combination of the catalog's events (GWTC releases) constrains the binary black hole mass spectrum — including the debated pair-instability mass gap — and the merger rate density, converting individual posteriors into astrophysics [8].

## 6 Limitations

The pipeline's weaknesses are as instructive as its strengths:

- **Non-Gaussian, non-stationary noise.** Real detector data contain glitches — scattered-light arches, blip transients, 60 Hz mains artifacts — that violate the Gaussian assumption underlying the matched filter's optimality. The \(\chi^2\) veto and time-slide background estimation are engineering mitigations, not cures; marginal events near threshold remain vulnerable to misclassification.
- **Waveform systematics at high SNR.** The Lindblom bound tightens as \(\rho^2\): third-generation detectors (Einstein Telescope, Cosmic Explorer) will routinely see \(\rho \gtrsim 100\) events for which current approximants' mismatches (\(\sim 10^{-3}\) for difficult corners of parameter space) induce biases larger than statistical errors. Eccentricity, matter effects beyond leading tidal order, and environmental perturbations are still only partially modeled.
- **Calibration uncertainty.** Amplitude calibration errors at the percent level propagate directly into distance (and hence \(H_0\)) measurements; marginalizing over calibration envelopes inflates credible intervals and will dominate the error budget of standard-siren cosmology once statistical errors shrink.
- **Computational cost.** Full Bayesian parameter estimation with precessing, higher-mode waveforms costs \(\mathcal{O}(10^6\)–\(10^7)\) likelihood evaluations and days of wall-clock time per event; the catalog era (hundreds of events) demands reduced-order models, relative binning, and machine-learning-accelerated samplers that are still being validated.
- **Selection effects.** The detected population is filtered through the search's sensitivity — louder, heavier, nearer binaries are overrepresented — so astrophysical conclusions require careful hierarchical modeling of the selection function [8].

## 7 Conclusion

Matched filtering converts the seemingly hopeless problem of detecting \(10^{-21}\) strain signals into a tractable exercise in optimal statistical inference: under Gaussian noise the cross-correlation \((s \mid h)\) is the best linear detector that exists, template banks extend it across the full astrophysical parameter space, and coincidence plus signal-based vetoes harden it against the realities of instrument glitches [1][8]. The waveform models at the filter's heart — the PN/EOB/Phenom/NR-surrogate hierarchy [4][5] — encode our best understanding of strong-field gravity and are held to the Lindblom faithfulness standard that tightens with every decibel of detector improvement. Bayesian parameter estimation [6][7] then extracts masses, spins, distances, and sky positions with rigorously quantified uncertainty, feeding tests of general relativity, population astrophysics, and standard-siren cosmology.

The trajectory is clear: as detectors approach design sensitivity and beyond, the data-analysis challenge shifts from *finding* signals to *modeling them faithfully enough*. The next decade's frontier — eccentric mergers, neutron-star matter, intermediate-mass black holes, and cosmological standard sirens at percent-level precision — will be won or lost in the waveform systematics budget. The mathematical machinery reviewed here, from Wiener's filter to nested sampling, is the foundation on which that frontier will be built.
