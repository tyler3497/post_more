# Byzantine Fault Tolerance: From the FLP Impossibility Result to HotStuff — Partial Synchrony and the Modern Consensus Frontier

## Abstract

This thesis traces the arc of Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus from impossibility to industrial deployment. We begin with the Fischer–Lynch–Paterson (FLP) impossibility result of 1985, which proves that no deterministic protocol can guarantee agreement in a fully asynchronous system with even one crash fault, and we reconstruct its bivalence argument with full rigor. We then examine how *partial synchrony*, formalized by Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer, re-opens the design space: once a global stabilization time guarantees eventual bounded delays, deterministic consensus becomes attainable. The Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance protocol of Castro and Liskov instantiated this theory as the first high-performance Byzantine state-machine replication system, and its three-phase paradigm dominated two decades of research. Finally, we analyze HotStuff, which achieves the long-sought combination of *linear* view change and *optimistic responsiveness* through pipelined three-chain commits and threshold signatures, and we survey Tendermint and DAG-based successors such as Narwhal and Tusk. Quantitative comparisons of latency, throughput, and communication complexity across protocols ground the theoretical narrative in measured performance.

---

##1 Intro

The consensus problem asks a deceptively simple question: given a set of processes, some of which may fail arbitrarily — lying, equivocating, or colluding — how can the non-faulty processes agree on a single value? In the taxonomy of faults, *Byzantine* faults are the most adversarial: a Byzantine process is constrained by nothing except cryptography and the limits of its computational model. This makes consensus under Byzantine faults the gold standard of distributed agreement, and its study the crucible in which four decades of distributed systems theory were forged [1][2].

The field's intellectual history is structured by one impossibility result and its successive evasions. FLP [1] showed determinism, asynchrony, and fault tolerance form an impossible triad — every practical protocol since 1985 is a *choice of which assumption to weaken*. Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer [2] weakened asynchrony just enough, admitting *partial synchrony*, to recover solvability; Castro and Liskov [3] engineered the result into a system only 3 percent slower than unreplicated NFS; and HotStuff [4] re-expressed the three-phase pattern in linear, pipelined form for the blockchain era.

This thesis reconstructs that lineage in detail. Our contributions are:

1. A self-contained, rigorous exposition of the FLP bivalence argument, with an executable Python model of adversarial scheduling.
2. A unified analysis of PBFT's three-phase protocol as an instance of the partial-synchrony framework, including a TLA+ sketch of a consensus round and quorum-intersection proofs.
3. A comparative treatment of Tendermint [5][6], HotStuff [4], and DAG-based protocols [7], quantifying the latency–throughput–complexity trade-offs with measured numbers.
4. An honest accounting of what the theory still cannot deliver: the limitations of the Byzantine model itself.

##2 Background

### 2.1 The consensus problem and its variants

In its canonical *binary consensus* form, each of *n* processes proposes a value from a binary domain, and the protocol must satisfy three properties:

- **Agreement:** no two non-faulty processes decide differently.
- **Validity:** if all non-faulty processes propose the same value *v*, then any decision must be *v*.
- **Termination:** every non-faulty process eventually decides.

For *state-machine replication* (SMR) — the formulation used by PBFT, HotStuff, and Tendermint — the problem generalizes to *atomic broadcast*: replicas must agree on a totally ordered sequence of client commands. Safety (agreement) and liveness (termination) remain the governing properties, but the protocol must now order an unbounded stream of decisions rather than a single one [3][4].

### 2.2 Timing models

The solvability of consensus turns almost entirely on the *timing model*:

| Model | Message delays | Process speeds | Deterministic consensus? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Synchronous** | Bounded by known Δ | Bounded, known | Yes, even with Byzantine faults (given *n* > 3*f*) |
| **Partially synchronous** | Eventually bounded after unknown GST | Eventually bounded | Yes (safety always, liveness after GST) |
| **Asynchronous** | Unbounded, unknown | Unbounded | **No** — FLP impossibility [1] |

> Theorem: *(FLP, 1985).* No deterministic protocol can solve consensus in an asynchronous system if even one process may crash [1].

The table above is the entire thesis in miniature: everything that follows is an exploration of the middle row.

### 2.3 The Byzantine Generals lineage

Lamport, Shostak, and Pease formalized the Byzantine model, showing *n* > 3*f* necessary and sufficient for synchronous agreement with oral messages, but with exponential message complexity. The practical history of BFT is the history of reducing that complexity while weakening synchrony: partial synchrony with PBFT [3], linear communication with HotStuff [4], decoupled dissemination with DAG designs [7].

##3 Methodology

Our method is *comparative and reconstructive*: we restate the field's central proofs — FLP bivalence [1], DLS partial synchrony [2], PBFT safety [3], HotStuff's three-chain rule [4] — in uniform notation, and tabulate the implicit performance claims (message complexity, commit latency, view-change cost) from reported measurements [4][7][8].

We adopt the standard partially synchronous model throughout: *n* = 3*f* + 1 replicas, at most *f* Byzantine, digital signatures available, and a global stabilization time GST after which all message delays are bounded by a known Δ. Safety must hold *always*; liveness need only hold *after* GST. This safety–liveness asymmetry is the load-bearing architectural insight of the field: an adversary that controls network timing can stall progress indefinitely, but must never be able to split the system into two committed halves [2][3].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The FLP bivalence argument, formalized

The FLP proof [1] operates on *configurations* — the complete state of the system, including process states and the multiset of in-flight messages. A configuration *C* is:

- **0-valent** if every admissible execution from *C* decides 0;
- **1-valent** if every admissible execution from *C* decides 1;
- **bivalent** if both a 0-deciding and a 1-deciding execution exist from *C*;
- **univalent** if it is 0-valent or 1-valent.

The proof proceeds in two lemmas.

> Lemma 1: Every correct consensus protocol has a bivalent initial configuration.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose all initial configurations were univalent. Adjacent initial configurations differing in one process's input must straddle a boundary between a 0-valent and a 1-valent configuration, by validity. Crashing the differing process immediately makes the two executions indistinguishable to all survivors, contradicting agreement. ∎

> Lemma 2: From any bivalent configuration, there exists a schedule of one step that keeps the system bivalent.

*Proof sketch.* If every one-step successor of bivalent *C* were univalent, both valences occur among them; a case analysis isolates a *critical* step whose delayed versus prompt application straddles the valence boundary, and an indistinguishable delayed execution contradicts agreement. Hence some successor is bivalent. ∎

Chaining Lemma 2 forever produces a non-terminating admissible execution: the adversary simply always schedules the bivalence-preserving step. Since the model is asynchronous, delaying any message indefinitely is admissible. **Determinism is load-bearing**: a randomized protocol can break the symmetry that the adversary exploits, which is why randomized asynchronous Byzantine agreement exists while deterministic consensus does not [1].

We can exhibit the adversary's power concretely. The following Python sketch models the bivalence game: two processes propose binary values, and an adversarial scheduler always chooses a bivalence-preserving step.

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Optional

@dataclass
class Config:
    """A toy configuration: decided values and pending critical step."""
    valence: Optional[int]  # None = bivalent, else 0 or 1
    steps: int = 0

def adversarial_step(cfg: Config) -> Config:
    """The FLP adversary: always schedule the step that preserves bivalence."""
    if cfg.valence is None:
        # Lemma 2: some one-step successor is bivalent; the adversary picks it.
        return Config(valence=None, steps=cfg.steps + 1)
    return cfg  # univalent: protocol may now terminate

def run_consensus(limit: int = 10_000) -> Optional[int]:
    cfg = Config(valence=None)  # Lemma 1: bivalent initial configuration exists
    for _ in range(limit):
        cfg = adversarial_step(cfg)
        if cfg.valence is not None:
            return cfg.valence  # termination
    return None  # non-termination: the FLP conclusion

print(run_consensus())  # None: the adversary wins every deterministic play
```

That is the impossibility result in twelve lines. Real networks defeat the adversary by *timing out* — treating a sufficiently delayed message as evidence of failure, which is precisely what partial synchrony licenses [2].

### 4.2 PBFT and the three-phase paradigm

Castro and Liskov's PBFT [3] was the first BFT system to demonstrate *fast* Byzantine agreement — a replicated NFS service within 3 percent of an unreplicated server on the Andrew benchmark. With *n* = 3*f* + 1 replicas and a designated *primary*, each request passes through three phases:

1. **Pre-prepare.** The client sends a request to the primary. The primary assigns a sequence number and multicasts a signed `PRE-PREPARE` to all backups.
2. **Prepare.** Each backup validates the pre-prepare and multicasts `PREPARE`. A replica that collects 2*f* matching prepares (plus the pre-prepare) becomes *prepared* — it has a *prepare certificate*.
3. **Commit.** Each prepared replica multicasts `COMMIT`. A replica that collects 2*f* + 1 matching commits becomes *committed*, executes the request, and replies to the client.

Safety rests on *quorum intersection*: with *n* = 3*f* + 1 and quorums of 2*f* + 1, any two quorums share at least *f* + 1 replicas, hence a non-faulty one — which never votes twice at one sequence number in one view. In Haskell notation:

```haskell
-- Quorum intersection: any two (2f+1)-quorums share a correct replica.
quorumIntersection :: Int -> Int -> Bool
quorumIntersection n f =
  let q = 2 * f + 1
      overlap = 2 * q - n        -- pigeonhole lower bound on intersection
  in overlap >= f + 1            -- so at least one member is non-faulty
-- For n = 3f+1: overlap = 2(2f+1) - (3f+1) = f+1. Safety follows.
```

Faulty primaries trigger a *view change* in which replicas multicast prepared certificates — a quadratic, intricate subprotocol that HotStuff would later linearize [4].

We sketch a single PBFT-style round in TLA+, capturing the phase transitions and the quorum condition:

```tla+
---- MODULE BftRound ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets, TLC

CONSTANTS Replicas, F, Quorum
ASSUME Quorum = 2 * F + 1

VARIABLES phase, prepared, committed, decided

TypeOK ==
    /\ phase \in {"preprepare", "prepare", "commit", "done"}
    /\ prepared \subseteq Replicas
    /\ committed \subseteq Replicas

Init ==
    /\ phase = "preprepare"
    /\ prepared = {}
    /\ committed = {}
    /\ decided = FALSE

CollectPrepare ==
    /\ phase = "preprepare"
    /\ \E r \in Replicas : prepared' = prepared \cup {r}
    /\ UNCHANGED <<phase, committed, decided>>

PrepareQuorum ==
    /\ phase = "preprepare"
    /\ Cardinality(prepared) >= Quorum - 1
    /\ phase' = "prepare"
    /\ UNCHANGED <<prepared, committed, decided>>

CollectCommit ==
    /\ phase = "prepare"
    /\ \E r \in Replicas : committed' = committed \cup {r}
    /\ UNCHANGED <<phase, prepared, decided>>

CommitQuorum ==
    /\ phase = "prepare"
    /\ Cardinality(committed) >= Quorum
    /\ phase' = "done"
    /\ decided' = TRUE
    /\ UNCHANGED <<prepared, committed>>

Next == CollectPrepare \/ PrepareQuorum \/ CollectCommit \/ CommitQuorum
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<phase, prepared, committed, decided>>
```

Safety in this model is the invariant that `decided` implies a quorum of replicas committed the same request — a property TLC can check for small *n*, and whose general proof is the quorum-intersection argument above [3].

### 4.3 Partial synchrony: the GST model of Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer

Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer's 1988 paper [2] is the theoretical keystone of the entire field. It introduces two formulations of partial synchrony:

- **Unknown bound:** there exists an unknown bound Δ on message delays and an unknown bound Φ on relative process speeds; the bounds hold from the start but are unknown to the protocol.
- **Eventual synchrony (GST):** the system is asynchronous until an unknown *global stabilization time* GST, after which delays and speeds are bounded by known Δ and Φ.

The paper's central result, stated as a theorem below, is that consensus is solvable in both models with *n* > 3*f* for Byzantine faults.

> Theorem: *(DLS, 1988).* In the partially synchronous model with eventual synchrony, deterministic Byzantine consensus is achievable for *n* > 3*f*, with safety guaranteed even before GST [2].

The DLS protocols use a *locking* mechanism — the direct ancestor of PBFT's prepared certificates and HotStuff's locked QCs [2][3][4].

### 4.4 HotStuff: linearity meets responsiveness

By 2018 the field faced a paradox: PBFT's pattern was proven, yet systems paid two taxes — *quadratic* steady-state communication and a *quadratic view-change* that stalled throughput on leader failure [4][8]. Tendermint [5][6] had brought BFT to proof-of-stake chains but kept gossip-based quadratic phases. The open question was whether a partially synchronous protocol could achieve both:

- **Linearity:** *O(n)* communication per decision, including during leader replacement after GST;
- **Optimistic responsiveness:** a correct leader drives consensus at the speed of the actual network delay δ, not the worst-case bound Δ [4].

HotStuff, by Yin, Malkhi, Reiter, Gueta, and Abraham [4], answered yes to both — the first protocol to do so. Its design rests on two innovations.

**Threshold signatures and the star topology.** Every phase is a uniform *propose–vote* pattern: the leader broadcasts, replicas vote to the leader, and *n* − *f* partial signatures aggregate into a single *quorum certificate* (QC). All communication is *O(n)* per phase; view change reduces to collecting the highest QC from *n* − *f* replicas [4].

**The three-chain commit rule and pipelining.** HotStuff uses three phases — *prepare*, *pre-commit*, *commit* — each generating a QC, and *chains* them: the QC from view *v* becomes the proposal payload for view *v* + 1. A block commits when three QCs chain over consecutive views. Since every view has the same propose–vote shape, views pipeline — the steady state is a single repeating pattern.

The extra phase buys *responsiveness with linearity*: two phases let a replica *lock* on a QC, and the third lets a later leader propose on the highest locked QC without waiting for Δ.

```rust
/// HotStuff vote aggregation: n - f partial signatures -> one QC.
struct VoteAccumulator {
    view: u64,
    phase: Phase,          // Prepare | PreCommit | Commit | Decide
    partials: Vec<PartialSig>,
    threshold: usize,      // n - f
}

impl VoteAccumulator {
    fn add_vote(&mut self, sig: PartialSig) -> Option<QuorumCertificate> {
        self.partials.push(sig);
        if self.partials.len() >= self.threshold {
            // Threshold aggregation: O(1)-sized certificate, O(n) messages.
            Some(QuorumCertificate::aggregate(
                self.view, self.phase, &self.partials,
            ))
        } else {
            None
        }
    }
}
```

Safety is an induction on views: a locked QC at view *v* is extended by every higher QC, since any two (n − f)-vote sets intersect in a correct replica that never votes against its lock [4]. PBFT, Tendermint, and DLS all embed in this framework — HotStuff unifies the field as much as it extends it. LibraBFT/DiemBFT adopted pipelined HotStuff; 2-chain variants like Jolteon trade the third phase for lower latency at the cost of quadratic view changes [8].

### 4.5 Tendermint and the DAG-based frontier

Tendermint [5][6] is the bridge between classical BFT and the blockchain era: a PBFT-like propose–prevote–precommit cycle per *height* with stake-weighted rotating proposers, and the now-standard separation of consensus engine from application state machine via ABCI. Its costs are quadratic gossip phases and worst-case commit latency in some rounds [6].

Narwhal and Tusk [7] decouple *data dissemination* from *ordering*: every validator reliably broadcasts its own batches into a shared DAG of certificates, and Tusk orders only small references into it. Dissemination proceeds at network speed in parallel, sustaining over 500,000 tx/sec with sub-second wide-area latency under failures [7].

| Protocol | Steady-state msgs/decision | Commit latency (message delays) | View change | Responsiveness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [3] | *O(n²)* | 3 | *O(n²)* | No (waits Δ in view change) |
| Tendermint [5][6] | *O(n²)* (gossip) | 3+ | *O(n²)* | Partial |
| HotStuff [4] | *O(n)* | 7 (chained) | *O(n)* | Yes — *O(δ)* |
| Fast-HotStuff [8] | *O(n)* | 5–6 | *O(n)*–*O(n²)* | Yes |
| Narwhal/Tusk [7] | *O(n)* amortized | ~4–6 | Leaderless DAG | Yes |

The trajectory is clear: each generation preserves the safety core while pushing communication toward linear and latency toward the physical floor.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

**Measured throughput and latency.** PBFT's prototype sustained tens of thousands of null ops/sec within 3 percent of unreplicated NFS [3]. HotStuff over 100+ replicas matched BFT-SMaRt's throughput and latency while replacing quadratic failover with linear communication [4]. Narwhal/Tusk pushed past 500,000 tx/sec with sub-second geo-distributed latency under injected faults [7]; Tendermint-based chains finalize in low single-digit seconds with hundred-validator sets [6].

**Proof obligations discharged.** Across the lineage, four proof patterns recur and compose:

1. *Bivalence preservation* (FLP [1]): the adversary's indefinite deferral argument, reproduced in §4.1.
2. *Quorum intersection* (PBFT [3]): any two (2*f* + 1)-quorums share a correct replica — the Haskell one-liner in §4.2.
3. *Lock monotonicity* (DLS [2], HotStuff [4]): a correct replica never votes against a higher locked QC, so committed values are stable across views.
4. *Chain induction* (HotStuff [4]): three chained QCs imply that a quorum is locked on the committed block in all higher views.

Together they yield the field's central safety theorem: with n > 3f, no two correct replicas commit conflicting values at the same log position, regardless of GST [2][3][4].

**Complexity accounting.** The dominant cost is authenticator complexity — signature verifications per decision. PBFT verifies *O(n²)* MACs per request; HotStuff verifies *O(n)* shares per phase; DAG designs amortize dissemination so per-transaction cost approaches *O(1)* [4][7] — which is why linear protocols scale to hundreds of replicas while quadratic ones stall near a hundred.

##6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating what four decades of progress have *not* achieved.

- **The one-third bound is fundamental.** For Byzantine agreement with authentication in partial synchrony, *n* > 3*f* is a hard floor, and it is tight. Every protocol surveyed here pays a threefold replication tax before tolerating a single adversary. Crash-fault protocols need only *n* > 2*f*; the Byzantine premium is real and unavoidable [1][2].

- **Liveness is never unconditional.** FLP is *priced in*: every deterministic protocol's liveness depends on eventual synchrony. Randomized protocols buy probabilistic termination at the cost of expected-round complexity [1].

- **The network is not the only adversary.** The model assumes a computationally bounded adversary and a correct cryptographic layer. It says nothing about implementation bugs, side channels, misconfigured PKI, or the social layer that selects validators. Production BFT failures — chain halts, equivocating validator cartels, governance capture — almost always occur *outside* the formal model [6].

- **Latency floors remain.** Even HotStuff's responsive design commits in seven message delays in its chained form; two-chain variants cut this to five but reintroduce quadratic view changes [8]. The speed of light and the number of sequential phases impose a floor that no pipelining removes.

- **Leader-centric designs concentrate power.** Stake weighting and MEV extraction reintroduce centralization pressures the consensus layer cannot see; DAG designs diffuse dissemination but still need an ordering rule with a distinguished role [7].

##7 Conclusion

The history of Byzantine fault tolerance is one impossibility result honored, dissected, and routed around. FLP [1] drew the boundary: determinism, asynchrony, and faults cannot coexist. Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer [2] found the narrow pass — partial synchrony, unconditional safety, eventual liveness — and every practical system has walked it since: PBFT [3] proved it carries real traffic, Tendermint [5][6] carried it into public blockchains, HotStuff [4] straightened it into a linear pipelined highway, and DAG designs [7] are widening it.

The deep lesson is architectural: *separate what must hold always from what may hold eventually*. Safety is unconditional; only liveness waits on the network. That separation remains the field's most important design principle. The frontier now lies in throughput, latency, and decentralization — but the theoretical core, from bivalence to quorum certificates, is settled science.
