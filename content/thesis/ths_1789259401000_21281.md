# Measurement-Induced Phase Transitions in Monitored Quantum Circuits: Entanglement Dynamics, Purification, and the Critical Point

## Abstract

Monitored quantum circuits — brickwork arrays of random unitary gates interspersed with local projective measurements — host a genuine non-equilibrium phase transition tuned by the measurement rate. Below a critical rate $p_c$ the steady state obeys a *volume law* for entanglement and a maximally mixed initial state purifies only after exponentially long times; above $p_c$ measurements win, entanglement obeys an *area law*, and purification is rapid. We review the discovery of this **measurement-induced phase transition (MIPT)**, its mappings to classical statistical mechanics — the minimal-cut/percolation picture of Skinner, Ruhman and Nahum [1] and the replica Potts-model formalism of Jian, You, Vasseur and Ludwig [4] — the associated purification transition and its interpretation as a dynamically generated quantum error-correcting code [3], and the critical theory at $p_c$. We survey numerical evidence, the quantum Zeno viewpoint of Li, Chen and Fisher [2], and recent superconducting [6] and trapped-ion experiments, and discuss the postselection barrier and open questions in the field [5,7].

---

## 1 Introduction

Equilibrium statistical mechanics derives its power from a simple recipe: identify an order parameter, find the point where it changes singularly, and classify the resulting universality class. For most of the twentieth century this machinery was reserved for systems in thermal equilibrium. The past decade has taught us that *dynamics* itself can be critical. **Monitored quantum circuits** — unitary evolution interrupted by measurements whose outcomes are recorded — provide the cleanest known example: as the measurement rate $p$ is increased through a critical value $p_c$, the entanglement structure of the late-time state changes abruptly from extensive to boundary-like scaling [1,2].

The physical tension is elementary. Random unitary gates *scramble* quantum information, spreading entanglement ballistically across the system and driving it toward the Page-entangled, volume-law states typical of chaotic evolution. Projective measurements do the opposite: they collapse local degrees of freedom, severing entanglement bonds. One might naively expect a smooth crossover between these tendencies — the intuition that sparse local measurements should gradually erode a volume law. That intuition is wrong. The competition is genuinely singular [1,5].

> **Theorem:** *(Entanglement phase transition.)* In a one-dimensional brickwork circuit of random unitary gates with projective measurements at rate $p$, the disorder-averaged late-time entanglement entropy $\overline{S_A}$ of a subsystem $A$ of linear size $\ell$ obeys $\overline{S_A} \sim s\,\ell$ (volume law) for $p < p_c$ and $\overline{S_A} \sim \mathrm{const}$ (area law) for $p > p_c$, with a non-analytic point at $p = p_c$.

The significance of the MIPT extends far beyond a curiosity of random circuits. It furnishes a laboratory in which *entanglement itself* plays the role of an order parameter for a dynamical phase transition [5]; it admits exact mappings to classical statistical mechanics models, including bond percolation and $n$-state Potts models [1,4]; it is secretly a statement about **quantum error correction** — the entangling phase is a phase in which the circuit dynamics protect quantum information against the measurements [3]; and it has now been observed on real quantum processors [6,7].

This article develops the subject from first principles. Section 2 fixes notation and the monitored-circuit model. Section 3 describes the diagnostics and simulation methods. Section 4 — the deep dive — works through the minimal-cut mapping, the replica statistical-mechanics formalism, the purification transition and its error-correction interpretation, and the critical theory. Section 5 surveys numerical and experimental evidence, Section 6 confronts the limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### Random unitary circuits

Consider a chain of $L$ qudits, each of local Hilbert-space dimension $q$ (we will mostly take $q = 2$, qubits). Time proceeds in discrete layers. In each layer, two-qudit gates drawn independently from the Haar measure (or from the Clifford group) act on a brickwork pattern of neighboring pairs — first on even bonds, then on odd bonds [5]. Starting from a product state, such a circuit generates entanglement ballistically: the half-chain von Neumann entropy $S_A = -\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_A \log \rho_A$ grows linearly in time before saturating at the Page value $S_A \sim (L/2)\log q$, a volume law. Unitary evolution alone never produces an area law from a product initial state; the dynamics are maximally scrambling.

### Adding measurements

A **monitored** (or hybrid) circuit augments the unitary layers with measurements: after each unitary layer, every qudit is projectively measured in the computational basis independently with probability $p$ [1,2]. Crucially, we work in the *quantum-trajectory* picture: the measurement outcomes are recorded, so the state remains pure along each trajectory and the dynamics are stochastic but linear conditioned on the record. Averaging is performed *after* computing nonlinear quantities such as $S_A$ — i.e., we study $\overline{S_A(\mathbf{m})}$, the average over measurement locations and outcomes $\mathbf{m}$ — because the Born probabilities themselves depend on the state.

### The competing effects

The two operations pull in opposite directions:

- **Unitary gates** create entanglement between neighbors and spread it; they also *hide* information by scrambling it into nonlocal degrees of freedom, so that a later local measurement extracts almost no information about the global state [7].
- **Projective measurements** collapse a qudit onto a definite outcome, disentangling it from the rest of the chain; the entanglement destroyed is, however, only the entanglement that the measurement can *access*.

The subtlety emphasized by Altman and collaborators [7] is that scrambling makes measurements progressively *less* informative: once information is delocalized, each local measurement destroys only a "ridiculously small" amount of entanglement. This is precisely why the entangling phase survives at finite $p$ rather than collapsing immediately, and why the transition is sharp.

### Clifford circuits and simulability

A decisive technical simplification is the restriction to **Clifford circuits**: gates drawn from the two-qubit Clifford group and Pauli measurements. By the Gottesman–Knill theorem, such circuits are efficiently simulable on a classical computer via the stabilizer tableau, even though they generate volume-law entanglement [2]. Nearly all large-scale numerics on the MIPT exploit this fact, and the universality class of the transition appears insensitive to whether gates are Clifford or Haar-random [5].

## 3 Methodology

### Model definition

The standard (1+1)-dimensional model is:

1. Initialize $L$ qubits in a product state $|0\rangle^{\otimes L}$ (or the maximally mixed state $\rho \propto \mathbb{1}$ for purification studies [3]).
2. For each time step $t = 1, \dots, T$:
   - Apply random two-qubit Clifford (or Haar) gates on a brickwork pattern.
   - With probability $p$ per site, projectively measure $Z_i$, recording the outcome.
3. Compute trajectory-averaged diagnostics in the steady state ($T \gg L$).

### Diagnostics

The workhorse observables are:

- **Half-chain entanglement entropy** $\overline{S_{L/2}}$ and its scaling with $L$.
- **Tripartite mutual information** $I_3(A:B:C)$, which is $\sim -L$ in the volume-law phase and $\sim 0$ in the area-law phase, giving a clean crossing diagnostic for $p_c$ [2].
- **Purification time** $\tau$: starting from $\rho \propto \mathbb{1}$ (maximally mixed, entangled with a reference), the time for the system entropy to decay to $O(1)$. It scales as $\tau \sim \exp(L)$ for $p < p_c$ and $\tau \sim \log L$ for $p > p_c$ [3].
- **Fisher information** $\mathcal{F}$ of the measurement record with respect to the initial state: it is analytic in the entangling phase and non-analytic at the transition, serving as an information-theoretic order parameter [4].

### Simulation sketch

Stabilizer simulation tracks the $2L \times (2L+1)$ binary tableau of Pauli generators. The core loop is:

```python
import random

def monitored_clifford_step(tableau, L, p):
    """One brickwork layer + measurements on a stabilizer tableau."""
    # Even bonds: random Clifford 2-qubit gates (H, S, CNOT compositions)
    for i in range(0, L - 1, 2):
        apply_random_clifford(tableau, i, i + 1)
    # Odd bonds
    for i in range(1, L - 1, 2):
        apply_random_clifford(tableau, i, i + 1)
    # Projective Z measurements with probability p per site
    for i in range(L):
        if random.random() < p:
            outcome = measure_pauli_z(tableau, i)  # Born-rule sampling
            record(outcome)
    return tableau

def estimate_S_half(tableau, L):
    """Half-chain entropy = rank of off-diagonal block of the tableau."""
    # For a stabilizer state, S_A = (rank of X/Z block across cut)/2
    return stabilizer_entanglement_entropy(tableau, range(L // 2))
```

Repeating `monitored_clifford_step` for $T \sim 4L$ steps and averaging $S_{L/2}$ over $\sim 10^4$ trajectories yields the finite-size curves whose crossings locate $p_c$, with finite-size scaling collapse $\overline{S}/L = f((p - p_c)L^{1/\nu})$ extracting the correlation-length exponent $\nu$ [2,5].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The minimal-cut picture: entanglement as percolation

The first analytic window into the MIPT came from Skinner, Ruhman and Nahum, who mapped the *zeroth* Rényi entropy $S_0(A) = \log \mathrm{rank}\,\rho_A$ onto a classical optimization problem [1]. Represent the circuit as a tensor network in spacetime: unitary gates are tensors, and each projective measurement *cuts* the corresponding bond (it fixes the local index, severing that leg of the network).

> **Theorem:** *(Minimal cut.)* For a circuit of the form above, $S_0(A)$ equals the size of the **minimal cut** — the smallest number of unbroken bonds whose removal separates region $A$ from the rest of the spacetime network [1].

> **Proof:** *(Sketch.)* $S_0$ counts the number of nonzero Schmidt coefficients, which for a tensor network is bounded by the product of bond dimensions along any cut separating $A$ from its complement, and this bound is saturated for generic (random) tensors. Minimizing over cuts gives the exact value. ∎

This is the celebrated "vandalized resistor grid" mapping [8]: the vandal (the set of measurements) breaks bonds at random with probability $p$, and the entanglement equals the cheapest cut through the surviving network. The minimal cut undergoes a **percolation transition**: for $p$ below the bond-percolation threshold, unbroken bonds percolate and any cut must sever $O(\ell)$ bonds (volume law); above threshold, broken bonds percolate and the cut can thread through the cracks at $O(1)$ cost (area law). On the square spacetime lattice the bond-percolation threshold is exactly $p_c = 1/2$, and the $S_0$ transition inherits the percolation exponents — e.g., $S_0 \sim (p_c - p)^{-4/3}\,\ell^{\cdots}$ near criticality [1].

Two caveats are essential. First, the mapping is exact only for $S_0$, not the von Neumann entropy; the higher Rényi entropies obey a modified cut rule with *weighted* bonds [5]. Second, the true $p_c$ for $S_1$ differs from $1/2$ (numerics give $p_c \simeq 0.16$ for Clifford circuits), so percolation governs the $S_0$ universality class while the von Neumann transition belongs to a distinct, richer class [2,4].

### 4.2 The replica statistical-mechanics mapping

A far-reaching framework, developed by Jian, You, Vasseur and Ludwig [4], treats the transition via the **replica trick**. To compute trajectory-averaged Rényi entropies $\overline{S_n}$ and the Fisher information $\mathcal{F}$, one replicates the circuit $n$ times (with conjugate copies), averages over the random unitaries analytically using Weingarten calculus, and obtains an effective *classical* spin model in two spacetime dimensions:

- The $n$-th moment maps to a classical **$n$-state Potts model** with ferromagnetic couplings set by the gate ensemble and symmetry-breaking fields set by the measurement rate [4].
- The entanglement entropy $\tilde{S}^{(n)}$ becomes the *excess free energy of a domain wall* terminating on the top boundary of the spin model.
- The Fisher information $\mathcal{F}^{(n)}$ becomes the *magnetization* on the bottom boundary.

The MIPT is then literally an **ordering transition** of the Potts model: in the ordered phase domain walls are cheap and pinned (area law), while in the disordered phase the domain wall wanders and its free energy is extensive (volume law). In the large-$q$ limit the critical measurement probabilities $p_c^{(n)}$ can be computed analytically for $n \geq 2$, and the different Rényi indices belong to *distinct* universality classes — a striking prediction [4]. Analytic continuation $n \to 1$ recovers bond percolation on the square lattice, unifying the replica picture with the minimal-cut result of Section 4.1 [1,4].

The Fisher-information perspective adds an operational meaning: $\mathcal{F}$ quantifies how much the *measurement record* reveals about the initial state. In the entangling phase the record is nearly useless (scrambling hides the information); at $p_c$ the learnability of the initial state changes non-analytically [4]. The transition is thus simultaneously a transition in entanglement, in information extraction, and in statistical-mechanics order.

### 4.3 The purification transition and quantum error correction

Perhaps the most consequential reframing is due to Gullans and Huse [3]. Instead of a pure initial state, begin with the **maximally mixed** state $\rho = \mathbb{1}/2^L$ — equivalently, $L$ system qubits each maximally entangled with a reference. The circuit dynamics then *purify* the system as measurements extract information. Define the purification time $\tau$ as the time for the system entropy to become $O(1)$:

| Phase | Measurement rate | Purification time $\tau$ | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mixed / coding | $p < p_c$ | $\tau \sim \exp(cL)$ | Information hidden; quantum memory |
| Pure | $p > p_c$ | $\tau \sim \log L$ | Rapid collapse |

The purification transition coincides with the entanglement transition [3]. Its significance is the **quantum error correction** connection: for $p < p_c$, the scrambling unitary dynamics encode the reference's quantum information into a *dynamically generated quantum error-correcting code* whose code space is protected against the local measurements — the measurements act as errors, and the code corrects them for exponentially long times. The critical point $p_c$ is then precisely an **error-correction threshold**, in direct analogy with fault-tolerance thresholds [3,5]. The volume-law phase is a *coding phase*: a robust dynamical quantum memory that requires no active intervention.

Li, Chen and Fisher reached a compatible conclusion from a complementary angle: they identified the high-$p$ phase with the **quantum Zeno effect** (frequent measurements freeze local dynamics) and the low-$p$ phase with a weakly measured regime, deriving the transition from an effective domain-wall theory [2].

### 4.4 Critical exponents and the conformal description

At $p = p_c$ the system is scale-invariant, and the natural conjecture is a **conformal field theory (CFT)** description of the spacetime critical point [5]. Numerics support several sharp predictions:

- The half-chain entropy at criticality grows *logarithmically*, $\overline{S_{L/2}} \sim \alpha \log L$, reminiscent of (1+1)-D quantum critical ground states, with an effective central charge $\alpha$ extracted from the prefactor [2,5].
- Entanglement growth from a product state follows $S(t) \sim t^{1/z}$ with dynamical exponent $z = 1$, consistent with emergent Lorentz invariance.
- The correlation-length exponent from finite-size scaling is $\nu \approx 1.3$, distinct from 2-D percolation ($\nu = 4/3$) — confirming that the von Neumann MIPT is *not* in the percolation class [5].
- The critical fluctuations are strongly **multifractal**: different moments of the entanglement scale with different exponents, a fingerprint of the replica limit $n \to 1$ and of non-unitary CFTs [5].

A fully controlled CFT for the MIPT remains elusive — the relevant field theories are non-unitary, logarithmic, and replica-valued — making this one of the most active theoretical frontiers in the subject [4,5].

## 5 Empirical and Theoretical Evidence

### Numerics

Large-scale stabilizer simulations pin down the transition quantitatively. Representative critical points in (1+1) dimensions:

| Gate ensemble | $p_c$ | $\nu$ | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clifford | $\simeq 0.16$ | $\simeq 1.3$ | Li–Chen–Fisher [2] |
| Haar-random | $\simeq 0.17$ | $\simeq 1.3$ | reviewed in [5] |
| Zeroth Rényi ($S_0$) | $= 1/2$ | $= 4/3$ | Skinner–Ruhman–Nahum [1] |

The agreement of $p_c$ and $\nu$ between Clifford and Haar ensembles is strong evidence for a single (1+1)-D MIPT universality class for the von Neumann entropy, independent of microscopic gate details [5]. The tripartite mutual information $I_3$ provides the cleanest finite-size crossings, since it cancels boundary contributions [2].

### Experiments

Observing the MIPT experimentally is formidably hard: verifying the phase of a *single trajectory* requires postselecting on its measurement record, whose probability is exponentially small in spacetime volume. Experiments therefore probe proxies. The landmark demonstration is Google Quantum AI's 70-qubit Sycamore experiment [6]: measuring a probe qubit's entanglement as a function of circuit depth, they observed a transition in entanglement scaling with critical depth $3 < T_c < 4$, and — by diluting gate density $\rho$ — a continuous transition at $\rho_c \approx 0.6$–$0.8$, with noise sensitivity itself serving as an order parameter distinguishing the phases [6]. Complementary observations include trapped-ion experiments (Noel *et al.*) resolving pure versus mixed/coding phases and superconducting few-qubit realizations of volume-law versus area-law scaling [7]. As Quanta Magazine's survey emphasizes, these experiments collectively confirm that the transition survives realistic noise, even though the idealized postselected critical point remains out of reach [7].

## 6 Limitations

The theory, for all its elegance, faces serious qualifications:

1. **The postselection barrier.** The MIPT is a property of the *conditional* state given a measurement record. Preparing that state requires postselection with success probability $\sim \exp(-c\,pLT)$ — exponentially costly. No scalable protocol is known to observe the sharp transition without exponential overhead, and whether a postselection-free order parameter exists remains open [5,7].
2. **Noise and decoherence.** Real devices suffer unmonitored errors, which act as an extra effective measurement channel and can round or shift the transition [6].
3. **Rényi-index dependence.** Different Rényi entropies appear to have different $p_c$ and universality classes [4]; the "the" transition is really a family of transitions, and their interrelations are not fully mapped.
4. **Higher dimensions.** The (2+1)-D MIPT is far less understood; analytic mappings are weaker and numerics costlier [5].
5. **The critical theory is unsettled.** The proposed non-unitary CFT lacks a derivation from first principles, and multifractal spectra are measured, not derived [4,5].
6. **Basis dependence.** The transition requires measurements in a *fixed local* basis; allowing measurements in arbitrary nonlocal bases destroys it, tying the phenomenon to locality and scrambling [4].

---

## 7 Conclusion

The measurement-induced phase transition reorganizes our understanding of what measurements do in many-body quantum dynamics. What began as a numerical surprise in 2019 — three groups independently finding that entanglement growth collapses at a critical measurement rate [1,2,7] — has grown into a rich interdisciplinary field touching classical percolation, replica statistical mechanics, quantum Shannon theory, and fault tolerance. The emerging synthesis is striking: *the entangling phase is a phase of dynamically protected quantum information*, the critical point is an error-correction threshold [3], and the measurement record's Fisher information is the natural order parameter [4].

Open directions abound: a first-principles CFT of the critical point, postselection-free probes of the transition, the higher-dimensional phase diagram, and the use of monitored dynamics for autonomous quantum memories. If history is a guide, the sharpest insights will come — as they did in 2019 — from the interplay of large-scale simulation, analytic mappings, and experiment [5,6].
