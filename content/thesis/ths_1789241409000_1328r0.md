# Photolithography at the Ångström Limit: EUV Patterning, Stochastic Defects, and High-NA Optics

## Abstract

Extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) lithography at 13.5 nm sustains semiconductor scaling below the 3-nm logic node, yet operates where classical optical models fail. With photon energies near 92 eV, a single exposure delivers only hundreds of absorbed photons per ten-nanometer pixel, so photon shot noise dominates over deterministic aberrations, driving stochastic print failures — microbridges, missing contacts, locally closed vias — that appear without repeating location. High-numerical-aperture (NA = 0.55) anamorphic projection pushes Rayleigh resolution toward 8-nm half-pitch but compresses depth of focus to tens of nanometers, forcing ultrathin resists that absorb still fewer photons. This thesis develops a unified physical picture: the optical scaling laws mandating high-NA, the Poisson statistics setting a hard floor on line-edge roughness, the secondary-electron cascade converting each photon into nanometer-scale chemical blur, and the response of chemically amplified and metal-oxide resists. We derive the resolution–roughness–sensitivity trade-off from first principles, analyze the exponential blur distribution behind rare catastrophic defects, and evaluate anamorphic imaging's costs — halved field, die stitching, shallow focus. Stochastic defect control, not nominal resolution, is now the binding constraint on patterning.

## 1 Introduction

The continuation of semiconductor scaling into the sub-3-nm regime depends on a single patterning step: extreme-ultraviolet lithography at 13.5 nm wavelength. Where deep-ultraviolet (DUV) immersion lithography at 193 nm reached its resolution ceiling near 38-nm half-pitch and survived only through multiple patterning, EUV offered a wavelength reduction of more than fourteen-fold in one step, restoring single-exposure patterning for the most critical layers [1]. The transition was not merely optical. At 13.5 nm, matter interacts with light through photoionization rather than photochemistry: every absorbed photon ejects a fast electron whose subsequent cascade determines what the resist records. Patterning thus became an exercise in applied radiation physics, with counting statistics as a first-order design variable.

Two converging developments define the current frontier. First, high-NA EUV systems with numerical aperture 0.55 have entered deployment, extending the classical Rayleigh resolution from roughly 16-nm half-pitch (NA = 0.33) toward 8-nm half-pitch, at the cost of a depth of focus measured in tens of nanometers and an anamorphic projection that halves the exposure field [2]. Second, the industry has recognized that the failure mode that matters most is no longer systematic defocus or aberration but *stochastic* print failure: isolated, non-repeating defects whose probability per feature is governed by the discreteness of photons and the randomness of electron scattering [3]. Unlike classical defects, stochastic failures cannot be eliminated by tighter process control; they are intrinsic to the physics of counting few quanta, and they must be driven below parts-per-billion levels by dose, chemistry, and optical design.

This thesis examines EUV patterning from photon to printed feature. Section 2 reviews the optical and chemical foundations. Section 3 describes the analytical and computational methodology used to connect photon statistics to defect probabilities. Section 4 develops the core physics in depth: Rayleigh scaling, anamorphic high-NA optics, shot noise, secondary-electron cascades, and resist chemistry. Section 5 presents quantitative arguments — including a Poisson derivation of the roughness floor and the RLS trade-off — alongside the empirical evidence from the literature. Section 6 states the limitations of current models and materials, and Section 7 concludes with the research directions that will determine whether patterning can reach the 1-nm node and beyond.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From DUV to EUV: why the wavelength had to change

Optical lithography resolves features according to the Rayleigh criterion,

$$R = k_1 \frac{\lambda}{NA}, \qquad DOF = k_2 \frac{\lambda}{NA^2},$$

where $R$ is the minimum resolvable half-pitch, $DOF$ the depth of focus, and $k_1, k_2$ process factors of order 0.3–0.6 [4]. At $\lambda = 193$ nm with water immersion ($NA = 1.35$), the single-exposure limit sits near 36–40-nm half-pitch; the industry bridged the gap to the 10-nm node with self-aligned double and quadruple patterning, paying in cost, overlay complexity, and design-rule restriction. EUV at $\lambda = 13.5$ nm changes the arithmetic by a factor of fourteen: even at $NA = 0.33$, $k_1 = 0.4$ gives $R \approx 16$ nm in a single exposure.

The price of this wavelength is that *all* materials absorb strongly at 13.5 nm, so refractive optics are impossible. EUV scanners are entirely reflective, using Bragg multilayer mirrors — typically 40–70 alternating Mo/Si bilayers tuned to the 13.5-nm resonance — each delivering roughly 65–70% reflectivity. With approximately eleven mirrors in the illuminator-plus-projection path, system throughput is only about 1–2% of the source power, which is why source brightness is a first-order economic variable [5].

### 2.2 The laser-produced-plasma source

Production EUV sources generate 13.5-nm radiation from a laser-produced plasma (LPP): microscopic tin droplets fall through vacuum and are struck by a pulsed CO$_2$ drive laser, creating a plasma hot enough that Sn$^{8+}$–Sn$^{14+}$ ions emit strongly in the 13.5-nm band. Modern sources deliver several hundred watts of in-band power at intermediate focus, and higher power remains the most direct lever against photon shot noise: more photons per unit dose is the only way to beat $\sqrt{N}$ statistics at fixed exposure time [2].

### 2.3 How EUV photons expose resist

A 13.5-nm photon carries $E = hc/\lambda \approx 91.8$ eV — far above molecular bond energies. Absorption ejects a photoelectron with roughly 80 eV of kinetic energy, which thermalizes through inelastic collisions, liberating a cascade of low-energy secondary electrons [6]. It is these secondary electrons, not the photon itself, that drive resist chemistry: in chemically amplified resists (CARs), they decompose photoacid generators (PAGs) to release acid; during post-exposure bake (PEB), acid catalyzes deprotection of the polymer, switching solubility. The spatial extent of the electron cascade — several nanometers, with a long exponential tail — convolves the optical aerial image with a stochastic blur kernel, setting a materials floor on roughness that no dose increase can fully remove [7].

### 2.4 Masks, pellicles, and the reflective mask problem

Because EUV cannot transmit through any pellicle or lens, the photomask is itself a Bragg mirror patterned with an absorber stack. Oblique chief-ray angles (about 6° at the mask for 0.33 NA, steeper for 0.55 NA) cause *mask 3D effects*: the absorber shadows features differently for horizontal and vertical orientations, producing telecentricity errors and pattern shifts that must be corrected in optical proximity correction (OPC) [2]. The fragility of the mask blank and the difficulty of a transmissive EUV pellicle — most materials absorb at 13.5 nm — remain persistent manufacturing concerns [1].
## 3 Methodology

This thesis is an analytical synthesis rather than a new experimental campaign: it unifies published optical scaling laws, photon-counting statistics, electron-transport theory, and resist-chemistry models into a single quantitative framework, and tests that framework against reported high-NA EUV data. The methodology comprises four elements.

1. **Optical scaling analysis.** We apply the Rayleigh and depth-of-focus relations to the 0.33 NA and 0.55 NA systems, computing the anamorphic field geometry and the focus budget imposed by the $1/NA^2$ dependence of DOF.
2. **Photon-statistics modeling.** From the source power, exposure dose, and resist absorption, we compute the mean number of absorbed photons per resolution element and propagate Poisson arrival statistics through to line-edge roughness via the Mack shot-noise model, deriving the $\mathrm{LER} \propto \mathrm{dose}^{-1/2}$ law and its breakdown at the materials floor [7].
3. **Secondary-electron transport.** We review Monte Carlo electron-scattering treatments (notably Fukuda's analysis of localized versus cascading generation) and the exponential blur distribution with ~5-nm scale that follows from low-energy electron mean free paths, connecting the electron blur kernel to stochastic defect nucleation [6].
4. **Resist-response and process-window analysis.** We contrast chemically amplified resists with metal-oxide (non-CAR) platforms on the resolution–LER–sensitivity (RLS) triangle, and evaluate how thinner resists, demanded by high-NA shadowing, trade etch resistance and photon capture against pattern collapse and contrast [5].

Wherever numerical values are quoted — photon counts, blur lengths, field dimensions, mirror reflectivities — they are grounded in the cited literature and supplier-published specifications.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Rayleigh criterion and why NA must rise

For EUV, $k_1$ factors near 0.3–0.4 are achievable with aggressive resolution-enhancement techniques. At $NA = 0.33$,

$$R_{0.33} = 0.4 \times \frac{13.5\ \mathrm{nm}}{0.33} \approx 16.4\ \mathrm{nm},$$

which supports 32-nm pitch, the workhorse of the 5-nm and 3-nm nodes. Printing below ~11-nm half-pitch demands either multiple patterning — economically unattractive after the industry's painful immersion experience — or higher NA. At $NA = 0.55$,

$$R_{0.55} = 0.4 \times \frac{13.5\ \mathrm{nm}}{0.55} \approx 9.8\ \mathrm{nm},$$

enabling 16–18-nm pitch in single exposure [2]. But the depth of focus collapses as $1/NA^2$:

$$DOF_{0.55} = k_2 \frac{13.5}{0.55^2} \approx k_2 \times 44.6\ \mathrm{nm}.$$

With $k_2 \approx 0.5$, the usable focus latitude is roughly **20–25 nm**, comparable to the total wafer topography after multilevel processing. Every nanometer of resist thickness, wafer non-flatness, and focus error now consumes a meaningful fraction of the process window — a regime in which atomic-level control of uniformity becomes a manufacturing prerequisite [3].

### 4.2 High-NA anamorphic optics: geometry and its costs

A symmetric 0.55 NA projection at the mask would require chief-ray angles so oblique that mask 3D shadowing becomes unmanageable. The solution adopted by ASML and Zeiss is *anamorphic* magnification: 4× reduction in the scan (x) direction and 8× in the slit (y) direction. Because the mask-side NA is the wafer-side NA divided by magnification, the 8× direction halves the mask-side angular spread, taming the 3D effects at the cost of a field that is 26 mm × 16.5 mm — exactly half the 26 mm × 33 mm standard field [2]. Large die must therefore be *stitched* from two exposures, introducing stitching overlay as a new critical parameter.

The anamorphic lens also carries a central obscuration, which removes the low spatial frequencies from the pupil. Computational lithography for high-NA consequently requires aberration characterization with basis functions adapted to annular pupils — Tatian polynomials rather than classical Zernikes — and source-mask optimization must be re-derived for the obscured, anamorphic pupil [2]. Nothing about the 0.33 NA computational infrastructure transfers without modification.

### 4.3 Photon shot noise: the Poisson floor of patterning

Consider a typical EUV dose of 30 mJ/cm². The photon fluence is

$$\Phi = \frac{30 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{J/cm^2}}{91.8\ \mathrm{eV} \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J/eV}} \approx 2.0 \times 10^{15}\ \mathrm{photons/cm^2} = 20\ \mathrm{photons/nm^2}.$$

A $10 \times 10$ nm² pixel — the area of a single minimum feature — therefore receives a mean of only $\bar{N} \approx 2000$ incident photons, and with resist absorption of order 20–40%, merely **400–800 absorbed photons** define whether that pixel prints. Poisson statistics give relative fluctuations $\sigma/\bar{N} = 1/\sqrt{\bar{N}} \approx 4$–5%. That fluctuation is imprinted directly on the latent image: Mack's shot-noise analysis shows that photon statistics alone produce a line-edge roughness that scales as $\mathrm{dose}^{-1/2}$, a law confirmed at low doses on microexposure tools before the materials floor intervenes [7].

The qualitative consequence is severe. A contact hole that should remain open can, by a local downward fluctuation of absorbed photons, fail to clear — and the *next* identical hole, exposed under nominally identical conditions, prints perfectly. These are stochastic print failures: microbridges, locally broken lines, and missing or merging contacts, randomly distributed and non-repeating [5]. Their probability per feature follows the tails of the photon-count distribution, so reducing the defect rate by an order of magnitude demands roughly a hundredfold increase in dose — economically impossible — or a fundamental change in resist absorption [4].

### 4.4 Secondary electrons: the cascade that blurs the image

Photon absorption is only the first step. The ~80-eV photoelectron scatters inelastically, and each scattering event spawns further secondaries, producing a cascade whose energy distribution runs from ~80 eV down to thermal. Fukuda's analysis distinguishes *localized* generation (a few secondaries near the absorption site) from *cascading* generation (an extended shower), identifying the cascade as a primary cause of stochastic defects [6]. The key result, emphasized by Chen's analysis of electron mean free paths, is that the effective chemical blur follows an approximately **exponential distribution with a ~5-nm scale** — but with a long tail: the 99.75th percentile reaches ~30 nm [8].

This distribution explains the phenomenology of stochastic defects better than photon statistics alone. For a *typical* 3.1-nm blur (46th percentile of the exponential), the printed image is practically unaltered; for a *rare* 30-nm event, the feature is essentially disrupted — a microbridge or a blocked contact appears from a single unlucky cascade [8]. Poisson photon noise sets the mean; the electron cascade supplies the rare, large excursions that nucleate defects. Acid diffusion in CARs is comparatively well-behaved, because diffusing acids are suppressed by the concentration gradient as they move outward; the electron blur has no such self-limiting mechanism.

### 4.5 Resist chemistry and the RLS triangle

Two resist families compete for high-NA insertion. **Chemically amplified resists** provide sensitivity through catalytic amplification — one acid deprotects many polymer sites — but pay with acid-diffusion blur and a fundamental LER floor observed even in virtual-resist simulations, indicating a materials limitation rather than a formulation accident [7]. **Metal-oxide resists** (notably tin-oxo clusters, e.g., Inpria's platform) are non-CAR systems in which EUV absorption directly drives ligand cleavage and cross-linking; their higher EUV absorption coefficient captures more photons per unit dose, and reported line-width roughness reaches $3\sigma < 3$ nm [1][4]. They trade sensitivity for resolution and roughness — the classic **RLS trade-off**: resolution, LER, and sensitivity cannot be optimized simultaneously, and the Pareto frontier is set by photon statistics and blur physics.

High-NA intensifies every term of the triangle. Oblique incidence at 0.55 NA shadows thick resist, forcing films down to ~30 nm or below; thinner films absorb fewer photons (worsening shot noise), offer less etch resistance, and are more vulnerable to pinholes and coating defects — while their lower aspect ratio beneficially suppresses pattern collapse [5]. The resist must therefore be *more absorbing per nanometer* at the same time as it is thinner: a materials-design problem with no slack.

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 The resolution–roughness–sensitivity trade-off, derived

> **Theorem (RLS Pareto bound):** For a resist of absorption coefficient $\alpha$ and thickness $t$ exposed at dose $D$, the photon-shot-noise contribution to line-edge roughness satisfies $\mathrm{LER}_{\mathrm{shot}} \propto (D\,\alpha\,t)^{-1/2}$, while the electron-blur contribution $\mathrm{LER}_{\mathrm{blur}}$ is dose-independent. Hence increasing dose reduces roughness only until the materials blur floor, and no dose increase can move the frontier past it.

*Proof sketch.* Absorbed photons per unit area $N = D\alpha t/(hc/\lambda)$ are Poisson with variance $N$. The latent-image edge position fluctuates as $\delta x \sim (\partial I/\partial x)^{-1} \delta I$, with $\delta I/I \sim N^{-1/2}$, giving the $D^{-1/2}$ law. The electron cascade convolves the aerial image with a fixed blur kernel of width $b \approx 5$ nm independent of $D$, leaving a residual roughness that does not vanish with dose. Simulations of virtual resists confirm the floor persists across formulations [7]. ∎

### 5.2 How many photons define a feature?

| Parameter | 0.33 NA (NXE) | 0.55 NA (EXE) |
|---|---|---|
| Wavelength | 13.5 nm | 13.5 nm |
| Rayleigh half-pitch ($k_1=0.4$) | ~16.4 nm | ~9.8 nm |
| Depth of focus ($k_2=0.5$) | ~62 nm | ~22 nm |
| Magnification | 4× / 4× | 4× / 8× (anamorphic) |
| Field size | 26 × 33 mm | 26 × 16.5 mm |
| Typical resist thickness | ~40 nm | ~30 nm |
| Absorbed photons per 10×10 nm² (30 mJ/cm², $\alpha t \approx 0.3$) | ~600 | ~450 |

The table makes the central irony quantitative: the higher-NA system, which prints smaller features, gives each feature *fewer* photons to define it, because thinner resist absorbs less. Shot noise therefore *worsens* as resolution improves, unless dose or absorption rises to compensate [3][5].

### 5.3 The exponential-blur model of stochastic defects

> **Theorem (Rare-event defect nucleation):** If the chemical blur of a single absorbed photon follows an exponential distribution with mean $b$, then the probability that one absorption event perturbs the image beyond a critical distance $d_c \gg b$ is $P = e^{-d_c/b}$, independent of dose; the defect rate per unit area scales with the number of absorption events.

This explains two observations: (i) stochastic defects are *rare* (the exponential tail is thin) yet *persistent* (they scale with event count, i.e., with area and dose, not with process drift); and (ii) increasing dose does not suppress the tail — it adds more events, each carrying the same tail probability [8]. The only remedies are to shorten the blur (resist chemistry), raise the critical distance (design rules, optical contrast), or absorb more photons per feature so that the mean image is robust even when individual events are extreme (absorption engineering, higher source power) [2][3].

### 5.4 Dose, source power, and the economic argument

Because $\mathrm{LER}_{\mathrm{shot}} \propto D^{-1/2}$, halving roughness by dose alone requires quadrupling dose — and quadrupling dose quarters scanner throughput at fixed source power, destroying cost-per-wafer economics. The industry's answer is to raise source power and resist absorption together, so that photons per feature rise at constant dose and throughput [2]. Source development and resist development are thus two handles on the same counting-statistics problem.

## 6 Limitations

The framework developed here has boundaries that must be stated plainly.

**Model fidelity.** The exponential-blur model is an effective description of a cascade whose true statistics depend on resist composition, density, and interface reflections; electron mean free paths at 1–100 eV remain uncertain, and interface reflection at the resist/underlayer boundary can reshape the blur [8]. Atomistic models of CAR deprotection exist but are computationally expensive and formulation-specific [7].

**Resist generality.** Quantitative claims about blur lengths and LER floors are drawn from specific resist platforms; metal-oxide resists, with direct EUV-driven chemistry and higher absorption, may obey different blur statistics, and their long-term etch-transfer and defectivity data are less mature than CAR data [4].

**Mask and source idealization.** This thesis treats the aerial image as given; mask 3D effects, flare, and source non-uniformity add systematic terms to the roughness and defect budgets that interact nonlinearly with stochastic terms [1]. High-NA source-power projections and their thermal and debris-management consequences are engineering variables outside the optical analysis.

**Metrology limits.** Stochastic defects at the single-nanometer scale strain even e-beam inspection: finding rare, isolated defects across a full wafer is slow, and the metrology itself can miss the events the models predict, biasing empirical defect densities downward [5].

**Scope.** We do not address complementary patterning (multi-patterning with high-NA), directed self-assembly, or beyond-EUV wavelengths (6.x-nm "BEUV"), each of which would change the photon-energy and optics assumptions substantially.

## 7 Conclusion

Photolithography at the Ångström limit is no longer governed by the classical question of how small a feature the optics can resolve. The optics — high-NA, anamorphic, multilayer-reflective — can resolve 8-nm half-pitch; the binding constraint is whether the few hundred photons and their secondary-electron cascades that define each feature can do so *reliably*. Photon shot noise sets a $\mathrm{dose}^{-1/2}$ law for roughness; the secondary-electron cascade adds an exponential-blur tail that nucleates rare catastrophic defects no dose increase can eliminate; and the RLS triangle confines resist engineering to a Pareto frontier fixed by these statistics. High-NA EUV sharpens every edge of this problem: it halves the field, collapses the depth of focus, thins the resist, and gives each smaller feature fewer photons.

The path forward is therefore a counting problem as much as an optics problem. Raising resist absorption per nanometer, raising source power, and engineering electron-blur chemistry are the levers that add photons and shorten tails; curvilinear mask correction and Tatian-polynomial aberration control adapt the computational infrastructure to the anamorphic pupil; and stitching, ultrathin-film uniformity, and e-beam inspection address the architectural costs of the 0.55 NA geometry [2][3]. If these advance together, single-exposure patterning can plausibly extend to the 1-nm node. If any one stalls, stochastic defectivity — not resolution — will be what ends scaling. The Ångström limit, in the end, is a limit on certainty, and the industry's task is to manufacture certainty out of countable quanta.
