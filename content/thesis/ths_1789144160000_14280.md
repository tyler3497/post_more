# The Module-Lattice Road to Post-Quantum Standardization: Module-LWE Hardness, the Fujisaki–Okamoto Transform, Fiat–Shamir with Aborts, and the Implementation-Attack Surface of FIPS 203 and FIPS 204

## Abstract

On 13 August 2024, the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology finalized FIPS 203 (Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism, ML-KEM) and FIPS 204 (Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm, ML-DSA), anchoring the post-quantum migration in the hardness of the *module* learning-with-errors and module short-integer-solution problems over the cyclotomic ring $\mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^{256}+1)$. This thesis dissects the two standards from first principles: the reductionist lineage from Regev's LWE to module-LWE/MSIS; the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform that lifts an IND-CPA public-key encryption scheme into an IND-CCA2 KEM, and how FIPS 203 modifies the classic FO pipeline with implicit rejection and a re-encryption check; the Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts paradigm, rejection sampling bounds, and the hint mechanism that make Dilithium-style signatures zero-knowledge in the secret; the concrete cryptanalysis economy (BKZ block sizes $\beta \in \{406, 623, 873\}$ and core-SVP bit levels) calibrated by lattice sieving models; and the physical attack surface exposed once mathematics meets silicon—KyberSlash division timings, loop-abort fault injection, single-trace power analysis—together with the masking and constant-time engineering discipline that closes it.

## 1 Introduction

Public-key cryptography as deployed today—RSA, Diffie–Hellman, ECDSA, Ed25519—rests on integer factorization and discrete logarithms, problems that Shor's algorithm reduces to *polynomial time* on a fault-tolerant quantum computer [1][2]. The "harvest now, decrypt later" adversary model converts this future threat into a present emergency for data with long confidentiality lifetimes, motivating NIST's 2016 call for post-quantum proposals and, on 13 August 2024, the publication of the first three finalized standards: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) [1][2]. Two of the three are *lattice-based*, descended from the CRYSTALS-Kyber and CRYSTALS-Dilithium submissions.

This article argues that the standardization of module-lattice cryptography comprises **four interlocking disciplines** rather than one mathematical breakthrough:

1. **Hardness design.** The module-LWE and module-SIS problems must be positioned in a parameter regime where the best known algorithms—BKZ reduction driven by lattice sieving—cost at least the target security level, with the BKZ block size $\beta$ acting as the principal security knob [6].
2. **Generic transforms.** An IND-CPA encryption scheme cannot be fielded directly: ciphertext malleability is fatal to confidentiality under active attack. The Fujisaki–Okamoto transform [3] converts the base PKE into an IND-CCA2 KEM, and its FIPS 203 instantiation departs from textbook FO in subtle, proof-relevant ways.
3. **Zero-knowledge engineering.** A lattice signature $z = y + cs_1$ must not leak the secret through $z$'s distribution. *Fiat–Shamir with aborts* [5] enforces, via rejection sampling, that accepted signatures are statistically independent of the secret.
4. **Implementation security.** Even nominally constant-time code can leak through secret-dependent division timings, power consumption, or faults in the rejection loop. The KyberSlash episode [7] demonstrates that standardization does not retire side-channel analysis; it relocates it from algorithm to deployment.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lattices, SVP, and LWE

A *lattice* $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})$ is the integer span of a full-rank basis $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$. The **shortest vector problem (SVP)** asks, given $\mathbf{B}$, to find a nonzero $v \in \mathcal{L}$ minimizing $\|v\|$. The $\gamma$-approximate variant asks for $\|v\| \le \gamma \cdot \lambda_1(\mathcal{L})$. Approximate SVP with $\gamma = \mathrm{poly}(n)$ is believed hard even for quantum algorithms and is *worst-case*: Ajtai's reduction shows that breaking random LWE instances is at least as hard as solving worst-case lattice problems [4].

> **Definition (LWE):** Fix $n, q$, and an error distribution $\chi$ over $\mathbb{Z}$. The *search* LWE problem asks to recover $s \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n$ given $m$ samples $(a_i, b_i = \langle a_i, s\rangle + e_i)$ with $a_i \gets \mathbb{Z}_q^n$, $e_i \gets \chi$. The *decision* variant asks to distinguish such samples from uniform $(a_i, b_i) \gets \mathbb{Z}_q^n \times \mathbb{Z}_q$.

The **module** generalization replaces $\mathbb{Z}_q$ by the polynomial ring $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^{256}+1)$: the secret is $s \in R_q^k$ and each equation is $b_i = \langle a_i, s\rangle + e_i \in R_q$. Module-LWE interpolates between ring-LWE ($k=1$) and plain LWE ($R = \mathbb{Z}_q$), trading assumption strength against bandwidth.

### 2.2 The Short Integer Solution problem

ML-KEM needs only MLWE; ML-DSA additionally needs **module-SIS**: given random $\mathbf{A} \in R_q^{k \times \ell}$, find a nonzero short $z \in R_q^{\ell}$ with $\mathbf{A}z = 0 \pmod q$ and $\|z\|_\infty \le B$. Unforgeability of the signature scheme reduces to the *SelfTargetMSIS* variant, in which the target vector $\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{A}s_1 + s_2$ is fixed as part of the public key [2][9].

### 2.3 The number-theoretic transform

Both standards perform polynomial multiplication in the *NTT domain*. Because $q = 3329$ (ML-KEM) and $q = 8380417$ (ML-DSA) admit primitive $256$-th roots of unity, $x^{256}+1$ splits completely, and multiplication becomes pointwise after a 256-point NTT: $\mathrm{NTT}(fg) = \mathrm{NTT}(f) \circ \mathrm{NTT}(g)$ [1][2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 From Kyber.CPAPKE to ML-KEM: the FO pipeline

FIPS 203 does not standardize a KEM directly. It standardizes **K-PKE**, an IND-CPA public-key encryption scheme, and defines ML-KEM via a Fujisaki–Okamoto-style transform [1][3][4]:

```python
# Kyber.CPAPKE — the IND-CPA base scheme (schematic)
def keygen():
    rho, sigma = random(32), random(32)
    A = expand_A(rho)                      # k×k matrix over R_q
    s, e = small_vector(k), small_vector(k)  # coefficients in B_η
    t = A @ s + e
    return (pk := (rho, encode(t)), sk := encode(s))

def encrypt(pk, m, coins):
    rho, t = decode(pk)
    A = expand_A(rho)
    r, e1 = small_vector(k), small_vector(k)
    e2 = small_scalar()
    u = compress(A.T @ r + e1, d_u)
    v = compress(t.T @ r + e2 + decompress(m, 1), d_v)
    return (u, v)

def decrypt(sk, (u, v)):
    s = decode(sk)
    w = decompress(v, d_v) - s.T @ decompress(u, d_u)
    return compress(w, 1)
```

The FO layer derives encryption coins *deterministically* from the message and then checks decapsulation validity by **re-encrypting**:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE ML_KEM_FO ------------------------------
(* TLA+ sketch of the FIPS 203 implicit-rejection decapsulation. *)
EXTENDS Integers
VARIABLES m_prime, Kbar_prime, r_prime, c_prime, K
Decaps(dkPKE, h, z, c) ==
    /\ m_prime   = KPKE_Decrypt(dkPKE, c)          \* recover candidate message
    /\ <<Kbar_prime, r_prime>> = G(m_prime || h)  \* derandomize coins
    /\ c_prime   = KPKE_Encrypt(ekPKE, m_prime, r_prime)
    /\ IF c_prime # c
          THEN K = KDF(z || H(c))                 \* implicit rejection
          ELSE K = KDF(Kbar_prime || H(c))         \* honest path
================================================================================
```

Three properties of the FIPS 203 FO instantiation deserve emphasis [1][3]:

- **Implicit rejection.** Instead of returning an error symbol on failed re-encryption, decapsulation outputs $\mathrm{KDF}(z \,\|\, H(c))$ for a secret 32-byte $z$ held in the decapsulation key. No explicit rejection oracle is exposed to the adversary.
- **Derandomization.** Encryption randomness is a deterministic function of the message, so re-encryption reproduces the honest ciphertext exactly and the validity check is well-defined.
- **Decryption-failure handling.** The IND-CCA2 reduction degrades with the failure probability $\delta$ of K-PKE; FIPS 203's parameter sets hold $\delta$ between $2^{-174.8}$ and $2^{-138.8}$, rendering the additive $O(q_{RO} \cdot \delta)$ term negligible [3].

The Hofheinz–Hövelmanns–Kiltz modular analysis [4] establishes that FO$^\perp$ (the implicit-rejection variant) lifts IND-CPA PKE to IND-CCA2 KEM *tightly* in the random-oracle model, and Duman et al. [10] extend the analysis to multi-user security in the quantum ROM—precisely the deployment model of TLS key exchange, where billions of ciphertexts target distinct public keys.

> **Theorem (IND-CCA2 of ML-KEM, informal [1][10]):** Modeling the hash functions as (quantum) random oracles, for any IND-CCA2 adversary $\mathcal{A}$ against ML-KEM making $q$ decapsulation queries there exist MLWE adversaries $\mathcal{B}, \mathcal{C}$ of comparable running time with
> $$\mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{CCA2}}_{\mathrm{ML\text{-}KEM}}(\mathcal{A}) \le 2\,\mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{MLWE}}(\mathcal{B}) + \mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{PRF}}(\mathcal{C}) + O(q\,\delta),$$
> where $\delta$ is the K-PKE decryption-failure rate. The bound is tight up to the small additive terms.

### 3.3 ML-DSA: Fiat–Shamir with aborts and rejection sampling

ML-DSA is a **Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts** signature [2][5]. The signer samples a masking vector $y$ with coefficients uniform in $[-\gamma_1+1, \gamma_1-1]$, computes the commitment $w_1 = \mathrm{HighBits}_q(\mathbf{A}y, 2\gamma_2)$, derives a sparse challenge $c$ with exactly $\tau$ coefficients in $\{-1, +1\}$ via $\mathrm{SampleInBall}$, and responds $z = y + cs_1$. The decisive step is *rejection*:

```rust
// ML-DSA.Sign rejection loop (schematic, FIPS 204 Alg. 2)
loop {
    y = expand_mask(rho_prime, kappa);          // ||y||_inf < gamma1
    w = A * y;
    w1 = high_bits(w, 2*gamma2);
    c_tilde = H(mu || w1_encode(w1));
    c = sample_in_ball(c_tilde);                // tau entries in {-1,1}
    z = y + c * s1;
    r0 = low_bits(w - c*s2, 2*gamma2);
    if z.inf_norm() >= gamma1 - beta { continue; }     // hide s1
    if r0.inf_norm() >= gamma2 - beta { continue; }    // hide s2
    if (c*t0).inf_norm() >= gamma2 { continue; }
    h = make_hint(-c*t0, w - c*s2 + c*t0, 2*gamma2);
    if h.hamming() > omega { continue; }
    return (c_tilde, encode(z), encode(h));      // accepted: ~4-6 trials
}
```

The bound $\|z\|_\infty < \gamma_1 - \beta$ with $\beta = \tau\eta$ ensures that, conditional on acceptance, the distribution of $z$ is *independent of the secret*: shifting a uniform interval by at most $\beta$ and truncating the $\beta$-tails on each side yields a distribution statistically close to uniform, with rejected tails symmetric in the secret. This is the *rejection-sampling lemma* at the heart of [5]: signatures leak nothing beyond the public key, which is why the scheme needs no trapdoor at all.

| Parameter set | Category | $(k,\ell)$ | $\eta$ | $\tau$ | $\gamma_1$ | $\gamma_2$ | $\beta$ | $\omega$ | sig (B) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA-44 | 2 | (4,4) | 2 | 39 | $2^{17}$ | $(q-1)/88$ | 78 | 80 | 2,420 |
| ML-DSA-65 | 3 | (6,5) | 4 | 49 | $2^{19}$ | $(q-1)/32$ | 196 | 55 | 3,309 |
| ML-DSA-87 | 5 | (8,7) | 2 | 60 | $2^{19}$ | $(q-1)/32$ | 120 | 75 | 4,627 |

Public keys are $1{,}312/1{,}952/2{,}592$ bytes; expanded secret keys $2{,}560/4{,}032/4{,}896$ bytes; $q = 8{,}380{,}417$ [2]. The **hint** $h$ lets the verifier reconstruct $w_1$ from the compressed public key $t_1$; the Hamming-weight cap $\omega$ stops the hint smuggling secret information into the signature [2].

### 3.4 Methodology of cryptanalysis

Security claims are quantified with the **lattice estimator** methodology [6]: the primal attack embeds the MLWE instance in a $d_0$-dimensional unique-SVP lattice; BKZ with block size $\beta$ reduces it; the *core-SVP* cost $2^{0.2925\beta}$ (classical sieving) or $2^{0.265\beta}$ (quantum sieving) prices one block-SVP call; and the number of such calls is estimated from the block-size requirement. Jaques [6] refines the model by pricing physical memory movement on a $3$-dimensional machine, recovering the RAM-model sieve exponent $2^{0.2925d}$ asymptotically while adding $7$–$23$ concrete bits to Kyber estimates and $8$–$22$ bits to Dilithium estimates.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The economics of module-LWE hardness

Every lattice attack is ultimately a **primal** or **dual** attack on the embedded unique-SVP instance. The primal attack constructs a lattice of dimension $d_0 = m_0 + n + 1$ containing the short $(e, s, 1)$; BKZ-$\beta$ succeeds when the root-Hermite factor closes the unique-SVP gap, while the dual attack builds a short dual vector to distinguish LWE from uniform. Both are priced by core-SVP at the minimal feasible $\beta$:

| Parameter set | Lattice dim. $d_0$ | BKZ block size $\beta$ | Core-SVP (classical) | Core-SVP (quantum) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-KEM-512 | 1,025 | 406 | 119 bits | 104 bits |
| ML-KEM-768 | 1,483 | 623 | 182 bits | 159 bits |
| ML-KEM-1024 | 1,950 | 873 | 255 bits | 223 bits |

(These are simplified estimator figures; full analyses add simulator-based GSA prediction and dual/hybrid-dual terms [6].) Two observations matter. *First*, the module structure is not the attack surface: the best attacks treat the ring as a dimension amplifier rather than an exploitable symmetry. *Second*, quantum speedups are modest—sieving improves $2^{0.2925\beta} \to 2^{0.265\beta}$, but there is no exponential quantum break of approximate SVP. This asymmetry—Shor devastates RSA while barely denting lattices—is the entire reason the standards exist.

```haskell
-- Pricing a primal attack (estimator core loop, schematic)
estimateMLWE :: Int -> Int -> (Int, Double)
estimateMLWE n logq =
    let candidates = [ (beta, d0, cost)
                     | beta <- [50,60..1200]
                     , d0 <- [n+1 .. 2*n]
                     , success beta d0
                     , let cost = 0.2925 * beta + log2 (8 * d0) ]
    in minimumBy (compare `on` thd3) candidates
```

The gap between NIST's *category* claims (AES-128/192/256 work equivalents) and raw core-SVP bits is deliberate conservatism: categories price *all* known attack avenues and implementation slack, not just sieving [1].

### 4.2 Anatomy of the FIPS 203 transform: why the re-encryption check is load-bearing

The FO re-encryption check is the mechanism that denies the adversary a **decryption oracle**: the reduction simulates decapsulation by scanning the random-oracle log for a message whose derived coins reproduce the queried ciphertext—which is exactly why derandomization is mandatory. FIPS 203 removed the randomness-hedging operations of round-3 Kyber for the same reason: randomness is no longer an input [1].

Round-3 Kyber hashed the ciphertext into the shared-secret derivation; FIPS 203's finalization changed the FO variant and its domain separation in response to NIST review comments on the security proof [3]. The KEM is not the PKE plus a hash: the transform's exact wiring is a security-relevant parameter, and substituting a "simpler" FO variant can invalidate the QROM proof [10].

### 4.3 Rejection sampling as zero-knowledge engineering

Fiat–Shamir with aborts [5] deserves to be understood as a *constructive zero-knowledge* technique rather than a mere efficiency trick. In a Schnorr-like lattice signature, the response $z = y + cs_1$ is a sum of a wide masker $y$ and a narrow secret-dependent term $cs_1$. Without rejection, the distribution of $z$ conditioned on $(c, \mathrm{pk})$ depends on $s_1$—a classic *statistical leak* exploitable by transcript accumulation. Rejection sampling carves out the dependence: accept $z$ only when $\|z\|_\infty < \gamma_1 - \beta$, where $\beta$ bounds $\|cs_1\|_\infty$.

> **Lemma (Rejection sampling, [5]):** Let $y$ be uniform over $[-\gamma_1+1, \gamma_1-1]^{256\ell}$ and $v = cs_1$ with $\|v\|_\infty \le \beta$. Then the distribution of $y + v$ conditioned on $\|y+v\|_\infty < \gamma_1 - \beta$ is within statistical distance $O(\beta/\gamma_1)$ of the distribution of a uniform vector conditioned on the same bound—*independent of $v$*, hence of the secret.

The price is repetition: acceptance per trial is roughly $(1 - \beta/\gamma_1)^{256\ell}$ times the low-bits acceptance, yielding $\approx 4$–$6$ expected trials. Dilithium deliberately chose the *uniform* target distribution over a Gaussian one: Gaussian sampling would shrink signatures but requires high-precision transcendental sampling, enlarging the side-channel surface [9]. *Simplicity is a security feature*—a theme that recurs in the hint mechanism, where the Hamming bound $\omega$ caps the verifier-assistance information.

The security reduction chains these pieces: strong unforgeability under chosen-message attack (SUF-CMA) reduces to the hardness of SelfTargetMSIS and decisional MLWE [2][5], and Kiltz–Lyubashevsky–Schaffner later modularized the analysis into a sound lifting from aborting identification schemes to signatures [par.nsf.gov].

### 4.4 The implementation-attack surface: when mathematics meets silicon

Mathematical security is a *necessary* but not *sufficient* condition for deployed security. Three attack families illustrate the gap.

**Timing: KyberSlash.** Bernstein et al. [7] discovered secret-dependent *integer division* in the Kyber reference implementation—in `poly_tomsg` (KyberSlash1, decapsulation) and `poly_compress` (KyberSlash2, encryption). On ARM cores, division latency depends on operand magnitudes, so decoding timings clustered by secret bits, enabling full key recovery in *minutes* (KyberSlash2) to *hours* (KyberSlash1) on a Raspberry Pi 2 and Cortex-M4 [7]. Responsibly disclosed, the flaws were patched across the reference code, liboqs, PQClean, and mlkem-native; the paper additionally proposed Valgrind-based detection of variable-time instructions operating on secret data, applied to over 1,000 SUPERCOP implementations [7].

**Faults: loop-abort attacks.** Espitau, Fouque, Gérard, and Tibouchi [8] showed that faulting the Fiat–Shamir rejection loop—aborting before a rejection check or zeroing the per-signature nonce—collapses the $y + cs_1$ blinding, so a handful of faulty signatures suffice for full key recovery. Deterministic signing (ML-DSA derives its mask from $K \| \kappa \| \mu$) reduces nonce-reuse faults but concentrates risk on the seed-expansion path, making fault protection of SHAKE expansion non-negotiable [8].

**Power/EM: single-trace analysis.** Simple power analysis on the NTT and polynomial sampling has recovered secrets from unmasked implementations in single traces, and timing fixes can shift leakage into the power domain on some targets. The standard countermeasure stack is effective but expensive:

1. **Constant-time coding** — no secret-dependent branches, memory accesses, or variable-latency instructions; post-KyberSlash, division is replaced by Barrett/Montgomery reduction [7];
2. **Masking** — splitting each secret into $d+1$ shares so any $d$-th-order moment is secret-independent (TVLA-clean masked hardware exists [csrc.nist.gov]);
3. **Shuffling and hiding** — randomizing operation order to raise the trace count for DPA;
4. **Redundancy** — recomputation or infective countermeasures against fault injection.

Dilithium's rejection loop adds a wrinkle: its runtime is variable by design, and naive masking of the loop leaks the iteration count. Modern masked ML-DSA designs mask the polynomial arithmetic while keeping the rejection predicate public—a compromise whose proofs are still being consolidated.

The net thesis of this section: **FIPS 203/204 standardize algorithms, not implementations.** The FO re-encryption check that secures the KEM mathematically doubles the side-channel-relevant code; the rejection sampling that secures the signature statistically introduces a fault-sensitive loop. Each proof-relevant construction choice has a physical dual [7][8].

## 5 Empirical Results / Formal Proofs

**Result 1 — Hardness calibration.** Estimator-derived core-SVP figures (119/182/255 classical bits for ML-KEM-512/768/1024; 104/159/223 quantum) exceed the claimed NIST categories by comfortable margins, and Jaques's physical-cost model [6] adds a further $7$–$23$ bits. No classical or quantum algorithm beats sieving-driven BKZ asymptotically.

**Result 2 — Tight FO reduction.** Under the (Q)ROM, ML-KEM's IND-CCA2 advantage is bounded by twice the MLWE advantage plus PRF and failure terms [1][10], with multi-user tightness from Duman et al.'s FO$^\perp$ analysis. The re-encryption check is the simulation-enabling mechanism of the entire proof.

**Result 3 — Zero-knowledge signatures.** The rejection-sampling lemma [5] gives statistical independence of accepted $z$ from $s_1$ up to distance $O(\beta/\gamma_1)$; SUF-CMA security of ML-DSA follows from SelfTargetMSIS + MLWE [2]. Expected signing cost is $4$–$6$ trials with deterministic randomness.

**Result 4 — Physical exploitability.** KyberSlash recovered ML-KEM keys in minutes-to-hours on commodity ARM targets from pure timing [7]; loop-abort faults recover lattice signing keys from a few faulty signatures [8]. Both are patched, but the *classes*—variable-time arithmetic, faultable rejection loops—persist as review obligations for every new implementation.

**Result 5 — Countermeasure validation.** Masked hardware implementations of Kyber pass TVLA leakage assessment at all sample points [csrc.nist.gov], at roughly $2$–$3\times$ area cost—quantifying the price of the physical-security regime the standards have entered.

## 6 Limitations

1. **The random-oracle and QROM gap.** All FO and Fiat–Shamir security arguments model hash functions as (quantum) random oracles [3][4][10]. Instantiating with SHAKE does not provably inherit these bounds; standard-model lattice KEMs and signatures exist but are far less efficient. The QROM bounds for the *explicit*-rejection FO variant remain substantially weaker, which is one reason FIPS 203 chose implicit rejection [3].
2. **Module structure as assumption risk.** Module-LWE is younger than plain LWE; no attack exploits the module structure, but the *assumption* is strictly stronger than worst-case approximate SVP. SIKE's 2022 classical break is the cautionary precedent for "extra structure" schemes.
3. **Decryption-failure and signing-failure semantics.** ML-KEM's $\delta \le 2^{-138.8}$ is negligible but nonzero; failure-boosting attacks in multi-target settings (Hövelmanns–Hülsing–Majenz failure-finding games) require the failure rate to be this low *per design*, not per hope [3]. ML-DSA's bounded-loop allowance (FIPS 204 permits implementations to cap iterations and return failure) introduces a new, rarely analyzed failure oracle [2].
4. **Side-channel proofs are per-implementation.** The masking order proved for one gadget composes only under verified composition rules; glitches, transitions, and coupling break textbook masking models. TVLA is a *detection* methodology, not a proof [csrc.nist.gov].
5. **Migration externalities.** Signature sizes ($2{,}420$–$4{,}627$ bytes) and public keys break size assumptions baked into X.509, DNSSEC, and embedded protocols; the cryptography is ready before the ecosystem is.

## 7 Conclusion

FIPS 203 and FIPS 204 mature lattice cryptography from a theoretical promise—worst-case-to-average-case reductions—into deployable, *attack-tested* infrastructure. The intellectual spine is a chain of four reductions, each addressing a distinct adversary: module-LWE/MSIS hardness against the cryptanalyst; the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform against the chosen-ciphertext adversary; Fiat–Shamir with aborts against the transcript-accumulating statistical adversary; and masking plus constant-time engineering against the physical adversary with a probe station. KyberSlash proves the final link is load-bearing: a standardized, proof-carrying KEM fell to *integer division*. The frontier is no longer "are lattices hard?" but "is this deployment of lattices hard?"—a question answered per implementation, per target, per masking order, forever.
