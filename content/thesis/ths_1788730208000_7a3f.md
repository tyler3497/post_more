# Scroll zkEVM: Bytecode-Level EVM Equivalence, Multi-Round zkEVM Circuits, KZG-Based Proof Aggregation, and Decentralized Prover Networks

## Abstract

Scroll is a bytecode-level equivalent zkEVM rollup that executes Ethereum bytecode natively and proves the execution with zero-knowledge circuits built on the halo2 proving system, Plonkish arithmetization, and KZG polynomial commitments. This thesis reconstructs the full architecture: the rollup pipeline (sequencer, coordinator, Rollers prover network, relayer), the decomposition of EVM semantics into subcircuits, the multi-round recursion strategy that aggregates chunk proofs into a single batch proof verified on Ethereum L1, and the economics of the decentralized prover network. We formalize the soundness argument for bytecode-level equivalence, analyze proving costs and the overhead of equivalence versus ZK-friendliness, and evaluate the Trail of Bits security assessment findings. We conclude that Scroll's design maximizes compatibility with existing Ethereum tooling at the price of proving overhead, and we identify open problems in prover decentralization, real-time proving latency, and post-quantum migration.

## 1 Introduction

The scalability trilemma of Ethereum — *decentralization*, *security*, and *scalability* — has motivated a decade of layer-2 designs. Among them, **zero-knowledge rollups (zk-rollups)** occupy a privileged position: they post compressed transaction data to layer 1 while submitting a succinct validity proof, inheriting L1 security without the week-long challenge windows of optimistic rollups.

The central technical artifact of a zk-rollup is the **zkEVM**: a circuit system that proves correct execution of the Ethereum Virtual Machine. Not all zkEVMs are created equal. Vitalik Buterin's taxonomy distinguishes five types, from Type 1 (fully Ethereum-equivalent, proving the unmodified stack) to Type 4 (high-level-language equivalent, recompiling Solidity into a ZK-friendly VM) [2]. The spectrum encodes a fundamental trade-off: **the more equivalent a zkEVM is to Ethereum, the more expensive it is to prove** [4].

**Scroll** chose the hard road deliberately. Rather than compiling to a custom instruction set (as zkSync Era does) or translating opcodes into a new assembly (as Polygon's approach), Scroll proves **EVM bytecode directly**: the same bytes that `geth` executes are the bytes the prover consumes [1][3]. The project's founders argued that bytecode-level equivalence is the *raison d'être*: it guarantees that "if you've got a relatively battle-tested protocol that's been running for a few years on mainnet without a hitch, you can be sure it will do the same" on the rollup, with no extra audits or compilers [6].

This thesis dissects how that promise is engineered:

1. **Rollup pipeline** — how L2 blocks become L1-finalized state through sequencer, rollup node, coordinator, provers, and relayer [1][7].
2. **zkEVM circuits** — how the execution trace is decomposed into EVM, state, keccak, bytecode, and MPT subcircuits in halo2 [1][5].
3. **Multi-round recursion** — how chunk proofs are aggregated into a single batch proof [1].
4. **Proving cryptography** — Plonkish arithmetization, the Plonk IOP, and KZG commitments [3].
5. **Decentralized proving** — the Rollers network and the Scroll Proving SDK [8].
6. **Security** — what the Trail of Bits audit found [4].

> **Theorem (Bytecode-Level Equivalence):** Let $f(S, T) = S'$ be the state transition function of the Ethereum Yellow Paper. Scroll's zkEVM accepts an execution trace $\tau$ and produces a proof $\pi$ such that $\pi$ verifies on L1 **iff** every opcode in $\tau$ was executed exactly per the Yellow Paper and $\tau$ begins at $S$ and ends at $S'$. No recompilation or semantic reinterpretation of $T$ occurs between execution and proving [1].

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The zkEVM taxonomy

Buterin's five-type taxonomy remains the conceptual anchor of the field [2]. Type 1 systems (Taiko, the PSE effort) prove the Ethereum stack itself, including its Keccak-heavy Merkle Patricia Trie — maximally equivalent, maximally expensive. Type 2 systems (Scroll, Linea) are **fully EVM-equivalent but not Ethereum-equivalent**: they preserve the EVM's semantics exactly while permitting changes beneath the EVM (e.g., different state-tree hashing) to reduce proving cost. Type 2.5 adjusts gas schedules; Type 3 (early Polygon zkEVM, Scroll's first milestone) accepts small application-layer deviations; Type 4 (zkSync Era, StarkNet) abandons bytecode equivalence entirely for proving speed [2].

Scroll sits firmly in **Type 2**: bytecode in, identical semantics out, with proving-friendly engineering only where the EVM abstraction boundary allows [3].

### 2.2 Why bytecode-level proving is hard

The EVM is *adversarial* to zero-knowledge proving. Three properties dominate the cost model:

| EVM property | Why it hurts proving | Scroll's response |
|---|---|---|
| 256-bit word arithmetic | Native field is ~254 bits; every ADD/MUL needs range checks | Custom halo2 gadgets with lookup-optimized arithmetic |
| Keccak-256 everywhere | Keccak is bitwise-heavy, ZK-hostile (thousands of constraints per hash) | Dedicated keccak circuit with optimized bit decomposition |
| Merkle Patricia Trie | Hex-prefix encoding, RLP, Keccak — enormous per-access cost | MPT circuit; Poseidon considered for future state trees |
| Dynamic gas & memory | Data-dependent control flow breaks static circuit shapes | Execution trace as witness; dynamic behavior in trace, static constraints in circuit |

The standard solution, pioneered by the PSE `zkevm-circuits` codebase that Scroll forked and extended, is the **execution trace as witness** paradigm: run the transaction in a real (instrumented) EVM, record every step, then prove the trace's correctness with fixed-shape circuits [1][5].

### 2.3 Related systems

- **PSE zkevm-circuits**: the halo2 circuit library Scroll's circuits descend from [5].
- **Polygon zkEVM**: STARK-based prover with a custom ROM; trades bytecode fidelity for prover efficiency [2].
- **zkSync Era**: LLVM-based compilation of Solidity/Yul to a custom VM; Type 4 [2].
- **Taiko**: Type 1, proving Reth/g eth execution directly — the maximal-equivalence endpoint [2].
- **Linea**: Type 2 competitor using the Corset constraint system and Vortex/Arcane provers.

### 3.1 Analytical framework

We treat the rollup as a composition of four functions:

```python
# Conceptual model of the Scroll rollup pipeline
def rollup_pipeline(l1_txs, l2_txs):
    blocks   = sequencer.execute(l1_txs, l2_txs)      # Phase 1: execution
    batches  = rollup_node.batch(blocks)             # Phase 2: commitment
    proofs   = coordinator.prove(batches)            # Phase 3a: proving
    receipt  = relayer.finalize(proofs)              # Phase 3b: L1 verification
    return receipt  # new state root finalized on L1
```

Each function is analyzed for (a) its trust assumptions, (b) its failure modes, and (c) its asymptotic cost.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The rollup pipeline: sequencer, coordinator, relayer

The whitepaper decomposes operation into three phases [1]:

**Phase 1 — Transaction execution.** Users submit transactions to the L1 bridge contract or the L2 sequencer. The sequencer's sync service pulls L1-originated messages and merges them with the L2 mempool, producing an ordered sequence of L2 blocks. Execution itself is performed by a lightly modified `l2geth`: the state transition function is *exactly* Ethereum's.

**Phase 2 — Batching and data commitment.** The rollup node watches L2 blocks and groups them into **chunks** (the proving unit) and **batches** (the finalization unit). Once batching criteria are met, the rollup relayer submits a *commit transaction* to the L1 rollup contract carrying the batch's calldata — the data-availability guarantee. Only after data is committed can proofs be generated; this ordering is what makes the rollup *trustless* rather than merely *valid*.

**Phase 3 — Proof generation and finalization.** The coordinator polls the database for new chunks and batches:

1. For each new **chunk**, it fetches execution traces from the sequencer and dispatches a *chunk proving task* to a randomly selected zkEVM prover.
2. For each new **batch**, it collects the chunk proofs and dispatches a *batch proving task* to an aggregation prover.
3. When the relayer observes a completed batch proof, it submits a *finalize transaction*; the L1 contract verifies the proof and updates the finalized state root [1][7].

The separation of *chunk* and *batch* proving is the key scalability move: chunk provers work in parallel on independent execution segments, while a single aggregation proof compresses arbitrarily many chunk proofs into one constant-size L1 verification [1].

### 4.2 The zkEVM circuits: from trace to constraints

Proving an EVM execution means proving the statement: *given initial world state $S$ and transaction $T$, the execution yields $S'$ according to the Yellow Paper* [1]. Scroll's circuit system reduces this to three sub-statements:

1. **The execution trace is correct.** Each opcode's effect on stack, memory, storage, and program counter matches the spec — e.g., `ADD` pops two stack words and pushes their sum.
2. **The opcode sequence is correct.** The trace's ordered opcodes are exactly those the transaction triggers, including correct calldata and contract bytecode loading.
3. **The endpoints match.** The trace starts at $S$ and ends at $S'$.

The implementation is a family of halo2 subcircuits [1][5]:

- **EVM circuit**: constrains per-opcode state transitions using the `bus-mapping` crate, which parses `geth` execution traces into structured witness. Each opcode handler (e.g., the `CREATE` handler audited in [4]) encodes stack/memory/storage effects as polynomial constraints.
- **State circuit**: proves consistency of all state accesses (account/storage reads and writes) against the state trie, enforcing read-write ordering and lexicographic access ordering.
- **Keccak circuit**: a dedicated, heavily optimized circuit for Keccak-256, since hashing dominates constraint count.
- **Bytecode circuit**: proves that the bytecode executed in the trace matches the bytecode committed in the state (code hash integrity).
- **MPT circuit**: proves Merkle Patricia Trie updates — the most intricate component, and the subject of the deepest audit findings [4].
- **Transaction and copy circuits**: constrain transaction-level fields (nonce, gas, signatures) and memory/calldata copying.

These subcircuits are linked by **lookups**: e.g., the EVM circuit's storage accesses are looked up in the state circuit's access table, so each sub-proof remains modular while the whole is sound [5].

In Rust, the pattern per opcode looks like this (simplified from the audited codebase) [4]:

```rust
// Simplified opcode handler pattern (cf. error_invalid_creation_code.rs [4])
fn configure(cb: &mut EVMConstraintBuilder<F>) -> Self {
    let opcode = cb.query_cell();
    let value_left = cb.query_word_rlc();
    cb.stack_pop(offset.expr());
    cb.stack_pop(length.expr());
    cb.require_true("is_create is true",
        cb.curr.state.is_create.expr());
    // memory lookups bind trace values to circuit witnesses ...
    cb.memory_lookup(0.expr(), addr, value_left.expr(), ...);
}
```

The audit's central lesson was that *every witness value must be constrained*: an unconstrained `first_byte` witness in the `CREATE` handler could, in principle, let a malicious prover equivocate between error and success states [4]. Soundness lives in the gap between trace and constraint — and the fix is always to bind the witness.

### 4.3 Plonkish arithmetization, the Plonk IOP, and KZG

Scroll's backend is a specific, well-motivated stack, explained by founder Ye Zhang as the composition of three choices [3]:

1. **Plonkish arithmetization** (the frontend): computations are expressed as polynomial constraints over a grid of advice/fixed/instance columns with custom gates and lookup arguments. Plonkish generalizes R1CS — where each constraint is degree-2 — by allowing higher-degree custom gates and copy constraints, which is essential for encoding 256-bit EVM arithmetic without exploding row counts [3].
2. **The Plonk IOP** (the backend protocol): a polynomial interactive oracle proof that reduces the circuit-satisfiability claim to a handful of polynomial identity checks, made non-interactive via Fiat–Shamir [3].
3. **KZG commitments** (the PCS): Kate–Zaverucha–Goldberg commitments bind each column polynomial to a single elliptic-curve point. Opening proofs are constant-size, and — critically — **KZG supports aggregation**: many polynomial openings can be batched into one pairing check [3].

> **Theorem (KZG evaluation binding):** Under the $q$-SDH assumption in the algebraic group model, a KZG commitment $C = [f(\tau)]_1$ binds the committer to $f$; no PPT adversary can produce a valid opening proof for $(C, z, y')$ with $y' \neq f(z)$ except with negligible probability.

Why this stack, and not STARKs or Groth16? KZG's aggregation-friendly, constant-size proofs minimize **L1 verification gas** — the binding economic constraint of any rollup — while Plonkish's flexibility keeps the EVM's irregular semantics expressible [3]. The price is the **trusted setup** (powers of $\tau$), which Scroll inherits from Ethereum's own KZG ceremony lineage.

### 4.4 Multi-round aggregation: chunks to batches to L1

A single Ethereum block's execution trace is far too large for one circuit. Scroll's answer is **recursive aggregation** [1]:

1. **Chunk proofs**: each chunk (a few blocks) gets a full zkEVM proof from the subcircuit system. These are the expensive, parallelizable work units.
2. **Batch proof**: an aggregation circuit verifies $k$ chunk proofs *inside* a circuit (verifying Plonk/KZG verification equations as constraints) and emits one proof attesting "all $k$ chunk proofs are valid and chain together consistently."
3. **L1 verification**: the rollup contract verifies the single batch proof with a constant number of pairings.

Recursion depth is a design parameter: deeper recursion means smaller per-circuit workloads but more layers of verification overhead. Scroll's two-level scheme (chunk → batch) reflects the empirical sweet spot for current prover hardware [1][7].

Solidity-side, finalization reduces to a precompile-assisted pairing check:

```solidity
// Conceptual L1 finalization (rollup contract)
function finalizeBatch(
    bytes calldata batchData,
    bytes32 prevStateRoot,
    bytes32 postStateRoot,
    bytes calldata kzgAggregatedProof
) external {
    // 1. data availability: batchData committed earlier via commitBatch
    // 2. proof verification: constant-size KZG/Plonk check
    require(verifier.verify(kzgAggregatedProof, publicInputs),
        "invalid batch proof");
    finalizedStateRoot = postStateRoot;
    emit BatchFinalized(batchIndex, postStateRoot);
}
```

Because verification cost is *independent of batch size*, throughput scales with prover capacity, not L1 gas — the defining economic property of validity rollups [1].

### 4.5 The decentralized prover network: Rollers

Proving is Scroll's scarcest resource, and centralizing it would reintroduce a trusted operator. Scroll's answer is the **Rollers** network: a permissionless market of provers that consume proving tasks from the coordinator [7].

The architecture has three notable properties [1][7][8]:

- **Random task assignment**: the coordinator dispatches chunk tasks to randomly selected provers, preventing targeted censorship and spreading load.
- **Hardware acceleration**: Rollers are expected to use GPUs and eventually ASICs/FPGAs; proving is embarrassingly parallel across chunks, so the network's throughput scales with aggregate hardware [7].
- **External proving via SDK**: the Scroll Proving SDK is a Rust library letting third parties run proving services against the SDK coordinator *without operating a full node* — the interface through which proving becomes a commodity service [8].

The open economic question is incentive design: provers must be paid enough to cover proving cost plus hardware amortization, but the fee mechanism must not let a cartel censor batches. Scroll's roadmap treats decentralized proving as a staged rollout — centralized coordinator first, then progressive decentralization — a sequencing the community debates but the engineering constraints largely dictate [7][8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The cost of equivalence

The whitepaper's pipeline analysis and founder lectures converge on a quantitative story [1][3]:

| Metric | Scroll (Type 2, bytecode) | Type 4 (compiled VM) |
|---|---|---|
| Developer migration cost | ~zero (existing bytecode, tooling, audits) [6] | Re-audit on new semantics |
| Proving overhead vs. native | High (Keccak/MPT dominate) | Low (ZK-friendly ISA) |
| L1 verification gas | Constant (one aggregated proof) | Constant |
| Trust assumptions | KZG trusted setup + L1 | Varies (often STARK, no setup) |

The equivalence premium is real: Zhang notes that "the more equivalent one is to the EVM, the higher the overhead," because every Keccak and every MPT node must be proven exactly as Ethereum defines them [6]. Scroll's bet is that **compatibility compounds** — the entire Ethereum toolchain, test suite corpus, and auditing industry transfer for free — while proving costs fall with hardware and cryptographic progress [3].

### 5.2 Audit evidence: the Trail of Bits assessment

The most rigorous public evidence about the circuit system is the Trail of Bits "wave 2" security review [4]. Its findings cluster into three classes:

1. **Unconstrained witnesses** (soundness): e.g., the `CREATE` handler's `first_byte` witness not bound to the memory value, theoretically permitting proof equivocation between error and success states [4, §8.1].
2. **Spec–implementation drift** (MPT circuit): constraints stated in the MPT specification (e.g., key constrained to zero in non-trie segments) omitted from the implementation [4, §4.3–4.6].
3. **Defense in depth**: recommendations for malicious-trace test generation — every soundness fix should ship with a trace that would have caught it [4].

The audit's meta-lesson is methodological: in a trace-witness architecture, **soundness = every witness cell is constrained or looked up**. The circuit is only as trustworthy as its most under-constrained handler.

### 5.3 Formal soundness sketch

We sketch the end-to-end argument. Let $\mathcal{C}$ be the conjunction of all subcircuit constraint systems, $\tau$ the execution trace, and $w$ the full witness (trace + auxiliary values).

> **Lemma (Trace completeness):** If $f(S,T) = S'$ per the Yellow Paper, the honest sequencer's trace $\tau$ extends to a witness $w$ satisfying $\mathcal{C}$.

> **Lemma (Trace soundness):** If $w$ satisfies $\mathcal-C$ (with all lookups into state/keccak/MPT tables valid), then the opcode sequence in $w$ executes per the Yellow Paper from $S$ to $S'$. *Proof sketch:* by induction over trace rows; each opcode handler's constraints encode exactly the Yellow Paper transition; the audit findings [4] are precisely the cases where this induction step had a gap.

> **Theorem (Rollup soundness):** If the L1 contract accepts a batch proof, then (under KZG/Plonk soundness and the trusted setup) the finalized $S'$ equals $f(S, T)$ for the committed batch. The data-availability commit guarantees $T$ is public.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Proving latency vs. real-time proving.** Chunk proving remains on the order of minutes; a fully real-time, slot-synchronous zkEVM is still aspirational. Hardware acceleration (GPU/FPGA/ASIC) narrows but does not close the gap [7].
2. **Centralized sequencing and coordination.** The sequencer and coordinator are currently centralized components. MEV, censorship resistance, and based-rollup designs remain active research [7].
3. **Trusted setup and quantum vulnerability.** KZG relies on elliptic-curve pairings and a trusted setup; a cryptographically relevant quantum computer breaks both. Migration paths (STARK-based recursion, lattice commitments) are speculative [3].
4. **State growth and the MPT albatross.** As long as Ethereum uses the Keccak-based MPT, Type 1/2 provers pay its cost. Verkle trees or Poseidon-based state would help provers but require L1 changes outside Scroll's control.
5. **Prover economics.** A permissionless prover market needs robust anti-collusion and liveness incentives; the mechanism design is unsolved at scale [8].
6. **Formal verification gap.** The circuits are audited [4], not formally verified end-to-end against the Yellow Paper. A machine-checked equivalence proof remains the field's grand challenge.

## 7 Conclusion

Scroll's zkEVM is the most literal interpretation of the zk-rollup idea: *take Ethereum's bytecode, execute it exactly, and prove it*. The architecture — sequencer to coordinator to Rollers to aggregation to L1 finalization [1][7] — is a careful decomposition of an enormous proving task into parallelizable, recursively composable units. The cryptographic stack (Plonkish + Plonk IOP + KZG) [3] is chosen to minimize the one cost that cannot be parallelized away: L1 verification gas. The audit record [4] shows both the maturity of the engineering and the unforgiving nature of the soundness requirement.

The deeper thesis is economic and ecosystemic: **equivalence is a moat**. Every hour of Ethereum auditing, every battle-tested contract, every developer tool transfers to Scroll unchanged [6]. Proving overhead is the tax; compatibility is the revenue. Whether that trade stays favorable as ZK hardware matures and as Type 1 systems close the gap will decide the next chapter of the rollup race — but Scroll has defined what the bytecode-equivalent endpoint looks like, and the field now measures itself against it.
