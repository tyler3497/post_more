# Analog In-Memory Computing with Phase-Change Memory Crossbars: Conductance Drift, Noise Compensation, and Analog Matrix–Vector Multiplication for DNN Inference

## Abstract

Analog in-memory computing promises to break the von Neumann memory wall by performing matrix–vector multiplication directly inside non-volatile memory arrays, where Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws evaluate a full synaptic dot product in a single physical step. Phase-change memory (PCM) — whose conductance encodes synaptic weights in the crystallinity of a chalcogenide alloy — is the most mature device for this paradigm, yet its analog states drift over time as the amorphous phase structurally relaxes, while write stochasticity and 1/f noise corrupt programmed weights. This thesis develops the physics, circuits, and algorithms of PCM-based analog inference. We derive the power-law drift model and the crossbar vector–matrix mapping, present iterative write-verify programming for multilevel conductance states, and analyze compensation strategies from global drift scaling to batch-normalization recalibration. Drawing on landmark hardware demonstrations — including ResNet mapping onto 723,444 PCM devices with one-day accuracy retention [3] and equivalent-accuracy analog training [4] — we quantify accuracy, energy, and endurance against digital accelerators and identify the device and system gaps that remain.

## 1 Introduction

Deep neural network inference is dominated by a single operation: the matrix–vector multiplication (MVM). In a conventional digital accelerator, each multiplication requires fetching a weight from SRAM or DRAM, moving it across an interconnect, computing, and writing the result back. For modern convolutional networks with tens of millions of parameters, this data movement consumes the overwhelming majority of system energy — the *memory wall* that has motivated a decade of research into non-von Neumann architectures [1][7].

Analog in-memory computing (AIMC) attacks the problem at its root: instead of moving weights to the compute units, it turns the memory devices themselves into the multipliers. A crossbar array of resistive memory devices stores a weight matrix as conductances; applying input activations as voltages along the wordlines produces, by Ohm's law, a current through each device proportional to the product of input and weight; Kirchhoff's current law then sums these contributions along each bitline. The entire MVM completes in effectively one time step, with energy scaling set by the physics of the array rather than by the memory hierarchy [1][6].

Phase-change memory is the leading device technology for this vision. A PCM cell stores information in the phase configuration of a chalcogenide alloy such as Ge2Sb2Te5: the crystalline phase is conductive, the amorphous phase resistive, and partial crystallization yields a continuum of intermediate conductance states suitable for analog weights [2]. PCM is already a commercial non-volatile memory technology, which gives it a maturity advantage over more exotic memristive devices, and million-device PCM arrays integrated with CMOS have been demonstrated for computational workloads [5].

The catch is that analog PCM is an imperfect analog computer. Conductances *drift* over time as the amorphous phase undergoes structural relaxation; programming is stochastic; read operations are corrupted by 1/f noise; and device-to-device variability spreads every parameter. This thesis is about taming those imperfections. We develop the device physics (§2) and the mapping and programming methodology (§3), examine each layer in depth (§4), report empirical results and complexity proofs (§5), discuss limitations (§6), and conclude (§7).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Phase-Change Memory Device Physics

A PCM cell is a resistor whose resistance is determined by the crystalline fraction of a nanoscale chalcogenide volume. Two operations define its behavior [2]:

- **RESET** (amorphization): a short, high-current pulse melts the active volume, which quenches into the disordered amorphous phase — high resistance, low conductance.
- **SET** (crystallization): a longer, moderate pulse heats the material above its crystallization temperature but below melting, growing the crystalline fraction — low resistance, high conductance.

By varying pulse amplitude and duration, partial-SET operations dial in a continuum of intermediate states, enabling multilevel — effectively analog — storage. The read operation applies a small voltage that does not disturb the phase configuration and senses the resulting current.

Three non-idealities dominate analog use. First, **conductance drift**: the amorphous phase is thermodynamically metastable and undergoes structural relaxation, so resistance increases over time following an empirical power law

> *G(t) = G(t0) · (t / t0)^(−ν)*,

where *ν* is the drift exponent, typically around 0.05 to 0.1 and *state-dependent* — higher for the more amorphous (lower-conductance) states [2][3]. Second, **1/f noise**: low-frequency current fluctuations that are particularly severe in PCM and corrupt readouts. Third, **write stochasticity and variability**: identical programming pulses produce a distribution of conductance outcomes, and device-to-device spreads are substantial [1][2].

### 2.2 The Crossbar as an Analog Accelerator

Arrange *N × M* PCM devices at the intersections of *N* wordlines and *M* bitlines. Encoding the weight matrix *W* as conductances *G* and driving the wordlines with voltages *V* proportional to the input vector *x*, each device contributes *G_ij V_i* and each bitline sums its column:

> *I_j = Σ_i G_ij V_i*, i.e. *I = GᵀV*.

Signed weights use differential pairs (*G⁺ − G⁻*); the bitline currents are digitized by analog-to-digital converters (ADCs). Because the weights never move, the energy per MVM is set by the array's read energy and the peripheral converters rather than by DRAM access — the source of the reported tenfold to thousandfold efficiency gains over CPUs and GPUs on matrix-heavy workloads [6].

### 2.3 Taxonomy of Non-Idealities and Mitigations

Table 1 organizes the principal device and circuit non-idealities and the strategies used to counteract them.

| Non-ideality | Physical origin | Effect on MVM | Standard mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conductance drift | Structural relaxation of amorphous phase [2] | Systematic accuracy decay over time | Global drift compensation; BN recalibration [3] |
| 1/f read noise | Charge trapping in amorphous volume | Random readout fluctuations | Noise-aware training; averaging |
| Write stochasticity | Melt-quench randomness | Programming error | Iterative write-verify |
| Device variability | Process spreads | Weight transfer error | Differential pairs; noise-injection training [3] |
| IR drop / sneak paths | Wire resistance; passive arrays | Position-dependent errors | 1T1R cells; tiled small arrays |
| ADC/DAC overhead | Peripheral conversion | Dominates system energy | Reduced precision; analog pipelines [6] |

*Table 1: Non-idealities in PCM crossbar accelerators and their standard mitigations.*

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Mapping Weights to Conductances

A trained weight matrix *W* is linearly mapped into the programmable conductance range of the devices. Each weight *w* is encoded differentially as *G⁺ − G⁻ = k·w* with a global scale *k*, so that common-mode drift partially cancels. Programming proceeds by **iterative write-verify**: a programming pulse is applied, the conductance is read, and the pulse parameters are adjusted until the read value falls within a tolerance band around the target. This closed loop suppresses write stochasticity at the cost of programming time and energy [3][6].

### 3.2 Drift-Aware Training

The decisive methodological advance for accurate analog inference is to make training *aware* of the hardware it will run on. Joshi et al. demonstrated that injecting additive noise into the synaptic weights during the forward pass of training — with noise magnitude proportional to the combined read and write conductance noise measured from a one-time hardware characterization — immunizes the network against the perturbations it will encounter after transfer [3]. Combined with weight clipping (a single additional hyperparameter, the clip scale *α*) and standard L2 regularization, this procedure finds flat minima whose accuracy survives the analog transfer with no appreciable loss.

### 3.3 Global Drift Compensation

Even a perfectly transferred network degrades as conductances drift. The **global drift compensation (GDC)** technique exploits the fact that, to first order, drift acts as a multiplicative scaling of the array's outputs. A small set of reference columns is programmed to known conductances; periodically reading their summed current yields an estimate *α̂* of the global drift factor, and inference outputs are rescaled by *1/α̂* [3]:

> *α̂ = (Σ_m I_m) / (V_cal · Σ_n Σ_m G_mn(t0))*.

Because *α̂* is measured from the devices themselves, the procedure requires no model of the drift dynamics and no timing information — only periodic calibration reads performed while the array is idle. Where GDC cannot reach — device-to-device *drift variability* and 1/f noise — periodic recalibration of the batch-normalization parameters provides a second line of defense at modest digital cost [3].

The following Python sketch simulates the full pipeline — differential weight mapping, power-law drift, and GDC recovery — on a synthetic layer:

```python
import numpy as np

def apply_drift(G, t, t0=1.0, nu=0.06):
    """Power-law conductance drift of PCM devices."""
    return G * (t / t0) ** (-nu)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
W = rng.normal(0, 0.5, size=(128, 64))          # trained weights
k = 20e-6                                       # conductance scale (S)
Gp = np.clip(W, 0, None) * k                     # differential pair
Gm = np.clip(-W, 0, None) * k

t_day = 86400.0
Gp_d, Gm_d = apply_drift(Gp, t_day), apply_drift(Gm, t_day)

x = rng.normal(0, 1, size=128)
y_ideal = (Gp - Gm).T @ x
y_drifted = (Gp_d - Gm_d).T @ x

# Global drift compensation from reference columns
alpha_hat = Gp_d.sum() / Gp.sum()
y_comp = y_drifted / alpha_hat
print("relative error, drifted: %.3f" % (np.linalg.norm(y_drifted - y_ideal) / np.linalg.norm(y_ideal)))
print("relative error, compensated: %.3f" % (np.linalg.norm(y_comp - y_ideal) / np.linalg.norm(y_ideal)))
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Drift Law and Its Microscopic Origins

Conductance drift is not a circuit artifact but a materials phenomenon. The melt-quenched amorphous phase of GST contains excess free volume and a broad distribution of structural relaxation modes; as these anneal out at room temperature, the bandgap widens and defect states are removed, raising resistance. The empirical power law *G(t) = G(t0)(t/t0)^(−ν)* captures this relaxation over many decades in time, but the exponent *ν* is itself state-dependent: highly amorphous (high-resistance) states drift faster than mostly crystalline ones, because they have further to relax [2][3]. This state dependence defeats naive global compensation for networks whose weights span the full conductance range — the motivation for per-layer batch-norm recalibration and for device engineering such as *projected PCM* cells, in which a parallel conduction path stabilizes the readout and reduces the effective drift coefficient by roughly an order of magnitude [3].

Temperature is the other master variable: elevated temperatures accelerate structural relaxation, so accelerators deployed in hot environments experience faster drift [7].

> **Theorem:** *Exactness of GDC under uniform drift.* If every device in an array drifts with a common exponent *ν* and the reference columns are representative, then rescaling the array outputs by *1/α̂* exactly recovers the drift-free MVM result.

*Proof sketch.* With uniform *ν*, each conductance satisfies *G_mn(t) = G_mn(t0)·(t/t0)^(−ν)*, so every column current scales by the same factor *(t/t0)^(−ν)*. The measured *α̂* equals this factor (reference columns see the same physics), and division restores the original outputs. ∎ The practical gap is precisely the non-uniformity the theorem excludes: state-dependent *ν* and per-device drift variability leave residual errors that grow with time [3].

### 4.2 Analog Matrix–Vector Multiplication: Circuits and Precision

The ideal *I = GᵀV* mapping is perturbed by every element of the readout chain. Digital-to-analog converters quantize inputs (typically 4–8 bits in prototypes); wire resistance causes IR drop that makes the effective voltage position-dependent; ADCs quantize outputs and contribute the dominant share of system energy and area in many designs [6]. The effective precision of the analog MVM is therefore not the 32-bit floating point of the training framework but something closer to 4–6 equivalent bits — sufficient for inference of well-regularized networks, as the experimental record shows, but a hard constraint on which workloads qualify [3][6].

Two architectural choices manage these effects. **1T1R cells** place a select transistor in series with each PCM device, eliminating sneak paths at the cost of cell area; passive **0T1R** arrays are denser but restricted to small tiles. **Tiling** partitions large weight matrices across many modest arrays (commonly 256×256 to 512×512), with digital reduction trees accumulating partial sums — reintroducing some data movement, but orders of magnitude less than a von Neumann fetch of every weight [6].

Alternative compensation schemes continue to emerge: non-linear current-scaling strategies that shape the read current as a function of its own magnitude have been shown to counteract drift-induced distribution shifts without any digital calibration loop [8].

### 4.3 Write-Verify Programming and Multilevel States

Programming analog conductance is an exercise in applied statistics. A single RESET pulse produces a broad resistance distribution; iterative write-verify narrows it by alternating partial-SET pulses with read checks, converging into a tolerance band around the target. The number of iterations trades programming energy and latency against precision, and the tolerance band is typically set from the noise-injection statistics used in training [3].

Differential encoding doubles the device count but buys two advantages: it represents signed weights without a reference subtraction, and common-mode disturbances — including the uniform component of drift and temperature-induced shifts — cancel in the subtraction *G⁺ − G⁻*. Joshi et al. programmed each of 361,722 ResNet-32 weights onto just two PCM devices in this configuration and retained above 93.5% CIFAR-10 accuracy over a full day [3].

### 4.4 From Training to Deployment: The Full Compensation Stack

No single technique suffices; accurate analog inference relies on a *stack* of complementary measures, applied in order:

1. **Noise-injection training**: additive weight noise during forward passes, calibrated to measured read and write noise, plus weight clipping — finds drift-robust minima [3].
2. **Write-verify programming**: iterative pulsing into tolerance bands — minimizes transfer error.
3. **Global drift compensation**: periodic reference-column reads rescale outputs — cancels the uniform drift component at negligible cost [3].
4. **Batch-norm recalibration**: re-estimating normalization statistics on a calibration set — absorbs residual drift variability and 1/f noise at modest digital cost [3].
5. **Architectural redundancy**: differential pairs, projected cells, and tiled 1T1R arrays — contain the physics at the device and circuit level [2][6].

Each layer addresses a distinct term in the error budget, and omitting any one measurably degrades accuracy retention — GDC, for instance, was found to be *critical* in the Joshi experiments, while BN recalibration recovered roughly a percentage point of accuracy over a day [3].

### 4.5 Benchmarks and the Energy Case

The strongest end-to-end demonstration to date remains the IBM Research prototype reported by Joshi et al.: ResNet-32 trained with the noise-injection methodology, its 361,722 weights programmed onto 723,444 PCM devices, achieving 93.7% CIFAR-10 accuracy immediately after transfer and retaining above 92.6% over 24 hours — 93.5% with BN recalibration — alongside 71.6% top-1 ImageNet accuracy for ResNet-34 in weight-transfer simulation [3]. Complementary results include equivalent-accuracy *training* (not just inference) on analog memory hardware [4] and the mixed-precision architecture that solved a 5,000-equation linear system on nearly a million PCM devices by pairing an analog core with digital iterative refinement [5].

On energy, the review literature reports processor-in-memory systems achieving tenfold improvements in speed and hundredfold to thousandfold improvements in energy efficiency over CPUs and GPUs on matrix-intensive tasks [6]. These headline figures describe the analog core; full-system comparisons must include ADC/DAC and data-movement overheads, which is why standardized, end-to-end benchmarking of AIMC systems remains an open community challenge — one that the rapidly growing literature, tracked in community bibliographies [9], has yet to resolve [7][10].

> **Theorem:** *Crossbar MVM complexity.* Under ideal devices with negligible wire resistance, an *N × M* PCM crossbar evaluates a matrix–vector product in *O(1)* time steps using *O(NM)* devices, versus *O(NM)* time on a sequential processor.

*Proof sketch.* Each of the *NM* devices contributes current *G_ij V_i* simultaneously by Ohm's law; Kirchhoff's law sums each column's *N* contributions in the same physical instant. Digitization adds *O(1)* ADC conversions per column. Latency is therefore independent of matrix dimensions, bounded in practice by wire parasitics and converter bandwidth. ∎

### 5.3 What the Experiments Prove

Three claims now rest on hardware evidence rather than simulation. First, *trainability transfers*: noise-injection training produces networks whose accuracy survives analog deployment, closing the simulation-to-hardware gap that stalled the field for years [3]. Second, *drift is manageable*: the combination of GDC and BN recalibration holds accuracy flat over timescales of a day, far beyond the uncompensated decay [3]. Third, *precision suffices*: 4–6 effective bits of analog precision, properly budgeted, are enough for competitive image-classification accuracy [4][6].

## 6 Limitations

1. **Drift variability and 1/f noise are uncompensated.** GDC cancels only the uniform component of drift; per-device drift variability and 1/f noise cause monotonic accuracy decay and fluctuations that no global scaling can remove [3]. These are materials problems, and projected-cell and novel-alloy research is still pre-product.
2. **Endurance.** Analog inference writes rarely, but write-verify programming and any on-chip adaptation consume endurance cycles; PCM endurance (often cited in the 10^6–10^9 cycle range) constrains training-on-chip ambitions [1][2].
3. **Peripheral dominance.** ADCs, DACs, and digital reduction trees can dominate system energy and area, eroding the analog core's advantage. Full-system efficiency lags the headline device-level figures [6].
4. **Precision ceiling.** Workloads requiring high dynamic range — scientific computing without mixed-precision refinement, or networks with outlier activations — remain out of reach for purely analog paths [5].
5. **Temperature sensitivity.** Drift accelerates with temperature, complicating deployment in thermally uncontrolled edge environments [7].
6. **Toolchain and benchmarking gaps.** No standard exists for end-to-end energy measurement of AIMC systems, and the path from a PyTorch model to a programmed crossbar remains research-grade software [7][10].
7. **Scalability of passive arrays.** Sneak paths and IR drop confine passive 0T1R arrays to small tiles; large models pay tiling and interconnect overhead [6].

## 7 Conclusion

Analog in-memory computing with phase-change memory has matured from a device curiosity into an engineering discipline with hardware-verified results. The power-law drift model [2] quantifies the central imperfection; Ohm's-law MVM [1][6] provides the computational primitive; iterative write-verify programming delivers multilevel precision; and the compensation stack — noise-aware training, global drift scaling, and batch-norm recalibration — has been shown to hold ResNet accuracy on real PCM hardware for a full day [3]. Complementary advances in analog training [4] and mixed-precision architectures [5] extend the paradigm beyond inference-only deployment.

The remaining challenges are concrete: taming drift variability and 1/f noise, reining in peripheral overhead, and building honest end-to-end benchmarks and toolchains [7]. The memory wall is a tax on every digital inference; PCM crossbars offer a credible, experimentally grounded route to stop paying it.
