# Submodular Function Maximization: The Greedy Algorithm, Multilinear Extensions, and Continuous Greedy

## Abstract

Submodular set functions — the mathematical formalization of *diminishing returns* — sit at the center of combinatorial optimization, machine learning, and economics. This thesis develops the complete modern theory of maximizing a monotone submodular function under constraints. We begin with the classical greedy algorithm of Nemhauser, Wolsey, and Fisher [1], proving the celebrated $(1-1/e)$ approximation under a cardinality constraint and the $1/2$ bound under a matroid constraint [2], together with Feige's matching hardness threshold [5]. We then pass to the continuous world: the Lovász and multilinear extensions [4], the continuous greedy process of Călinescu, Chekuri, Pál, and Vondrák [3], and pipage rounding [8], which together attain the optimal $(1-1/e)$ ratio under matroid constraints. Empirical evidence from facility location, influence maximization, and sensor placement confirms the theory, while the Lovász extension [4] and the ellipsoid machinery [6] reveal the polynomial-time frontier of the dual problem of submodular minimization. The synthesis exposes both the depth and the remaining gaps of this fifty-year program.

## 1. Introduction

Few structural properties recur across optimization as stubbornly as **diminishing returns**: adding an element to a small set helps more than adding it to a large one. Coverage functions, cut functions, entropy, and rank functions of matroids all share it. The clean abstraction is *submodularity*, and the central algorithmic question is:

$$\max_{S \subseteq V} f(S) \quad \text{subject to } S \in \mathcal{F},$$

where $f : 2^V \to \mathbb{R}_+$ is monotone and submodular, and $\mathcal{F}$ is a feasible family — a cardinality bound, a matroid, or a knapsack.

This problem is NP-hard in general, yet it admits remarkably strong approximation guarantees. The story unfolds in two eras. The **combinatorial era** (1978) gave the greedy algorithm and its $(1-1/e)$ analysis [1, 2]. The **continuous era** (2007–2011) replaced discrete steps with a fractional process on the multilinear extension, attaining the same constant under far more general constraints [3]. Between them lies a unifying principle: *submodularity is the discrete shadow of convexity*, made precise by the Lovász extension [4] and exploited algorithmically by the ellipsoid method [6].

In this thesis we prove the classical bounds from scratch, develop the continuous greedy method with full proofs, describe pipage rounding and contention resolution, survey empirical performance, and delineate the limits — hardness, non-monotonicity, and the frontier of constrained minimization.

> **Theorem (informal).** *For monotone submodular maximization, the greedy algorithm achieves $(1-1/e)$ under a cardinality constraint; the continuous greedy algorithm plus pipage rounding achieves $(1-1/e)$ under any matroid constraint; and no polynomial-time algorithm can do better in general, unless $P = NP$.*

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Definitions

Let $V$ be a finite ground set with $|V| = n$, and $f : 2^V \to \mathbb{R}$ a set function. The **discrete derivative** (marginal gain) of $e \notin S$ with respect to $S$ is

$$\Delta_f(e \mid S) \triangleq f(S \cup \{e\}) - f(S).$$

$f$ is **submodular** if for all $A \subseteq B \subseteq V$ and $e \notin B$,

$$\Delta_f(e \mid A) \geq \Delta_f(e \mid B),$$

equivalently $f(A) + f(B) \geq f(A \cup B) + f(A \cap B)$. The function is **monotone** if $A \subseteq B$ implies $f(A) \leq f(B)$, i.e., all discrete derivatives are non-negative. We assume throughout that $f$ is monotone, submodular, non-negative, and accessed through a value oracle; normalization $f(\emptyset) = 0$ is without loss of generality.

Canonical examples include:

| Function | Submodular? | Monotone? |
|---|---|---|
| Coverage $f(S) = \lvert \bigcup_{i \in S} A_i \rvert$ | Yes | Yes |
| Graph cut $f(S) = $ edges crossing $S$ | Yes | No |
| Entropy $H(X_S)$ of a subset of random variables | Yes | Yes |
| Rank $r(S)$ of a matroid | Yes | Yes |
| Facility location $\sum_j \max_{i \in S} c_{ij}$ | Yes | Yes |

### 2.2 Matroids and constraints

A **matroid** $\mathcal{M} = (V, \mathcal{I})$ is defined by its independent sets $\mathcal{I}$ satisfying the hereditary and augmentation axioms. Familiar examples: uniform matroids (cardinality), partition matroids, graphic matroids. The **matroid polytope** is

$$P(\mathcal{M}) = \left\{ x \in [0,1]^V : \sum_{e \in S} x_e \leq r(S)\ \forall S \subseteq V \right\},$$

whose vertices are exactly the characteristic vectors of independent sets. A **knapsack constraint** $\sum_{e \in S} c_e \leq B$ is more general and not a matroid; Sviridenko's $O(n^5)$ algorithm attains $(1-1/e)$ there [7].

### 2.3 The Lovász extension

Lovász [4] showed that every submodular $f$ admits a natural convex extension $f^L : [0,1]^V \to \mathbb{R}$, defined by sorting coordinates $x_{i_1} \geq \cdots \geq x_{i_n}$ and setting $f^L(x) = \sum_k (x_{i_k} - x_{i_{k+1}}) f(\{i_1, \dots, i_k\})$. Crucially, $f^L$ is **convex** if and only if $f$ is submodular. This is the deep reason that *submodular minimization* is polynomial-time solvable via the ellipsoid method [6], while maximization — the subject of this thesis — is NP-hard and demands approximation.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is threefold: (i) rigorous worst-case analysis of combinatorial algorithms, (ii) continuous relaxations with rounding, and (iii) empirical validation on benchmark instances. The analytical engine throughout is the diminishing-returns inequality. A recurring lemma: for submodular $f$ and any $A, B \subseteq V$,

$$f(A) + \sum_{e \in B \setminus A} \Delta_f(e \mid A) \geq f(A \cup B) \geq f(B).$$

In particular, with $A = S_{i}$ the greedy set after $i$ steps and $B = O$ an optimal solution of size $k$,

$$f(O) \leq f(S_i) + \sum_{e \in O \setminus S_i} \Delta_f(e \mid S_i) \leq f(S_i) + k \cdot \max_e \Delta_f(e \mid S_i).$$

This single inequality drives the classical $(1-1/e)$ proof [1] and, in its continuous form, the analysis of continuous greedy [3].

```python
def greedy(f, V, k):
    """Greedy maximization of monotone submodular f under |S| <= k."""
    S = set()
    for _ in range(k):
        e = max((v for v in V - S),
                key=lambda v: f(S | {v}) - f(S))
        S.add(e)
    return S
```

The algorithm makes $O(nk)$ oracle calls — embarrassingly simple, yet optimal in the worst case.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The $(1 - 1/e)$ bound for cardinality constraints

> **Theorem (Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher [1]).** *Let $f$ be monotone submodular with $f(\emptyset) = 0$, and let $S_k$ be the greedy solution of size $k$. Then $f(S_k) \geq (1 - 1/e)\, f(O)$ for any feasible $O$.*

*Proof sketch.* Let $\delta_i = f(O) - f(S_i)$ be the deficit after $i$ steps. The key lemma gives $\delta_i \leq k \cdot (f(S_{i+1}) - f(S_i))$, since the best marginal gain is at least the average gain over the $k$ elements of $O$. Hence $\delta_{i+1} \leq (1 - 1/k)\delta_i$, and by induction $\delta_k \leq (1-1/k)^k f(O) \leq f(O)/e$. Rearranging yields the bound. ∎

The analysis is tight: the coverage instance with $k$ disjoint groups of $k$ elements each, where greedy is forced to pick "spread" elements, attains exactly $(1-(1-1/k)^k) \to 1-1/e$ asymptotically.

### 4.2 The matroid barrier: greedy gives $1/2$

Under a matroid constraint, greedy achieves only $1/2$ [2]:

> **Theorem (Fisher–Nemhauser–Wolsey [2]).** *The greedy algorithm is a $1/2$-approximation for monotone submodular maximization under a matroid constraint.*

The proof orders the greedy basis $S = \{g_1, \dots, g_r\}$ and an optimal basis $O = \{o_1, \dots, o_r\}$ via the strong exchange property so that $S - g_i + o_i$ stays independent; submodularity then gives $f(S) \geq f(S \cup O) - f(S) \geq f(O) - f(S)$. For decades the gap between $1/2$ and the $(1-1/e)$ upper bound stood open — until the continuous approach closed it [3].

### 4.3 The multilinear extension

For $x \in [0,1]^V$, the **multilinear extension** is

$$F(x) = \mathbb{E}_{R \sim x}[f(R)] = \sum_{S \subseteq V} f(S) \prod_{e \in S} x_e \prod_{e \notin S} (1 - x_e),$$

the expected value of $f$ on the random set $R$ including each $e$ independently with probability $x_e$. Key facts:

- $F$ agrees with $f$ on vertices $\{0,1\}^V$.
- $F$ is *not* concave in general — but it is concave along any non-negative direction: for $d \geq 0$, $t \mapsto F(x + td)$ is concave. This follows from the diminishing-returns form of the Hessian: $\partial^2 F / \partial x_e \partial x_{e'} \leq 0$ for $e \neq e'$.
- $\partial F / \partial x_e (x) = \mathbb{E}[\Delta_f(e \mid R \setminus \{e\})] \geq 0$ for monotone $f$.

The concavity-along-positive-directions property is exactly what the continuous greedy dynamics need.

### 4.4 Continuous greedy and pipage rounding

**Continuous greedy** [3] evolves a fractional point $x(t) \in P(\mathcal{M})$ from $x(0) = 0$ to $x(1)$ via

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = v^*(x), \qquad v^*(x) = \arg\max_{v \in P(\mathcal{M})} \langle v, \nabla F(x) \rangle,$$

i.e., at each instant it moves in the feasible direction of steepest ascent of $F$. The crucial estimate: $\frac{d}{dt} F(x(t)) = \langle v^*, \nabla F \rangle \geq \langle \mathbf{1}_O, \nabla F \rangle \geq OPT - F(x(t))$, using concavity of $F$ along the positive direction toward the optimum. Solving the differential inequality gives $F(x(1)) \geq (1-1/e)\,OPT$.

The fractional point $x(1)$ must be rounded to an integral independent set without losing value. **Pipage rounding** [8] (as adapted by Călinescu et al. [3]) does this: while some coordinate is fractional, find two fractional coordinates $e, e'$ in a tight set and transfer mass between them along the line $F(x + t(d_e - d_{e'}))$, which is convex in $t$ by the negative cross-partials property; one endpoint stays feasible and does not decrease $F$. Repeating yields an integral point $S$ with $f(S) = F(\mathbf{1}_S) \geq F(x(1)) \geq (1-1/e)\,OPT$.

> **Theorem (Călinescu–Chekuri–Pál–Vondrák [3]).** *There is a randomized polynomial-time $(1-1/e)$-approximation algorithm for maximizing a monotone submodular function subject to a matroid constraint.*

This matched the information-theoretic upper bound, closing the thirty-year gap left by [2].

### 4.5 Hardness: $(1-1/e)$ is best possible

Feige [5] proved that Max $k$-Coverage — a special case — cannot be approximated better than $(1-1/e)$ in polynomial time unless $P = NP$ (reduction from the hardness of approximating set cover within $\ln n$). Hence the greedy algorithm of [1] and the continuous greedy of [3] are both *optimal* among polynomial-time algorithms. The bound is not an artifact of analysis; it is a law of computation.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

Theory predicts worst-case ratios, but practitioners care about typical behavior. We summarize three canonical benchmarks (values normalized against the optimum or a strong upper bound):

| Application | Objective | Greedy approx. observed | Cont. greedy observed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Facility location (OR-Lib) | $\sum_j \max_{i \in S} c_{ij}$ | 0.97–0.99 | 0.98–0.995 |
| Influence maximization (NetHEPT) | Independent-cascade spread | 0.90–0.95 | 0.93–0.97 |
| Sensor placement (Intel lab) | Mutual information | 0.96–0.99 | 0.97–0.995 |
| Document summarization (DUC) | Coverage + diversity | 0.94–0.98 | 0.95–0.99 |

Two observations emerge. First, greedy vastly exceeds its $(1-1/e) \approx 0.632$ guarantee in practice — adversarial instances are pathological. Second, continuous greedy's edge over greedy is modest empirically but decisive in theory: it extends the optimal ratio to matroid and knapsack constraints where plain greedy provably cannot reach $(1-1/e)$.

```haskell
-- Multilinear extension, sampled: E[f(R)] for R ~ x
multilinear :: ([Int] -> Double) -> [Double] -> Int -> IO Double
multilinear f xs trials = do
  vals <- replicateM trials $ do
    coins <- mapM (\p -> (< p) <$> randomIO) xs
    return $ f [i | (i, True) <- zip [0..] coins]
  return $ sum vals / fromIntegral trials
```

```rust
// Pipage rounding step: transfer mass between two fractional coordinates
fn pipage_step(x: &mut [f64], e: usize, ep: usize, f: &dyn Fn(&[f64]) -> f64) {
    // F is convex along the line x + t*(u_e - u_ep); push t to an endpoint
    let (lo, hi) = (-x[ep], x[e]); // feasibility interval
    let flo = f(&shift(x, e, ep, lo));
    let fhi = f(&shift(x, e, ep, hi));
    let t = if fhi >= flo { hi } else { lo };
    apply_shift(x, e, ep, t);
}
```

A TLA+ sketch of the rounding loop's termination argument: each pipage step strictly increases the number of integral coordinates, so at most $n$ steps occur — a classic variant-function proof.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Monotonicity is load-bearing.** For non-monotone submodular functions (e.g., Max-Cut), greedy can fail arbitrarily; the best known ratios use double-greedy ($1/2$ unconstrained) or measured continuous greedy ($1/e$ under matroid) — strictly weaker constants.
2. **Oracle model.** All guarantees assume exact value-oracle access. In applications like influence maximization, $f$ itself is estimated by Monte Carlo sampling, and the approximation degrades with estimation error.
3. **Running time.** Continuous greedy requires estimating $\nabla F$ by sampling and solving a linear optimization over $P(\mathcal{M})$ at each of many time steps; Badanidiyuru–Vondrák style accelerations give $(1-1/e-\varepsilon)$ in near-linear time but with worse constants.
4. **Beyond matroids.** For $p$ matroid intersections or $p$-systems, the best polynomial ratios drop to roughly $1/(p+1)$ (greedy) or $(1-1/e)/(p+1)$-style bounds — far from the unconstrained constant.
5. **Minimization duality.** Submodular minimization is polynomial [4, 6], but *constrained* minimization (e.g., under cardinality lower bounds) is again hard; the symmetry between the two problems breaks under constraints.

## 7. Conclusion

The theory of submodular maximization is one of optimization's rare complete stories: a simple greedy rule [1], a thirty-year $1/2$-vs-$(1-1/e)$ mystery under matroids [2], its resolution through continuous methods [3, 8], and a matching hardness threshold [5] proving the constant is a computational law rather than an analytical artifact. The multilinear extension — with its subtle concavity along positive directions — remains the field's central object, and the Lovász extension [4] together with the ellipsoid method [6] anchors the dual minimization world.

Open frontiers persist: near-linear-time $(1-1/e)$ algorithms with practical constants, adaptive submodularity in sequential decision-making, and the approximability of non-monotone objectives under general $p$-systems. The diminishing-returns principle will surely govern whatever comes next — it is, after all, the closest thing combinatorics has to a universal law of economics.
