# Mechanistic Interpretability: Circuits, Superposition, and the Reverse-Engineering of Transformer Computations

## Abstract

Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer the algorithms implemented by trained neural networks, treating model weights not as inscrutable matrices but as compiled programs awaiting decompilation. This thesis synthesizes the Transformer Circuits research program into a unified account of how modern language models compute. We develop the mathematical framework of the residual stream and the query-key/output-value circuit decomposition, show how attention-head composition gives rise to induction heads as a mechanism for in-context learning, and analyze superposition — the compression of more features than dimensions into near-orthogonal directions — as the central obstacle to and explanation of polysemanticity. We present sparse autoencoders as a dictionary-learning microscope that recovers monosemantic features, review causal-intervention methodology including activation and path patching, and survey empirical milestones from the indirect-object-identification circuit to grokking in modular arithmetic. We close with the field's open limitations: scaling to frontier models, completeness and faithfulness of explanations, and the unresolved question of whether full reverse-engineering is tractable.

## 1 Introduction

Deep neural networks are among the most consequential artifacts ever produced by an optimization process, and among the least understood. A frontier language model contains hundreds of billions of parameters whose joint action yields fluent reasoning, code synthesis, and apparent generalization — yet the *mechanism* by which any particular behavior arises remains, to a first approximation, unknown. This ignorance is not merely an academic embarrassment; it is a practical and safety-critical gap. Capabilities surface years after training that their creators did not anticipate, including deceptive reasoning, prompt-injection vulnerabilities, and emergent tool use. If we cannot attribute behaviors to internal causes, we cannot reliably predict, steer, or constrain them.

Mechanistic interpretability is the research program that refuses to accept this ignorance as permanent. Where behavioral evaluation treats the model as a black box and probing treats it as a statistical oracle, mechanistic interpretability treats it as a *program*: the weights are compiled machine code, and our task is to decompile them into human-comprehensible algorithms [1]. The aspiration is not metaphorical. A transformer is, at bottom, a deterministic differentiable function composed of linear maps, softmax nonlinearities, and elementwise activations. In principle, every computation it performs is written down in its parameters. The difficulty is representational: the natural units of the architecture — neurons, attention heads, layers — do not coincide with the natural units of the computation — features, circuits, algorithms.

This thesis presents a coherent synthesis of the most productive strand of this program: the *Transformer Circuits* line of work pioneered by Anthropic's interpretability team [1][2][3][4]. ## 2 Background

### 2.1 The transformer as a residual architecture

The modern decoder-only transformer processes a sequence of token embeddings through a stack of layers, each containing a multi-head self-attention sublayer and a positionwise multilayer perceptron (MLP) sublayer. Crucially, both sublayers are *residual*: they read from and additively write to a shared high-dimensional vector, the **residual stream**. If x denotes the residual stream state at a given position, each layer transforms it as:

```
x ← x + Attn(x) + MLP(x + Attn(x))
```

where the attention output is itself a sum over heads. This additive structure is the interpretability researcher's best friend: because everything communicates through one shared channel, the model's computation can be decomposed into a sum of independent contributions, each attributable to a specific component [1].

## 3 Methodology

Mechanistic interpretability is an *experimental* science conducted inside the weights of a model. Its methodology blends techniques from causal inference, neuroscience, and program analysis. We survey the principal instruments.

- **Logit attribution and direct effect measurement.** Because the residual stream is additive, one can decompose the final logits into a sum of per-component contributions and read off which heads and MLPs push toward or away from a given token [1]. This is the coarsest instrument: it identifies *where* in the network a behavior lives, but not *how*.
- **Activation patching (causal tracing).** Run the model on a clean prompt and a corrupted prompt, then re-run the corrupted prompt while overwriting a chosen activation with its clean value; the recovery of correct behavior measures that component's causal contribution [8]. Variants include zero ablation, mean ablation, and resample ablation, each with distinct tradeoffs between staying on-distribution and isolating causal roles.
- **Path patching.** A refinement of activation patching that isolates the effect flowing through a *specific path* — e.g., through one head's OV circuit into another head's query — by patching only the downstream sender's contribution while freezing the receiver's other inputs [5]. Path patching is what elevates patching from component localization to genuine circuit discovery.
- **Ablation and causal scrubbing.** Systematically removing components (heads, MLPs, directions) and measuring behavioral degradation tests whether a hypothesized circuit is *necessary*; restoring a hypothesized circuit's function in an ablated model tests *sufficiency*.
- **Automated circuit discovery (ACDC).** Because hand-tracing circuits does not scale, Conmy et al. [6] introduced an algorithm that starts from the full computational graph and greedily prunes edges whose removal least degrades a behavioral metric, yielding a minimal faithful subgraph — an automatically discovered circuit.
- **Dictionary learning with sparse autoencoders.** Train an autoencoder with a sparsity penalty on a model's internal activations to decompose them into a sparse combination of learned dictionary directions; the directions that emerge are far more interpretable than raw neurons [4].

> Theorem: *The Causal Sufficiency Criterion.* A hypothesized circuit C is a sufficient mechanistic explanation of behavior B on input distribution D only if (i) ablating C degrades B to near-chance on D, and (ii) no proper subcircuit of C achieves comparable degradation. Necessity without minimality is localization, not explanation.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Residual Stream as a Communication Channel

The residual stream is best understood not as a hidden state in the recurrent-network sense but as a **shared bus** over which semi-independent modules communicate. Each attention head and MLP reads the full stream, computes a function of it, and writes its result back additively. Because the stream is high-dimensional — typically thousands of coordinates — many features can coexist as nearly orthogonal directions with negligible interference, provided the features that co-occur are few relative to the dimension.

This bandwidth picture has a quantitative consequence. If features are sparse — each input activates only a small subset — then the network can represent far more features than it has dimensions, at the cost of *interference* between the overlapping directions. The residual stream is thus a compressed channel, and the central quantitative question of interpretability is how much compression is actually happening. The answer, developed in the superposition literature [3], is: a great deal. Models appear to represent an order of magnitude or more features than dimensions, which is why reverse-engineering them is hard and why the raw neuron basis is the wrong coordinate system in which to look.

### 4.2 Induction Heads: The First Complete Circuit

An **induction head** is an attention head that implements a simple algorithm: given a sequence in which token A is followed by token B, and A later recurs, the head attends back to the earlier B and boosts its logit — completing the pattern A B … A with B [2]. Mechanistically, this is realized by *two* heads in composition. A **previous-token head** in an earlier layer copies information about each token into the next position's residual stream (K-composition target); the induction head itself then uses its QK circuit to match the current token against keys that carry previous-token information — *prefix matching* — and its OV circuit to copy the attended token forward — *copying* [1][2].

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def induction_score(W_QK, W_OV, W_E, prev_head_out):
    # Score how induction-like a head is, given its circuits.
    # W_QK: query-key circuit (d_model x d_model)
    # W_OV: output-value circuit (d_model x d_model)
    # W_E: token embedding matrix (vocab x d_model)
    # prev_head_out: output of the previous-token head per position
    # Prefix matching: does the head attend to tokens whose
    # predecessor matches the current token?
    match = torch.einsum("vd,de,ev->v", W_E, W_QK, prev_head_out.T)
    # Copying: does the OV circuit boost the attended token's logit?
    copy = torch.einsum("vd,de,ev->v", W_E, W_OV, W_E.T).diag()
    return F.cosine_similarity(match, copy, dim=0)
```

The empirical case for induction heads is unusually strong. In small attention-only models, ablating induction heads destroys in-context learning while leaving other capabilities intact — causal evidence [2]. Across model scales, induction heads appear at precisely the training step where in-context learning ability undergoes a sharp phase change — a "bump" in the loss curve — suggesting they are not merely correlated with but constitutive of the capability [2]. The induction head is therefore the field's proof of concept: a behavior (few-shot pattern completion) reduced to weights (two composed circuits) with causal validation.

| Circuit | Layers | QK behavior | OV behavior | Explains |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Previous-token head | 1 | Attends to position t−1 | Copies token identity forward | Positional memory |
| Induction head | 2 | Prefix matching on composed keys | Copies attended token to output | In-context learning |
| Duplicate-token head | 1 | Attends to identical tokens | Suppresses or flags repeats | Repetition detection |
| Name-mover head | 2+ | Attends to name tokens | Copies name to output position | Indirect object identification [5] |

### 4.3 Superposition and the Geometry of Features

**Superposition** is the hypothesis that neural networks represent more features than they have dimensions by embedding features as *almost-orthogonal directions* in activation space and relying on sparsity — few features active at once — to keep interference manageable [3]. When features are sparse, the interference between overlapping directions acts as tolerable noise that downstream nonlinearities (like ReLU or the attention softmax) can filter out. The network thus achieves compression beyond what any linear model could, at the price of entangling its features in the neuron basis.

Elhage et al. [3] made this precise with toy models: small ReLU autoencoders trained on synthetic data with known sparse features. The results are striking. As feature sparsity increases, the models transition through *phase changes* — sharp reorganizations of the learned geometry — and the feature directions self-organize into highly symmetric configurations: antipodal pairs, tetrahedra, and other uniform polytopes that optimally pack many directions into few dimensions. The geometry is not arbitrary; it is the solution to a sphere-packing problem the optimizer discovers on its own.

The consequences for interpretability are profound:

1. **Polysemanticity is expected, not pathological.** If features outnumber neurons, most neurons *must* respond to multiple unrelated features. A neuron that fires for both baseball statistics and Python code is not a bug; it is the predictable result of optimal packing under a dimension budget [3][4].
2. **The neuron basis is privileged only by the architecture, not by the computation.** Because features are directions, interpretability must operate in a basis-independent way — or find the right basis via dictionary learning.
3. **Interference bounds interpretability.** Any method that reads features from activations must contend with the superposition noise floor; monosemantic features are recoverable only insofar as sparsity makes them separable.

Superposition also reframes scaling: larger models may owe part of their capability gains not to richer algorithms but to *less* superposition — more dimensions per feature, hence cleaner representations and less interference [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The research program's credibility rests on a sequence of results that move from toy models to frontier systems, each tightening the link between hypothesized mechanism and observed behavior.

1. **Induction heads cause in-context learning (small models).** In two-layer attention-only transformers, ablating the identified induction heads eliminates the in-context learning bump while preserving baseline language modeling; the heads' QK and OV circuits provably implement prefix matching and copying [1][2]. This is the gold standard: a complete circuit with causal verification.
2. **The indirect-object-identification (IOI) circuit in GPT-2.** Wang et al. [5] used path patching to reverse-engineer how GPT-2 Small completes sentences like "John and Mary went to the store; John gave a drink to ___" with "Mary". The discovered circuit spans multiple head classes — duplicate-token heads, S-inhibition heads, and name-mover heads — each with a characterized algorithmic role, and the full circuit was validated by ablation to be both necessary and sufficient for the behavior. The IOI circuit remains the most complete end-to-end circuit ever extracted from a real language model.
3. **Grokking as circuit formation.** Power et al. [7] discovered that small transformers trained on modular arithmetic memorize the training set quickly but generalize only after vastly more training — a delayed phase transition dubbed *grokking*. Mechanistic follow-ups showed grokking coincides with the model discovering an efficient algorithmic circuit (based on discrete Fourier features) and discarding its memorization circuitry under weight-decay pressure. Grokking is thus what circuit formation looks like from the outside: a sudden reorganization of internal algorithms invisible to training loss.
4. **Monosemantic features at scale.** SAEs trained on production-scale models recover millions of interpretable features, including features for code vulnerabilities, deceptive reasoning patterns, and multilingual concepts, with causal steering confirming their functional reality [4]. Dictionary learning has moved from proof of concept to infrastructure.

> Theorem: *The Superposition Capacity Bound (informal).* In a d-dimensional activation space with feature sparsity s, the number of features representable with interference below threshold epsilon grows superlinearly in d but is bounded by the sphere-packing number at angular separation determined by s and epsilon. Empirically, trained models saturate a substantial fraction of this bound, confirming that superposition is not incidental but near-optimal compression [3].

## 6 Limitations

The program's successes notwithstanding, formidable limitations remain — some technical, some possibly fundamental.

- **Scale.** The most complete circuits (induction heads, IOI) come from models with at most a few hundred million parameters. Frontier models are three orders of magnitude larger, with hundreds of layers and MLP blocks whose internal structure resists the clean QK/OV decomposition. Whether hand-tractable circuits exist at that scale, or whether automation (ACDC [6], attribution graphs) can bridge the gap, is the field's central open question.
- **Completeness and faithfulness.** There is no agreed-upon metric for whether an interpretation is *complete* — capturing everything the model does — as opposed to merely *plausible*. Patching-based evidence is correlational with respect to the full causal graph: a component can appear causal through one path while the true computation flows through another [8]. Recent work on interaction effects in activation patching shows that standard estimands conflate pure indirect effects with component interactions, complicating causal claims.
- **The MLP problem.** Attention heads yield to circuit analysis because their bilinear structure is mathematically transparent. MLPs — which contain the majority of parameters — are far less understood; they appear to implement key-value memories and feature transformations in superposition, but no comparably complete account exists.
- **Feature granularity and ontology.** SAEs recover *a* dictionary, not necessarily *the* dictionary: different hyperparameters yield different feature decompositions, and there is no ground truth against which to validate completeness [4]. The field lacks a principled answer to what counts as a feature.
- **Tractability in principle.** It is possible that sufficiently capable models implement computations that are simply too complex, too distributed, or too alien for human-comprehensible reverse-engineering — the "compiled binary" may resist decompilation not for lack of tools but because the source was never human-shaped. The program proceeds on the optimistic hypothesis that training discovers compressible, modular algorithms; this remains a hypothesis.

## 7 Conclusion

Mechanistic interpretability has, in half a decade, moved from aspiration to a productive empirical science. The residual-stream framework [1] gave the field a mathematical language; induction heads [2] gave it a first causal triumph; superposition theory [3] explained why the problem is hard; and sparse autoencoders [4] gave it a scalable instrument. Along the way, the methodology matured from visualization to causal intervention — activation patching, path patching, automated discovery [5][6][8] — and the phenomena under study grew from toy circuits to grokking phase transitions [7] and million-feature dictionaries.

The stakes of the program extend beyond scientific curiosity. As models grow more capable and more widely deployed, the gap between what they *do* and what we *understand* becomes a load-bearing risk. Behavioral testing can tell us that a model failed; only mechanistic understanding can tell us *why*, whether the failure mode generalizes, and how to excise it without breaking everything else. The decompiler's work is slow, painstaking, and incomplete — but it is the only path from alchemy to engineering. If the optimistic hypothesis holds — that trained networks are, at bottom, comprehensible programs written in the language of circuits and features — then the coming decade will see the first true reverse-engineering of machine intelligence. This thesis has mapped the foundations on which that effort stands.
