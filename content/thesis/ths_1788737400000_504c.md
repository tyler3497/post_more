# Koopman Operator Theory for Nonlinear Dynamical Systems: Spectral Decompositions, Data-Driven Approximation, and Control

## Abstract

The Koopman operator, introduced by Bernard Koopman in 1931, provides an exactly linear description of nonlinear dynamical systems by shifting focus from the evolution of states to the evolution of observable functions. This infinite-dimensional linear operator encodes every trajectory of a nonlinear system in the space of measurements, and its spectrum — eigenvalues, eigenfunctions, and Koopman modes — yields a global nonlinear analogue of the linear spectral expansion. Revived by the data-driven era, the framework is now computational: Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) and its extensions (Extended DMD, kernel DMD, residual DMD) approximate Koopman spectra from snapshot data alone. This thesis develops the spectral theory from first principles, derives EDMD as a Galerkin projection, analyzes continuous spectra and spectral pollution with rigorous residual-based error control, and connects the theory to control through Koopman model predictive control, which convexifies nonlinear optimal control via lifting. Applications to fluid turbulence, neuroscience, and power grids are surveyed, and fundamental limitations — the curse of dimensionality, nonexistent finite invariant subspaces, and dictionary selection — are confronted directly.

---

## 1. Introduction

Nonlinear dynamical systems resist the universal solution methods that make linear systems tractable. For a linear discrete-time system **x**ₖ₊₁ = **A x**ₖ, spectral analysis of the matrix **A** yields everything: stability, transients, asymptotic behavior, and an explicit closed-form solution. No such universal machinery exists for a general nonlinear map **F** or vector field **f** with **ẋ** = **f**(**x**) on a state manifold ℳ ⊂ ℝⁿ. Classical geometric approaches — linearization at equilibria via the Hartman–Grobman theorem, Lyapunov functions, center-manifold reduction — are inherently local or require formidable analytical insight.

The Koopman operator, introduced in 1931 by B. O. Koopman [1], offers a strikingly different bargain: *exact linearity at the price of infinite dimensionality*. Rather than tracking the state **x**(t) through its nonlinear evolution, one tracks the evolution of every possible measurement — every scalar- or vector-valued observable function g : ℳ → ℂ — and discovers that observables evolve under a *linear* operator, regardless of how nonlinear the underlying dynamics are. This is not a linearization; it is an exact reformulation. The operator's spectrum — its eigenvalues and eigenfunctions — then provides a global nonlinear analogue of the linear spectral expansion, the so-called *Koopman mode decomposition*.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the operator-theoretic foundations: the definition of the Koopman operator, its spectral decomposition, Koopman eigenfunctions and modes, and the intimate connection to ergodic theory. Section 3 develops the data-driven methodology, deriving Dynamic Mode Decomposition and Extended DMD as finite-dimensional approximations, and discussing kernel formulations, dictionary learning, and deep-learning embeddings. Section 4 presents a deep dive into four technical pillars: the spectral geometry of Koopman eigenfunctions, convergence theory and spectral pollution, residual DMD with rigorous error control, and Koopman-based model predictive control. Section 5 gives empirical evidence and proof sketches, including the analytically solvable quadratic example and benchmark results on fluid flows. Section 6 confronts the limitations honestly, and Section 7 concludes with an outlook on the field's frontier.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Koopman operator

Consider a discrete-time dynamical system defined by a smooth map **F** : ℳ → ℳ, so that **x**ₖ₊₁ = **F**(**x**ₖ), or equivalently a continuous-time flow **S**ᵗ with **x**(t) = **S**ᵗ(**x**₀). An *observable* is any measurement function g : ℳ → ℂ, typically drawn from a suitable function space ℱ such as L²(ℳ, μ) for an invariant measure μ, or a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. The **Koopman operator** 𝒦 is defined by composition with the dynamics [2]:

> **Definition (Koopman operator).** For g ∈ ℱ, (𝒦g)(**x**) ≜ g(**F**(**x**)). Equivalently, (𝒦g)(**x**ₖ) = g(**x**ₖ₊₁).

The crucial observation is that 𝒦 is *linear*: 𝒦(αg₁ + βg₂) = α𝒦g₁ + β𝒦g₂ for all scalars α, β and observables g₁, g₂, even when **F** is violently nonlinear. The cost is that 𝒦 acts on an infinite-dimensional space. For measure-preserving (e.g., Hamiltonian) systems, 𝒦 is unitary on L²(ℳ, μ), so its spectrum lies on the unit circle — a fact Koopman himself exploited to connect the spectrum to integrability and ergodicity [1].

### 2.2 Eigenfunctions, eigenvalues, and Koopman modes

The spectral objects of 𝒦 are pairs (λⱼ, φⱼ) satisfying the eigenfunction equation 𝒦φⱼ = λⱼφⱼ, i.e., φⱼ(**F**(**x**)) = λⱼφⱼ(**x**). Each eigenfunction evolves by pure exponential scaling along trajectories, exactly like a linear mode. A vector-valued observable **g** : ℳ → ℂᵖ can then be expanded in the eigenfunction basis:

> **Theorem (Koopman spectral expansion).** *If the eigenfunctions {φⱼ} span the observable **g**, then*
>
> **g**(**x**ₖ) = Σⱼ φⱼ(**x**₀) λⱼᵏ **v**ⱼ,
>
> *where the coefficients **v**ⱼ ∈ ℂᵖ are the Koopman modes of **g**.*

This is the precise nonlinear analogue of the linear spectral expansion: the dynamics are decomposed into coherent spatiotemporal structures (**v**ⱼ) oscillating and growing at rates encoded by λⱼ, with the initial observable values φⱼ(**x**₀) serving as coordinates. Mezić showed that this decomposition generalizes the Hartman–Grobman theorem to entire basins of attraction [2], and that level sets of eigenfunctions partition state space into invariant and ergodic components — the *ergodic partition* — so that the spectrum literally encodes the system's global geometry.

### 2.3 Historical context and the data-driven renaissance

After decades as a niche tool of ergodic theory, Koopman analysis was revived for applications by Mezić and collaborators [2], Rowley et al.'s connection to DMD [9], and the comprehensive review of Brunton, Budišić, Kaiser, and Kutz [5]. The turning point was the realization that the Dynamic Mode Decomposition of Schmid — originally a fluid-dynamics algorithm for extracting coherent structures — computes approximations to Koopman eigenvalues and modes from data alone [9].

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Dynamic Mode Decomposition

Given snapshot pairs {(**x**ₖ, **y**ₖ)} with **y**ₖ = **F**(**x**ₖ), k = 1, …, m, DMD seeks the best-fit linear operator **A** minimizing Σₖ ‖**y**ₖ − **A x**ₖ‖². Arranging snapshots into matrices **X** = [**x**₁ … **x**ₘ] and **Y** = [**y**₁ … **y**ₘ], the solution is **A** = **Y X**⁺ (Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse), computed efficiently via the SVD **X** = **U Σ V***. Projecting onto the leading r singular vectors gives the reduced operator **Ã** = **U***ᵣ **Y V**ᵣ **Σ**⁻¹ᵣ, whose eigendecomposition **Ã W** = **W Λ** yields DMD eigenvalues λⱼ and modes **Φ** = **Y V**ᵣ **Σ**⁻¹ᵣ **W**. Rowley et al. proved that when the snapshots lie in a Koopman-invariant subspace, these coincide exactly with Koopman eigenpairs [9]. In general, DMD is the *linear-observable* special case of the framework below: it approximates 𝒦 using only the identity observable g(**x**) = **x**, and therefore cannot capture genuinely nonlinear transients.

### 3.2 Extended DMD: Galerkin projection onto dictionaries

Extended DMD (EDMD), introduced by Williams, Kevrekidis, and Rowley [3], lifts the state through a *dictionary* of nonlinear observables **ψ**(**x**) = [ψ₁(**x**), …, ψ_N(**x**)]ᵀ — e.g., monomials, radial basis functions, or Fourier modes — and fits a matrix **K** ∈ ℂ^{N×N} such that **ψ**(**y**ₖ) ≈ **K ψ**(**x**ₖ). With dictionary data matrices **Ψ**_X and **Ψ**_Y, the EDMD operator is **K** = **Ψ**_Y **Ψ**_X⁺ = **G**⁺**A**, where **G** = (1/m)Σₖ **ψ**(**x**ₖ)**ψ**(**x**ₖ)* and **A** = (1/m)Σₖ **ψ**(**y**ₖ)**ψ**(**x**ₖ)* are empirical Gram matrices. This is precisely a Galerkin approximation: **K** is the matrix representation of the L²-orthogonal projection of 𝒦 onto span{ψⱼ}. As N → ∞ and m → ∞ (with the dictionary becoming dense in ℱ), the EDMD eigenpairs converge to the true Koopman spectrum in the large-data limit [3]. The dictionary is everything: a poor dictionary yields a poor projection, and dictionary selection remains the central practical art.

### 3.3 Kernel DMD and dictionary learning

When N is enormous — or the dictionary is implicitly infinite-dimensional — the kernel trick rescues computation. Williams, Rowley, and Kevrekidis showed that EDMD can be kernelized: all inner products ⟨**ψ**(**x**ᵢ), **ψ**(**x**ⱼ)⟩ are replaced by a Mercer kernel k(**x**ᵢ, **x**ⱼ), so that only m × m Gram matrices (m = number of snapshots) are ever formed [4]. Polynomial and Gaussian kernels implicitly realize infinite dictionaries, dramatically expanding the approximation space at fixed data cost. Parallel developments attacked dictionary selection directly: Li et al. introduced dictionary learning for EDMD [10], and Lusch, Kutz, and Brunton used deep autoencoders to *learn* a coordinate transformation in which the dynamics are linear — a nonlinear EDMD where the dictionary is a neural network, trained with an auxiliary-network loss enforcing linearity of the latent evolution [7].

### 3.4 Algorithmic summary

The data-driven Koopman pipeline proceeds in five stages:

1. **Collect** snapshot pairs (**x**ₖ, **y**ₖ) from experiment or simulation.
2. **Choose** a dictionary **ψ** (or kernel k, or learned embedding).
3. **Regress** the finite matrix **K** = argmin ‖**Ψ**_Y − **K Ψ**_X‖_F.
4. **Diagonalize** **K** to obtain (λⱼ, φⱼ ≈ **w**ⱼᵀ**ψ**, **v**ⱼ).
5. **Validate** via residuals, prediction error on held-out trajectories, and spectral stability under dictionary refinement.

```python
import numpy as np

def edmd(X, Y, dictionary, rank=None):
    """Extended DMD: K ≈ Psi(Y) @ pinv(Psi(X))."""
    PsiX = np.stack([dictionary(x) for x in X.T], axis=1)  # N x m
    PsiY = np.stack([dictionary(x) for x in Y.T], axis=1)  # N x m
    U, s, Vh = np.linalg.svd(PsiX, full_matrices=False)
    r = rank or np.sum(s > 1e-10 * s[0])
    Ur, sr, Vhr = U[:, :r], s[:r], Vh[:r, :]
    Ktilde = (Ur.T @ PsiY @ Vhr.T) / sr[None, :]           # r x r projected Koopman matrix
    lam, W = np.linalg.eig(Ktilde)
    Phi = (PsiY @ Vhr.T / sr[None, :]) @ W                 # Koopman modes
    eigfun = lambda x: W.conj().T @ (Ur.T @ dictionary(x)) # eigenfunctions
    return lam, Phi, eigfun
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Spectral geometry: what eigenfunctions reveal about dynamics

Koopman eigenfunctions are not mere computational artifacts; they are geometric objects that expose the skeleton of the dynamics. For a system with a globally attracting fixed point, the principal eigenfunctions are smooth and their level sets foliate the basin of attraction; the associated eigenvalues are the linearization eigenvalues at the attractor, and the eigenfunction coordinates provide the nonlinear normal-form transformation conjugating the system to its linearization on the *entire* basin — the global Hartman–Grobman theorem of Lan and Mezić. For the undamped pendulum, eigenfunctions with |λ| = 1 foliate phase space into invariant tori, while the zero-frequency eigenfunction (λ = 1) is constant on ergodic components and thus *labels* them: its level sets are precisely the ergodic partition. This yields a remarkable data-driven capability: from trajectory data alone, EDMD can segment state space into dynamically distinct regions — basins of attraction, ergodic components, isochrons of limit cycles — without any prior model. The Koopman *modes* **v**ⱼ then assign to each frequency a spatial structure, generalizing normal modes, Floquet modes, and global stability eigenmodes within a single formalism [5][9].

| Spectral object | Governing equation | Dynamical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Eigenvalue λⱼ | — | Growth rate log\|λⱼ\|/Δt, frequency arg(λⱼ)/Δt |
| Eigenfunction φⱼ | φⱼ∘**F** = λⱼφⱼ | Intrinsic coordinate evolving exponentially; level sets partition state space |
| Koopman mode **v**ⱼ | **g** = Σ φⱼ**v**ⱼ | Coherent spatial structure at frequency arg(λⱼ) |
| λ = 1 eigenfunctions | φ∘**F** = φ | Invariants labeling ergodic components / basins |

### 4.2 Continuous spectra and the limits of finite expansions

Not all Koopman operators admit eigenfunction expansions. For chaotic or mixing systems — the Lorenz attractor, turbulent shear flows — the Koopman operator on L² possesses a *continuous* spectrum: there are no nontrivial eigenfunctions, only approximate ones, and the spectral measure must be described via the spectral theorem for unitary operators. Finite-dimensional DMD-type approximations then exhibit two pathologies: **spectral pollution** (spurious eigenvalues far from the true spectrum, an artifact of the non-normal Galerkin projection) and **spectral invisibility** (missing true spectral content because the dictionary cannot resolve it). Colbrook and collaborators addressed this with *residual DMD* (ResDMD): alongside the EDMD matrices, one computes a data-driven residual

> **Definition (ResDMD residual).** *For a candidate eigenpair (λ, g = **v*** **ψ**), the relative residual res(λ, g) = ‖𝒦g − λg‖ / ‖g‖ is estimated from snapshot data via the additional Gram matrix **L** = (1/m)Σₖ **ψ**(**y**ₖ)**ψ**(**y**ₖ)*, yielding res² = (**v*** (**L** − λ̄**A*** − λ**A** + |λ|²**G**)**v**)/(**v*****G v**).*

Only eigenpairs with res(λ, g) ≤ ε are retained — a *no-spectral-pollution* guarantee: as data and dictionary grow, every computed eigenvalue converges to the true spectrum [6][11]. Moreover, smoothed approximations of the spectral *measure* (not just eigenvalues) converge with explicit high-order rates, finally giving rigorous access to continuous spectra — demonstrated on turbulent boundary layers, wall jets, and even a 20,046-dimensional protein folding trajectory [6].

### 4.3 Convergence theory: when Galerkin approximations can be trusted

The theoretical backbone is Galerkin convergence in the large-data, large-dictionary limit. Under ergodicity and mild regularity, the empirical matrices **G**, **A** converge to their population counterparts ⟨ψᵢ, ψⱼ⟩ and ⟨ψᵢ, 𝒦ψⱼ⟩, so **K** converges to the Galerkin projection P_N 𝒦 P_N; as the dictionary becomes dense, P_N → I strongly and the finite-section spectra converge for operators with *pure point* spectrum and no spectral pollution [3]. The sharp modern results go further: for self-adjoint (reversible) systems, Hermitian DMD preserves self-adjointness and its spectral measures converge weakly to those of 𝒦 — including unbounded generators — via a general finite-section theorem of independent interest [6]. The practical moral is a hierarchy of trust: **EDMD/DMD eigenvalues** are useful but unvalidated; **ResDMD-filtered eigenvalues** are certified against pollution by the residual; and **spectral measures** are the correct object when continuous spectra dominate. Dictionary quality remains the uncontrolled variable: convergence theorems assume a *fixed* dictionary growing dense, but practitioners choose finite dictionaries heuristically, so a posteriori validation — prediction on held-out data and residual thresholds — is the core of honest Koopmanism.

### 4.4 Koopman model predictive control: convexifying nonlinear control

Perhaps the most consequential application lifts not just analysis but *control* into the linear realm. Consider the nonlinear control system **x**ₖ₊₁ = **f**(**x**ₖ, **u**ₖ). If the dictionary is chosen so that the lifted state **z**ₖ = **ψ**(**x**ₖ) evolves *linearly* in both state and input,

**z**ₖ₊₁ = **A z**ₖ + **B u**ₖ, **x**ₖ = **C z**ₖ,

then nonlinear model predictive control — a nonconvex optimization — becomes a *convex quadratic program* in the lifted coordinates, solvable in milliseconds [8][12]. Korda and Mezić constructed such linear predictors from data via EDMD with control (EDMDc), demonstrating on the Van der Pol oscillator and bilinear systems that Koopman MPC matches nonlinear MPC performance at a fraction of the computational cost [12]. Subsequent refinements removed the dictionary-selection burden: Korda and Mezić's optimization-based eigenfunction construction proves the learned eigenfunctions span all continuous functions, with control incorporated through multistep prediction-error minimization — entirely convex, with no neural networks required [13]. Recent work extends guarantees to exponential stability of the closed loop, robust MPC under intermittent stochastic measurements, and online adaptive dictionaries for industrial processes [14]. The conceptual payoff is profound: the same lifting trick that linearizes prediction also linearizes *optimization*, because convexity is preserved under the linear lifted dynamics [8][12][13].

### 4.5 Applications: fluids, brains, and grids

**Fluid dynamics** was the cradle of applied Koopmanism: DMD extracts the von Kármán vortex-shedding frequency and its harmonics from cylinder-wake PIV data as Koopman modes, and ResDMD extends this to fully turbulent boundary layers and jets with verified spectra [11]. **Neuroscience** uses Koopman embeddings to identify low-dimensional linearizable coordinates of neural population activity, where eigenfunctions reveal metastable states and transition rates. **Power grids** employ Koopman mode decomposition of PMU data to detect inter-area oscillations and predict transient stability margins after faults. In each domain the pattern is identical: high-dimensional nonlinear measurements enter, and a handful of Koopman modes with clear frequencies and growth rates emerge, enabling prediction, estimation, and control with linear tools [5].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 An analytically solvable benchmark: the quadratic map family

Consider the scalar system xₖ₊₁ = a xₖ + b xₖ² with |a| < 1. Seeking eigenfunctions as power series φ(x) = Σ_{n≥1} c_n xⁿ and imposing φ(ax + bx²) = λφ(x) yields, at order x¹, λ = a with φ₁(x) = x + (b/(a² − a))x² + ⋯ — a globally valid eigenfunction on the basin of attraction, computable term-by-term. The full point spectrum is {aⁿ : n ∈ ℕ}: powers of the principal eigenfunction are eigenfunctions, φ_n = φ₁ⁿ with eigenvalue aⁿ. EDMD with a monomial dictionary {1, x, x², …, x^N} recovers these eigenpairs to machine precision — a clean verification that the algorithm reproduces exact spectral theory when the dictionary spans a finite Koopman-invariant subspace.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: EDMD as Galerkin projection

> **Proposition.** *Let **G**, **A** be the population Gram matrices ⟨ψᵢ,ψⱼ⟩, ⟨ψᵢ,𝒦ψⱼ⟩ in L²(μ). Then the EDMD matrix **K** = **G**⁻¹**A** is the matrix of P_N 𝒦 P_N in the dictionary basis, where P_N is the orthogonal projection onto span{ψⱼ}.*

*Proof.* For any g = **c**ᵀ**ψ** in the dictionary span, the Galerkin condition requires ⟨ψᵢ, 𝒦g − **d**ᵀ**ψ**⟩ = 0 for all i, i.e., **A c** = **G d**, giving **d** = **G**⁻¹**A c**. ∎

Empirical EDMD replaces population inner products with ergodic averages; under ergodicity these converge almost surely, and with them the matrices and (for isolated eigenvalues of finite multiplicity) the eigenpairs [3]. The failure mode is equally instructive: when 𝒦 has continuous spectrum, P_N 𝒦 P_N can produce eigenvalues anywhere in the numerical range — hence pollution — and no amount of data cures a dictionary orthogonal to the relevant spectral subspace.

### 5.3 Empirical performance on fluid benchmarks

On the canonical cylinder wake at Re = 100, DMD with 200 snapshots recovers the Strouhal shedding frequency to within 1% of DNS values, and the leading Koopman mode reproduces the von Kármán street spatial structure [9][11]. For a turbulent wall jet (state dimension > 100,000), standard EDMD returns hundreds of modes of dubious provenance; ResDMD with ε = 10⁻² retains only a few dozen certified modes while additionally resolving the continuous spectral background as a smoothed measure with documented convergence rates [11]. In Koopman MPC benchmarks on the Van der Pol oscillator, the lifted QP controller achieves tracking error within 5% of full nonlinear MPC while reducing per-step solve time by over an order of magnitude [12].

## 6. Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating plainly what the Koopman framework does *not* deliver.

**The curse of dimensionality in observable space.** Lifting trades n-dimensional nonlinearity for N-dimensional linearity with N ≫ n — often N in the thousands for EDMD, or infinite for kernels. The regression **K** = **Ψ**_Y**Ψ**_X⁺ costs O(min(N²m, m³)) and the eigendecomposition O(N³) (or O(m³) kernelized). For high-dimensional systems this is frequently more expensive than integrating the original nonlinear model, and the lifted dimension must be paid at every MPC step.

**No finite invariant subspace need exist.** For systems with multiple disjoint attractors, 𝒦 admits no finite-dimensional invariant subspace containing the state observable: any finite dictionary leaks across basins. EDMD then produces a compromise projection valid nowhere globally. The eigenfunction-construction methods of [13] mitigate this by exploiting spectral richness away from attractors, but global linearizability remains exceptional rather than typical.

**Continuous spectra defeat modal truncation.** In mixing and turbulent systems, the point spectrum may be trivial; the dynamics live in the continuous spectrum, where finite modal sums are the wrong ansatz and only spectral measures are meaningful [6]. Practitioners who plot "the DMD spectrum" of turbulence without residuals are visualizing pollution as often as physics.

**Dictionary selection is unprincipled.** Every convergence theorem assumes the dictionary; none tells you how to choose it. Cross-validation, sparsity promotion, and deep learning help empirically [7][10], but the method remains closer to an art — with failure modes that are silent rather than loud.

**Control lifting is not free.** Koopman MPC requires the input to enter the lifted dynamics linearly (**z**ₖ₊₁ = **A z**ₖ + **B u**ₖ); state-dependent input gains **B**(**x**) break convexity. State constraints become nonconvex in lifted coordinates, and the reconstruction **x** = **C z** is exact only if the state lies in the dictionary span.

## 7. Conclusion

The Koopman operator reframes nonlinear dynamics as linear dynamics in the space of observables — an exact, global, and measurement-native reformulation whose spectral theory generalizes linear modal analysis to nonlinear systems. The data-driven turn, from DMD through EDMD and kernel variants to residual DMD with rigorous error control, has converted a 1931 ergodic-theoretic curiosity into a practical technology for prediction, modal decomposition, and convex control of nonlinear systems. Yet the framework's power is bounded by honest mathematics: infinite dimensionality cannot be wished away, continuous spectra demand measures rather than modes, and every finite computation is a Galerkin projection whose quality stands or falls on the dictionary. The frontier is therefore not a single breakthrough but a disciplined program — *verified Koopmanism*: residuals as standard output, spectral measures alongside eigenvalues, learned dictionaries with generalization guarantees, and control designs with closed-loop certificates. Where that discipline is practiced, as in recent fluid-dynamics and MPC results, the Koopman perspective delivers what it promises: the linear toolbox, finally applicable to the nonlinear world.
