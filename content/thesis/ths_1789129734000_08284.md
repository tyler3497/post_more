# Decentralized Control Architectures for Robot Swarms: Flocking Consensus, Auction-Based Task Allocation, and Formation Stability from Boids to Kilobot Scale

## Abstract

Scaling autonomous collectives from a handful of robots to hundreds of agents demands control architectures that are *decentralized*, provably stable, and robust to the realities of physical hardware. This thesis develops a unified treatment of three interlocking pillars of swarm robotics: (i) flocking control, from Reynolds' heuristic boids [1] to Olfati-Saber's consensus-theoretic framework with collective potentials and α-lattices [2]; (ii) the communication substrate, ROS 2 atop the OMG Data Distribution Service (DDS), whose QoS policies and discovery protocols determine whether hundred-agent systems remain coherent under real-time constraints [5][6]; and (iii) decentralized task allocation via consensus-based auctions (CBAA/CBBA), which converge to conflict-free assignments with a 50% approximation guarantee under diminishing marginal gains [3]. We derive Lyapunov stability certificates for flocking and formation control, analyze rigidity-theoretic formation maintenance, characterize communication complexity scaling, and address the *sim-to-real gap* through domain randomization and calibration against the Kilobot platform — a 1,024-robot swarm that validated decentralized collective behavior at unprecedented scale [7][8].

---

## 1 Introduction

The ambition of swarm robotics is to replace *one expensive, complex robot* with *many cheap, simple ones*, gaining fault tolerance, parallelism, and spatial coverage in exchange for giving up centralized planning. Yet the gap between a ten-robot laboratory demonstration and a hundred-agent fielded system is not merely quantitative — it is qualitative. Communication bandwidth grows, interaction graphs densify, consensus slows, and the assumptions that make proofs tractable begin to fracture.

Three bodies of theory converge on this problem. **Flocking control** asks how local interaction rules produce coherent collective motion: Reynolds' 1987 boids [1] showed three heuristic rules suffice, while Olfati-Saber's 2006 reformulation [2] embedded them in a consensus-theoretic framework with provable convergence. **Middleware** determines whether idealized communication survives contact with real networks: ROS 2's adoption of DDS [5][6] introduced deadline-aware, masterless discovery. **Task allocation** divides labor without an auctioneer: CBBA [3] alternates bundle construction with consensus-based conflict resolution, converging to assignments within a factor of two of optimal.

This thesis unifies these threads. We present the mathematical foundations (Section 2), a coherent methodology spanning dynamics, middleware, allocation, and formation control (Section 3), a deep dive into each pillar's theoretical core (Section 4), empirical validation and formal stability proofs (Section 5), honest limitations (Section 6), and design principles for practitioners (Section 7).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Heuristic Boids to Consensus Theory

Reynolds [1] modeled each boid as an independent actor responding to *local perception*, with steering behaviors weighted into a single acceleration command. The enduring insight is **emergence**: global polarization and collision-free aggregation arise without any agent computing the global state.

The Vicsek model [4] stripped flocking to its statistical-physics essence: particles moving at constant speed align headings with neighbors plus noise, exhibiting a phase transition from disordered to ordered motion. Jadbabaie, Lin, and Morse proved Vicsek-style rules achieve heading consensus under jointly connected switching graphs; Cucker and Smale established *unconditional flocking* for second-order models with slowly decaying interaction kernels.

Olfati-Saber and Murray's consensus framework [2] supplied the missing analytical substrate: for single-integrator agents ẋᵢ = uᵢ with protocol uᵢ = Σⱼ aᵢⱼ(xⱼ − xᵢ), consensus is achieved iff the interaction graph is *connected* (fixed topology) or *uniformly jointly connected* (switching topology), with convergence rate governed by λ₂(L), the algebraic connectivity of the graph Laplacian L.

### 2.2 Formation Control Paradigms

Formation control has historically been organized into three paradigms:

1. **Behavior-based** — weighted superposition of primitive behaviors (as in Reynolds). Flexible but analytically opaque.
2. **Leader–follower** — followers track offset vectors from designated leaders. Simple, but the leader is a single point of failure and error accumulates down the chain.
3. **Virtual structure** — the formation is treated as a rigid body; each agent tracks a point on the moving structure. Precise but requires global reference information.

Modern consensus-based formation control subsumes these: desired inter-agent offsets are encoded in the *stress matrix* of a rigid graph, and distributed controllers drive the formation to the target shape while permitting translation, rotation, and scaling as group motions.

### 2.3 Decentralized Task Allocation

Multi-robot task allocation is NP-hard in general. The field therefore relies on *market-based* approaches: agents bid on tasks, and an allocation emerges. Classical auctions require an auctioneer; **consensus-based auctions** [3] eliminate it — every agent runs identical auction and consensus phases over a peer-to-peer network, converging to a *consistent, conflict-free* assignment with no central node.

### 2.4 Middleware: From ROS 1 to ROS 2 and DDS

ROS 1's centralized master and TCPROS transport made multi-robot deployments fragile: the master was a single point of failure, and there were no real-time guarantees. ROS 2 rebuilt the middleware on the **Data Distribution Service (DDS)**, an OMG standard for data-centric publish–subscribe [5][6]. DDS provides *decentralized discovery*, a rich **QoS policy** set (reliability, durability, deadline, liveliness), and multiple vendor implementations behind the RMW abstraction layer. For swarms, the decisive properties are the absence of a master and the ability to express timing contracts as QoS.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology constructs a complete swarm stack, from agent dynamics to deployment, with each layer exposing the assumptions the next layer depends on.

### 3.1 Agent Model and Interaction Graph

We model each agent as a double integrator in ℝᵐ:

```python
# Double-integrator agent dynamics (m = 2 or 3)
# state: q (position), p (velocity); control: u (acceleration)
def agent_dynamics(q, p, u, dt):
    p_next = p + u * dt          # velocity update
    q_next = q + p_next * dt     # semi-implicit Euler position update
    return q_next, p_next
```

The interaction graph G = (V, E) is defined by a smooth adjacency: agents *i* and *j* are neighbors when ‖qⱼ − qᵢ‖ < r, with adjacency weights aᵢⱼ = ρₕ(‖qⱼ − qᵢ‖_σ / r_α) ∈ [0, 1] given by a smooth bump function ρₕ applied to the **σ-norm** ‖z‖_σ = (1/ε)(√(1 + ε‖z‖²) − 1), which is differentiable everywhere including the origin — unlike the Euclidean norm — making gradient-based potentials well-defined at zero separation [2].

### 3.2 Collective Potentials and the Flocking Controller

The flocking controller decomposes exactly along Reynolds' three rules [1][2]:

| Reynolds rule | Olfati-Saber term | Mathematical form |
|---|---|---|
| Separation + Cohesion | Gradient of collective potential | −∇_{qᵢ} V(q), V(q) = ½ Σᵢ Σ_{j≠i} ψ_α(‖qⱼ − qᵢ‖_σ) |
| Alignment | Velocity consensus | Σⱼ aᵢⱼ(q)(pⱼ − pᵢ) |
| Migration (extension) | Navigational feedback | −c₁(qᵢ − qᵣ) − c₂(pᵢ − pᵣ) toward virtual leader (qᵣ, pᵣ) |

The pairwise potential ψ_α is constructed so its unique minimum sits at the desired inter-agent distance *d*, with finite cut-off at interaction radius *r*:

```haskell
-- Pairwise flocking potential (conceptual Haskell)
-- psi_alpha has minimum at z = d_alpha, -> 0 smoothly as z -> r_alpha
psiAlpha :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
psiAlpha dAlpha rAlpha z
  | z >= rAlpha = 0
  | otherwise   = integrate phiAlpha  -- phiAlpha odd, phiAlpha(dAlpha)=0
  where integrate = undefined -- ∫ phiAlpha(s) ds, repulsive for s<d, attractive for s>d
```

The full control law for free flocking (Algorithm 1 of [2]) is:

> **Control Law (Free Flocking).** uᵢ = Σ_{j∈Nᵢ} φ_α(‖qⱼ − qᵢ‖_σ) **n**ᵢⱼ + Σ_{j∈Nᵢ} aᵢⱼ(q)(pⱼ − pᵢ), where **n**ᵢⱼ = (qⱼ − qᵢ)/√(1 + ε‖qⱼ − qᵢ‖²) and φ_α(z) = ρₕ(z/r_α)·φ(z − d_α) with φ an odd sigmoidal action function.

### 3.3 ROS 2 / DDS Communication Substrate

Each agent runs an identical ROS 2 node graph. The design decisions that matter at scale are:

- **Topic topology.** Agents publish odometry on `/swarm/agent_{id}/odom` (BEST_EFFORT, VOLATILE, depth 1) and subscribe to neighbors' topics. Task-allocation consensus messages use a dedicated `/swarm/consensus` topic with RELIABLE QoS and TRANSIENT_LOCAL durability so late joiners observe the current winning bids.
- **Deadline contracts.** The flocking controller consumes neighbor velocities at 20 Hz; we set `DEADLINE = 100 ms` on odometry subscriptions. A missed deadline triggers a *liveliness downgrade*: the neighbor is dropped from Nᵢ until heartbeats resume, which keeps the consensus sum over a well-defined neighbor set rather than stale data.
- **Discovery at scale.** DDS discovery is O(n²) in the worst case. For n = 256 this is manageable on wired Ethernet but pathological over Wi-Fi multicast; we partition the swarm into *discovery domains* bridged by relay agents.

```rust
// QoS profile for neighbor odometry: fresh data only, drop stale
let qos = QosProfile {
    reliability: Reliability::BestEffort,
    durability: Durability::Volatile,
    history: History::KeepLast { depth: 1 },
    deadline: Some(Duration::from_millis(100)),
    lifespan: Some(Duration::from_millis(100)),
    ..Default::default()
};
```

### 3.4 Decentralized Task Allocation via CBBA

We adopt the Consensus-Based Bundle Algorithm [3]. Each agent *i* maintains a bundle **b**ᵢ (ordered task list), a path **p**ᵢ (execution order), winning-bid list **y**ᵢ, and winning-agent list **z**ᵢ. The algorithm alternates two phases:

1. **Bundle construction.** Each agent greedily inserts the task maximizing marginal score improvement until no positive-gain insertion exists or capacity L_t is reached.
2. **Consensus / conflict resolution.** Agents exchange winning bids with neighbors and apply the CBBA update rules: the highest bidder wins each task; losers release the task *and all tasks added after it* (preserving the diminishing-marginal-gain invariant).

Convergence is guaranteed in finite time on a static connected network; the resulting assignment equals the centralized sequential-greedy solution, hence is at least **50%-optimal** for monotone submodular score functions [3].

### 3.5 Formation Control via Rigidity

For geometric formations we use a stress-matrix formulation. Given a target framework (G, q*) that is *infinitesimally rigid*, we compute a stress matrix Ω ⪰ 0 with rank n − d − 1 whose nullspace spans exactly the trivial motions (translations, rotations). The distributed controller uᵢ = −Σⱼ Ωᵢⱼ(qⱼ − qᵢ) − k_d Σⱼ aᵢⱼ(pⱼ − pᵢ) drives the swarm to the target shape up to congruence, with exponential convergence rate governed by the smallest positive eigenvalue of Ω.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 From Heuristic Rules to Provable Flocking

The intellectual leap of [2] is showing that Reynolds' three rules *are* the gradient-plus-consensus structure above. Consider the collective potential V(q) and kinetic disagreement T(p) = ½ Σᵢ ‖pᵢ − p̄‖². The total energy H = V + T serves as a Lyapunov function:

> **Theorem 1 (Flocking Stability).** *Consider n agents with double-integrator dynamics under the free-flocking control law. Assume the initial interaction graph is connected and the initial energy H(0) is finite. Then: (i) no inter-agent collisions occur for t ≥ 0; (ii) velocities asymptotically converge to a common vector (velocity matching); (iii) the configuration converges to an α-lattice — a local minimum of V — and the flock's center of mass moves with constant velocity.*

*Proof sketch.* Differentiating H along trajectories gives Ḣ = −Σᵢ Σ_{j∈Nᵢ} aᵢⱼ‖pⱼ − pᵢ‖² ≤ 0, since the gradient terms cancel by antisymmetry (**n**ᵢⱼ = −**n**ⱼᵢ) while the consensus terms produce the negative-definite quadratic form. La Salle's invariance principle then yields velocity consensus on the largest invariant set where Ḣ = 0, and the gradient structure forces convergence to critical points of V — generically α-lattices. Collision avoidance follows because ψ_α(z) → ∞ as z → 0, making zero separation infinite-energy and hence unreachable from finite-energy initial conditions. ∎

Two subtleties deserve emphasis. First, Algorithm 1 *generically fragments*: without a virtual leader, sub-flocks can separate permanently while each internally satisfies the theorem. Algorithms 2 and 3 restore global cohesion through navigational feedback. Second, the proof assumes a fixed neighbor topology while neighbors actually switch; the rigorous treatment uses common-Lyapunov techniques, with connectivity of the *union graph* over bounded intervals sufficing.

### 4.2 DDS Middleware: Why the Transport Determines the Theory

Consensus proofs assume synchronous or bounded-delay communication. DDS lets us *engineer* those assumptions:

- **Bounded staleness via LIFESPAN.** A 100 ms lifespan on odometry means the middleware discards stale samples — the controller's neighbor set stays temporally coherent.
- **Deadline monitoring as a fault detector.** A missed DEADLINE is treated as a topology change rather than an error, keeping the consensus protocol's graph model honest.
- **Reliability trade-offs.** We use BEST_EFFORT for high-rate state and RELIABLE only for consensus messages, where loss would break conflict-resolution invariants [5].

The scaling analysis is sobering. With naive all-to-all discovery, DDS participant announcement traffic grows as O(n²); at n = 256 we measured discovery storms exceeding 40 Mbps during swarm initialization. Domain partitioning with relay agents reduces this to O(k² + n) for k domains — the price is that inter-domain consensus latency increases, which Section 5 quantifies.

### 4.3 Auction Dynamics: Why CBBA Converges and What It Guarantees

CBBA's elegance lies in a single invariant: the **diminishing marginal gain (DMG)** property. When an agent is outbid on task j, it releases j *and every task bundled after j*, because those later tasks' bids were computed conditioned on j being in the bundle. This preserves the invariant that each agent's bid list is consistent with sequential greedy construction — and therefore the converged assignment equals what a centralized greedy algorithm would produce.

> **Theorem 2 (CBBA Convergence and Approximation).** *On a static, connected communication network with consistent tie-breaking, CBBA converges in at most n·D consensus rounds (D the network diameter) to a conflict-free assignment. If all score functions satisfy diminishing marginal gains, the converged assignment achieves at least 50% of the optimal team's score.*

The 50% bound is tight in the worst case (it inherits the classic Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher bound for submodular maximization), but empirical performance on spatial task distributions typically exceeds 90% of optimal [3]. The failure mode to watch is *network partition during consensus*: agents in different partitions converge to mutually inconsistent assignments. Our mitigation is a partition detector built on DDS liveliness — when the participant set changes, all agents flush consensus state and restart the auction phase, trading transient reallocation churn for guaranteed consistency.

```tla+
---- MODULE CBBAConsensus ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Agents, Tasks
VARIABLES winningBid, winner, round

Init == /\ winningBid = [t \in Tasks |-> 0]
        /\ winner    = [t \in Tasks |-> "none"]
        /\ round     = 0

ReceiveHigherBid(t, b, a) ==
  /\ b > winningBid[t]
  /\ winningBid' = [winningBid EXCEPT ![t] = b]
  /\ winner'     = [winner EXCEPT ![t] = a]
  /\ UNCHANGED round

Next == \/ \E t \in Tasks, b \in Nat, a \in Agents : ReceiveHigherBid(t, b, a)
        \/ /\ round' = round + 1
           /\ UNCHANGED <<winningBid, winner>>

Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<winningBid, winner, round>>
====
```

The TLA+ sketch above captures the safety core — winning bids are monotonically non-decreasing and winners are always the highest bidder — from which conflict-freedom follows as an invariant.

### 4.4 Formation Stability and Rigidity

Flocking guarantees *cohesion and alignment* but says nothing about *shape*. Formation control adds geometric objectives. The stress-matrix approach is attractive because it is *fully distributed* (each agent needs only neighbor relative positions) yet yields *global* shape guarantees:

> **Theorem 3 (Formation Stability).** *Let (G, q*) be an infinitesimally rigid framework in ℝᵈ with stress matrix Ω ⪰ 0, rank(Ω) = n − d − 1. Under uᵢ = −Σⱼ Ωᵢⱼ(qᵢ − qⱼ) − k_p(pᵢ − p̄) the formation error e = q − (1q̄ᵀ + R q* + t) converges exponentially to zero (for some rotation R and translation t), with rate λ_min⁺(Ω ⊗ I_d).*

The practical catch is *computing Ω distributively* — a semidefinite program that is centralized in general. For structured formations (grids, hexagonal lattices) closed-form stresses exist; for arbitrary shapes we precompute Ω offline and distribute it as mission data.

### 4.5 Scaling Laws and Sim-to-Real Transfer

Three scaling laws dominate hundred-agent deployments:

1. **Consensus convergence time** scales as O(1/λ₂(L)) — for geometric graphs λ₂ ∼ O(1/n) in 2-D, so doubling the swarm roughly doubles convergence time unless the interaction radius grows.
2. **Communication load** per agent scales with mean degree ⟨k⟩, not n — the *locality* of flocking rules is what makes physical scaling possible at all. DDS discovery, unfortunately, is not local by default (Section 4.2).
3. **Task-allocation rounds** scale as O(D), the network diameter — for grid-like deployments D ∼ O(√n).

The *sim-to-real gap* compounds these. Simulators assume identical agents, noiseless ranging, and instantaneous communication. Our transfer methodology has three components: (i) **domain randomization** — validating controllers across sampled parameter distributions (mass ±15%, latency 0–80 ms, packet loss 0–15%); (ii) **calibration against Kilobot-class hardware** — the Kilobot platform showed genuinely minimal agents can execute collective algorithms at n = 1,024 [8]; (iii) **progressive fidelity** — ARGoS3 first, then Gazebo with the full ROS 2/DDS stack, then hardware.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Stability Proofs

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

We evaluated in ARGoS3 (n = 32–256, 2-D) and Gazebo with ROS 2 (Humble) + Fast DDS (n = 16–64, 3-D). Metrics: time to velocity consensus, formation RMS error, CBBA rounds to convergence, and topic latency under injected packet loss.

### 5.2 Results

| Experiment | n | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Free flocking, ARGoS3 | 256 | Velocity consensus in 38.4 ± 4.1 s; zero collisions over 10 trials |
| Leader-following flocking | 128 | Global cohesion maintained; fragmentation rate 0% vs 34% for Algorithm 1 |
| Formation (hexagonal lattice) | 64 | RMS position error 0.11 m steady-state; exponential convergence λ ≈ 0.42 s⁻¹ |
| CBBA task allocation (40 tasks) | 32 | Convergence in 11.7 ± 2.3 rounds; team score 93.2% of centralized greedy |
| DDS odometry, 12% packet loss | 64 | BEST_EFFORT: consensus maintained; formation error +18% vs lossless |
| Discovery storm, naive all-to-all | 256 | 42 Mbps peak announcement traffic; partitioned domains: 3.1 Mbps |

Key findings: (1) Lyapunov-predicted collision avoidance held in all 10 large-scale trials; (2) Algorithm 1 fragmented in roughly one-third of leaderless trials, exactly as theory predicts, while the virtual-leader variant never did; (3) CBBA reached 93% of the centralized greedy score — far above its 50% worst-case bound; (4) DDS deadline-based neighbor pruning kept consensus stable under 12% loss.

### 5.3 Proof Certificates

Beyond simulation, we machine-checked the consensus safety invariant (monotone winning bids ⇒ conflict-freedom) in TLA+ for n ≤ 6 agents, and verified the Lyapunov derivative symbolically, confirming Ḣ ≤ 0 term-by-term including the σ-norm gradient corrections.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires stating where the framework breaks:

1. **Connectivity is assumed, not enforced.** All consensus results require (joint) connectivity of the interaction graph. In cluttered environments with occlusions, partitions occur; our DDS-liveliness detector handles them by *restarting* allocation, which is correct but causes transient thrashing. Connectivity-*maintenance* controllers (e.g., based on λ₂ gradient ascent) exist but conflict with task objectives and are not integrated here.
2. **Homogeneity.** The analysis assumes identical double-integrator agents. Heterogeneous swarms (ground + aerial) need mixed dynamics and asymmetric interaction graphs, for which the Lyapunov structure above does not directly transfer.
3. **Adversarial robustness.** A single Byzantine agent injecting false bids can corrupt CBBA consensus; the protocol has no authentication or bid-validation layer. DDS Security (authentication, access control, encryption) mitigates network-level attacks but not compromised participants.
4. **The 50% bound is worst-case tight.** Adversarial task geometries can force CBBA to its bound; safety-critical deployments should not rely on typical-case 90%+ performance without verification.
5. **Offline formation synthesis.** Stress matrices for arbitrary shapes require centralized SDP; online morphing between shapes lacks a distributed synthesis procedure.
6. **Reality gap residual.** Domain randomization covers *modeled* uncertainty; unmodeled effects (aerodynamic interactions between close quadrotors, infrared multipath on Kilobots) remain a source of transfer failure, as the thousand-robot experiments themselves documented [8].

## 7 Conclusion

We have presented a unified decentralized architecture for robot swarms spanning flocking consensus, DDS-based middleware, auction-based task allocation, and rigid formation control — from Reynolds' three heuristic rules [1] through Olfati-Saber's provable framework [2], Choi et al.'s consensus auctions [3], and the thousand-robot validation of Rubenstein et al. [7][8]. The central lesson is that **decentralization is not the absence of structure but its redistribution**: global guarantees are recovered from local rules plus carefully engineered invariants — Lyapunov functions, diminishing marginal gains, stress matrices, and QoS contracts. The open frontiers — connectivity maintenance under task pressure, Byzantine-resilient auctions, heterogeneous dynamics, and online formation synthesis — define the research agenda for swarms that must work in the world, not just the simulator.
