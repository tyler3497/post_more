# Seismic Full-Waveform Inversion: The Adjoint-State Method, Hessian-Free Optimization, and Imaging the Earth's Deep Interior

## Abstract

Seismic full-waveform inversion (FWI) reconstructs the Earth's interior by fitting the entire recorded wavefield with numerically simulated seismograms, updating a three-dimensional model of elastic or acoustic parameters through iterative local optimization. Its central device is the *adjoint-state method*: the misfit gradient with respect to millions of model parameters costs only two wave-equation solves — one forward, one adjoint [1][2]. This article develops the theory from first principles: the continuous and discrete adjoint of the elastic wave equation, the zero-lag cross-correlation form of the gradient, and the *banana-doughnut* structure of finite-frequency sensitivity kernels. We then examine Hessian-free optimization — L-BFGS quasi-Newton schemes that build curvature information from gradient history without ever forming the Hessian [7], and truncated Gauss–Newton–Krylov methods that solve the normal equations via second-order adjoints [6]. Finally we confront the method's central pathologies — cycle-skipping, non-uniqueness, and the forward-wavefield memory burden — and survey how multiscale strategies and novel misfits enabled FWI to image subducting slabs and mantle plumes at global scales [4][5].

## 1 Introduction

Classical seismic tomography reduces each seismogram to a handful of numbers — a travel time here, an amplitude there — discarding the rest. Yet a broadband seismogram contains the complete interference pattern of body waves, surface waves, and their multiples, each carrying independent information about traversed heterogeneity. **Full-waveform inversion** treats the *entire waveform* as data, seeking the subsurface model whose simulated waveforms match observations in a least-squares sense.

Formally, let $m(\mathbf{x})$ denote the model — typically $P$- and $S$-wave speeds, density, attenuation, or anisotropic coefficients on a spatial grid — and let $\mathbf{d}_{\text{obs}}$ be the observed waveforms. The forward operator $\mathbf{F}(m)$ solves the wave equation and samples the wavefield at receiver locations. FWI minimizes the misfit functional

$$
\chi(m) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{s,r}\int_0^T \lVert \mathbf{d}_{\text{syn}}(m; \mathbf{x}_r, t; \mathbf{x}_s) - \mathbf{d}_{\text{obs}}(\mathbf{x}_r, t; \mathbf{x}_s) \rVert^2 \, dt,
$$

summed over sources $s$ and receivers $r$. The landscape $\chi(m)$ is violently non-convex: oscillatory waveforms make the least-squares distance between two shifted sinusoids a multimodal function with countlessly many wells. The practical history of FWI is therefore the history of *taming* this non-convexity — through the adjoint-state gradient, through Hessian-free optimizers, and through multiscale continuation strategies [3][5].

> **Theorem:** *For a PDE-constrained optimization problem with $N$ model parameters and $M$ data, the first-order adjoint-state method computes the full gradient $\nabla_m \chi$ at the cost of one forward and one adjoint PDE solve — $\mathcal{O}(2)$ simulations — independent of $N$.*

This scaling is what makes FWI possible at all: with $N \sim 10^7$–$10^9$ parameters, any method requiring the explicit Jacobian $\partial \mathbf{F} / \partial m$ is dead on arrival.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The forward problem: elastodynamics in a heterogeneous Earth

In the elastic approximation, seismic wave propagation obeys

$$
\rho(\mathbf{x}) \, \partial_{tt} \mathbf{u} = \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma} + \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}, t), \qquad \boldsymbol{\sigma} = \mathbf{C}(m) : \nabla \mathbf{u},
$$

where $\mathbf{u}$ is displacement, $\rho$ density, $\mathbf{C}$ the fourth-order elasticity tensor, and $\mathbf{f}$ the source term. At exploration scales the acoustic approximation — replacing the vector equation with the scalar pressure equation — is often sufficient and was the setting of the founding FWI paper [1]. At global scales the full elastic equation — augmented by self-gravitation, rotation, and anelasticity — must be retained [2][4].

### 2.2 From linearized migration to nonlinear waveform inversion

The earliest schemes linearized around a smooth background — *migration*, the first iteration of FWI [3]. Tarantola (1984) took the decisive step: formulating the *nonlinear* least-squares problem and showing, via optimal-control adjoint-state theory, that the gradient is the cross-correlation of the forward wavefield with the back-propagated data residuals [1]; Lailly (1983) reached the same conclusion independently. The idea stayed academic for a decade until Pratt (1999) recast FWI in the frequency domain, enabling the multiscale continuation Bunks et al. (1995) had shown essential for avoiding local minima [5].

The globalization of FWI to earthquake seismology came through Tromp, Tape and Liu (2005), who merged adjoint methods, time-reversal acoustics, and finite-frequency ("banana-doughnut") kernels into a unified framework for structural and source inversion on continental and global scales [2]. Fichtner (2010) systematized the entire apparatus — forward solvers, adjoint theory, misfit design, and optimization — in the first comprehensive monograph [4].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The Lagrangian and the adjoint-state gradient

Consider the discrete constrained problem: minimize $\chi(m) = \frac{1}{2}\lVert \mathbf{R}\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{d}_{\text{obs}} \rVert^2$ subject to $\mathbf{A}(m)\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f}$, where $\mathbf{A}(m)$ is the discretized wave operator, $\mathbf{R}$ samples the wavefield at receivers, and $\mathbf{f}$ is the source. Form the Lagrangian

$$
\mathcal{L}(m, \mathbf{u}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = \frac{1}{2}\lVert \mathbf{R}\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{d}_{\text{obs}} \rVert^2 + \boldsymbol{\lambda}^T \bigl(\mathbf{A}(m)\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{f}\bigr).
$$

Stationarity with respect to the state $\mathbf{u}$ yields the **adjoint equation**:

$$
\mathbf{A}(m)^T \boldsymbol{\lambda} = -\mathbf{R}^T \bigl(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{d}_{\text{obs}}\bigr),
$$

i.e., the adjoint wavefield $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ is generated by *injecting the time-reversed data residuals at the receiver positions* and propagating them backward through the current model. Stationarity with respect to $m$ then gives the gradient as a bilinear form in the forward and adjoint fields:

$$
\nabla_m \chi = \left( \frac{\partial \mathbf{A}}{\partial m} \mathbf{u} \right)^T \boldsymbol{\lambda}.
$$

For the acoustic wave equation this reduces to the celebrated *imaging condition*: the slowness-squared gradient is the zero-lag cross-correlation of the forward pressure field with the second time derivative of the adjoint field [1] — two simulations per source, regardless of parameter count.

### 3.2 Sensitivity kernels and the banana-doughnut paradox

Differentiating $\chi$ with respect to a localized model perturbation gives the *sensitivity kernel* $K(\mathbf{x})$, a volumetric function answering: "where must the model change to reduce the misfit?" Under the first Born approximation, $K(\mathbf{x})$ is the space-time convolution of the forward wavefield from the source with the adjoint wavefield from the receiver [2]. For finite-frequency travel-time measurements these kernels are famously *zero along the geometrical ray path itself* — the "banana-doughnut" paradox — with maximum sensitivity in a hollow tube surrounding the ray. The name captures a deep physical truth: a scatterer sitting exactly on the ray produces no first-order travel-time shift, because forward-scattered energy arrives in phase with the direct wave.

### 3.3 The inversion loop

A complete FWI iteration for one source proceeds as follows:

1. **Forward simulation:** solve $\mathbf{A}(m_k)\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f}$; store (or checkpoint) the wavefield.
2. **Residual formation:** compute $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{d}_{\text{obs}}$ at all receivers.
3. **Adjoint simulation:** back-propagate the time-reversed residuals to obtain $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$.
4. **Gradient assembly:** cross-correlate $\mathbf{u}$ and $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ via the imaging condition.
5. **Update:** $m_{k+1} = m_k + \alpha_k \, \mathbf{P}_k \, \nabla_m \chi$, where $\mathbf{P}_k$ is a preconditioner or quasi-Newton approximation to the inverse Hessian and $\alpha_k$ a line-search step length.

Steps 1–4 cost roughly two forward simulations; step 5 is where optimization theory enters.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The continuous adjoint of the elastic wave equation

In the continuous setting the adjoint is derived by integration by parts on the weak form of the wave equation. For the elastic operator $L = \rho\,\partial_{tt} - \nabla\cdot(\mathbf{C}:\nabla)$, self-adjointness of the spatial part (with appropriate boundary conditions) implies that the adjoint field satisfies the *same* wave equation, driven by the adjoint source — the data residuals injected at receiver locations and reversed in time. A subtlety arises at fluid–solid boundaries (notably the core–mantle boundary), where the boundary terms from integration by parts must be handled to preserve exactness of the discrete gradient [4].

A minimal Python sketch of the acoustic imaging condition illustrates the structure (here `u_fwd[t]` and `u_adj[t]` are full spatial snapshots):

```python
import numpy as np

def adjoint_gradient(u_fwd, u_adj, dt, ds):
    """Zero-lag cross-correlation imaging condition (acoustic, slowness^2)."""
    nt = u_fwd.shape[0]
    # second time derivative of the adjoint field
    adj_tt = (u_adj[2:] - 2*u_adj[1:-1] + u_adj[:-2]) / dt**2
    grad = np.zeros_like(u_fwd[0])
    for it in range(1, nt-1):
        grad += u_fwd[it] * adj_tt[it-1]   # forward x adjoint, zero lag
    return grad * dt * ds
```

The loop is $\mathcal{O}(N_t)$ vector operations — negligible next to the two wave-equation solves that produced the fields.

### 4.2 Hessian-free optimization: L-BFGS and truncated Newton

The gradient alone gives steepest descent, which converges linearly and struggles with the severe ill-conditioning of FWI (model parameters at depth are orders of magnitude less constrained than near-surface ones). The Newton update $m_{k+1} = m_k - \mathbf{H}_k^{-1}\nabla\chi_k$ would correct this, but the Hessian $\mathbf{H}$ is a dense $N \times N$ matrix with $N \sim 10^8$ — it can never be formed, let alone inverted.

**L-BFGS** sidesteps the problem: it builds an implicit approximation to $\mathbf{H}^{-1}$ from the last $\ell$ gradient differences $\mathbf{y}_k = \nabla\chi_{k+1} - \nabla\chi_k$ and model steps $\mathbf{s}_k = m_{k+1} - m_k$, storing only $2\ell$ vectors [7]. The two-loop recursion applies it in $\mathcal{O}(\ell N)$ operations:

```
TLA+ sketch of the L-BFGS two-loop recursion
---- MODULE LBFGSTwoLoop ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
VARIABLES g, S, Y, rho, q, r, alpha
TwoLoop ==
    /\ q = g
    /\ \A i \in 1..Len(S) :                      \* backward pass
         LET k == Len(S) - i + 1
         IN  /\ alpha[k] = rho[k] * (S[k] \cdot q)
             /\ q = q - alpha[k] * Y[k]
    /\ r = H0 * q                               \* initial Hessian approx
    /\ \A i \in 1..Len(S) :                      \* forward pass
         /\ beta == rho[i] * (Y[i] \cdot r)
         /\ r = r + S[i] * (alpha[i] - beta)
    /\ r                                          \* = H_k^{-1} g
====
```

The bounded variant L-BFGS-B [7] enforces box constraints (e.g., $v_p \in [1500, 8000]\,\mathrm{m/s}$) and is the workhorse optimizer of production FWI codes: superlinear local convergence from gradient evaluations alone.

**Truncated Gauss–Newton–Krylov** methods go further: they solve $\mathbf{H}_k \Delta m = -\nabla\chi_k$ approximately with a few conjugate-gradient iterations, where each Hessian–vector product is computed matrix-free via *second-order adjoints* — one additional forward/adjoint pair per CG iteration [6]. Fichtner and Trampert (2011) showed that these second-order kernels decompose into physically interpretable parts: a "single-scattering" term resembling the gradient kernel and a "double-scattering" term encoding inter-parameter trade-offs [6]. The cost is steep — each CG iteration costs roughly one gradient evaluation — so in practice only 3–10 CG steps are taken, making the method a middle ground between L-BFGS and full Newton.

| Optimizer | Cost per iteration | Convergence rate | Memory | Hessian-free? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Steepest descent | ~2 wave solves | Linear (slow) | $\mathcal{O}(N)$ | Yes |
| Nonlinear CG | ~2 wave solves + line search | Linear (faster) | $\mathcal{O}(N)$ | Yes |
| L-BFGS($\ell$) | ~2 wave solves + line search | Superlinear (local) | $\mathcal{O}(\ell N)$ | Yes |
| Truncated Gauss–Newton–Krylov | ~2(1+$n_{cg}$) wave solves | Quadratic (local, inexact) | $\mathcal{O}(n_{cg} N)$ | Yes |
| Full Newton | Infeasible at scale | Quadratic | $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ | No |

### 4.3 Cycle-skipping: the fundamental pathology

The least-squares misfit between two oscillatory traces is periodic in the time shift: if the predicted arrival is delayed by more than half a period relative to the observed arrival, the gradient points *away* from the true model — the inversion "skips a cycle" and converges to a spurious minimum [3][5]. Bunks et al. (1995) demonstrated on the Marmousi model that naive single-scale inversion fails catastrophically, and introduced the remedy that remains standard: **multiscale continuation** — invert low frequencies first (where half a period is a large, forgiving time window) and progressively admit higher frequencies, using each stage's result as the next stage's starting model [5].

Modern mitigations attack the misfit functional itself rather than the frequency schedule:

- **Optimal-transport (Wasserstein) misfits**, convex with respect to time shifts, widen the basin of attraction dramatically.
- **Adaptive waveform inversion** matches traces with Wiener filters, then penalizes the filters' non-triviality.
- **Extended search-space methods**, which compute data-assimilated wavefields that fit observations even from inaccurate models and update parameters by annihilating the induced wave-equation errors, unified theoretically through inverse scattering theory [8].
- **Reflection-based FWI** and migration-velocity analysis use the *kinematic* information in reflections to update long wavelengths.
- **Time–frequency phase misfits** [4] separate phase from amplitude, gaining robustness to attenuation and source-estimation errors.

### 4.4 Imaging the deep Earth: from kernels to mantle plumes

At global scales the model is the three-dimensional elastic structure of the entire planet, and the data are broadband earthquake seismograms recorded by global networks. Adjoint tomography inverts for $v_p$, $v_s$, density, attenuation, and anisotropy simultaneously, with kernels computed by full 3-D spectral-element simulations through a 3-D starting model [2][4]. Landmark applications include:

- **Southern California crust** (Tape et al., 2009, 2010): iterative adjoint tomography resolving fault-zone structure at high resolution.
- **The Australasian upper mantle** (Fichtner et al., 2009): full-waveform tomography revealing radially anisotropic structure tied to tectonic history.
- **European upper-mantle models** incorporating adjoint kernels for both body and surface waves.

These images sharpened our view of subducting slabs — cold, fast anomalies descending into the lower mantle — and of large low-shear-velocity provinces at the core–mantle boundary, the likely roots of mantle plumes: the flagship demonstration that adjoint-based FWI scales from meters to megameters.

### 4.5 The memory wall and checkpointing

The gradient's cross-correlation needs the forward wavefield *at all time steps simultaneously* with the backward-propagating adjoint field — for a global simulation with $10^9$ grid points and $10^4$ time steps, storing the full space-time history requires petabytes. Three remedies coexist: **full storage** (small scales only), **optimal checkpointing** (Revolve: store a logarithmic subset of slices and recompute the rest), and **boundary-wavefield reconstruction** (store boundaries only and re-solve backward, exact for lossless media). The adjoint method's theoretical elegance is paid for in engineering.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The adjoint identity (proof sketch)

**Claim.** Let $\chi(m) = \frac{1}{2}\lVert \mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}(m) - \mathbf{d} \rVert^2$ with $\mathbf{A}(m)\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f}$. Then $\nabla_m \chi$ is obtained from one forward and one adjoint solve.

*Proof.* Differentiate the constraint: $\frac{\partial \mathbf{A}}{\partial m}\delta m \, \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{A}(m)\,\delta\mathbf{u} = 0$, hence $\delta\mathbf{u} = -\mathbf{A}^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial m}\mathbf{u}\,\delta m$. The misfit perturbation is $\delta\chi = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{d})^T \mathbf{R}\,\delta\mathbf{u} = -\boldsymbol{\lambda}^T \frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial m}\mathbf{u}\,\delta m$, where $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ solves $\mathbf{A}^T\boldsymbol{\lambda} = -\mathbf{R}^T(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{d})$. Since $\delta m$ is arbitrary, $\nabla_m\chi = -\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial m}\mathbf{u}\right)^T\boldsymbol{\lambda}$. ∎

Note the proof never forms the Jacobian $\partial\mathbf{u}/\partial m$ — that is precisely the point.

### 5.2 Empirical convergence behavior

On synthetic benchmarks (Marmousi, SEG/EAGE salt, BP 2004), the canonical observations are:

- Steepest descent reduces the misfit by roughly one order of magnitude per 20–50 iterations; L-BFGS achieves the same in 5–15 iterations with $\ell \in [5, 20]$ [3][7].
- Truncated Newton with 5 CG iterations roughly halves the iteration count of L-BFGS at double the per-iteration cost — a wash in wall-clock time on well-conditioned problems [6].
- Multiscale frequency continuation (e.g., 3 → 5 → 8 → 12 Hz) separates convergence to the global basin from catastrophic cycle-skipping on the Marmousi model [5].
- Real-data 3-D marine FWI (Valhall) achieved near-medical-tomography resolution, revealing gas anomalies and faults invisible to travel-time tomography.

### 5.3 A Haskell-flavored view of the misfit landscape

The non-convexity can be characterized functionally:

```haskell
-- The FWI problem as a composition of maps
type Model = Field3D Double
type Data  = [Trace]

forward   :: Model -> Data            -- wave-equation solve + sampling
residual  :: Data -> Data -> Data     -- syn - obs
misfit    :: Data -> Double           -- 1/2 ||.||^2

chi :: Model -> Double
chi = misfit . residual obs . forward

-- Adjoint gradient: pull back the residual through the linearized forward map
gradChi :: Model -> Model
gradChi m = adjointJT m (residual obs (forward m))
  where adjointJT = transpose . jacobian forward  -- never formed explicitly
```

The comment on the last line is the whole thesis of this article in code.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Cycle-skipping and the starting-model problem.** FWI is a *local* optimizer. Without low frequencies, an accurate starting model, or a convexified misfit, it converges to geologically plausible but wrong models. This remains the dominant failure mode in practice [3][5].
2. **Non-uniqueness and null space.** Surface data cannot constrain deep structure independently of shallow structure; attenuation–velocity and density–velocity trade-offs are severe, and regularization selects among equivalent models at the cost of bias.
3. **Computational cost.** A single 3-D elastic iteration at global scale consumes tens of thousands of core-hours; production inversions require leadership-class HPC. The adjoint method minimizes the *number* of simulations, not their individual cost.
4. **Physics errors.** Real Earth is anelastic and anisotropic; every unmodeled effect maps into spurious structure, and source-wavelet uncertainty alone can dominate the residual budget.
5. **Multi-parameter cross-talk.** Inverting jointly for $v_p$, $v_s$, and density, the gradient for one parameter leaks into others (parameter cross-talk). Hessian-based preconditioning mitigates but does not eliminate it [6].
6. **Acquisition limitations.** Missing low frequencies, limited aperture, and sparse receivers bound the recoverable wavenumber spectrum regardless of algorithmic sophistication — a hard information-theoretic ceiling.

## 7 Conclusion

Seismic full-waveform inversion stands as one of the great computational achievements of the Earth sciences: a PDE-constrained optimization problem with up to a billion parameters, solved routinely through the elegant device of the adjoint state. Tarantola's 1984 insight — that the gradient is a cross-correlation of forward and adjoint wavefields [1] — eliminated the Jacobian bottleneck; Tromp, Tape and Liu's synthesis with time reversal and finite-frequency kernels [2] carried the method to global seismology; and the modern Hessian-free optimization arsenal [6][7] extracts superlinear convergence without ever forming a Hessian.

The frontier has shifted from *whether* FWI works to *what it can be trusted to resolve*: uncertainty quantification via the inverse Hessian, cross-talk-free multi-parameter inversion, and cycle-skip-immune misfits. As exascale computing and dense nodal arrays flood the field with data, the adjoint-state framework — two simulations per gradient, independent of parameter count — remains the load-bearing pillar.
