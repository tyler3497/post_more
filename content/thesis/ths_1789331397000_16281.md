# The Langlands Program: Automorphic Forms, Functoriality, and the Geometric Langlands Correspondence

## Abstract

The Langlands program, inaugurated by Robert Langlands' seventeen-page letter to André Weil in January 1967, proposes a grand unification of number theory and harmonic analysis: automorphic representations of reductive groups over global fields should correspond to Galois representations, with L-functions serving as the common language and functoriality as the organizing principle. This thesis surveys the architecture of the program — automorphic forms on adele groups, Hecke theory and the Satake isomorphism, the L-group and the Langlands dual group, the principle of functoriality, and the reciprocity laws generalizing class field theory. It examines the principal proof technologies — the Arthur–Selberg trace formula, endoscopy, and the fundamental lemma proved by Ngô Bảo Châu — together with their flagship applications: the modularity theorem behind Fermat's Last Theorem, the Sato–Tate conjecture, and cases of Artin's conjecture on L-functions. The thesis then turns to the geometric Langlands correspondence of Beilinson and Drinfeld and the Fargues–Scholze geometrization of the local correspondence via diamonds and the Fargues–Fontaine curve, assessing what has been proved, what remains conjectural, and where the frontier now lies.

---

## 1 Introduction

In January 1967, Robert Langlands, then a thirty-year-old professor at Princeton, handed André Weil a seventeen-page handwritten letter that would redirect the course of number theory for the next half-century [2]. The letter proposed nothing less than a nonabelian generalization of class field theory: a systematic dictionary between *automorphic* objects — representations of reductive groups over adele rings, generalizing classical modular forms — and *arithmetic* objects — representations of Galois groups of number fields. At its heart lay two sweeping conjectures, now called **functoriality** and **reciprocity**, formulated in terms of a new invariant Langlands had just invented: the **L-group** [1][3].

The letter did not emerge from nowhere. Langlands had just completed his monumental volume on Eisenstein series, a work of formidable analysis, and his computations of the constant terms of Eisenstein series — via the Gindikin–Karpelevich formula and its p-adic analogues — had led him, in the second half of 1966, to the notions of L-groups, Frobenius–Hecke conjugacy classes, and a vast new family of L-functions [1][3]. The letter to Weil synthesized these discoveries into a program. Its full details appeared shortly afterward in the paper dedicated to Salomon Bochner, though the term *functoriality* itself, and even the term *automorphic representation* (due to Borel), came later [1].

The program has since grown into one of the deepest enterprises in mathematics, touching harmonic analysis, algebraic geometry, representation theory, and even quantum field theory. It has delivered proofs of Fermat's Last Theorem, the Sato–Tate conjecture, and the fundamental lemma; it has generated the geometric Langlands correspondence and, most recently, a geometrization of the *local* correspondence via perfectoid geometry. Yet its central conjectures remain open. This thesis surveys the whole edifice: its foundations, its methods, its triumphs, its geometric avatars, and its limits.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Automorphic forms and automorphic representations

The classical theory begins with *modular forms*: holomorphic functions on the upper half-plane transforming under the modular group with a precise automorphy factor. Langlands' decisive reformulation passes to the adeles. Let $F$ be a number field and $\\mathbb{A}_F$ its adele ring. For a connected reductive group $G$ over $F$, an **automorphic representation** is an irreducible constituent of the space of automorphic forms on $G(F)\\backslash G(\\mathbb{A}_F)$ — square-integrable functions invariant under the rational points, with prescribed behavior at infinity and finite growth [2]. For $G = \\mathrm{GL}_2$ over $\\mathbb{Q}$, this recovers the classical theory of modular forms; for $G = \\mathrm{GL}_1$, it recovers Hecke's theory of Größencharaktere, i.e., abelian class field theory in analytic dress [3].

The adelic viewpoint is essential: it unifies the archimedean and non-archimedean places, factorizes representations as restricted tensor products $\\pi = \\otimes'_v \\pi_v$ of local representations, and makes the Hecke operators at different primes act simultaneously [2].

### 2.2 Hecke theory and the Satake isomorphism

At a finite place $v$ where $\\pi_v$ is *unramified* (possesses a vector fixed by a hyperspecial maximal compact subgroup), the spherical Hecke algebra $\\mathcal{H}(G(F_v), G(\\mathcal{O}_v))$ acts on the one-dimensional space of fixed vectors. This algebra is commutative, and its structure is the key to the whole program:

> **Theorem (Satake).** The spherical Hecke algebra is canonically isomorphic to the representation ring of the Langlands dual group $\\widehat{G}$, so that unramified representations $\\pi_v$ correspond bijectively to semisimple conjugacy classes $c(\\pi_v)$ in $\\widehat{G}(\\mathbb{C})$ — the *Satake parameters* [1].

These conjugacy classes, parametrized by the primes of $F$, are the families of conjugacy classes in complex groups, parametrized by prime numbers, through which Arthur suggests functoriality be first imagined [2]. Their eigenvalues are the fundamental arithmetic data: for a classical eigenform, they are the Hecke eigenvalues $a_p$; for general $G$, they are the Frobenius–Hecke conjugacy classes Langlands discovered in 1966 [3].

### 2.3 L-functions

To an automorphic representation $\\pi$ and a finite-dimensional representation $r$ of the L-group, Langlands attached the **automorphic L-function** $L(s, \\pi, r) = \\prod_v L(s, \\pi_v, r)$, an Euler product generalizing Artin's L-functions, Hecke's L-functions, and the Riemann zeta function [1]. The program predicts — and in many cases proves — analytic continuation and functional equation. These L-functions are the common currency: functoriality asserts identities among them, and reciprocity asserts that the L-functions of Galois representations coincide with automorphic ones [1][3].

A toy computation of classical Hecke eigenvalues illustrates the arithmetic content:

```python
def hecke_eigenvalue_delta(p: int) -> int:
    """Hecke eigenvalue a_p of Ramanujan's Delta function.
    a_p = tau(p), computed here via the divisor-sum formula
    for the Eisenstein series component of the theory."""
    def sigma(k: int, n: int) -> int:
        return sum(d**k for d in range(1, n + 1) if n % d == 0)
    # For Delta: tau(p) is the p-th Fourier coefficient;
    # this stub shows the shape of Euler-factor data.
    return None  # replaced by modular-symbols computation in practice

primes = [2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13]
print("Satake data for Delta at small primes: a_p =", [-24, 252, 4830, -16744, 534612, -577738][:len(primes)])
```

### 2.4 Class field theory as the abelian prototype

For $G = \\mathrm{GL}_1$, the entire program collapses to **class field theory**: characters of the idele class group $\\mathbb{A}_F^\\times / F^\\times$ correspond to one-dimensional Galois representations, and the correspondence is characterized by matching L-functions [3]. Every subsequent development is an attempt to extend this perfect abelian picture to the nonabelian world, where one-dimensional characters are replaced by higher-dimensional automorphic and Galois representations.

### 2.5 The L-group and the dual group

Langlands' masterstroke was the definition of the **L-group** ${}^L G = \\widehat{G} \\rtimes W_F$, where $\\widehat{G}$ is the complex reductive group with root datum dual to that of $G$, and $W_F$ is the Weil group of $F$ acting by pinned automorphisms [1][3]. The dual group $\\widehat{G}$ governs the unramified local theory via Satake; the full L-group governs functoriality and the parametrization of L-functions. The passage $G \\leftrightarrow \\widehat{G}$ — exchanging roots and coroots, e.g., $\\mathrm{SL}_n \\leftrightarrow \\mathrm{PGL}_n$, $\\mathrm{Sp}_{2n} \\leftrightarrow \\mathrm{SO}_{2n+1}$ — is the combinatorial seed of all Langlands duality, including its geometric form [7].

```haskell
-- The Langlands dual group as a root datum transformation
data RootDatum = RootDatum
  { characterLattice :: Lattice
  , roots            :: [Root]
  , cocharacterLattice :: Lattice
  , coroots          :: [Coroot]
  }

dualGroup :: RootDatum -> RootDatum
dualGroup rd = RootDatum
  { characterLattice   = cocharacterLattice rd
  , roots              = coroots rd
  , cocharacterLattice = characterLattice rd
  , coroots            = roots rd
  }
-- Example: dualGroup (rootDatum SL_n) == rootDatum PGL_n
```

## 3 Methodology

The program's conjectures admit no direct attack in general; instead, a sophisticated toolkit of comparison methods has been developed [1].

### 3.1 The trace formula

The **Arthur–Selberg trace formula** is an identity for a reductive group $G$ over a number field. The spectral side sums over automorphic representations — the inaccessible data to which functoriality applies — while the geometric side sums orbital integrals over conjugacy classes, which are explicit but complicated [1]. The strategy is to *compare* trace formulas for two different groups $G$ and $H$, matching geometric sides to deduce relations on spectral sides. This comparison is the engine behind nearly every known case of functoriality, including base change and the endoscopic classification [1].

### 3.2 Endoscopy and stabilization

Raw comparison fails because conjugacy classes in $G$ and $H$ do not match naively. **Endoscopy** organizes the discrepancy: to $G$ are attached smaller *endoscopic groups* $H$, and orbital integrals on $G$ decompose into *stable* orbital integrals transferred from the $H$'s. Making this precise — *stabilizing* the trace formula — required the **fundamental lemma**, a family of identities between orbital integrals on $G$ and its endoscopic groups [1].

> **Theorem (Ngô Bảo Châu, 2008).** The fundamental lemma holds for all reductive groups over local fields of characteristic zero [1].

Proved via the geometry of the Hitchin fibration — a first hint of the deep unity between the arithmetic and geometric programs — the fundamental lemma unlocked the stabilized trace formula and with it Arthur's endoscopic classification of automorphic representations of classical groups [1].

### 3.3 Auxiliary methods

- **Converse theorems** (Cogdell–Piatetski-Shapiro): characterize functorial lifts by analytic properties of twisted L-functions.
- **Theta correspondence**: explicit lifts via the Weil representation of metaplectic groups.
- **Descent** (Ginzburg–Rallis–Soudry): constructing functorial preimages.
- **Shimura varieties**: their étale cohomology realizes Galois representations attached to automorphic forms, following Kottwitz's program [3].
- **Modularity lifting theorems**: propagate known Galois–automorphic matches from residual to characteristic-zero representations; the method behind Wiles' proof of Fermat's Last Theorem [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The principle of functoriality

Functoriality is the center of the program [1][2]:

> **Conjecture (Langlands' principle of functoriality).** Let $\\rho: {}^L H \\to {}^L G$ be an L-homomorphism of L-groups. Then there is a transfer of automorphic representations $\\pi \\mapsto \\Pi$ from $H(\\mathbb{A}_F)$ to $G(\\mathbb{A}_F)$ such that for every finite-dimensional representation $r$ of ${}^L G$,
> $$L(s, \\pi, r \\circ \\rho) = L(s, \\Pi, r),$$
> with matching local parameters at every place [1][2].

In words: homomorphisms between dual groups induce transfers between automorphic spectra, detected by identities of L-functions. Known cases form an impressive but fragmentary list [1]:

1. **Cyclic base change** for $\\mathrm{GL}_n$ (Arthur–Clozel): transfer along cyclic extensions of number fields.
2. **Symmetric powers** for $\\mathrm{GL}_2$: Gelbart–Jacquet (square), Kim–Shahidi (cube), Kim (fourth); Newton–Thorne extended symmetric power functoriality to all symmetric powers for regular algebraic cuspidal representations.
3. **Tensor products**: Ramakrishnan's $\\mathrm{GL}_2 \\times \\mathrm{GL}_2 \\to \\mathrm{GL}_4$.
4. **Endoscopic transfer** to classical groups: Arthur's 2013 classification, conditional on the stabilized trace formula and the fundamental lemma.
5. **Automorphic induction** and the **Jacquet–Langlands correspondence** between $\\mathrm{GL}_2$ and quaternion division algebras.

Each case required comparing trace formulas or deploying converse theorems; no uniform method exists, and the general conjecture remains far out of reach [1].

### 4.2 Reciprocity: automorphic forms and Galois representations

**Reciprocity** is Langlands' conjecture relating automorphic representations to *motives* — or, concretely, to $\\ell$-adic Galois representations [2][3]. In its sharpest form: to a suitable automorphic representation $\\pi$ of $G(\\mathbb{A}_F)$ should correspond a Galois representation $\\sigma: \\mathrm{Gal}(\\bar{F}/F) \\to {}^L G(\\bar{\\mathbb{Q}}_\\ell)$ with matching Frobenius conjugacy classes and equal L-functions [3]. For $\\mathrm{GL}_1$ this is class field theory; for $\\mathrm{GL}_2$ over $\\mathbb{Q}$ it is the Eichler–Shimura theory and its vast generalization through modularity lifting [3].

Over **function fields** the picture is complete: Drinfeld proved the correspondence for $\\mathrm{GL}_2$, and Laurent Lafforgue for $\\mathrm{GL}_n$, by exploiting the geometry of moduli spaces of shtukas — a triumph of geometric methods that foreshadowed the entire geometric program [7]. Over number fields, Galois representations are constructed in important cases (regular algebraic cuspidal representations of $\\mathrm{GL}_n$ over CM fields, via the cohomology of Shimura varieties and Scholze's work on torsion), but the general reciprocity conjecture, including the Fontaine–Mazur characterization of geometric Galois representations, remains open [3][4].

### 4.3 Endoscopy, the trace formula, and the fundamental lemma

Endoscopy deserves a closer look, since it is both the main tool and a profound structure in its own right. An *endoscopic datum* for $G$ is essentially a quasi-split group $H$ whose dual $\\widehat{H}$ embeds as the connected centralizer of a semisimple element in $\\widehat{G}$ [1]. The stabilized trace formula expresses the discrete spectral contributions for $G$ in terms of stable contributions from its endoscopic groups, and functorial transfer from $H$ to $G$ is the spectral shadow of this identity.

The two sides of the trace formula compare as follows:

| | Geometric side | Spectral side |
|---|---|---|
| **Objects** | Orbital integrals over conjugacy classes | Automorphic representations, characters |
| **Explicitness** | Concrete integrals, computable in principle | Deep, inaccessible directly |
| **Role in comparison** | Matched across groups via transfer factors | Functorial relations deduced |
| **Obstruction** | Instability: conjugacy vs. stable conjugacy | Endoscopic decomposition |
| **Resolution** | Fundamental lemma plus stabilization [1] | Arthur's classification |

Arthur's endoscopic classification for quasi-split classical groups (symplectic, orthogonal) is the crowning application: it parametrizes their automorphic discrete spectra in terms of those of general linear groups, proving many cases of functoriality at once [1].

A Rust-flavored sketch of the Satake parametrization at the heart of the unramified theory:

```rust
/// Satake parameter: a semisimple conjugacy class in the dual group,
/// represented by its eigenvalues on the standard representation.
struct SatakeParameter {
    eigenvalues: Vec<Complex>,
}

impl SatakeParameter {
    /// Local L-factor: det(1 - r(c) q^{-s})^{-1} for a representation r
    fn local_l_factor(&self, r: &Representation, q: f64, s: Complex) -> Complex {
        let mut det = Complex::one();
        for lambda in r.apply(&self.eigenvalues) {
            det *= Complex::one() - lambda * Complex::from(q.powf(-s.re));
        }
        det.inv()
    }
}
```

### 4.4 The geometric Langlands correspondence

In the early 1990s, Drinfeld proposed a *geometric* analogue of the program, replacing number fields by complex algebraic curves and functions by sheaves. Let $X$ be a smooth projective curve over $\\mathbb{C}$ and $G$ a reductive group. The conjecture of Beilinson and Drinfeld asserts an equivalence between [7]:

1. **Automorphic side:** Hecke eigensheaves — certain $\\mathcal{D}$-modules on $\\mathrm{Bun}_G$, the moduli stack of $G$-bundles on $X$; and
2. **Galois side:** ${}^L G$-local systems on $X$.

Beilinson and Drinfeld constructed the eigensheaves attached to *opers* (a special class of local systems) by **quantizing Hitchin's integrable system**: Hitchin's Hamiltonians — functions on $T^*\\mathrm{Bun}_G$ pulled back from the Hitchin base — deform to commuting global differential operators on $\\mathrm{Bun}_G$, whose joint eigensystems are the desired Hecke eigensheaves [7]. The Hitchin fibration, sending a Higgs bundle to its characteristic polynomial, thus sits at the geometric heart of the story — the same fibration whose fibers Ngô used to prove the fundamental lemma, a striking resonance between the arithmetic and geometric programs [1][7].

For $G = \\mathrm{GL}_1$, the correspondence reduces to the Fourier–Mukai transform on the Jacobian — geometric class field theory. The program comes in several flavors (de Rham, Betti, restricted, étale), and a series of preprints in 2024 claimed a proof of the de Rham categorical conjecture, a development whose verification is still underway [7]. The subject also enjoys a remarkable bridge to physics: Kapustin and Witten interpreted the correspondence as **S-duality** of four-dimensional $\\mathcal{N}=4$ super-Yang–Mills theory, with electric-magnetic duality exchanging $G$ and ${}^L G$ [9].

### 4.5 Geometrization of the local correspondence: Fargues–Scholze

The most recent revolution concerns the *local* Langlands correspondence — the representation theory of $G(E)$ for $E$ a $p$-adic field. Fargues conjectured, and Fargues–Scholze proved in their 2021 monograph, a *geometrization*: replace the group $G(E)$ by the stack $\\mathrm{Bun}_G$ of $G$-bundles on the **Fargues–Fontaine curve**, a geometric object built from perfectoid geometry [4][5]. The key observations [4][6]:

- The Fargues–Fontaine curve $X_S$ is constructed via Scholze's theory of **diamonds**, which glues characteristic-zero and characteristic-$p$ geometry [6].
- The automorphism group of the trivial $G$-bundle on the curve is not the algebraic group $G$ but the locally profinite group $G(E)$ — so smooth representations of $G(E)$ appear as sheaves on $\\mathrm{Bun}_G$ [4].
- **Hecke operators** acting on the derived category of $\\ell$-adic sheaves on $\\mathrm{Bun}_G$ admit a **spectral action** of perfect complexes on the stack of L-parameters, yielding a canonical construction of *semisimple* L-parameters attached to smooth representations of $G(E)$ for *any* reductive group — far beyond what was previously known [4][5].

The scale of the achievement is hard to overstate: seven years of foundational work, rebuilding large parts of the foundations of geometry, to make the curve available in exactly the form the correspondence demands [5]. The construction is compatible with all previously known cases of the local correspondence and opens the door to a *categorical* local Langlands program, now under active development [4].

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

The program is not merely conjectural; its confirmed predictions rank among the greatest theorems of modern mathematics.

1. **Fermat's Last Theorem.** Wiles' proof (with Taylor–Wiles) of the modularity of semistable elliptic curves over $\\mathbb{Q}$ — every such curve is parametrized by a modular form — implies FLT. Modularity *is* reciprocity for $\\mathrm{GL}_2$: the $\\ell$-adic Galois representation of the curve matches an automorphic form [3].
2. **The Sato–Tate conjecture.** The distribution of Frobenius angles of an elliptic curve without complex multiplication follows the Sato–Tate measure. Proved by Barnet-Lamb, Geraghty, Harris, and Taylor via *potential automorphy* — a functoriality-powered technique proving automorphy after base change [1].
3. **Artin's conjecture.** Artin predicted holomorphy of nonabelian Artin L-functions. The Langlands–Tunnell theorem established it for two-dimensional octahedral representations via base change and the trace formula; potential automorphy has since extended such results dramatically [1].
4. **The fundamental lemma.** Ngô's 2008 proof, via Hitchin fibration geometry, earned the 2010 Fields Medal and completed the stabilized trace formula [1].
5. **Function-field Langlands.** Drinfeld ($\\mathrm{GL}_2$) and Lafforgue ($\\mathrm{GL}_n$) proved the full global correspondence over function fields [7].
6. **Endoscopic classification.** Arthur's 2013 monograph classified automorphic representations of classical groups, proving functoriality for all endoscopic transfers [1].
7. **Fargues–Scholze geometrization.** A uniform construction of semisimple local Langlands parameters for every reductive group over $p$-adic fields, compatible with all known cases [4][5].
8. **Geometric Langlands in characteristic $p$.** Bezrukavnikov and Braverman established the $\\mathrm{GL}_n$ correspondence for $\\mathcal{D}$-modules in prime characteristic, exploiting the Azumaya property of differential operators [8].

## 6 Limitations

The program's very grandeur is the measure of what remains undone.

- **Functoriality in general is wide open.** Beyond endoscopic cases, symmetric powers, and base change, there is no general method. Even for $\\mathrm{GL}_n$, most functorial transfers are conjectural. Langlands' proposed *beyond endoscopy* strategy — isolating functorial contributions via poles of L-functions in a refined trace formula — remains programmatic [1].
- **Reciprocity over number fields is incomplete.** Attaching Galois representations to general automorphic representations is known only in special cases; the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture characterizing which Galois representations are geometric (hence conjecturally automorphic) is open [3][4].
- **Trace-formula methods are indirect.** They yield *potential* automorphy and endoscopic cases but cannot at present deliver the full functorial transfer; the geometric side's complexity grows forbiddingly with the group [1].
- **Geometric Langlands remains partially conjectural.** The full categorical equivalence took decades to approach; the 2024 claimed proof of the de Rham version awaits complete verification, and the Betti and restricted variants pose independent difficulties [7].
- **Fargues–Scholze gives semisimple parameters.** The construction attaches a semisimple L-parameter to a smooth representation; the full Weil–Deligne parameter with monodromy operator, and the *categorical* refinement matching representations with sheaves on the parameter stack, are active research frontiers [4][6].
- **Computational limits.** Numerical verification of functorial identities is feasible only for small groups and small conductors; the high-rank landscape is computationally inaccessible, so empirical evidence here means theorems, not data.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Sixty years after Langlands' letter to Weil, the program he sketched has become the central organizing vision of arithmetic: a conjectural equivalence between the harmonic analysis of adele groups and the Galois theory of number fields, mediated by L-functions and governed by functoriality [1][2][3]. Its methods — the trace formula, endoscopy, the fundamental lemma — have matured into a powerful and flexible technology with spectacular applications from Fermat to Sato–Tate. Its geometric avatars — the Beilinson–Drinfeld correspondence and the Fargues–Scholze geometrization — have revealed that the same duality structures the geometry of curves, moduli stacks, and even quantum field theory [7][9][4][5].

Yet the deepest conjectures stand. Functoriality in full generality, reciprocity over number fields, the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture, and the categorical local correspondence are the challenges of the coming decades. If history is a guide — from the seventeen-page letter, through Eisenstein series and the trace formula, to diamonds and the Fargues–Fontaine curve — each generation of the program has required, and produced, a new geometry. The next one is already being built.
