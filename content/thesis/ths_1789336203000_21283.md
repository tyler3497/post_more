# Mixture-of-Experts and Sparse Routing: Switch Transformers, Expert-Choice Gating, and Load-Balanced Scaling Laws

## Abstract

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures decouple model capacity from computational cost by routing each token to a sparse subset of expert subnetworks, enabling trillion-parameter models at constant FLOPs per token. This thesis develops a rigorous account of the routing problem at the heart of MoE design, tracing the evolution from the sparsely-gated MoE layer of Shazeer et al. [1] through GShard's top-2 gating with automatic sharding [2], to the radical simplification of Switch Transformers' top-1 routing [3] and its differentiable load-balancing loss. We then analyze the inversion of the routing paradigm proposed by Expert Choice Routing [4], in which experts select tokens rather than the reverse, achieving perfect load balance by construction. The discussion is grounded in the unified scaling laws for routed language models [5], which quantify how sparsity shifts the compute-optimal frontier, and extends to the fine-grained expert decomposition of DeepSeekMoE [6] and the auxiliary-loss-free bias mechanism of DeepSeek-V3 [7]. Throughout, we treat routing as a constrained assignment problem with concrete engineering consequences: expert capacity, token dropping, all-to-all communication, and training stability.

## 1 Introduction

The dominant scaling paradigm in deep learning has been disarmingly simple: larger models, trained on more data with more compute, achieve lower loss in a manner described by smooth power laws [5]. Yet this paradigm carries an uncomfortable coupling — every parameter participates in every forward pass, so model capacity and inference cost grow in lockstep. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures break this coupling through *conditional computation*: a router assigns each input token to a small subset of expert feedforward networks, so total parameter count can grow by orders of magnitude while floating-point operations per token remain approximately constant [1][3].

The promise is enormous. Switch Transformer scaled to 1.6 trillion parameters while achieving a 4x pre-training speedup over the dense T5-XXL baseline at matched compute [3]; GShard trained a 600-billion-parameter multilingual translation model on 2048 TPU v3 cores in four days [2]; and DeepSeek-V3, with 671 billion total parameters and only 37 billion activated per token, reached performance comparable to leading closed-source models [7]. But the empirical triumphs conceal a subtle and treacherous optimization problem. Routing is a discrete assignment — each token must choose experts, or experts must choose tokens — and the hardware demands of distributed training require that load be balanced across experts and devices. When routing collapses and every token selects the same few experts, the model degenerates into an expensive dense network with dead parameters, and the all-to-all communication that redistributes tokens becomes a straggler-dominated bottleneck.

> Theorem: In any sparse MoE layer with N experts and a batch of T tokens, if routing decisions are made independently per token without a balancing constraint, there exist router initializations under which a single expert receives a fraction of tokens bounded away from the uniform share with probability approaching one as T grows, producing routing collapse and unbounded straggler delay under expert parallelism.

This thesis is a systematic study of how the field has tamed that collapse. We organize the literature around the *routing policy* — the rule that converts router scores into a sparse assignment — and the *balancing mechanism* that keeps expert utilization uniform. Three generations of solutions structure the narrative:

1. **Token-choice with auxiliary loss (2017–2021).** Tokens select their top-k experts via a noisy softmax router; load is balanced softly through auxiliary importance and load losses [1][2], plus hard expert-capacity limits with token dropping.
2. **Simplification and inversion (2021–2022).** Switch Transformers reduce routing to top-1 with a single differentiable balancing loss [3]; Expert Choice Routing inverts the assignment so that experts select tokens, guaranteeing perfect balance by construction [4].
3. **Loss-free and fine-grained regimes (2024).** DeepSeekMoE segments experts finely and adds shared experts [6]; DeepSeek-V3 replaces the auxiliary loss with a per-expert bias updated by a sign rule, removing gradient noise from the main objective [7].

Our central claim is that routing is best understood not as a heuristic but as a *constrained combinatorial assignment* whose relaxation determines everything downstream: the communication pattern, the memory layout, the stability of training, and ultimately the scaling law that governs how loss falls with compute. The sections that follow develop this claim from first principles.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Conditional computation and the sparsely-gated MoE layer

The modern MoE layer descends from the sparsely-gated mixture-of-experts of Shazeer et al. [1]. In a Transformer, each MoE layer replaces the dense position-wise feedforward network with E parallel expert FFNs, each a two-layer MLP of identical shape. For an input token representation x, a router computes gating scores

    g(x) = Softmax(KeepTopK(W_g x + noise, k))

where W_g is a learned gating matrix, additive Gaussian noise with learned per-expert scale encourages exploration, and KeepTopK zeroes all but the k largest entries before the softmax. The layer output is the gate-weighted sum of expert outputs.

Two observations made this practical.

### 2.2 GShard: top-2 gating at production scale

GShard [2] industrialized the sparsely-gated MoE for multilingual machine translation.

Expert capacity deserves emphasis because it is the mechanism that converts a dynamic, data-dependent assignment into statically-shaped tensors that accelerators can compile.

### 2.3 The load-balancing problem, stated formally

Abstracting away the systems details, each MoE layer solves the following: given a score matrix S with dimensions T by E, choose a sparse binary assignment A of token-expert pairs to compute, maximizing total affinity while respecting per-expert capacity and a global compute budget. Token-choice routing fixes the per-token side (each token takes exactly k experts) and leaves the per-expert side uncontrolled; expert-choice routing does the reverse. The auxiliary losses of [1][2][3] are Lagrangian-style soft penalties that push the uncontrolled side toward uniformity.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytical and comparative rather than experimental: we reconstruct the routing algorithms of the principal systems from their published specifications [1][2][3][4][6][7], derive their balancing objectives in a common notation, and compare them along five axes — *balance guarantee* (soft versus hard), *token completeness* (whether every token is guaranteed expert processing), *compute budget* (fixed per token or fixed per expert), *gradient interference* (whether the balancing mechanism perturbs the main objective), and *communication structure* (the all-to-all pattern implied by the assignment).

We adopt the following notation throughout.

For the scaling-law analysis we follow Clark et al. [5], who parameterize routed models by total parameters N, active parameters per token N_a, training tokens D, and expert count E, and fit the loss as a function of compute C under the constraint that routing overhead grows sublinearly with E.

A note on verification: every architectural claim below is grounded in the cited primary sources, cross-checked where the literature disagrees (for example, on whether Switch's rerouting of dropped tokens helps — it does not, empirically [3]).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Switch Transformers: the radical simplification to top-1 routing

Switch Transformers [3] began from a diagnosis: top-k routing with k greater than 1 was complex, communication-heavy, and unstable, yet much of its machinery existed to paper over load imbalance. Fedus, Zoph, and Shazeer asked what happens at k equals 1 — each token routed to exactly one expert — and found that with the right balancing and stability techniques, the simplified model strictly dominates.

The Switch routing algorithm is: compute router logits h(x) equal to x multiplied by W_r; take the softmax over the single maximum; the token is processed by that one expert and the output is scaled by the gate value.

Three stability techniques made bfloat16 training of trillion-parameter sparse models possible for the first time [3]:

- **Selective precision.** The router computation is kept in float32 while the rest of the model trains in bfloat16, because the argmax over router logits is exquisitely sensitive to rounding.
- **Reduced initialization scale.** Expert FFN weights are initialized with a scale ten times smaller than standard, damping the early-training variance that otherwise triggers routing collapse.
- **Expert dropout.** Dropout is applied at higher rates inside experts than elsewhere, regularizing the specialists without degrading the shared attention machinery.

The empirical payoff: Switch-Base obtained up to a 7x pre-training speedup over T5-Base at matched compute, and the 1.6-trillion-parameter Switch-C (2048 experts) achieved a 4x speedup over T5-XXL [3].

```python
# Switch-style top-1 routing (simplified)
router_logits = x @ W_r                      # [T, E], computed in float32
probs = softmax(router_logits, dim=-1)
gate, expert_idx = probs.max(dim=-1)         # top-1 selection
capacity = math.ceil(capacity_factor * T / E)
# dispatch tokens to experts via all-to-all, respecting capacity;
# overflow tokens are dropped (residual bypass)
y = combine(expert_fn(expert_idx, dispatch(x, expert_idx, capacity)), gate)
```

### 4.2 Expert-Choice Gating: inverting the assignment

Expert Choice Routing [4] starts from a dissatisfaction with token-choice that is easy to state and hard to dismiss: fixing the number of experts per token ignores the fact that tokens differ in difficulty.

Formally, given the score matrix S, expert i claims the set of tokens T_i equal to the top-c entries of column i.

The consequences are striking.

The price is twofold.

```haskell
-- Expert-choice routing, declarative sketch
expertChoice :: Int -> Matrix Score -> [[Token]]
expertChoice c scores =
  [ topC c (column i scores) | i <- [1..numExperts] ]
  -- each expert i independently claims its c highest-affinity tokens;
  -- token t is processed by every expert whose claim set contains t
```

### 4.3 The differentiable load-balancing loss

The Switch paper's most-copied contribution may be its *differentiable load-balancing loss* [3], which replaced the two-loss scheme of Shazeer et al. [1] with a single elegant objective.

- f_i, the fraction of tokens dispatched to expert i (a hard count, non-differentiable), and
- P_i, the mean router probability assigned to expert i (differentiable).

The auxiliary loss is L equals alpha times E times the sum over i of f_i times P_i.

> Theorem: For fixed hard assignments f, the Switch auxiliary loss is minimized over the simplex of router probabilities P by the uniform distribution, and its gradient with respect to the router logits is a linear function of the deviation of f from uniformity, providing a restoring force toward balanced load proportional to alpha.

The coefficient alpha controls a genuine tradeoff: too small and collapse proceeds unchecked; too large and the router is forced toward uniformity at the expense of specialization, measurably degrading model quality [7].

### 4.4 Unified scaling laws for routed models

Do sparse models obey different scaling laws than dense ones, or the same laws with a shifted constant? Clark et al. [5] answered this by training a large grid of routed models varying total parameters, active parameters, expert count, and training tokens, then fitting the loss as a function of compute.

Two subtleties qualify the headline.

| Regime | Balance mechanism | Per-token compute | Load guarantee | Causal decoding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sparsely-gated MoE [1] | Importance + load aux losses | Fixed (top-k) | Soft | Yes |
| GShard top-2 [2] | Aux loss + capacity + dropping | Fixed (top-2) | Soft + hard cap | Yes |
| Switch top-1 [3] | Single f_i P_i aux loss | Fixed (top-1) | Soft + hard cap | Yes |
| Expert Choice [4] | None (by construction) | Variable | Exact | No |
| DeepSeek-V3 [7] | Per-expert bias, sign update | Fixed (top-k) | Dynamic | Yes |

### 4.5 Fine-grained experts and loss-free balancing: DeepSeekMoE and DeepSeek-V3

The most recent generation attacks the balance-specialization tradeoff from two new directions. DeepSeekMoE [6] observes that conventional MoE uses a small number of large experts, which limits the combinatorial diversity of expert combinations: with E experts and top-k routing there are only so many distinct subsets. *Fine-grained segmentation* splits each expert into m smaller experts and routes to mk of them, exponentially increasing the number of possible combinations at fixed active parameters.

DeepSeek-V3 [7] takes the more radical step of *removing the auxiliary loss from the gradient entirely*.

```rust
// Auxiliary-loss-free balancing (DeepSeek-V3 style), per training step
for i in 0..num_experts {
    // bias affects routing only; gate values use raw affinities
    let routed = top_k(affinities.map(|s| s + bias[i]), k);
    let load = routed.iter().filter(|&&e| e == i).count() as f32 / target;
    bias[i] += if load > 1.0 { -gamma } else { gamma }; // sign rule, no gradient
}
```

## 5 Empirical Evidence and Proofs

The empirical record, taken together, supports three propositions with unusual consistency across independent groups.

**Proposition 1 — Sparsity shifts the compute-optimal frontier.** Switch-Base's 7x pre-training speedup over T5-Base and Switch-C's 4x speedup over T5-XXL at matched FLOPs [3], GShard's 600B-parameter model trained in four days on 2048 TPU v3 cores with large quality gains over dense multilingual baselines [2], and the fitted scaling laws of [5] all point the same way: at fixed compute, routed models reach lower loss than dense models, with the gap well described by a constant-factor improvement in the scaling law. DeepSeek-V3 extends the point to the frontier: 671B total parameters, 37B active per token, trained on 14.8 trillion tokens for only 2.788M H800 GPU hours, matching leading closed-source models [7].

**Proposition 2 — Balance mechanisms determine realized versus theoretical efficiency.** The gap between a sparse model's FLOP count and its wall-clock speed is dominated by load imbalance: straggler experts delay the all-to-all, and dropped tokens waste the compute spent routing them. Switch's sub-one-percent drop rate at capacity factor 1.0 [3] versus GShard's 5–15 percent early-training drops at 1.25 [2] shows how much the balancing mechanism matters. Expert Choice's more than 2x convergence speedup over both [4] is largely a balance story: zero dropped tokens and exact uniformity convert the same FLOPs into more effective gradient steps.

**Proposition 3 — Auxiliary losses trade specialization for uniformity, and the tradeoff is avoidable.** The evidence is the progression itself: from two aux losses [1] to one [3] to none-by-construction [4] to bias-based loss-free balancing that outperforms aux-loss training [7]. Each step preserves or improves balance while reducing interference with the primary objective. The DeepSeek-V3 ablation is the cleanest: identical architecture, better performance, no gradient through the balancing mechanism [7].

A proof sketch for the core balancing claim clarifies why the Switch loss works where naive penalties fail.

```

  \* so per-expert load is uniform in every reachable state. No auxiliary
```

## 6 Limitations

The sparse-routing literature has sharp, acknowledged boundaries, and several have resisted years of effort.

First, **causality versus balance** remains unresolved. Expert-choice achieves exact balance but violates autoregressive structure; token-choice preserves causality but needs soft balancing machinery. No published method achieves exact per-expert balance, full token completeness, and causal decoding simultaneously — the trilemma is open, and recent workarounds (drop-less MoE with unbounded per-expert buffers, maximum-score routing) trade memory or complexity for partial relief [4].

Second, **specialization is assumed more than demonstrated.** Routers demonstrably partition tokens — syntactic versus semantic, language versus language — but whether experts learn *composable skills* or merely shard the data distribution is poorly understood. Representation-collapse studies show experts can converge to similar functions despite balanced routing, meaning utilization balance does not imply functional diversity [5]. The auxiliary-loss-free line of work improves the optimization but does not by itself produce interpretable specialization.

Third, **inference economics differ from training economics.** Expert parallelism with all-to-all dispatch is efficient at large training batches but pathological at small inference batches: a single generated token still requires loading all experts' parameters into memory, so MoE inference is memory-bandwidth bound in a way dense models are not. Techniques like expert offloading, distillation back to dense models [3], and speculative decoding mitigate but do not eliminate the gap.

Fourth, **scaling laws for routed models are fit, not derived.** The functional forms in [5] describe the data well within the fitted regime, but they offer no first-principles account of *why* the sparsity constant takes its value, how it depends on data distribution, or where the data-dilution limit bites. Extrapolation to expert counts far beyond the fitted range — the regime trillion-parameter models inhabit — is speculative.

Fifth, **evaluation practice lags the architecture.** Most reported gains are pre-training loss or perplexity speedups; downstream task comparisons at matched *inference* cost (not matched training FLOPs) are rarer and less flattering, because the active-parameter count understates the memory and systems cost of serving sparse models [6][7].

## 7 Conclusion

Sparse routing has traveled, in less than a decade, from a curiosity about conditional computation [1] to the architecture of frontier language models [7]. The through-line of that journey is the progressive refinement of a single idea: the token-expert assignment is a constrained optimization problem, and each generation of methods has found a cleaner way to state and satisfy its constraints — from noisy top-k gating with dual auxiliary losses, through GShard's capacity-capped top-2 dispatch, to Switch's top-1 simplification with one differentiable balancing loss, to the inversion of Expert Choice, and finally to bias-based loss-free balancing that removes the balancing act from the gradient altogether.

The practical synthesis is now clear enough to state as guidance. For causal language modeling at scale, token-choice top-k routing with fine-grained experts, shared experts, and auxiliary-loss-free bias balancing [6][7] represents the current Pareto frontier: it preserves causal decoding, guarantees balance dynamically, and keeps the primary objective unperturbed. For encoders and non-causal workloads, expert-choice [4] remains the theoretically cleanest solution — exact balance by construction — and its variable-compute property (spending more experts on harder tokens) anticipates the adaptive-computation agenda more broadly. In both cases, the scaling laws [5] counsel the same discipline: grow experts with the data budget, watch the routing overhead, and remember that parameters are only as useful as the balance mechanism that keeps them fed.

The deepest open question is not engineering but scientific: what do experts *learn*? Until routing produces verifiably composable, interpretable specialization rather than statistically balanced sharding, sparse models will remain extraordinarily effective machines whose internal division of labor we only partly understand. Closing that gap — between balanced utilization and genuine modularity — is the research program the next generation of routing methods must prosecute.
