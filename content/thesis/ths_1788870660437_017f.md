# Planetary-Scale Transactions: TrueTime, Hybrid Logical Clocks, and the Mechanics of External Consistency

## Abstract

Globally distributed databases face a deceptively simple requirement: if a client observes transaction *T₁* complete before *T₂* begins, the database must serialize *T₁* before *T₂* — a property known as *external consistency*, the transaction-level analogue of linearizability. Achieving it across continents forces systems to confront the physical reality that clocks lie: NTP steps backward, oscillators drift, and leap seconds smear. This thesis dissects the two production-proven answers to this problem. Google Spanner's TrueTime exposes time as an interval [*earliest*, *latest*] with an engineered uncertainty *ε* typically below 10 ms, and buys external consistency through a *commit-wait* protocol that delays visibility until the assigned timestamp lies definitively in the past [1][2]. CockroachDB instead pairs wall-clock time with *Hybrid Logical Clocks*, capturing causality without atomic clocks, and recovers Spanner-like guarantees through *closed timestamps*, read refresh, and commit-wait analogues for global tables — paying with protocol complexity and fail-fast clock-offset enforcement [4][5]. We formalize both protocols, prove their ordering guarantees from first principles, account for their latency taxes, and analyze their failure modes under clock skew.

## 1. Introduction

In 2012, the Spanner paper made a startling claim: the first database to provide *externally consistent* distributed transactions at global scale [1]. The claim was startling because the community had long treated strict cross-datacenter ordering as achievable only through centralized timestamp oracles — single points of failure and latency no planetary system could tolerate. Spanner's insight was not a faster consensus protocol but a reframing of what a clock may be. Rather than pretending each machine knows the exact time, TrueTime *admits uncertainty explicitly*: `TT.now()` returns an interval, and every protocol above it reasons about the interval, never a point [1][2][10].

CockroachDB instead rejects TrueTime's specialized hardware: most operators have NTP and commodity oscillators, not GPS receivers and atomic clocks in every datacenter. It therefore fuses physical and logical time into *Hybrid Logical Clocks* (HLCs) — due to Kulkarni, Demirbas, Madappa, Avva, and Leone [3] — which capture causality like a Lamport clock while staying near wall-clock time. The missing ingredient, TrueTime's honest uncertainty, is replaced by a *configured* maximum tolerated skew, enforced ruthlessly: a node whose clock drifts too far from its peers terminates itself [5]. Where Spanner *waits out* uncertainty, CockroachDB *bounds and polices* it.

These designs are often presented as unrelated points in the design space. This thesis argues they are the same idea in different clothes: **both convert a bound on clock uncertainty into a global ordering guarantee, and both pay for the conversion in commit latency**. Spanner pays in engineered hardware and a commit-wait delay; CockroachDB pays in protocol machinery — closed timestamps, read refresh, future-dated writes — and in availability, since skew beyond the bound is treated as a crash fault. We contribute a unified formal treatment of both protocols with machine-checkable sketches, a latency accounting of where uncertainty hides, and a failure-mode analysis covering GPS outages, unbounded skew, and lease transitions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Time, order, and the lies clocks tell

Distributed ordering begins with Lamport's *happens-before* partial order [4]: Lamport timestamps guarantee *e → f ⇒ L(e) < L(f)*, but the converse fails, and vector clocks restore it at *O(n)* space — untenable per key version. Physical clocks *lie about order*: NTP steps backward, so one machine's successive writes need not be monotonic, and a fast clock wins every last-writer-wins conflict silently and permanently.

The property at stake is *external consistency* [1], or *strict serializability*: serializability plus the real-time constraint that if *T₁* commits before *T₂* starts in absolute time, *T₁* precedes *T₂* in the serialization order — strictly above snapshot isolation in Adya's formalism [9]. A merely serializable system may order *T₂* before *T₁* even when every observer saw *T₁* finish first, breaking read-your-writes, consistent backups, and audit semantics tied to wall-clock reality.

### 2.2 The systems under study

**Spanner** shards data into tablets, each replicated by a Paxos group on *spanservers* spread across zones and universes (datacenters) [1]. Writes run two-phase commit across participant Paxos groups; reads can be lock-free snapshots at sufficiently old timestamps. The linchpin is TrueTime: dedicated *time masters* per datacenter armed with GPS receivers and atomic clocks, whose daemons discipline local oscillators via Marzullo-style interval intersection. Reported uncertainty *ε* is "generally less than 10 ms" [1].

**CockroachDB** partitions data into *ranges*, each Raft-replicated with a *leaseholder* serving consistent reads and coordinating writes [5]. Timestamps come from HLCs [3]; transactions run at serializable snapshot isolation over MVCC with optimistic concurrency and write intents. Each range tracks a *closed timestamp* — a promise that no write will ever land at or below it — advanced continuously by the leaseholder and propagated to followers, enabling low-latency *follower reads* with bounded staleness [5][6]. *Global tables* extend the idea: writes scheduled slightly in the future let every replica serve consistent present-time reads, a software analogue of commit-wait [7].

## 3. Methodology

This thesis is an *analytical comparative study*. Our method has four strands:

1. **Formal protocol modeling.** We restate each timestamping discipline as abstract state machines — a TLA⁺ sketch of Spanner's commit-wait (§4.1) and executable Python pseudocode for HLC rules (§4.2) — so ordering arguments can be checked, not trusted.
2. **Inequality-chain proofs.** External consistency reduces to chains like *s₁ < tabs(e₁, commit) < tabs(e₂, start) ≤ s₂* [10], with each step's physical assumption explicit.
3. **Latency accounting.** We trace where the uncertainty bound *ε* (or the configured max skew) sits on the critical path of commits and reads, distinguishing latency *hidden* under unavoidable replication round trips from latency *intrinsic* to the guarantee.
4. **Failure-mode analysis.** We enumerate behavior when founding assumptions break: TrueTime masters losing GPS, HLC nodes exceeding max offset, lease transfers racing closed timestamps.

Our sources are the primary papers and official documentation [1][2][3][5][6][7], the clock literature [4][9], and the consensus literature motivating why a global oracle must be avoided [8].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 TrueTime: Interval Time and the Commit-Wait Protocol

The TrueTime API is small [1][2]: `TT.now()` returns `[earliest, latest]` bracketing the absolute invocation time; `TT.after(t)` holds only if *t* definitely passed; `TT.before(t)` only if *t* is definitely future. The implementation keeps the interval narrow through redundancy: per-datacenter masters combine *diverse* references — GPS for accuracy, atomic clocks for holdover — while daemons poll masters and widen the result via Marzullo interval intersection plus local oscillator drift between polls. The width has exactly two physical contributors: *synchronization RTT* and *clock drift* [2]. When masters disagree, the API does not lie — it widens, and every protocol above *slows down to wait out the uncertainty* [1].

The coordinator leader assigns *sᵢ* satisfying: (i) *sᵢ ≥ TT.now().latest* sampled after the commit request arrives (*e_{i,server}*); (ii) *sᵢ* ≥ every participant's prepare timestamp; (iii) *sᵢ* exceeds all prior timestamps [10]. It logs via Paxos, then **commit-waits**: the decision is withheld until `TT.after(sᵢ)` — i.e., *sᵢ < TT.now().earliest* — before locks release.

> **Theorem (External consistency via commit-wait).** If *T₁*'s commit precedes *T₂*'s start in absolute time, then *s₁ < s₂*.

*Proof.* Let *tabs(e)* be absolute time. Commit-wait gives *s₁ < tabs(e₁, commit)*; assumption gives *tabs(e₁, commit) < tabs(e₂, start)*; causality gives *tabs(e₂, start) ≤ tabs(e₂, server)*; the start rule gives *tabs(e₂, server) ≤ s₂*. Transitivity yields *s₁ < s₂*. ∎ [1][10]

Reads exploit the same timestamps without locks. Each replica tracks *safe time* *t_safe = min(t_Paxos_safe, t_TM_safe)* [10]: *t_Paxos_safe* is the highest applied Paxos write, and *t_TM_safe = minᵢ(s_{i,g}^{prepare}) − 1* precedes every prepared-but-uncommitted transaction since *sᵢ ≥ s_{i,g}^{prepare}*. Snapshot reads at any *t ≤ t_safe* are lock-free: no future commit can land at or below *t_safe*. TrueTime also guards *leader leases*: an abdicating leader must wait for `TT.after(s_max)` — the maximum timestamp it ever used — preserving disjointness of successive leaders' timestamp domains [10]. One interval primitive thus underwrites both transaction ordering and consensus leadership.

```tla
---- MODULE CommitWait ----
EXTENDS Naturals
CONSTANTS Eps
VARIABLES s_commit, earliest, latest, visible
Init == /\ s_commit = 0
        /\ earliest \in Nat /\ latest = earliest + Eps
        /\ visible = FALSE
Assign == /\ ~visible /\ s_commit' = latest
           /\ UNCHANGED <<earliest, latest, visible>>
Tick == /\ earliest' = earliest + 1 /\ latest' = latest + 1
        /\ UNCHANGED <<s_commit, visible>>
Wait == /\ ~visible /\ s_commit < earliest /\ visible' = TRUE
        /\ UNCHANGED <<s_commit, earliest, latest>>
Next == Assign \/ Tick \/ Wait
\* Any later txn samples latest after visible: its s2 >= latest > s_commit.
====
```

### 4.2 Hybrid Logical Clocks: Causality Without Special Hardware

Where TrueTime is honest about uncertainty, the HLC is honest about causality while staying near wall-clock time. A timestamp *(l, c)* pairs *l* — the maximum physical time ever observed, locally or in a message — with a logical tie-breaker *c* [3]:

```python
def local_or_send(state, phys):
    l, c = state
    l_next = max(l, phys)
    return (l_next, c + 1 if l_next == l else 0)

def receive(state, phys, msg):
    l, c = state
    lm, cm = msg
    l_next = max(l, lm, phys)
    if l_next == l == lm:      c_next = max(c, cm) + 1
    elif l_next == l:          c_next = c + 1
    elif l_next == lm:         c_next = cm + 1
    else:                      c_next = 0
    return (l_next, c_next)
```

| Property | Statement | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Monotonicity** | A node's HLC never decreases | Per-node versions totally ordered |
| **Causality** | *e → f ⇒ HLC(e) < HLC(f)* | Lamport-like capture at *O(1)* size |
| **Bounded drift** | *l* within max inter-node skew of physical time | Usable for TTLs, `AS OF SYSTEM TIME` |

> **Theorem (HLC causality).** For *e → f* (program order or send–receive edge), *HLC(e) < HLC(f)* lexicographically.

*Proof sketch.* Each rule strictly increases the pair: local/send advances *l* or increments *c*; receive maxes *l* across local, remote, and physical time and sets *c* above both counters on ties. Transitivity extends to arbitrary causal chains. ∎ [3]

But HLC is a *point*, not an interval: it cannot say "I am unsure," and the max operation never forgets — one skewed clock drags every node's *l* forward permanently. Without a *max-drift rejection bound*, the system does not self-heal [3]. CockroachDB supplies that bound operationally: nodes must stay within a configured maximum offset (default 500 ms, commonly 250 ms), and a node too far out of sync with half the cluster *terminates itself* [5] — fail-fast availability traded for timestamp integrity, the inverse of TrueTime's degrade-to-latency. The bound also surfaces inside the protocol as an *uncertainty window* driving read refresh and commit-wait analogues (§4.3).

### 4.3 Closed Timestamps and Serializable Snapshot Isolation

CockroachDB runs serializable snapshot isolation over MVCC: versions carry HLC commit timestamps, reads take the newest version at or below the transaction timestamp, and optimistic concurrency refreshes read timestamps on conflict. The geo-replication problem is serving reads from nearby replicas without contacting the leaseholder — Spanner's lock-free snapshot reads, minus TrueTime's *t_safe*.

The mechanism is the **closed timestamp**: *no write can ever be introduced at or below it* [5][6]. The leaseholder advances it continuously, a few seconds behind the present, propagating it to followers piggybacked on the Raft log or via side transport. Because the replication stream is synchronized with timestamp closing, a follower that has applied the log through the leaseholder's specified position *provably holds every write ≤ closed timestamp* and may serve reads at any *t ≤ closed timestamp* locally — bounded-staleness *follower reads* with no leaseholder round trip. Three subtleties make the promise airtight [6]:

- **Lease transfers preserve it.** The origin store never lets a new lease write at or below a closed timestamp; closed timestamps survive leadership change.
- **Violations bump writers.** A write arriving at *ts ≤ closed timestamp* is forced to a higher timestamp, triggering *read refresh* — serializability preserved at the cost of a retry.
- **Followers must observe the lease.** Reads are keyed to the *Lease Applied Index* (MLAI), forcing lagging followers to catch up on the Raft log before serving.

*Global tables* push further: consistent reads at the *present* on *every* replica [7]. Writes are scheduled slightly *in the future* (hundreds of milliseconds) under a "non-blocking transaction" variant where reads never block on write commits; the client is acknowledged only once current time catches the write timestamp — a **commit-wait** identical in structure to Spanner's, with the uncertainty budget paid by the writer. The max-offset window reappears: a read at *t* encountering a value at *t + 5 ms* with 10 ms max skew must adopt *t + 5 ms* and refresh prior reads — and if that timestamp is future, the reader itself commit-waits [7].

### 4.4 Uncertainty Budgets and Latency Accounting

Spanner's commit-wait lasts ~*2ε* — yet the coordinator must also replicate through Paxos, costing at least a cross-datacenter round trip. Uncertainty adds *no* commit latency while it stays below the Paxos round-trip time: the wait hides underneath [2]. The tax is *latent*, surfacing only when *ε* grows — latency is the shock absorber; correctness is never negotiable.

CockroachDB spends its budget in three places: follower reads lag the present by the closed-timestamp interval (seconds) [5]; global-table writes pay hundreds of milliseconds of commit-wait [7]; and the max-offset bound converts clock excursions into process crashes.

| Dimension | Spanner / TrueTime | CockroachDB / HLC |
|---|---|---|
| Time primitive | Interval `[earliest, latest]`, bounded *ε* | Point `(l, c)`: physical + logical |
| Hardware | GPS + atomic clocks per datacenter | Commodity NTP clocks |
| Uncertainty source | Sync RTT + oscillator drift [2] | Configured max offset (e.g., 500 ms) |
| Commit cost | Commit-wait ≤ ~2ε, hidden under Paxos RTT | Future-dated writes wait ~100s of ms; follower reads seconds stale |
| Read path | Lock-free snapshot at *t ≤ t_safe* | Follower reads at *t ≤ closed ts*; refresh on window violation |
| Skew failure | *ε* widens → latency degrades, correctness holds | Node self-terminates; availability hit |
| Guarantee | External consistency | Serializable SI + external consistency via uncertainty windows |

Uncertainty infects *leases*, not just transactions: Spanner leaders abdicate only after `TT.after(s_max)`; CockroachDB lease transfers must respect closed timestamps [6][10].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Stress-testing the commit-wait chain

The §4.1 proof chain concentrates all risk in one link: the interval must truly bracket absolute time. A time master that *under-reports* uncertainty breaks the commit-wait link silently — making the daemon's widening arithmetic, not the database, the system's most security-critical component. The assumption link needs no clock at all (it is the external ground truth); the causality and start-rule links are clock-independent or reduce to interval correctness.

### 5.2 Proof: closed-timestamp reads are consistent

> **Theorem.** A follower that applied the Raft log through the position associated with closed timestamp *cs*, serving a read at *t ≤ cs*, observes every committed write with timestamp *≤ t*, and no write with timestamp *≤ t* will ever subsequently appear.

*Proof.* By the invariant, no write is ever introduced at or below *cs* [5]. The leaseholder propagates *cs* together with the log position synchronizing the replication stream with timestamp closing [6]; applying through that position materializes every write *≤ cs*, hence *≤ t*. The invariant forbids later writes *≤ cs*, so the snapshot is stable. Lease transfers never permit a new lease to write at or below a previously closed timestamp, so the theorem survives leadership change. ∎

With read refresh — transactions observing values inside their uncertainty window re-read at the higher timestamp [7] — this yields serializable snapshot isolation with bounded-staleness reads.

### 5.3 Reported engineering numbers

Spanner's paper states TrueTime uncertainty "generally less than 10 ms" via GPS and atomic references [1] — the number that lets commit-wait hide under Paxos latency. Software TrueTime instead demands bounded clock *rate* error in hardware timestamp counters [2]. CockroachDB's docs put closed timestamps "a few seconds in the past" [5]; the follower-reads RFC details Raft-piggybacked and side-transport propagation [6]; global tables quantify future-dating at "hundreds of milliseconds" of writer wait [7]; the max-offset default (500 ms, commonly 250 ms) sets the refresh window [5]. Qualitatively both systems agree on the fundamental trade: *uncertainty × availability = constant* — Spanner pays the constant in datacenter hardware, CockroachDB in crash-on-skew.

## 6. Limitations

**TrueTime's hardware dependence.** Interval honesty needs references better than the oscillators being disciplined. GPS jamming or datacenter-level outages widen *ε*; the system degrades to latency, but *every commit on Earth slows simultaneously* — a correlated failure mode no logical-clock scheme shares. Software TrueTime removes the atomic clocks but demands bounded clock rate error [2].

**HLC's non-self-healing skew.** The max operation never forgets: one clock at the offset edge drags every peer's *l* forward, with no convergence when the offender recovers. The crash policy converts clock faults into availability faults — one bad NTP peer can repeatedly remove itself, flapping leases. The bound buying timestamp integrity is also a liveness denial-of-service vector.

**Non-Byzantine time assumption.** Both designs assume clocks drift but do not lie adversarially. A compromised time master or malicious NTP response inside the offset bound can manufacture timestamps satisfying every protocol check while violating external consistency. Neither system claims Byzantine fault tolerance for its time plane.

**Closed timestamps buy staleness.** Follower reads are seconds stale by construction [5]; present-time reads from followers require global tables and the writer-side wait [7] — no configuration gives both. The MLAI bookkeeping securing follower reads across lease transfers is among the transaction layer's most intricate code, and its correctness argument (sketched in §5.2) has never, to our knowledge, been machine-checked end to end.

**External consistency is client-relative.** Both guarantees are indexed to *client-observed* commit and start events. A client with a skewed clock may perceive an order no server-side timestamping can reconstruct; the database guarantees consistency with respect to the order in which *it* made effects visible — the last mile belongs to the application.

## 7. Conclusion

Spanner and CockroachDB are usually filed on opposite sides of a hardware divide: exotic clocks versus commodity NTP. This thesis has argued the divide is superficial. Both implement the same abstract machine — *a bound on clock uncertainty, converted into a global ordering guarantee, paid for in commit latency* — differing only in where the bound comes from and what currency the payment takes. TrueTime manufactures the bound with physics and spends it as commit-wait hidden under Paxos round trips [1][2]. HLCs manufacture causality from message passing, take the bound as a configured parameter enforced by self-termination, and spend it as closed-timestamp lag, read refresh, and future-dated writes [3][5][6][7].

Three principles emerge. First, *make uncertainty explicit*: intervals beat points, because a protocol that can say "I don't know" degrades gracefully while one that must guess fails silently. Second, *unify the clock discipline across leadership and data planes*: both systems' subtlest invariants live at the lease/timestamp boundary. Third, *choose the failure mode deliberately*: widening *ε* into latency and crashing on skew are both legitimate, but the choice decides whether a clock incident becomes a slow day or an outage.

The broader lesson concerns what "now" means in a distributed system. Lamport taught us to order events without clocks [4]; TrueTime and HLC teach us to order them *with* clocks, provided we are honest about what clocks cannot do. Planetary scale needs no perfect time — only *bounded* imperfection, and the discipline to wait out, or crash on, the remainder.
