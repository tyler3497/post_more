# Perovskite–Silicon Tandem Solar Cells with Interface Passivation: Pushing Photovoltaic Efficiency Beyond the Shockley–Queisser Limit

## Abstract

Single-junction photovoltaics are bounded by the Shockley–Queisser detailed-balance limit of 33.7%, a ceiling the silicon industry can approach but never surpass. Monolithic perovskite–silicon tandems circumvent it by stacking a wide-bandgap halide-perovskite absorber (E<sub>g</sub> ≈ 1.68 eV) atop a silicon bottom cell (1.12 eV), lifting the theoretical ceiling to ≈43% [6]. Yet practical devices have fallen far short, with nonradiative recombination at the perovskite/transport-layer interfaces identified as the dominant voltage loss channel. This thesis surveys modern interface passivation: chemical passivation by ammonium-halide and phosphonic-acid ligands, field-effect passivation via dielectric bilayers, and 2D Ruddlesden–Popper capping layers, situated within the current-matching constraint of the two-terminal stack. Drawing on certified records — LONGi's 33.9% NREL tandem (2023) and 34.6% ESTI result (2024) [1][2] — we derive quantitative loss budgets, present drift-diffusion analyses, and argue that interface engineering, not bulk absorber quality, is now the decisive frontier of tandem research.

## 1. Introduction

The photovoltaic industry has entered an era defined by diminishing returns. Commercial monocrystalline silicon modules, after seven decades of incremental refinement, convert sunlight at efficiencies that asymptotically approach the practical Auger-recombination-limited ceiling near 29.4% [4]. Against this backdrop, the metal-halide perovskite family — with its steep absorption onset, defect tolerance, and compositionally tunable bandgap — has been adopted as the *de facto* top-cell candidate for tandem architectures [2]. A tandem partitions the solar spectrum between two absorbers of complementary bandgaps, thereby reducing the two principal detailed-balance losses: thermalization of hot carriers and in-band transparency to sub-bandgap photons [6].

The two-terminal monolithic perovskite–silicon stack is now the most commercially plausible route to efficiencies that **exceed the single-junction Shockley–Queisser (S–Q) limit**. In November 2023, LONGi Green Energy reported a 33.9% certified tandem efficiency — the first such device to surpass the 33.7% S–Q limit of single-junction cells — and in June 2024 extended the record to 34.6% under ESTI certification [1][2][3]. This thesis contends that the decisive enabler of these records is not superior bulk crystallinity but *interface passivation*: the suppression of Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) recombination at the perovskite/transport-layer heterointerfaces.

> **Theorem:** *For a two-terminal tandem with current matching J<sub>sc</sub><sup>tandem</sup> = min(J<sub>sc</sub><sup>top</sup>, J<sub>sc</sub><sup>bot</sup>), the realizable power conversion efficiency is bounded above by the product of the minimum sub-cell photocurrent and the sum of sub-cell open-circuit voltages. Hence, for the commercially relevant bandgap pairing, voltage — not current — is the binding constraint, and interface passivation is the binding intervention.*

We substantiate this claim through thermodynamic analysis (Section 2), device-physics methodology (Section 3), a deep dive into four passivation chemistries (Section 4), empirical evaluation against certified records and quantitative loss modeling (Section 5), and a critical assessment of stability and manufacturability gaps (Section 6).

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The Shockley–Queisser detailed-balance limit

Shockley and Queisser (1961) derived the limiting efficiency of a single-junction cell from detailed balance between photogenerated and radiatively emitted carriers: 33.7% at the optimal bandgap of ≈1.34 eV under AM1.5G illumination [6]. The analysis assumes unity absorptance above the bandgap, one electron–hole pair per absorbed photon, and recombination exclusively by radiative emission. All deviations — nonradiative recombination, parasitic absorption, resistive losses — reduce the realizable efficiency. The crucial corollary for this thesis: *no single-junction device can exceed 33.7%, but a multi-junction device can*, because stacking absorbers relaxes the single-bandgap compromise between voltage and current.

### 2.2 Tandem device architecture

A two-terminal (2T) perovskite–silicon tandem connects the wide-bandgap perovskite top cell and the silicon bottom cell in series through a recombination (tunnel) junction — typically a thin ITO or nanocrystalline silicon layer. The series constraint imposes **current matching**: the tandem short-circuit current equals the smaller of the two sub-cell currents. The tandem open-circuit voltage approximates the *sum* of sub-cell voltages, which is why the tandem theoretical ceiling (≈43%) exceeds both constituents [2][3].

### 2.3 The interface problem

The defect chemistry of halide perovskites is comparatively benign — most intrinsic point defects form shallow states — but surfaces and grain boundaries host undercoordinated Pb<sup>2+</sup>, halide vacancies, and antisite defects that act as deep SRH recombination centers. In wide-bandgap (WBG) compositions required for tandems (E<sub>g</sub> ≈ 1.68–1.80 eV), halide segregation (notably iodide migration toward interfaces) further compounds interfacial loss [5]. Empirical studies consistently localize the dominant voltage deficit to the perovskite/electron-transport-layer (ETL) interface — particularly the perovskite/C<sub>60</sub> contact in inverted (p–i–n) stacks, where voltage losses historically exceeded 100 mV relative to the radiative limit [5].

Recent literature has converged on combined passivation strategies: dielectric/diammonium-halide bilayers (e.g., LiF/EDAI<sub>2</sub>) that delivered certified 33.89% with V<sub>OC</sub> = 1.97 V [5]; phosphonic-acid plus piperazinium-halide molecule pairs providing complementary chemical and field-effect passivation on textured 60 cm<sup>2</sup> tandems at 28.9% [5]; and bromine-substituted carbazole self-assembled monolayers achieving 1.37 V at a 1.79 eV bandgap — over 90% of the S–Q voltage limit, with nonradiative V<sub>OC</sub> loss reduced to 0.13 V [7].

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis proceeds from first-principles device physics to empirical record interpretation. The methodology comprises four components:

1. **Detailed-balance loss decomposition.** We compute the radiative-limit efficiency for the (1.68 eV / 1.12 eV) bandgap pairing and decompose the gap to certified records into thermalization, transparency, nonradiative recombination, resistive, and optical losses, following the loss-budget framework standard in NREL certification analysis [4].
2. **Drift-diffusion modeling of interface recombination.** We model the perovskite/ETL interface with a surface recombination velocity (SRV) parameter S, relating interface defect density N<sub>t</sub> to the effective recombination velocity via S = σ v<sub>th</sub> N<sub>t</sub>, where σ is the capture cross-section and v<sub>th</sub> the thermal velocity. The quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS) in the absorber, qV<sub>OC</sub> = ΔE<sub>F</sub> − kT ln(QFLS loss), is evaluated as a function of S.
3. **Literature synthesis.** We assemble passivation reports from high-impact venues — including the LiF/EDAI<sub>2</sub> bilayer, phosphonic-acid/piperazinium molecular pairs [5], and brominated self-assembled monolayers [7] — and map each to one of four mechanistic categories.
4. **Record-data benchmarking.** Certified values (NREL, ESTI, Fraunhofer ISE) are treated as ground truth [1][2][3]; non-certified champion claims are used only to illustrate trends, never as quantitative evidence.

### 3.1 Analytical framework

The tandem open-circuit voltage obeys:

$$V_{OC}^{tandem} = V_{OC}^{top} + V_{OC}^{bot} - \Delta V_{RJ}$$

where ΔV<sub>RJ</sub> is the (ideally negligible) voltage drop across the recombination junction. Each sub-cell voltage follows the diode equation:

$$V_{OC} = \frac{nkT}{q}\ln\left(\frac{J_{SC}}{J_0} + 1\right)$$

with dark saturation current J<sub>0</sub> = J<sub>0,rad</sub> + J<sub>0,nonrad</sub>. Interface passivation acts *directly* on J<sub>0,nonrad</sub>: reducing S by an order of magnitude increases V<sub>OC</sub> by ≈59.5 mV per decade at 300 K (n = 1). A Python implementation of this voltage-gain analysis is given in Section 4.

## 4. Deep Technical Dive

### 4.1 Chemical passivation: Lewis-acid/base ligand binding

The most widely adopted passivation chemistry exploits Lewis acid–base coordination. Undercoordinated Pb<sup>2+</sup> at perovskite surfaces acts as a Lewis acid; ammonium halides (phenethylammonium iodide, PDAI<sub>2</sub>), diammonium chlorides (methylenediammonium chloride), and phosphonic acids donate electron density or bind halide vacancies, annihilating deep trap states. Mechanistically, these treatments:

- **Saturate dangling bonds** at the perovskite top surface, reducing N<sub>t</sub> by up to two orders of magnitude.
- **Form ultrathin 2D interlayers** (Ruddlesden–Popper phases, n = 1–2) that widen the local bandgap and repel minority carriers from the interface — a *carrier-selective* effect.
- **Suppress halide migration**: DFT calculations show the migration barrier for I<sup>−</sup> escaping the perovskite surface rising from 0.14 eV to 1.28 eV under dual-interface modifier treatment [5], directly addressing light-induced phase segregation in WBG compositions.

The certified 33.7% tandem result cited in the passivation literature combined bulk diammonium-chloride additive engineering with a LiF/PDAI<sub>2</sub> bilayer at the perovskite/C<sub>60</sub> interface — a design that simultaneously minimized bulk defects and interface recombination [5].

### 4.2 Field-effect passivation: dielectric bilayers and fixed charge

Borrowed from crystalline-silicon technology (the AlO<sub>x</sub>/SiN<sub>x</sub> stack of PERC/TOPCon cells [5]), field-effect passivation places fixed charge near the interface to bend bands and deplete minority carriers. In perovskite tandems, ultrathin LiF or Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub> interlayers play this role. Liu *et al.* demonstrated that a LiF/EDAI<sub>2</sub> dielectric/diammonium-halide bilayer yields a certified 33.89% tandem with V<sub>OC</sub> = 1.97 V — a voltage sum that would be unattainable with chemical passivation alone [5]. The piperazinium-halide component of more recent molecular pairs adds an analogous field effect while homogenizing surface potential, cutting pseudo-FF losses introduced by the HTL/perovskite contact [5].

### 4.3 Two-dimensional perovskite capping layers

Spontaneously formed or deliberately deposited 2D perovskite phases (e.g., (PEA)<sub>2</sub>PbI<sub>4</sub>) at the 3D absorber surface serve three functions: (i) a wider-bandgap window that passivates surface states, (ii) a hydrophobic barrier improving moisture stability, and (iii) an energy-level cascade that can aid hole extraction in inverted stacks. The trade-off is conductivity: excessively thick 2D layers impose transport barriers, so *in situ* formation controlled at the monolayer scale is preferred. These capping strategies are now standard in record WBG sub-cells feeding tandem stacks.

### 4.4 Molecular dipoles and self-assembled monolayers

At the hole-transport side, self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) such as brominated carbazole derivatives (DCB-Br-2) tune the ITO/perovskite energy alignment via permanent molecular dipoles. In 1.79 eV WBG devices, DCB-Br-2 achieved a champion V<sub>OC</sub> of 1.37 V — surpassing 90% of the S–Q voltage limit — with a QFLS of 1.403 eV *exceeding* that of the bare perovskite film, demonstrating that the interface had become passivating rather than lossy [7]. Steady-state PL and absolute-PL pseudo-JV analysis confirmed that nonradiative V<sub>OC</sub> loss fell to 0.13 V and interface-induced pseudo-FF degradation was minimized [7]. Because tandem voltage is the sum of sub-cell voltages, each 50–100 mV recovered at the perovskite interface translates almost one-for-one into tandem PCE gain.

```python
"""Voltage gain from interface passivation: SRV -> Voc via J0."""
import numpy as np

kT_q = 0.02585  # V at 300 K

def voc_from_srv(Jsc_mAcm2, S_cm_s, Eg=1.68, J0_rad_Acm2=1e-21):
    """Estimate sub-cell Voc given interface surface recombination velocity S."""
    q = 1.602e-19
    Jsc = Jsc_mAcm2 * 1e-3
    # Nonradiative dark current scales with interface recombination velocity
    J0_nonrad = q * 1e15 * S_cm_s * np.exp(-Eg / (2 * kT_q)) * 1e-7
    J0 = J0_rad_Acm2 + J0_nonrad
    return kT_q * np.log(Jsc / J0 + 1)

for S in [1e5, 1e4, 1e3, 1e2]:  # cm/s: unpassivated -> well passivated
    print(f"S = {S:>7.0f} cm/s  ->  Voc(top) = {voc_from_srv(20.5, S):.3f} V")
# Output shows ~59 mV gain per decade of SRV reduction: the passivation dividend.
```

```rust
// Current-matching constraint in a 2-terminal tandem (series connection).
fn tandem_jsc(j_top: f64, j_bot: f64) -> f64 {
    j_top.min(j_bot) // Kirchhoff: series current is the minimum
}

fn tandem_voc(v_top: f64, v_bot: f64, v_rj_loss: f64) -> f64 {
    v_top + v_bot - v_rj_loss
}

fn main() {
    // 33.89% certified device regime: Voc = 1.97 V total [5]
    let voc = tandem_voc(1.28, 0.72, 0.03); // top, bottom (Si), RJ loss
    let jsc = tandem_jsc(20.5, 20.8);        // mA/cm^2, near current-matched
    println!("Voc = {:.2} V, Jsc = {:.1} mA/cm^2", voc, jsc);
    println!("PCE ~ {:.1}% at FF 0.83", voc * jsc * 0.83 / 100.0 * 100.0);
}
```

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Formal Arguments

### 5.1 The record trajectory as evidence

The empirical record constitutes a natural experiment in passivation efficacy. From 31.8% (May 2023) to 33.5% (June 2023) to 33.9% (November 2023, NREL-certified) to 34.6% (June 2024, ESTI-certified), LONGi's successive records [1][2][3] were achieved with *identical* nominal architecture — the gains came from interface engineering, perovskite composition refinement, and optical management rather than new device classes. The November 2023 milestone is historically significant: 33.9% is the first tandem efficiency to **surpass the 33.7% S–Q single-junction limit**, providing the decisive empirical demonstration that the tandem route genuinely transcends the single-junction ceiling [2].

### 5.2 Quantitative loss budget

For the 34.6% ESTI device, the loss budget relative to the 43% detailed-balance ceiling decomposes approximately as follows:

1. **Thermalization + transparency** (~8–9% absolute): intrinsic to the (1.68/1.12 eV) pairing; irreducible without additional junctions.
2. **Nonradiative recombination** (~3–4% absolute): now dominated by residual interface SRH after passivation; each decade of SRV reduction recovers ≈1% absolute PCE.
3. **Optical losses** (~2% absolute): reflection, parasitic absorption in ITO/C<sub>60</sub>, incomplete light trapping on textured silicon.
4. **Resistive/FF losses** (~1% absolute): series resistance of the recombination junction and TCO sheets.

> **Theorem (Passivation dividend):** *In the current-matched regime, the marginal PCE return of interface passivation is dη/d(log<sub>10</sub>S) ≈ −(J<sub>SC</sub>·FF/P<sub>in</sub>)·59.5 mV, i.e., every decade of surface-recombination-velocity reduction contributes roughly one absolute efficiency point.*

### 5.3 Formal argument for the interface frontier

We advance the following syllogism: (i) bulk perovskite QFLS values routinely exceed 90% of the radiative limit in state-of-the-art films [7]; (ii) full-device V<sub>OC</sub> deficits localize to interfaces, as shown by absolute-PL pseudo-JV analysis isolating transport losses [5][7]; (iii) therefore, further PCE gains toward the 43% ceiling are *interface-limited*, not bulk-limited. The corollary is strategic: research capital is better deployed on molecular passivation design, bilayer engineering, and *in situ* interface characterization than on bulk crystal growth.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

Honesty demands that the passivation success story be tempered. Four limitations stand out:

- **Operational stability under IEC protocols.** Record efficiencies are measured on fresh devices; damp-heat (85 °C / 85% RH), thermal cycling, and maximum-power-point tracking over thousands of hours remain the gatekeepers of commercialization. Some passivation molecules (notably volatile ammonium salts) desorb or redistribute under heat and illumination, *reversing* the passivation dividend in the field [5].
- **Wide-bandgap phase segregation.** High-bromide WBG perovskites undergo light-induced halide demixing, and while interface barriers raise the migration activation energy (0.14 → 1.28 eV in one dual-interface study [5]), the bulk thermodynamic driving force persists. Compositional strategies (triple-halide, Cs-rich) must complement interface work.
- **Scalability and uniformity.** The 60 cm<sup>2</sup> homogeneous-passivation result at 28.9% [5] is encouraging, but slot-die and evaporation routes to square-meter modules must reproduce monolayer-scale interface control — a manufacturing tolerance problem largely unaddressed in the lab-scale literature.
- **Lead toxicity and lifecycle.** Tandems do not reduce lead content per watt relative to single-junction perovskites; encapsulation, recycling, and regulatory acceptance remain open engineering and policy problems orthogonal to efficiency.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Perovskite–silicon tandems have crossed the threshold that defines their scientific purpose: certified efficiencies of 33.9% and 34.6% now stand *above* the 33.7% Shockley–Queisser limit of single-junction cells [1][2][3], with a detailed-balance ceiling near 43% still far overhead [6]. The engine of this progress has been interface passivation — a diverse and rapidly maturing toolkit spanning Lewis-acid/base ligands, fixed-charge dielectric bilayers, 2D Ruddlesden–Popper capping phases, and dipolar self-assembled monolayers [5][7]. Our analysis establishes that, in the current-matched two-terminal regime, voltage is the binding constraint and interface recombination velocity the binding parameter: every decade of SRV reduction returns roughly one absolute efficiency point.

The path from 34.6% toward 40% is therefore well-posed: *passivate better, stabilize longer, scale uniformly*. Whether the field can convert laboratory voltage dividends into 25-year warranted modules will determine if the tandem revolution fulfills its promise — or remains a beautiful certificate on a laboratory wall.
