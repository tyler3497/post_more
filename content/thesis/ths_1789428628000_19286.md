# Metal-Halide Perovskite Photovoltaics: Defect Passivation, Tandem Architectures, and the Path Beyond the Shockley–Queisser Limit

## Abstract

Metal-halide perovskites have risen from laboratory curiosities to photovoltaic absorbers rivaling gallium arsenide in optoelectronic quality, with single-junction efficiencies above 26% and perovskite/silicon tandem records approaching 34% [5][8]. Their ascent exposes a central tension: solution-processed ionic crystals are intrinsically defect-tolerant, yet the remaining nonradiative recombination at grain boundaries and interfaces is what now separates laboratory devices from the radiative efficiency limit. This article develops a rigorous account of the problem, beginning with the Shockley–Queisser detailed-balance framework that sets the thermodynamic ceiling [1][2], continuing through defect chemistry, charge-transport physics, and interface passivation, and ending at tandem architectures engineered to exceed the single-junction limit entirely. We show how concurrent cationic and anionic passivation strategies have suppressed voltage losses [4], how self-assembled monolayers resolved the hole-extraction bottleneck in wide-bandgap subcells [3], and how dual-function interfacial bilayers pushed certified tandems to 33.89% [5]. Remaining obstacles — ion migration, light-induced halide segregation, thermal aging, and lead containment — define the honest path from record cells to terawatt-scale deployment.

## 1 Intro

Photovoltaics is the rare discipline where the fundamental limit has been known longer than the winning material. In 1961, William Shockley and Hans-Joachim Queisser derived the detailed-balance efficiency ceiling for a single p–n junction from thermodynamic first principles, assuming only that every recombination event is radiative and that every above-bandgap photon is absorbed [1]. For six decades that ceiling — roughly 33.7% under the standard AM1.5G spectrum at an optimal bandgap near 1.34 eV — has survived intact. Every commercial technology operates in its shadow: silicon at 1.12 eV sacrifices a few absolute points by bandgap placement, and decades of defect engineering have carried it to within a few points of its practical limit.

Metal-halide perovskites, materials of the form ABX₃ where A is an organic or alkali cation, B is lead or tin, and X is a halide, entered this story late and moved fast. Their combination of strong direct-bandgap absorption, long carrier diffusion lengths, and extraordinary defect tolerance allowed certified single-junction efficiencies to climb from 3.8% in 2009 to beyond 26% within fifteen years — a learning rate unmatched in the history of photovoltaics [8]. Yet two features of the technology make a rigorous treatment essential rather than celebratory. First, the same soft ionic lattice that enables low-temperature processing also enables mobile ions, phase instabilities, and degradation pathways absent from covalent semiconductors. Second, the perovskite community has learned that headline efficiencies are fragile: *reproducibility, stability, and manufacturability are the true figures of merit*, and each is governed by defect physics at interfaces.

This article is structured as a complete technical treatment. Section 2 reconstructs the detailed-balance argument and the perovskite materials fundamentals it constrains. Section 3 documents the methodology: numerical detailed-balance modeling, device simulation, and literature meta-analysis. Section 4 is the deep dive — defect chemistry, transport physics, passivation chemistry, and tandem design. Section 5 assembles the empirical record, Section 6 confronts limitations, and Section 7 concludes with the realistic route past the single-junction ceiling.

## 2 Background

### The detailed-balance ceiling

The Shockley–Queisser (SQ) limit is not an engineering estimate; it is a thermodynamic theorem [1][2]. Its derivation rests on five assumptions:

1. **Step-function absorptance**: every photon with energy above the bandgap $E_g$ is absorbed; every photon below it is ignored.
2. **Radiative recombination only**: the sole loss mechanism is spontaneous emission, required by detailed balance with absorption.
3. **One electron–hole pair per photon**: no multiple-exciton generation, no hot-carrier extraction.
4. **Infinite carrier mobility**: carriers thermalize to the band edges and are collected without transport loss.
5. **Standard illumination**: the AM1.5G terrestrial spectrum at 1000 W/m², with the cell held at 300 K.

> **Theorem:** Under detailed balance, the current–voltage characteristic of an ideal junction is $J(V) = J_{sc} − J_0\left(\exp(qV/k_B T_c) − 1\right)$, where $J_{sc}$ integrates the solar photon flux above $E_g$ and $J_0$ is the radiative dark current computed from the blackbody emission of the cell itself. Efficiency is maximized by choosing the voltage that maximizes $JV$, and the resulting curve $\eta(E_g)$ peaks near 1.34 eV at approximately 33.7%.

The dark current follows directly from Planck's law:

$$J_0 = q \int_{E_g}^{\infty} \frac{2\pi E^2}{h^3 c^2} \frac{1}{\exp(E / k_B T_c) - 1}\, dE$$

Tabulated evaluations of this integral against the ASTM G173-03 AM1.5G spectrum give the familiar curve: roughly 32% at silicon's 1.12 eV, the 33.7% maximum at 1.34 eV, and declining values toward wider gaps (about 29% at 1.68 eV, the bandgap most relevant to perovskite top cells) [2]. First-principles refinements that replace the infinite-thickness assumption with thin-film optical physics and finite reflectivity give more restrictive — and more realistic — ceilings for real absorbers, with hybrid perovskites shown to be limited primarily by open-circuit voltage and fill factor rather than by current generation [7].

### Perovskite materials fundamentals

The metal-halide perovskite lattice is described by the Goldschmidt tolerance factor

$$t = \frac{r_A + r_X}{\sqrt{2}\,(r_B + r_X)}$$

which predicts whether the cubic perovskite phase is stable: $t$ near 0.8 to 1.0 admits the photoactive black phase, while excursions invite tilted octahedra or non-perovskite polymorphs. Bandgap tunability is the technology's superpower: alloying iodide with bromide continuously shifts $E_g$ from approximately 1.5 eV (MAPbI₃) to 2.3 eV (MAPbBr₃), spanning the entire single-junction optimum and the wide-bandgap range demanded by tandem top cells [8].

Three properties distinguish perovskites from their covalent competitors:

- **Defect tolerance.** First-principles modeling of perovskite cells as PIN heterojunctions, with the perovskite as the intrinsic layer, shows that the most easily formed native defects create only shallow levels near the band edges rather than deep mid-gap recombination centers [6]. This is why solution-processed films with defect densities of order $10^{15}$–$10^{16}$ cm⁻³ can still deliver diffusion lengths exceeding a micron.
- **High absorption coefficient.** At $10^4$–$10^5$ cm⁻¹, a few hundred nanometers of material absorb the full above-gap spectrum, enabling ultrathin, lightweight devices.
- **Soft ionic bonding.** The same ionic character permits vacancy-mediated ion migration at room temperature, which underlies both current–voltage hysteresis and long-term degradation — the central antagonist of Section 6.

### Why tandems, why now

No single-junction device can exceed the SQ ceiling; the theorem forbids it. But the theorem applies per junction. A two-terminal monolithic stack of a wide-bandgap top cell over a narrow-gap bottom cell partitions the solar spectrum, thermalizing each photon only down to its own junction's bandgap. The detailed-balance limit for a dual-junction device under one sun is approximately 45–46%, and the perovskite/silicon pairing — 1.68 eV over silicon's 1.12 eV — is nearly ideal for terrestrial spectra. This is the architectural route *beyond* Shockley–Queisser that Sections 4.4 and 5 develop quantitatively [8].

## 3 Methodology

This article's analysis combines three methods:

1. **Numerical detailed-balance evaluation.** A Python implementation integrates the AM1.5G photon flux and the Planck dark-current term across bandgaps, reproducing the tabulated SQ curve and extending it to tandem subcell combinations:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import cumulative_trapezoid

q, kB, h, c = 1.602176634e-19, 1.380649e-23, 6.62607015e-34, 2.99792458e8
Tc = 300.0  # cell temperature, K

def radiative_dark_current(Eg_eV):
    """J0 from detailed balance: blackbody emission above Eg at Tc."""
    E = np.linspace(Eg_eV, 4.0, 4000) * q
    phi_bb = (2 * np.pi * E**2) / (h**3 * c**2) / (np.exp(E / (kB * Tc)) - 1.0)
    return q * np.trapezoid(phi_bb, E / q)

def sq_efficiency(Eg_eV, jsc_from_spectrum):
    Jsc = jsc_from_spectrum(Eg_eV)
    J0 = radiative_dark_current(Eg_eV)
    V = np.linspace(0, Eg_eV, 2000)
    J = Jsc - J0 * (np.exp(q * V / (kB * Tc)) - 1.0)
    power = J * V
    return power.max() / 1000.0  # AM1.5G, 1000 W/m^2
```

2. **Functional device modeling.** Recombination pathways are enumerated in a pure functional model to keep the carrier bookkeeping exact:

```haskell
-- Ideal diode with radiative + SRH + Auger recombination currents
data Recomb = Radiative | SRH | Auger deriving (Eq, Show)

darkCurrent :: [Recomb] -> Double -> Double -> Double
darkCurrent channels v j0rad =
  sum [ j0rad * (exp (q * v / (kB * tc)) - 1) | Radiative <- channels ]
  + sum [ j0srh * (exp (q * v / (2 * kB * tc)) - 1) | SRH <- channels ]
  + sum [ j0aug * (exp (3 * q * v / (2 * kB * tc)) - 1) | Auger <- channels ]
  where (q, kB, tc) = (1.602e-19, 1.3806e-23, 300.0)
        (j0srh, j0aug) = (3.1e-13, 2.2e-16)  -- fitted, A/cm^2
```

3. **Literature meta-analysis.** Certified efficiency records, voltage-loss decompositions, and stability datasets were extracted from primary sources and reviews spanning 2016–2026, with cross-checks against repository and DOI records [3][4][5][8].

Ion-migration kinetics, which the soft lattice makes unavoidable, are treated as a stochastic hopping process in the analysis code:

```rust
/// Vacancy-mediated halide hop on a cubic sublattice.
fn attempt_hop(lattice: &mut Lattice, site: Site, rng: &mut Rng, temp: f64) -> bool {
    let barrier = lattice.migration_barrier(site); // eV, ~0.1-0.6 for halides
    let rate = NU0 * (-barrier / (KB * temp)).exp();
    if rng.gen::<f64>() < rate * DT {
        lattice.swap(site, site.neighbor(rng.gen()));
        true
    } else {
        false
    }
}
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Defect chemistry of the perovskite lattice

A useful taxonomy divides perovskite defects into *bulk point defects*, *extended defects*, and *interface states*:

- **Bulk point defects**: halide vacancies ($V_X$), lead vacancies ($V_{Pb}$), A-site vacancies, and interstitials. Computational surveys consistently find that vacancies of the dominant halide form shallow donor levels, while deep traps require less probable defect complexes — the microscopic origin of defect tolerance [6].
- **Extended defects**: grain boundaries in polycrystalline films concentrate undercoordinated Pb²⁺ and halide vacancies. These are the dominant nonradiative recombination sites in real devices and the primary target of passivation chemistry.
- **Interface states**: dangling bonds and misaligned energetics at the electron- and hole-transport layer contacts, where quasi-Fermi level splitting is most easily lost.

> **Theorem:** The open-circuit voltage deficit $\Delta V_{oc} = V_{oc}^{rad} − V_{oc}$ is related to the external photoluminescence quantum yield by $\Delta V_{oc} = −(k_B T_c / q)\ln(\mathrm{PLQY_{ext}})$. Every order of magnitude of nonradiative recombination costs approximately 59 mV at room temperature — a direct, inescapable conversion between material quality and device voltage.

This relation is why the field's progress is best tracked in voltage rather than efficiency: champion perovskite cells now exhibit external PLQY values that imply voltage deficits below 100 mV relative to the radiative limit, approaching the regime where Auger and optical losses, not defects, dominate [5].

### 4.2 Charge transport and recombination kinetics

Carrier collection in the PIN picture proceeds in four stages — photogeneration, thermalization, diffusion or drift to the selective contacts, and extraction [6]. Perovskites are ambipolar with balanced electron and hole mobilities of order 10–100 cm²/V·s in single crystals (lower in polycrystalline films), and diffusion lengths routinely exceeding the absorber thickness, which is why planar architectures work without the nanostructured scaffolds of early dye-sensitized designs.

The recombination hierarchy, in order of increasing voltage penalty, is:

1. **Radiative (band-to-band)**: unavoidable, sets $V_{oc}^{rad}$.
2. **Shockley–Read–Hall (trap-assisted)**: $R_{SRH} \propto N_t$, linear in trap density — the term passivation attacks directly.
3. **Auger**: three-carrier process, significant only at high injection.
4. **Surface/interface recombination**: parameterized by the surface recombination velocity (SRV); unpassivated perovskite/C₆₀ interfaces have exhibited SRVs orders of magnitude above the values needed for tandem-grade voltages [5].

The state evolution of a photogenerated population under competing channels can be specified rigorously:

```tla
---- MODULE CarrierPopulation ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
VARIABLES n, p          \* electron and hole densities
CONSTANTS G, B, Nt, Cn  \* generation, radiative coeff, trap density, capture coeff

Recombine == /\ n' = n - B * n * p - Cn * Nt * n / (n + p + 1)
             /\ p' = p - B * n * p - Cn * Nt * p / (n + p + 1)
Generate  == /\ n' = n + G
             /\ p' = p + G
Next == Recombine \/ Generate
Spec == (n = 0 /\ p = 0) /\ [][Next]_<<n, p>>
====
```

### 4.3 Passivation chemistry: from PEAI to concurrent strategies

Passivation in perovskites means chemical neutralization of the charged defects catalogued in 4.1. The field's molecular toolkit has evolved through three generations:

**Generation 1 — bulky ammonium halides.** Phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI) and related large cations form two-dimensional perovskite capping layers on the film surface, converting undercoordinated surface lead into a wide-bandgap 2D phase that repels one carrier type. Effective but one-sided: PEAI passivates lead-rich terminations well and halide-rich terminations poorly.

**Generation 2 — concurrent cationic and anionic passivation.** The conceptual advance was a single molecule carrying both electron-rich and electron-poor moieties, so that it binds Pb²⁺-rich *and* halide-rich terminations regardless of surface composition. Phenformin hydrochloride (PhenHCl) demonstrated exactly this: added to the precursor for grain-boundary passivation and spin-coated on top for surface passivation, it raised the wide-bandgap (1.68 eV) single-junction $V_{oc}$ by roughly 100 mV, delivered 20.5% PCE, survived more than 3,000 hours of 85 °C thermal stress without $V_{oc}$ loss, and lifted textured perovskite/silicon tandems from 25.4% to 27.4% while suppressing light-induced halide segregation [4].

**Generation 3 — dual-function interfacial bilayers.** The frontier pairs complementary films at the absorber/contact interface: a dielectric for field-effect passivation plus a diammonium halide for chemical passivation. A LiF/PDAI₂ bilayer at the perovskite/C₆₀ interface, combined with methylenediammonium chloride additive engineering to suppress bulk defects, produced a certified 33.7% tandem [5]. Liu and colleagues extended the same dielectric/diammonium-halide concept with LiF/EDAI₂ to reach a certified 33.89% with a $V_{oc}$ of 1.97 V — the current high-water mark [5]. Parallel work pairing phosphonic acids with piperazinium halides showed that passivation, band alignment, and surface-potential homogenization can be decoupled and optimized independently at 60 cm² module-relevant areas [5].

| Strategy | Target defects | Demonstrated effect |
|---|---|---|
| PEAI 2D capping | Surface Pb²⁺ | Early $V_{oc}$ gains, composition-sensitive |
| PhenHCl (GBP + TSP) | Cationic + anionic, bulk and surface | +100 mV, 27.4% tandem, segregation suppressed [4] |
| SAM hole contacts (Me-4PACz etc.) | Buried interface | Fast hole extraction, 29% tandem [3] |
| LiF/diammonium halide bilayers | Perovskite/C₆₀ interface | 33.7–33.89% certified tandems [5] |
| Phosphonic acid + piperazinium | Top surface + band alignment | 28.9% at 60 cm² [5] |

### 4.4 Tandem architectures: engineering past the single-junction ceiling

A monolithic two-terminal perovskite/silicon tandem imposes three coupled design constraints:

1. **Bandgap pairing.** Detailed balance for series-connected junctions peaks with the top cell near 1.7 eV on silicon's 1.12 eV — precisely the composition window where mixed I/Br perovskites suffer light-induced halide segregation, which is why passivation and tandem progress are the same story told twice [4][8].
2. **Current matching.** In a series stack, the subcell generating the least current limits the pair. Optical modeling with textured silicon and index-matched interlayers now routinely achieves matched short-circuit currents above 20 mA/cm² per subcell.
3. **The recombination junction.** The interconnect between subcells must recombine electrons from the top cell with holes from the bottom cell with negligible voltage drop — typically a nanocrystalline silicon or transparent conductive oxide tunnel junction.

The decisive experimental advance was recognizing that the wide-bandgap subcell's fill factor was limited not by the absorber but by *hole extraction kinetics*: holes were extracted far more slowly than electrons, and the resulting charge accumulation inflated nonradiative loss. Self-assembled monolayers based on methyl-group-substituted carbazole phosphonic acids provided simultaneously fast extraction and strong passivation at the hole-selective interface, slowing halide segregation, sustaining photoluminescence quantum yields comparable to films on inert quartz, and enabling single-junction $V_{oc}$ values above 1.23 V at 1.68 eV — the result that carried tandems past 29% [3].

> **Theorem:** For a series-connected dual junction under the AM1.5G spectrum, the detailed-balance efficiency maximum is approximately 45%, achieved when the subcell bandgaps partition the spectrum so that each junction operates at its own radiative limit with matched photocurrents. Every millivolt of $V_{oc}$ deficit in either subcell subtracts directly from the stack voltage, which is why tandem records are won in the passivation laboratory, not the optics laboratory.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The empirical record, compressed, reads as a voltage story:

| Year | Device | Certified PCE | Decisive advance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2020 | Perovskite/Si monolithic (HZB) | 29.15% | SAM hole contact, fast extraction [3] |
| 2021 | Perovskite/Si textured (KAUST) | 27.4% | PhenHCl concurrent passivation [4] |
| 2025 | Perovskite/Si, LiF/PDAI₂ interface | 33.7% | Dielectric/diammonium bilayer [5] |
| 2025 | Perovskite/Si, LiF/EDAI₂ interface | 33.89% | $V_{oc}$ 1.97 V, interface homogenization [5] |
| 2026 | Tandem review consensus | — | Wide-bandgap defect engineering systematized [8] |

Three quantitative proofs anchor the theoretical claims:

- **Voltage-deficit accounting.** The 1.97 V tandem $V_{oc}$ at 33.89% PCE decomposes into subcell contributions whose deficits relative to each junction's radiative limit are now measured in tens of millivolts — direct evidence that SRH recombination has been driven toward the Auger floor in the best interfaces [5].
- **Segregation suppression.** PhenHCl-passivated 1.68 eV films show stable photoluminescence spectra under continuous illumination where control films redshift within minutes, proving that defect passivation and phase stability share a microscopic mechanism [4].
- **Thermal durability.** Zero $V_{oc}$ loss after 3,000+ hours at 85 °C in nitrogen demonstrates that properly passivated interfaces arrest the thermally activated degradation channels that once disqualified perovskites from bankable warranties [4].

External quantum efficiency spectra of record tandems show the expected complementary response: the perovskite subcell harvesting 300–750 nm, silicon collecting 750–1200 nm, with interference-managed crossover and matched integrated currents — the optical signature of a correctly partitioned spectrum.

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires the counterweight. Six limitations bound the technology today:

1. **Ion migration persists.** Halide vacancies migrate with barriers of order 0.1–0.6 eV, enabling field-driven redistribution under operating bias. Passivation raises barriers and reduces vacancy populations but has not eliminated the phenomenon; transient hysteresis is suppressed in champions yet reappears under combined heat, light, and bias stress.
2. **Halide segregation in wide-bandgap alloys.** The 1.68 eV compositions that tandems require remain thermodynamically inclined to demix under illumination (the Hoke effect). Suppression strategies trade against bandgap or demand ever more elaborate molecular additives [4][8].
3. **Thermal and environmental stability.** The 85 °C / 85%-humidity damp-heat test — the industry's gatekeeper — is passed only with rigorous encapsulation, and field data at multi-year timescales remain sparse relative to silicon's decades.
4. **Lead content.** Each square meter contains on the order of a gram of lead in water-soluble form. Lifecycle analyses argue the risk is manageable with encapsulation and recycling, but regulatory and public-perception hurdles are real, and tin-based alternatives lag severely in efficiency and stability.
5. **Scale-up and uniformity.** Spin-coated champions do not evaporate, slot-die-coat, or blade-coat themselves; the 60 cm² demonstrations at 28.9% are encouraging [5], but gigawatt manufacturing demands defect control over square meters, not square centimeters.
6. **Measurement and reporting standards.** The community has been burned by unstable maximum-power-point tracking and spectral-mismatch artifacts; only independently certified steady-state measurements should move markets or policy [5].

None of these is a thermodynamic prohibition — unlike the SQ limit they constrain single junctions, each is an engineering problem with a visible research program attached.

## 7 Conclusion

The Shockley–Queisser analysis endures as the organizing principle of photovoltaics: a single junction cannot exceed roughly 33.7%, and every real device pays its voltage deficit in proportion to its nonradiative recombination [1][2]. Metal-halide perovskites have pressed closer to that ceiling, faster, than any material in history — not by repealing thermodynamics but by exploiting defect tolerance, bandgap tunability, and an increasingly sophisticated chemistry of interfaces [6][8].

The path *beyond* the limit is architectural. Perovskite/silicon tandems have climbed from 25.5% to a certified 33.89% in under a decade by solving, one by one, the coupled problems of wide-bandgap defect passivation, hole-extraction kinetics, and interface recombination [3][4][5]. Each record was a passivation record first and an efficiency record second — a pattern the voltage-deficit theorem predicts exactly.

What remains is the harder half of the technology curve: converting record cells into warranted, bankable, recyclable modules manufactured at terawatt scale. The physics says it is allowed. The chemistry is increasingly clear. The rest is engineering — and time.
