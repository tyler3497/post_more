# Register Allocation via Graph Coloring: Chaitin's Formulation, Iterated Coalescing, and Linear Scan

## Abstract

Register allocation is the compiler back-end task of mapping an unbounded set of program temporaries onto a finite set of *K* machine registers, inserting spill code only when unavoidable. This thesis reconstructs the four-decade arc of the graph-coloring formulation: Gregory Chaitin's 1981 reduction of allocation to *K*-coloring of the interference graph and its cost/degree spill heuristic [1][2]; Briggs, Cooper, and Torczon's optimistic coloring, conservative coalescing, and live-range splitting refinements [3]; George and Appel's iterated register coalescing, which interleaves simplification, coalescing, freezing, and spilling in a single worklist-driven loop [4]; and the linear-scan counter-revolution of Poletto and Sarkar, which abandons graphs entirely for interval arithmetic in pursuit of JIT-scale compile speed [5]. We develop the SSA-era breakthrough of Hack, Grund, and Goos — SSA interference graphs are chordal and therefore optimally colorable in quadratic time [6] — and Smith, Ramsey, and Holloway's generalized plug-in allocator [7]. Empirical evidence and complexity theorems are presented alongside executable-style pseudocode; we conclude with the irreducible limits of the problem: *K*-colorability is NP-complete, coalescing is NP-complete, and the quality–speed frontier is fundamental [8][9][10].

## 1. Introduction

Of all the phases in a modern optimizing compiler, few exert as much influence on generated-code performance as *register allocation*. The register file is the fastest tier of the memory hierarchy, and every temporary a compiler can keep in a register is one fewer load or store executed at run time. The formulation of the problem is deceptively simple:

> **Definition.** Given a program *P* with a set of temporaries *T* and a machine with *K* allocatable registers, a register allocation is a mapping *color : T → {r₁, …, r_K, ⊥}* such that no two temporaries that are simultaneously live share a register. A temporary mapped to *⊥* is *spilled*: its value is stored to memory and reloaded at each use.

Early compilers — the original FORTRAN compiler of the 1950s included — assigned registers locally within basic blocks using ad hoc heuristics. The breakthrough arrived in 1981, when Gregory Chaitin and colleagues at IBM's Thomas J. Watson Research Center observed that the *interference* relation between temporaries induces an undirected graph, and that allocating registers is equivalent to coloring that graph [1]. This single insight converted register allocation from a bag of heuristics into a combinatorial optimization problem with a crisp mathematical statement, launching a research program that continues today.

This thesis examines that program in full. We begin with Chaitin's formulation and its celebrated *simplify–spill–select* heuristic (§3), trace Briggs' corrections to its two most damaging weaknesses — pessimistic spilling and reckless coalescing — (§4.2), present George and Appel's iterated register coalescing as the canonical integrated allocator (§4.3), and contrast it with the linear-scan family that dominates just-in-time compilers (§4.4). We then analyze how static single assignment (SSA) form restructured the theory: SSA interference graphs are chordal, which makes coloring tractable (§4.5). Throughout, we weigh empirical measurements against complexity-theoretic lower bounds, because the field's history is precisely the history of negotiating between them.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Liveness and the Interference Graph

Dataflow analysis computes, for every program point, the set of temporaries that are *live*: defined earlier, used later, and not redefined in between. Two temporaries *interfere* when they are live at a common program point (or, in Chaitin's original formulation, when one is live at the definition of the other) — they cannot share a register and are therefore connected by an edge in the *interference graph* *G = (V, E)*. Register allocation with *K* registers is then exactly the problem of *K*-coloring *G*: assigning colors (registers) to vertices so that adjacent vertices differ [2].

The central difficulty is that *K*-colorability is **NP-complete** for *K ≥ 3*. Chaitin acknowledged this from the start; the practical question was never optimality but the design of heuristics that color real interference graphs well. Empirically, interference graphs arising from programs are "easy" — sparse, with low degeneracy — which is why greedy heuristics succeed where worst-case theory despairs.

### 2.2 The Spill/Coalesce Duality

Two subproblems complicate pure coloring:

1. **Spilling.** When *G* is not *K*-colorable, some live ranges must be *split* and their values spilled to memory: loads and stores are inserted, and allocation repeats. Spilling is the escape hatch, but it is expensive — memory traffic — so the choice of *what* to spill dominates code quality.
2. **Coalescing.** Copy instructions *t ← s* introduce an opportunity: if *s* and *t* receive the same register, the copy can be deleted. Merging the two vertices (coalescing) removes the copy but adds edges, potentially destroying colorability. Deciding *which* copies to coalesce while preserving colorability is itself **NP-complete** [10].

Coalescing uses *move-related* pairs — also called *preference edges* — that are distinct from interference edges: they express a *desire* to share a color, not a *prohibition*. Managing the tension between interference and preference is the heart of the George–Appel allocator.

### 2.3 Live Ranges and Splitting

A *live range* is the maximal region over which a temporary is live, possibly spanning many basic blocks. *Live-range splitting* breaks a long range into shorter ones connected by copies, reducing interference and enabling finer-grained spilling. Briggs showed that splitting before spilling — particularly around procedure calls, which clobber caller-saved registers — sharply improves allocation quality [3].

## 3. Methodology

Our exposition follows the classical *build–simplify–spill–select* decomposition, with the allocator itself presented as executable pseudocode. The interference graph is built from liveness information; a greedy *simplify* pass repeatedly removes vertices of degree less than *K* (such vertices can always be colored once their neighbors are, regardless of the colors chosen); if simplification stalls, a *potential spill* candidate is chosen and pushed onto the stack anyway; finally, *select* pops vertices and assigns the lowest available color [2].

The spill heuristic is Chaitin's famous **cost/degree** metric: for each vertex *v*,

> *spillPriority(v) = estimatedSpillCost(v) / degree(v)*

The estimated cost sums, over all uses and definitions, a static estimate of execution frequency (e.g., 10^loopDepth) — spilling inside loops is punishing — divided by degree, which normalizes against the vertex's burden on the graph. Vertices local to a single basic block with no intervening definitions receive *infinite* spill cost: spilling them cannot help, since they interfere with nothing removable.

```python
def simplify_select(interference_graph, K):
    """Chaitin's heuristic: returns a color assignment or spill candidates."""
    stack, graph = [], interference_graph.copy()
    while graph:
        # Simplify: peel off trivially colorable vertices
        peelable = [v for v in graph if graph.degree(v) < K]
        if peelable:
            v = peelable[0]
        else:
            # Potential spill: lowest cost/degree ratio
            v = min(graph, key=lambda x: spill_cost(x) / graph.degree(x))
        stack.append(v)
        graph.remove(v)
    color = {}
    spilled = []
    while stack:  # Select: color in reverse removal order
        v = stack.pop()
        used = {color[u] for u in interference_graph.neighbors(v) if u in color}
        free = [c for c in range(K) if c not in used]
        if free:
            color[v] = free[0]
        else:
            spilled.append(v)   # Chaitin spills here; Briggs defers (see §4.2)
    return color, spilled
```

This optimistic-pushing, greedy-coloring skeleton is the chassis on which every subsequent allocator in this thesis is built.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Chaitin's Formulation and the Spill Heuristic

Chaitin's 1981 paper, *"Register Allocation via Coloring"* [1], and its 1982 successor on spilling [2], established three durable principles. First, **interference as coloring**: the reduction itself, mapping temporaries to vertices, registers to colors. Second, the **simplify invariant**:

> **Theorem:** If every vertex of *G* has degree less than *K*, then *G* is *K*-colorable by the greedy algorithm.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Order the vertices arbitrarily and color each with the lowest color unused by its already-colored neighbors. A vertex has fewer than *K* neighbors, so at least one of the *K* colors is always free. ∎

Third, the **cost/degree spill metric**, which elegantly balances a live range's *value* (keeping it in a register saves expensive memory traffic) against its *cost* (its degree measures how many other ranges it blocks). The allocator iterates: build, simplify, spill, rewrite, repeat until the graph colors cleanly [2].

### 4.2 Briggs' Conservative Coalescing and Optimistic Coloring

Chaitin's allocator had two structural flaws. First, its coalescing was *reckless*: Chaitin merged every copy-related pair that did not directly interfere, which could — and did — produce uncolorable graphs, trading a saved move for a spill. Second, its coloring was *pessimistic*: any vertex of degree ≥ *K* was spilled during simplification, even when a color would have been available at select time.

Briggs' Rice dissertation and the TOPLAS 1994 paper with Cooper and Torczon [3] repaired both:

- **Optimistic coloring.** Instead of spilling high-degree vertices immediately, push them onto the stack like any other vertex. During *select*, a pushed vertex may still find a free color — its high-degree neighbors might not all use distinct colors. Spilling becomes a last resort, committed only when select genuinely fails. Briggs' measurements show optimistic coloring is *never worse* and often strictly better [3].
- **Conservative coalescing.** Merge a move-related pair *(u, v)* into *uv* only when it is *provably safe*:

> **Briggs criterion:** Coalesce *u* and *v* only if the number of neighbors of the merged node *uv* with degree ≥ *K* is strictly less than *K*.

> *Why it is safe.* Simplification removes all vertices of degree < *K* before spilling is ever considered. After coalescing, the merged node *uv* has fewer than *K* "significant" (degree ≥ *K*) neighbors; hence *uv* itself can be simplified away exactly as Chaitin's invariant requires, and colorability is preserved. ∎

- **Live-range splitting.** Briggs aggressively splits live ranges at selected points — notably around calls — *before* coloring, folding splitting into the Chaitin framework rather than treating it as a post-pass.

The conservative criterion is safe but timid: it declines many beneficial merges. This conservatism motivated the next advance.

### 4.3 Iterated Register Coalescing

George and Appel's *"Iterated Register Coalescing"* [4] — presented at POPL 1996 and in TOPLAS 18(3) — interleaves simplification and coalescing so that each enables the other. The algorithm maintains four node worklists and five move sets:

| Worklist | Contents |
|---|---|
| `simplifyWorklist` | degree < *K*, not move-related |
| `freezeWorklist` | degree < *K*, move-related |
| `spillWorklist` | degree ≥ *K* |
| `worklistMoves` / `activeMoves` / `frozenMoves` / `coalescedMoves` / `constrainedMoves` | move partition by status |

The main loop repeats five phases — **Build, Simplify, Coalesce, Freeze, Potential Spill** — until the worklists drain, then **Select** assigns colors (with precolored machine registers fixed in advance):

1. **Build** constructs the interference graph and move lists.
2. **Simplify** pops low-degree, non-move-related nodes onto the stack.
3. **Coalesce** attempts each enabled move with a stronger test than Briggs':

> **George criterion:** Coalesce *u* and *v* if, for every neighbor *t* of *u*, either *t* interferes with *v* or *degree(t) < K*.

> *Safety argument.* Let *S* be the neighbors of *u* with degree < *K*. Simplification alone would remove all of *S*. After coalescing, *uv*'s neighbor set is a subset of *u*'s ∪ *v*'s; every neighbor not shared with *v* lies in *S* and is still simplifiable — so the coalesced graph's simplifiable core is a *subgraph* of the original's, and no new spill is forced. ∎

4. **Freeze** abandons coalescing for moves adjacent to low-degree nodes (moving the node to `simplifyWorklist`) when no coalescing is currently possible — simplification then unblocks further coalescing.
5. **Potential Spill** selects a spill candidate by cost/degree and optimistically pushes it.

Iterated register coalescing (IRC) became the canonical textbook allocator — the imperative pseudocode in [4] is implemented, nearly verbatim, in production compilers including CompCert. Its strength is the *interaction*: coalescing lowers degrees, which feeds simplification, which enables more coalescing. Later work proposed *optimistic coalescing* (Park & Moon) as a less conservative alternative, but IRC remains the reference design.

### 4.4 Linear Scan: Abandoning the Graph

For just-in-time compilers and interactive environments, even IRC is too slow: building and coloring interference graphs costs *O(n²)* in practice. Poletto and Sarkar's *"Linear Scan Register Allocation"* [5] (TOPLAS 1999) replaces the graph with a single sweep over *live intervals*:

```python
def linear_scan(intervals, K):
    """Poletto–Sarkar linear scan. Intervals sorted by start point."""
    active = []          # currently live, sorted by increasing end point
    assignment = {}
    for i in sorted(intervals, key=lambda x: x.start):
        # Expire intervals that ended before i starts
        for j in [a for a in active if a.end <= i.start]:
            active.remove(j)
            free_register(assignment[j])
        if len(active) == K:
            spill_at_interval(i, active)   # spill i or a longer-lived member
        else:
            assignment[i] = allocate_register()
            active.append(i)
            active.sort(key=lambda x: x.end)
    return assignment
```

The algorithm is linear in the number of intervals (after sorting) and dramatically simpler to implement. Its Achilles' heel is that intervals *overestimate* liveness — an interval spans from first definition to last use, including "holes" where the value is dead — so linear scan spills where a graph allocator would not. Nevertheless, Poletto and Sarkar measured it as *several times faster* than even a fast graph-coloring allocator, producing code within **12%** of IRC's quality on nearly all benchmarks [5]. Traub, Holloway, and Smith's *second-chance binpacking* [9] trades some of that speed back for quality by reconsidering spill decisions, and Wimmer and Mössenböck's *interval splitting* [11] closes much of the remaining gap by splitting long intervals at loop boundaries — splitting being the linear-scan analogue of Briggs' live-range splitting.

### 4.5 SSA Form: Chordality Changes Everything

The deepest theoretical advance came from static single assignment form. Hack, Grund, and Goos proved in 2006 that the interference graphs of SSA-form programs are **chordal** — every cycle of length ≥ 4 has a chord — and chordal graphs admit a *perfect elimination order* (PEO) [6]:

> **Theorem (SSA chordality):** The dominance relation of an SSA program induces a perfect elimination order on its interference graph; hence SSA interference graphs are chordal.
>
> *Consequence.* A chordal graph can be colored *optimally* — with exactly *χ(G)* colors, where *χ* is the chromatic number — in *O(K·|V|)* time by greedy coloring along the PEO, without ever building the graph explicitly. A post-order walk of the dominator tree yields the order.

This *decouples* the three tasks that IRC fuses: **spilling** reduces register pressure to *K* (a separate, well-defined problem), **coloring** is then optimal and trivial, and **coalescing** becomes an independent post-pass. The catch, proved by Bouchez et al. [10], is that coalescing remains **NP-complete even on SSA programs** — chordality buys optimal coloring but not optimal copy elimination. SSA φ-functions are handled as *parallel copies* on control-flow edges, i.e., register permutations implementable by swaps; minimizing these copies is the job of coalescing heuristics, recoloring passes, or register *biasing* during assignment [6].

Smith, Ramsey, and Holloway's generalized allocator [7] takes the complementary engineering view: rather than fixing one policy, it factors the graph-coloring allocator into pluggable components (build, simplify, coalesce, spill, select), letting compiler writers mix Chaitin-style, Briggs-style, and IRC-style policies within one framework — an explicit recognition that no single heuristic dominates all programs and machines.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Complexity Landscape

| Problem | Complexity | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| *K*-coloring interference graphs (*K ≥ 3*) | NP-complete | [2] |
| Aggressive coalescing (max copies removed) | NP-complete | [10] |
| Coalescing on SSA-form programs | NP-complete | [10] |
| Coloring chordal (SSA) interference graphs | *O(K·|V|)*, optimal | [6] |
| Linear scan allocation | *O(n log n)* incl. sort | [5] |
| IRC (George–Appel) | subquadratic in practice | [4] |

### 5.2 Measured Quality and Speed

- **Linear scan vs. graph coloring** [5]: up to several times faster compile time than a fast graph-coloring allocator; generated code within 12% of aggressive IRC-based coloring on all but two benchmarks.
- **Second-chance binpacking** [9]: closes part of the quality gap at the cost of additional compile time, establishing the speed–quality Pareto frontier for linear methods.
- **CISC machines with few registers** [8]: Appel and George measured IRC producing 84 spill instructions on a 32-register machine but 22,123 on an 8-register machine (~14% of all instructions); their ILP-based optimal spilling plus optimistic coalescing yielded Pentium code 9.5% faster than SSA-splitting with IRC — demonstrating that few-register targets stress-test every heuristic in this thesis.

### 5.3 Proof Sketch: Optimality on Chordal Graphs

> **Theorem:** Greedy coloring along a perfect elimination order uses exactly *χ(G)* colors on a chordal graph.
>
> *Proof sketch.* In a PEO, each vertex's later neighbors form a clique. When greedy coloring reaches vertex *v*, its already-colored neighbors that constrain it are a subset of a clique of size at most *χ(G) − 1*; hence a color ≤ *χ(G)* is always available, and *χ(G)* is a lower bound. ∎

This is the mathematical core of [6]: SSA gives the PEO for free from the dominator tree, turning the NP-complete coloring problem into a linear-time greedy procedure.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Intractability is fundamental.** *K*-coloring is NP-complete [2] and coalescing is NP-complete even under SSA [10]; every allocator in this thesis is a heuristic, and adversarial programs defeat all of them.
2. **Conservative criteria leave moves on the table.** Both the Briggs and George criteria are *sufficient* but not *necessary* for safe coalescing — profitable merges are declined. Optimistic coalescing recovers some, at the cost of occasional de-coalescing complexity.
3. **Linear scan's liveness overestimation.** Intervals lack "holes": a value dead across a long stretch still occupies its register, causing avoidable spills [5]. Exact lifetime-hole representations exist but forfeit linearity.
4. **SSA's coalescing gap.** Chordality solves coloring, not copy elimination; φ-lowering introduces parallel copies that the allocator must then remove, and that removal is NP-hard [6][10].
5. **Machine irregularities.** Register classes, aliasing (e.g., x86's `al`/`ax`/`eax`), precolored calling-convention registers, and multi-register values all violate the uniform-*K*-colors model; Briggs' multigraph extensions [3] address some of this at added complexity.
6. **Few-register pathologies.** On machines with ≤ 8 registers, IRC spills excessively and coalesces too conservatively; optimal ILP-based spilling [8] wins but does not scale to large procedures.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The graph-coloring formulation of register allocation has proven extraordinarily durable: Chaitin's 1981 reduction [1] still describes the problem, and every major advance since has been a refinement of *how* to color, *what* to spill, and *which* copies to merge. Briggs taught the field to be optimistic about coloring and conservative about coalescing [3]; George and Appel showed that interleaving the phases in one worklist loop compounds their benefits [4]; Poletto and Sarkar demonstrated that abandoning the graph entirely buys order-of-magnitude compile speed for a single-digit quality cost [5]; and Hack, Grund, and Goos revealed that SSA form had made the hard part — coloring — tractable all along [6]. The remaining frontier is coalescing: NP-complete in general and in SSA [10], it is where allocation quality is now won or lost, and where the next four decades of heuristics will be aimed.
