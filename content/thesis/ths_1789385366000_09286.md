# Suffix Arrays and the Burrows–Wheeler Transform: Linear-Time Construction, the FM-Index, and Repetition-Aware Indexing for Pangenomes

## Abstract

The suffix array and the Burrows–Wheeler transform (BWT) form the theoretical core of modern compressed full-text indexing. This thesis traces the arc from Manber and Myers' suffix array through linear-time construction by induced sorting (SA-IS), the FM-index and its backward search, to the run-length *r*-index that exploits repetitiveness, and finally to pangenome graph indexing. We formalize the LF-mapping, prove the correctness of backward search, analyze space bounds in terms of the number *r* of BWT runs, and survey empirical behavior on genomic and versioned collections. The unifying thesis is that the BWT run count *r* has emerged as the central repetitiveness measure around which an entire family of optimal indexes is organized.

---

## 1 Intro

Pattern matching is the primitive operation of text processing: given a pattern *P* of length *m* and a text *T* of length *n*, report all occurrences of *P* in *T*. Online algorithms such as Knuth–Morris–Pratt and Boyer–Moore scan *T* in time proportional to *n*, but in information retrieval and genomics the text is static while thousands of queries arrive over time. A *full-text index* preprocesses *T* once so that each query runs in time depending only on *m* and the output size.

The suffix array, introduced by Manber and Myers in 1990, is the canonical full-text index: it stores the permutation of text positions that sorts all suffixes lexicographically, supporting pattern search by binary search in *O(m log n)* time. The Burrows–Wheeler transform, devised in 1994 for block-sorting compression, turned out to be far more than a compression trick. Ferragina and Manzini showed in their FOCS 2000 paper — the FM-index — that the BWT simultaneously *compresses* the text and *indexes* it, supporting counting of pattern occurrences in time proportional to the pattern length alone, without ever decompressing.

Three decades of research have refined every corner of this pipeline. Suffix array construction went from *O(n log n)* prefix doubling to true linear time via skew algorithms and, most elegantly, induced sorting (SA-IS) [1][2]. The FM-index begat the compressed suffix array, the bidirectional BWT, and the compressed suffix tree, all constructible in small space in (randomized) linear time [2]. When texts became *highly repetitive* — thousands of human genomes, versioned codebases — entropy-based compression stalled, and the field pivoted to repetitiveness-aware measures. The number *r* of equal-letter runs in the BWT emerged as the measure of choice: the *r*-index of Gagie, Navarro and Prezza counts and *locates* in *O(r)* space, closing a decade-old open problem [4][5]. Today the same machinery indexes entire pangenome graphs [8]. This thesis presents the theory end to end.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Suffix arrays and the LCP array

Let *T* be a string of length *n* over an ordered alphabet Σ, terminated by a sentinel *$* smaller than every character. The *suffix array* SA is the permutation of the positions 0 through n−1 such that the suffixes *T*(SA(0)), *T*(SA(1)), …, *T*(SA(n−1)) are in lexicographic order. Its inverse, ISA, satisfies ISA(SA(i)) = i. The *longest common prefix* array LCP records, for each adjacent pair of suffixes in sorted order, the length of their shared prefix. Together SA and LCP capture the entire suffix trie in *O(n)* integers and support pattern matching by binary search: each comparison walks at most *m* characters, yielding *O(m log n)* counting time.

> **Theorem:** With SA and LCP, the number of occurrences of *P* in *T* can be counted in *O(m + log n)* time.

*Proof sketch.* Binary search over the SA interval is accelerated by the LCP array: the standard Kasai-enhanced search maintains how many characters of *P* are already known to match the current interval boundaries, so each step extends the match by at least one character or halves the interval. Hence total character comparisons are *O(m)* and interval halvings *O(log n)*. ∎

### 2.2 The Burrows–Wheeler transform

Consider the *n × n* matrix of all cyclic rotations of *T*, sorted lexicographically. Let *F* be its first column and *L* its last. The BWT of *T* is simply the column *L*. Equivalently, once SA is known,

```
BWT[i] = T[(SA[i] - 1) mod n]
```

For *T* = `BANANA$`, the sorted rotations give *F* = `$AAABNN` and *L* = `ANNB$AA`, so BWT(`BANANA$`) = `ANNB$AA`. Two facts make the BWT invertible and indexable. First, *F* is just the characters of *T* in sorted order, so it is recoverable from *L* by counting. Second, the *LF-mapping* pairs each row's last character with the row beginning with the same occurrence of that character: the *k*-th occurrence of character *c* in *L* corresponds to the *k*-th occurrence of *c* in *F*. This stability of equal characters under sorting is the entire engine of BWT-based indexing.

Formally, let *C(c)* be the number of characters in *T* strictly smaller than *c*, and let *Occ(c, i)* count occurrences of *c* in *L* up to position *i*. Then

```
LF(i) = C(L[i]) + Occ(L[i], i)
```

maps a row to the row prefixed by its last character. Iterating LF from the row ending in *$* reconstructs *T* backwards — the BWT is a permutation, hence a lossless transform.

### 2.3 Rank, select, and wavelet trees

Backward search needs *Occ(c, i)* — a *rank* query — answered fast on a compressed representation of *L*. The classic solution is the *wavelet tree*, which recursively partitions the alphabet and answers rank in *O(log σ)* time using *n log σ + o(n log σ)* bits, or *O(1)*-ish time with Huffman shaping. *Select* queries (position of the *k*-th *c*) are symmetric. These primitives turn the BWT string into a navigable index.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Generations of suffix array construction

Suffix array construction algorithms fall into three generations:

1. **Prefix doubling** (Manber–Myers, Larsson–Sadakane): sort by the first *2^k* characters, doubling *k* each round. *O(n log n)* time, simple and cache-friendly; the basis of the widely used *divsufsort* family in practice.
2. **Recursive skew** (Kärkkäinen–Sanders–Burkhardt, 2006): sort a *difference cover* sample of suffixes (e.g. positions not divisible by 3), sort the sample recursively after renaming triples to ranks, then merge. Achieves deterministic *O(n)* time with a clean divide-and-conquer structure.
3. **Induced sorting** (SA-IS; Nong, Zhang, Chan, 2009): classify suffixes as *L-type* or *S-type*, sort only the LMS (leftmost S-type) substrings recursively, then *induce* the full order in linear scans. Also *O(n)* but with tiny constants and excellent locality.

Modern small-space construction builds the BWT directly in deterministic *O(n)* time using *O(n log σ)* bits of working space, and derives the compressed suffix array, compressed suffix tree, and bidirectional BWT index in randomized linear time within the same budget [2]. For highly repetitive inputs, optimal construction runs in time proportional to *n / log_σ n + r · polylog n*, which is optimal since merely reading the input costs *n / log_σ n* packed time [6].

### 3.2 From suffix array to FM-index

The FM-index replaces *T* with three structures: the BWT string *L* (in a wavelet tree or run-length encoding), the *C* array (σ integers), and a sampled suffix array for locating. *Counting* uses backward search; *locating* walks LF-steps from a BWT row until hitting a sampled SA position, then subtracts the steps taken; *extracting* a substring walks LF from a known position. All operations rest on rank queries over *L*.

### 3.3 Repetition-aware methodology

Classical FM-indexes compress to the *k*-th order empirical entropy, which cannot exploit long-range repetitiveness: one thousand near-identical genomes have nearly the same entropy as one. The methodological shift of the 2010s was to measure compressibility by *r*, the number of runs in the BWT. For a collection of similar genomes, *r* grows roughly with the amount of *variation*, not the total length — *r* is to pangenomes what entropy is to single texts. The run-length FM-index (RLFM) stores *L* as *r* runs, answering rank via predecessor structures over run boundaries [3]. The *r*-index adds locating in *O(r)* space [4], and refined variants accelerate counting to *O(m log log_w(σ + n/r))* [7].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 SA-IS: induced sorting in linear time

SA-IS is the most elegant linear-time suffix array algorithm, and its ideas recur in grammar-based indexes [1]. Suffix *i* is *S-type* if the suffix at *i* is lexicographically smaller than the suffix at *i+1*, else *L-type* (the last suffix is S-type by convention). Types are computed right-to-left in one pass: position *i* is S-type iff *T(i) < T(i+1)*, or *T(i) = T(i+1)* and *i+1* is S-type.

An *LMS position* is an S-type position whose left neighbor is L-type; LMS substrings are the substrings spanning consecutive LMS positions. The algorithm proceeds in four phases:

1. **Sort LMS substrings** by naive bucketed insertion into character buckets.
2. **Induce L-types**: scan SA left to right; for each placed suffix at position *p > 0*, if *p−1* is L-type, insert it at the current head of its bucket.
3. **Induce S-types**: scan SA right to left; for each placed suffix at position *p > 0*, if *p−1* is S-type, insert it at the current tail of its bucket.
4. **Recurse**: rename the sorted LMS substrings to ranks, forming a reduced string of length at most *n/2*; solve it recursively; then repeat the induced passes with the true LMS order.

> **Theorem (Nong–Zhang–Chan):** SA-IS computes the suffix array in *O(n)* time.

*Proof sketch.* Each phase is a constant number of linear scans. The recursion is on a string of length at most *n/2*, giving *T(n) ≤ T(n/2) + O(n) = O(n)*. Correctness hinges on the *induction invariant*: when all LMS suffixes are correctly ordered, the left-to-right pass places every L-type suffix before any smaller L-type suffix sharing its first character, because L-type suffixes are ordered primarily by their first character and secondarily by the already-placed suffix that follows. A symmetric argument holds for the right-to-left S-type pass. ∎

The practical consequence is a construction that is both asymptotically optimal and fast in practice — *divsufsort*-class implementations of induced sorting remain the default in bioinformatics toolchains.

### 4.2 The FM-index and backward search

Backward search maintains the SA interval of suffixes prefixed by the pattern suffix processed so far, extending it leftward one character at a time. Let the current interval be the half-open range *(sp, ep)* of rows whose suffixes start with the processed suffix of *P*. Prepending character *c*:

```python
def backward_search(P, C, rank):
    # rank(c, i): occurrences of c in BWT[0:i]
    sp, ep = 0, n            # whole SA interval
    for c in reversed(P):    # extend pattern to the left
        sp = C[c] + rank(c, sp)
        ep = C[c] + rank(c, ep)
        if sp >= ep:
            return None      # pattern absent
    return sp, ep            # occ = ep - sp
```

> **Theorem (Ferragina–Manzini):** Backward search returns exactly the SA interval of *P* in *O(m · t_rank)* time, where *t_rank* is the rank-query cost.

*Proof sketch.* By induction on the processed suffix. Base: the empty suffix matches all rows, interval *(0, n)*. Step: assume *(sp, ep)* is the interval of suffixes starting with *S*. The rows whose rotations start with *cS* are precisely those whose *L* character is *c* within the current interval, and by the LF-mapping these map bijectively to *F*-interval *(C(c) + Occ(c, sp), C(c) + Occ(c, ep))*. Rank queries implement *Occ*. With a wavelet tree, *t_rank = O(log σ)*; over an integer alphabet with a *O(1)*-time rank structure, counting is *O(m)*. ∎

*Locating* each occurrence then costs *O(log n)* LF-steps to reach a sampled SA value with sampling rate *s*, for *O(s)* extra space; *extracting* ℓ characters costs *O(ℓ)* LF-steps. The *bidirectional* BWT index extends the same idea to both directions, supporting extensions of the pattern on either end in *O(1)* amortized time per character [2].

### 4.3 The r-index: optimal indexing of repetitive texts

Store the BWT in run-length form: *r* runs, each a *(character, length)* pair, in *O(r)* space. Rank over run boundaries reduces to predecessor search, giving counting in *O(m log log_w(σ + n/r))* [4]. Counting was never the hard part — the RLFM-index already did it. The decade-old open problem was *locating* in *O(r)* space, since the standard SA sample costs *O(n/s)* and blowing it up to *O(r)* samples breaks the space bound for insufficiently repetitive texts.

The breakthrough has two ingredients [5][4]. First, the **toehold lemma** of Policriti and Prezza: during backward search, at least one occurrence's position can be tracked using only *O(r)* stored SA values at run boundaries — specifically, the SA value preceding each run head in text order is recoverable. This yields one occurrence "for free" while counting. Second, Gagie, Navarro and Prezza showed that from one known occurrence, the others are reachable by walking a predecessor structure over the run-boundary samples, each in *O(log log_w(n/r))* time:

> **Theorem (Gagie–Navarro–Prezza, SODA 2018):** There is an index occupying *O(r)* words that counts occurrences of *P* in *O(m log log_w(σ + n/r))* time and locates each in *O(log log_w(n/r))* time.

*Proof sketch.* The *O(r)* samples store SA values at BWT run boundaries together with a data structure for *φ* (the suffix-array successor permutation restricted to sampled positions). The toehold lemma guarantees the backward search ends holding the text position of one occurrence. Repeatedly applying a predecessor query over the sampled *φ* values enumerates the remaining occurrences in SA order, each step costing a predecessor search over *r* keys. ∎

A refined construction reduces locate to *O(1)* per occurrence at *O(r log log_w(n/r))* space [4]. The *r*-index therefore matches the query-time profile of the classic FM-index while its space tracks *repetitiveness* rather than length. Recent work extends the r-index to the dynamic setting, supporting insertions and deletions in *O((m + L_max) log n)* time [3].

### 4.4 Pangenome graph indexing

A linear reference genome biases alignment: reads carrying non-reference alleles map poorly. The pangenomic response is to index a *variation graph* whose paths spell all known genomes. Indexing graphs is strictly harder than indexing strings — the number of distinct paths can be exponential — so practical indexes bound the complexity. The GCSA2 index (Sirén et al.) generalizes the BWT to graphs by sorting path prefixes up to a bounded order, effectively building the BWT of a pruned de Bruijn-like expansion; it powers the *vg* toolkit's variation-graph queries.

A complementary, string-based route concatenates all genomes with separators and builds the BWT of the collection (multi-string BWT). Here repetitiveness is extreme — human genomes are 99.9 percent identical — so *r* grows with the number of *variants*, not genomes, and the r-index becomes the natural pangenome index [8]. Key adaptations are needed:

- **Separator handling**: distinct terminators per genome (or a single shared one with tie-breaking) keep suffixes of different genomes distinguishable without inflating *r*.
- **Document listing**: occurrence positions must be mapped back to genome identifiers, typically via a compressed document array over the SA samples.
- **MEM finding**: maximal exact matches between a read and the pangenome are found by backward search that tracks match length, seeding alignment without enumerating all occurrences.

The theoretical upshot is a convergence: *r* has been shown equivalent, up to polylogarithmic factors, to every other known repetitiveness measure — LZ77 phrase count, substring complexity, grammar size — making it a universal yardstick for pangenomic compressibility [8].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Query-time landscape

| Index | Space (words) | Count | Locate per occ | Extract ℓ chars |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Suffix array | *O(n)* | *O(m + log n)* | *O(1)* | *O(ℓ)* |
| FM-index | *nH_k + o(n)* bits | *O(m)* | *O(log n)* | *O(ℓ)* |
| RLFM-index | *O(r)* | *O(m log log n)* | — (no efficient locate) | — |
| **r-index** | *O(r)* | *O(m log log_w(σ + n/r))* | *O(log log_w(n/r))* | *O(log(n/r) + ℓ)* |
| r-index (fast locate) | *O(r log log_w(n/r))* | *O(m log log_w(σ + n/r))* | *O(1)* | *O(log(n/r) + ℓ)* |

The table makes the contribution of [4] precise: it is the first row with *O(r)* space *and* efficient locate, dominating the RLFM row on every operation.

### 5.2 How small is r in practice?

Reported *n/r* ratios (average run length in the BWT) illustrate the regimes [3][5]:

| Collection | *n/r* (approx.) | r-index size |
|---|---|---|
| Extremely repetitive (e.g. versioned Wikipedia snapshots) | 500–10,000 | 0.06–0.2 bits/symbol |
| Versioned software / document collections | 40–160 | small constant factor over LZ |
| Bacterial genome collections | ~100 | ≈0.9 bits/symbol |
| Virus genomes / read sets | 4–50 | approaches plain-text size |

The pattern is instructive: *r* degrades faster than the LZ77 phrase count *z* as repetitiveness drops (r ≈ 3z at *n/r* ≈ 1000, but r ≈ 4z–7z at *n/r* ≈ 10–50), so the r-index is most compelling exactly where pangenomics lives — collections of closely related genomes [3].

### 5.3 Construction costs

SA-IS constructs the suffix array of a 3 GB human genome in minutes with ~5n bytes of RAM in optimized implementations; BWT construction in *O(n log σ)* bits of workspace removes the memory bottleneck for large alphabets [2]. Building the run-length BWT and the *r*-index sampling on top adds a small constant factor. For highly repetitive inputs, the optimal *O(n / log_σ n + r · polylog n)* construction is effectively linear in the *compressed* size [6]. Grammar-augmented RLBWT hybrids further shrink the index for long patterns while preserving backward-search speed [1].

### 5.4 Correctness proof: LF-mapping is a permutation

> **Theorem:** LF is a bijection on row indices, and iterating it from the *$*-row enumerates the text backwards.

*Proof.* Rows are totally ordered lexicographically; equal first characters keep their relative *L*-order by the stability argument (the *k*-th *c* in *L* precedes the same rotation shifted right, which sorts identically among *c*-rows). Hence LF maps the *k*-th *c* in *L* to the *k*-th *c* in *F* injectively, and domain and codomain are finite sets of equal size — a bijection. Starting from the row whose rotation begins with *$* (i.e. the full text), each LF step prepends the preceding character, walking *T* backwards. ∎

## 6 Limitations

- **Dynamic texts.** The classic FM-index and r-index are static: a single edit can change Θ(n) BWT runs in the worst case. The dynamic r-index supports updates in *O((m + L_max) log n)* [3], but with larger constants and no mature implementation comparable to static tools.
- **Graph indexing blowup.** GCSA2-style graph BWTs prune high-complexity regions to stay feasible; on tangled graphs the index can explode or lose paths. String-based pangenome indexes sidestep this but cannot represent nested variation as compactly as graphs.
- **Alphabet and integer assumptions.** Linear-time bounds assume an integer alphabet with σ ≤ n; over huge alphabets (e.g. word-based indexes) the log σ factors and wavelet-tree overheads bite.
- **Locate lower bounds.** The *O(r)*-space locate of [4] is optimal up to the *log log* factor, but the constant factors of predecessor structures make the r-index slower than a plain FM-index on non-repetitive data — there is no free lunch outside the repetitive regime.
- **Construction memory.** Even *O(n log σ)*-bit construction needs several bytes per symbol transiently; terabase-scale pangenomes still require distributed or external-memory construction (prefix-doubling in external memory, or BWT merging of partitions).
- **Beyond substrings.** Backward search answers substring queries; approximate matching, regular-expression search, and order-statistic queries on graphs need heavier machinery (hybrid indexes, bidirectional search with edits) with weaker guarantees.

## 7 Conclusion

From Manber and Myers' binary-searched permutation to Ferragina and Manzini's self-index, from SA-IS's elegant induced sorting to the r-index's optimal repetitive-text bounds, the suffix array / BWT lineage shows a rare unity of theory and practice: the same LF-mapping that inverts a compression transform also counts, locates, and extracts, and the same run count *r* that measures repetitiveness also sizes the optimal index. The open frontier is dynamic and graph-structured data — making the r-index fully mutable at low overhead, and indexing variation graphs as efficiently as strings. Given that *r* is now known to be a universal repetitiveness measure [8], indexes parameterized by *r* are likely to remain the foundation of pangenomic search for the coming decade.
