# Neural-Guided Symbolic Regression: Program Search with Large Language Models and Pareto-Optimal Expression Discovery

## Abstract

Symbolic regression (SR) — the discovery of closed-form mathematical expressions from data — sits at the intersection of program synthesis, evolutionary computation, and scientific discovery. Classical approaches based on genetic programming (GP) treat expression discovery as an unguided evolutionary search over the combinatorial space of expression trees, achieving impressive results but suffering from exponential search complexity and a persistent tension between *accuracy* and *parsimony*. This thesis develops a unified framework for **neural-guided symbolic regression**, in which large language models (LLMs) provide a scientific prior over the space of candidate programs, and Pareto-optimal search reconciles model fidelity with syntactic complexity. We formalize expression discovery as program search in a typed expression grammar, analyze the sample-complexity advantages of an LLM-informed prior, and present a Pareto-frontier formulation that subsumes minimum-description-length and information-criterion model selection. Through the lens of the SRBench suite [6], the Feynman physics-equation database [8], and the recent LLM-SRBench [2], we compare neural-guided methods — LLM-SR [3], LaSR [4], InsightSR [5], and LLM-PySR [7] — against classical GP baselines such as PySR [9], derive convergence-relevant theorems, and show how dimensional-analysis constraints prune the hypothesis space by orders of magnitude. We conclude with open problems in agentic, memory-augmented, and influence-guided program search.

## 1 Introduction

The *unreasonable effectiveness* of mathematical equations in describing natural phenomena — from Newton's $F = ma$ to the Einstein field equations — makes equation discovery one of the most ambitious targets for automated scientific reasoning. Symbolic regression formalizes this ambition: given a dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ with $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$, find an expression $f$ composed of elementary operators $\{+, -, \times, \div, \sin, \exp, \log, \dots\}$ and fitted constants such that $f(\mathbf{x}_i) \approx y_i$ for all $i$, while $f$ itself remains *interpretable* and *parsimonious*.

> **Definition 1.1 (Symbolic regression problem).** Let $\mathcal{G}$ be a context-free grammar generating expressions over variables $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_d)$, real constants, and a finite operator alphabet $\Sigma$. Given $\mathcal{D}$ and a complexity functional $C : \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}) \to \mathbb{N}$ (typically the number of nodes in the expression tree), the symbolic regression problem is the bi-objective optimization
>
> $$\hat{f} \in \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})} \left( \mathcal{L}(f, \mathcal{D}), \; C(f) \right), \quad \mathcal{L}(f, \mathcal{D}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (f(\mathbf{x}_i) - y_i)^2.$$

The first term rewards fidelity; the second rewards parsimony. Unlike black-box regression, SR must produce *symbolic* hypotheses — programs — whose structure a human scientist can read, audit, and falsify. This makes SR fundamentally a problem of **program search** rather than parameter estimation.

Classical SR, rooted in Koza's genetic programming (GP) [10], evolves a *population* of expression trees through mutation, crossover, and fitness-proportionate selection. GP-based SR systems — Eureqa, Operon, and the modern PySR [9] — dominate the SRBench benchmark [6] in raw accuracy, but they search *blindly*: the proposal distribution over expressions is syntactic, ignorant of physics, and prone to bloat and premature convergence. Neural-guided SR replaces the blind proposal distribution with a learned prior — first via deep sequence-to-sequence models (e.g., NeSymReS, Deep Symbolic Regression [11]), and more recently via **large language models**, whose pretraining corpora encode vast scientific priors about which expressions are plausible in which domains.

This thesis makes three contributions:

1. **A formal framework** for neural-guided program search in SR, casting the LLM as a *prior distribution* $p_{\theta}(f \mid \text{context})$ over expression skeletons and the evolutionary loop as an *importance-weighted posterior sampler*.
2. **A Pareto-frontier analysis** of accuracy–complexity trade-offs, proving that dimensional-analysis constraints (à la AI Feynman [8]) induce an exponential contraction of the search space.
3. **A comparative empirical synthesis** across SRBench [6], the Feynman database [8], and LLM-SRBench [2], locating each modern method — LLM-SR [3], LaSR [4], InsightSR [5], LLM-PySR [7], IGSR [1] — in the taxonomy of *generator-guided* versus *controller-guided* architectures.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Genetic Programming for Symbolic Regression

Koza [10] formulated genetic programming as evolution over program trees: each individual is an abstract syntax tree (AST) whose internal nodes are operators and whose leaves are variables and ephemeral constants. Fitness is typically the mean squared error (or its negative), and selection follows tournament or Pareto-tournament schemes. Modern systems augment GP with:

- **Constant optimization** via non-linear least squares (e.g., BFGS) at each generation, separating *structure search* from *parameter fitting*.
- **Simulated-annealing mutation acceptance** (PySR [9]) to preserve diversity.
- **Multi-objective selection** maintaining a Pareto frontier of expressions by $(R^2, \text{complexity})$.

> **Theorem 2.1 (Search-space cardinality).** Let $\Sigma$ contain $b$ binary operators and $\ell$ nullary symbols (variables plus constants), and let $T(k)$ be the number of distinct expression trees with $k$ nodes. Then $T(k) = \Theta\left(\frac{(4b)^{k/2} \ell^{(k+1)/2}}{k^{3/2}}\right)$ for odd $k$; in particular the hypothesis space grows exponentially in tree size $k$.

*Proof sketch.* A full binary tree with $k$ nodes has $(k+1)/2$ leaves and $(k-1)/2$ internal nodes; the number of plane shapes is the Catalan number $C_{(k-1)/2} \sim 4^{(k-1)/2}/((k-1)/2)^{3/2}\sqrt{\pi}$. Labeling internal nodes with $b$ choices and leaves with $\ell$ choices yields the stated bound. ∎

Exponential growth implies that unguided search is hopeless beyond modest tree sizes — the fundamental motivation for *informed* priors.

### 2.2 Neural Symbolic Regression

Deep symbolic regression (DSR) [11] treats expression generation as autoregressive sequence modeling: an RNN emits expressions in Polish notation, optimized by a risk-seeking policy gradient whose reward is the recovery-adjusted fit. Pretrained transformer variants (NeSymReS) amortize inference by training on millions of synthetic equation–data pairs, then decode candidate expressions conditioned on a new dataset. These methods reduce *wall-clock* search cost but remain *context-free*: they ignore variable names, units, and domain semantics.

### 2.3 Large Language Models as Scientific Priors

LLMs change the game because they are pretrained on the scientific literature itself. An LLM conditioned on a prompt describing "orbital dynamics data with variables $r$ (distance, meters) and $v$ (velocity, m/s)" assigns far higher probability to $v \approx \sqrt{GM/r}$ skeletons than to syntactically random alternatives. The thesis of this work is that this prior is best understood *Bayesianly*: the LLM defines $p_{\theta}(f \mid \text{context})$, and the evolutionary or Monte-Carlo loop performs approximate inference over the posterior $p(f \mid \mathcal{D}, \text{context}) \propto p(\mathcal{D} \mid f) \, p_{\theta}(f \mid \text{context})$.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Program Search with an LLM Prior

We model candidate discovery as search over *equation programs* — Python functions with placeholder constants — following LLM-SR [3]. The loop alternates between **proposal** (LLM generates skeleton $s$ from an in-context experience buffer) and **evaluation** (constants fitted by BFGS, fitness computed on $\mathcal{D}$):

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def search_loop(llm, data, ops, islands, budget):
    """Islands-model evolutionary search over LLM-proposed skeletons."""
    pops = [[] for _ in range(islands)]  # per-island experience buffers
    for t in range(budget):
        for pop in pops:
            parents = select_topk(pop, k=2)              # scalar MSE fitness
            skeleton = llm.propose(parents, data.desc, ops)  # p_theta(f | ctx)
            prog = compile(skeleton)
            if timeout(prog): continue
            params = minimize(lambda c: mse(prog(c), data), x0=skeleton.init)
            pop.append((skeleton, params, params.fun))
        migrate(pops)  # island model diversity preservation
    return best_overall(pops)
```

Two design axes distinguish the recent literature, summarized in Table 1.

| Method | LLM role | Search substrate | Feedback granularity |
|---|---|---|---|
| LLM-SR [3] | Skeleton generator (mutation op) | Islands-model EA | Scalar MSE per program |
| LaSR [4] | Concept library (guides mutation) | PySR GP | Scalar fitness + concepts |
| InsightSR [5] | Search-space transformer | PySR GP | Feature utility feedback |
| LLM-PySR [7] | Search planner / controller | PySR executor | Multi-metric gates |
| IGSR [1] | Basis-function proposer | MCTS + influence pruning | Granular term influence $\Delta_j$ |
| DrSR | Reflective prior updater | LLM-only refinement | Scalar + reflection text |

*Table 1: Taxonomy of LLM-guided symbolic regression methods (2024–2026).*

### 3.2 Pareto-Optimal Expression Discovery

Rather than collapsing accuracy and complexity into a single scalar, we maintain the **Pareto frontier** $\mathcal{F} \subset \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})$:

> **Definition 3.1 (Pareto frontier).** Expression $f$ dominates $g$ if $\mathcal{L}(f) \le \mathcal{L}(g)$ and $C(f) \le C(g)$ with at least one strict inequality. The Pareto set is the set of all non-dominated expressions discovered; the user then selects a knee point or applies a score such as
>
> $$\text{score}(f) = -\frac{\Delta \log \mathcal{L}(f)}{\Delta C(f)},$$
>
> the *marginal log-loss improvement per unit complexity* used by PySR [9].

> **Lemma 3.2 (Frontier monotonicity).** As search iterations $t \to \infty$, the empirical Pareto frontier $\mathcal{F}_t$ converges (in Hausdorff distance over the $(C, \mathcal{L})$ plane) to the true frontier of the enumerated hypothesis space. The rate is governed by the KL divergence between the proposal prior $p_\theta$ and the frontier-concentrated target distribution.

In practice, the score-based "best" selection of PySR picks the expression maximizing marginal improvement — a principled, description-length-flavored criterion that consistently recovers ground-truth physics equations on SRBench's Feynman subset [6].

### 3.3 Dimensional-Analysis Constraints

AI Feynman [8] demonstrated that enforcing **dimensional consistency** and exploiting *symmetries* (translational, scaling, separability) recursively decomposes high-dimensional SR problems into low-dimensional subproblems. We formalize this as a constraint on the grammar:

> **Theorem 3.3 (Dimensional pruning).** Suppose variables carry physical units from a dimension lattice $\mathbb{Z}^m$ (mass, length, time, ...). Let $\mathcal{G}_{\text{dim}} \subset \mathcal{G}$ be the sub-grammar generating only dimensionally consistent expressions with a fixed target unit vector $\mathbf{u}$. If a fraction $\alpha < 1$ of all trees are consistent, the effective search space shrinks to $\alpha \cdot T(k)$ — empirically $\alpha \sim 10^{-3}$–$10^{-5}$ for physics problems [8] — converting exponential search into a tractable one.

```haskell
-- Typed expression grammar with dimensional analysis (Haskell sketch)
data Dim = D { mass :: Int, len :: Int, time :: Int } deriving (Eq, Show)

data Expr = Var String Dim | Const Double | Add Expr Expr | Mul Expr Expr
          | Pow Expr Int | Sin Expr | Exp Expr

dimOf :: Expr -> Maybe Dim
dimOf (Add a b) = if dimOf a == dimOf b then dimOf a else Nothing  -- additive homogeneity
dimOf (Mul a b) = liftM2 addDim (dimOf a) (dimOf b)
dimOf (Sin e)   = if dimOf e == Just (D 0 0 0) then Just (D 0 0 0) else Nothing
```

Only expressions returning `Just u` (the target dimension) survive proposal — the grammar itself becomes the prior.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The LLM as Proposal Distribution: Generator vs. Controller Architectures

Early neural-guided methods (LLM-SR [3]) use the LLM as a **generator**: each evolutionary iteration prompts the model with parent skeletons and their MSEs, asking for offspring variants. This is powerful but *token-hungry* — LLM-PySR [7] reports ~2.2M tokens per benchmark run for full-LLM generation, versus ~91k tokens when the LLM acts as a **controller** that plans the search (operator sets, depth bounds, engineered features) and lets PySR execute the enumeration. The empirical finding is striking: the *search-controller* role achieves higher accuracy (ACC@0.1 of 0.958 vs. 0.892 on the AI-Feynman benchmark) at roughly **24× lower token cost** [7].

> **Theorem 4.1 (Prior quality and search efficiency).** Let $p^\star$ be the (unknown) distribution concentrating on the true frontier and $p_\theta$ the LLM prior. The expected number of proposals to first hit the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood of the truth scales as $\exp(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^\star \| p_\theta))$. A controller that *restricts support* (admissible operators, dimensional types) reduces this KL divergence more efficiently per token than a generator that merely reweights within the full space.

*Intuition.* Shrinking the grammar prunes entire subtrees of the search space at once; reweighting proposals prunes them only probabilistically. This explains why InsightSR's **search-space transformation** [5] — where the LLM proposes dimensionally consistent functional skeletons and nonlinear feature expansions that PySR then assembles into shallow trees — achieves 95% exact recovery on the Feynman benchmark.

### 4.2 Concept Libraries and Learned Abstractions

LaSR [4] introduces a **learned concept library**: the LLM distills abstract textual concepts ("exponential decay", "periodic modulation", "saturation") from successful equations, and these concepts bias GP mutation and crossover toward scientifically plausible structures. Conceptually, this is a *hierarchical prior*: $p_\theta(f \mid \text{context}) = \int p(f \mid z) \, p_\theta(z \mid \text{context}) \, dz$ where $z$ ranges over natural-language scientific concepts. The abstraction layer transfers across problems — "exponential decay" learned from radioactive-decay data guides enzyme-kinetics discovery — providing the kind of cross-domain generalization that pure syntactic GP lacks.

### 4.3 Granular Feedback: From Scalar Fitness to Influence Scores

A key limitation of LLM-SR-style loops is the **coarse feedback signal**: a single scalar MSE tells the LLM *that* a skeleton failed but not *which subexpression* is at fault. IGSR [1] addresses this by decomposing candidates into basis functions $\psi_j(\mathbf{x})$ of a linear model and computing **influence scores** $\Delta_j$ quantifying each term's marginal contribution to generalization accuracy. Integrated into Monte Carlo Tree Search, influence-guided pruning performs systematic structural credit assignment:

```python
def influence_prune(basis, data, val):
    """Prune basis functions by marginal generalization influence (IGSR [1])."""
    base = mse(fit(basis, data), val)
    scored = []
    for j, psi_j in enumerate(basis):
        reduced = [p for k, p in enumerate(basis) if k != j]
        delta = mse(fit(reduced, data), val) - base   # Δ_j: harm of removing ψ_j
        scored.append((delta, psi_j))
    return [p for d, p in sorted(scored, reverse=True) if d > 0]  # keep helpful terms
```

This converts "incremental trial-and-error" into *systematic improvement*: the MCTS balances exploration of novel functional forms against exploitation of high-influence components, and the authors demonstrate genuine discovery — a novel DNA-methylation/RNA-Pol-II-pausing relationship subsequently supported by wet-lab experiments [1].

### 4.4 Dimensional Analysis and the Feynman Benchmark

The Feynman Symbolic Regression Database [8] — 100 physics equations from the *Feynman Lectures* plus 20 from other seminal texts — remains the gold standard for *scientific* SR evaluation: ground truth is known, and recovery is verified symbolically via SymPy (either $f - \hat{f}$ or $\hat{f}/f$ reduces to a constant [6]). Two findings stand out:

1. **Units are priors.** Prompting the LLM with variable *semantics and units* (not just columns of numbers) is the single largest lever on recovery rate; it lets the prior assign near-zero mass to dimensionally absurd candidates, realizing Theorem 3.3 in practice [2][5].
2. **Memorization is a confound.** Canonical equations (e.g., $E = mc^2$-style forms) likely appear in LLM pretraining data, so strong Feynman performance may reflect *recitation* rather than discovery. LLM-SRBench [2] was constructed precisely to defeat this: its **LSR-Transform** split re-expresses known equations in unfamiliar mathematical representations, and its **LSR-Synth** split combines known terms with synthetic novel ones, spanning physics, chemistry, biology, and materials science (239 problem instances).

> *The honest interpretation: LLMs do not obviate search; they concentrate it. The search space is still combinatorial, but the prior now encodes three centuries of physics, so the first samples land near the truth.*

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Benchmark Landscape

SRBench [6] evaluates 14+ methods over 252 problems (Feynman + Strogatz ODEs + black-box PMLB tasks) along three axes: test $R^2$, expression complexity (AST node count), and symbolic ground-truth recovery. Its headline result: GP-based methods (Operon, PySR [9]) dominate accuracy and recovery, while no single method dominates all three axes — the accuracy–simplicity trade-off is irreducible, which is precisely why the Pareto formulation of §3.2 is the right evaluation frame.

On the AI-Feynman benchmark, the LLM-PySR controller architecture [7] reports:

| Method | ACC@0.1 ↑ | NMSE ↓ | LLM tokens ↓ |
|---|---|---|---|
| LLM-PySR (controller) | **0.958 ± 0.165** | 0.034 ± 0.023 | 91,273 ± 74,873 |
| PySR (no LLM) | 0.922 ± 0.237 | 0.104 ± 0.170 | — |
| LLM-SR (full-LLM) | 0.892 ± 0.270 | 0.051 ± 0.028 | 2,208,135 ± 464,128 |
| DrSR (full-LLM) | 0.665 ± 0.385 | 0.169 ± 0.068 | 2,835,147 ± 893,327 |

*Table 2: AI-Feynman results from [7]. The controller architecture wins on accuracy at a fraction of the token budget.*

InsightSR [5] reports 95% exact recovery on Feynman and 80.18% on LLM-SRBench LSR-Transform — the strongest published neural-guided numbers — by shifting the LLM's effort from *generating expressions* to *transforming the search space*.

### 5.2 A Sketch of Convergence

> **Theorem 5.1 (Pareto-frontier PAC bound, informal).** Fix a grammar $\mathcal{G}$, complexity bound $K$, and confidence $\delta$. With $N$ evaluated expressions drawn from a proposal $p_\theta$ whose support covers all trees of complexity $\le K$, the empirical frontier $\mathcal{F}_N$ satisfies: with probability $\ge 1 - \delta$, every expression on the true $K$-bounded frontier is $\varepsilon$-approximated in the $(C, \mathcal{L})$ plane by some $f \in \mathcal{F}_N$, where $\varepsilon = O(\sqrt{\log(|\mathcal{G}_{\le K}|/\delta)/N_{\text{eff}}})$ and $N_{\text{eff}} = N / \max_f (p^\star(f)/p_\theta(f))$ is the effective sample size.

The bound makes the role of the LLM precise: a good prior maximizes $N_{\text{eff}}$ by minimizing the worst-case importance ratio — i.e., by never assigning vanishing probability to expressions the truth might resemble. Dimensional constraints (Theorem 3.3) and concept libraries (LaSR [4]) both act by *raising the prior's floor* on plausible structures.

### 5.3 Case Study: Rediscovering Kepler's Third Law

Consider data $(r, T)$ for planetary orbits. A blind GP must discover $T^2 \propto r^3$ (or $T = 2\pi\sqrt{r^3/GM}$) from raw numbers. A neural-guided pipeline proceeds as follows:

1. **Controller** [7]: LLM reads variable semantics ("orbital radius in m", "period in s") and emits a plan: operators $\{+,-,\times,\div,\text{pow},\text{sqrt}\}$, target dimension $[\text{time}]$, engineered feature $r^3$.
2. **Executor**: PySR searches the dimensionally constrained grammar and finds $T = c_1 \sqrt{r^3}$ with $c_1 = 2\pi/\sqrt{GM}$ via BFGS.
3. **Pareto selection**: candidates $T = c_1 r^{1.5}$ (complexity 5), $T = c_1\sqrt{r^3}$ (complexity 6), and overfit polynomials (complexity 12+) are compared; the score criterion of §3.2 selects the marginal-improvement knee — the true law.

The dimensional prior alone prunes candidates like $T = r + \sin(r)$, which are syntactically legal but physically meaningless.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Memorization vs. discovery.** As LLM-SRBench [2] emphasizes, canonical equations are in-distribution for LLMs; reported Feynman recovery rates conflate recitation with reasoning. LSR-Transform/Synth splits are a partial remedy, but truly held-out *scientific* discovery remains unbenchmarked.
2. **Token and compute cost.** Full-LLM generator loops consume millions of tokens per problem [7]; even controller architectures require careful budget management. Classical GP remains cheaper per problem.
3. **Noisy and high-dimensional data.** Symbolic recovery degrades sharply with noise; most methods cap at ~6 input features [1], far below real scientific datasets. Dimensional analysis helps only when units are known.
4. **The constant-fitting bottleneck.** Every skeleton requires non-convex BFGS optimization; this dominates wall-clock time and introduces local-optima noise into fitness signals.
5. **Interpretability is not guaranteed.** Low node count $\neq$ human insight: expressions like $\sin(\log(\exp(x^2)))$ are simple yet opaque. Semantic simplicity (concepts [4]) and dimensional transparency are needed complements.
6. **Verification gap.** SymPy-based recovery checks [6] certify algebraic equivalence but not *physical* correctness; a dimensionally consistent but causally wrong equation still passes.

## 7 Conclusion

Neural-guided symbolic regression reframes a fifty-year-old program-search problem: the LLM is not an oracle that emits equations, but a **prior over programs** that concentrates an exponentially large combinatorial search onto scientifically plausible regions. The empirical record — from LLM-SR's skeleton-evolution [3] through LaSR's concept libraries [4], InsightSR's search-space transformation [5], LLM-PySR's controller architecture [7], and IGSR's influence-guided MCTS [1] — converges on a single lesson: *the highest leverage comes from using the LLM to shrink or reshape the hypothesis space (grammar, dimensions, concepts), not from asking it to guess the answer directly.*

The Pareto formulation ties the field together: accuracy and complexity are irreducibly competing objectives, and the right output of SR is not one equation but a **frontier** from which scientists select the knee. Dimensional analysis remains the most powerful known space-pruner, and its integration with LLM priors — units as types in the expression grammar — is, in our view, the most promising path toward genuine, trustworthy machine-driven equation discovery.

**Open problems:** (i) benchmarks that separate memorization from discovery at scale; (ii) noise-robust symbolic recovery with calibrated uncertainty over the frontier; (iii) agentic loops that autonomously request *new experiments* (active equation discovery); (iv) influence-style granular feedback extended to full tree-structured credit assignment; and (v) formal verification of discovered equations against conservation laws.
