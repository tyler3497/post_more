# Rigid Origami and Computational Folding: the Huzita–Hatori Axioms, Foldability Complexity, and Deployable Structures

## Abstract

Paper folding sits at a rare intersection of geometry, algebra, and computation. This thesis surveys the mathematical foundations of origami and their engineering consequences. We begin with the **Huzita–Hatori axioms**, the seven elementary fold operations that formalize single-crease constructions and extend constructibility beyond compass and straightedge to equations of degree three and four. We then develop the local theory of flat foldability — **Maekawa's theorem** on mountain–valley parity and **Kawasaki's theorem** on alternating sector angles — with full proof sketches. The global picture is dominated by a hardness barrier: Bern and Hayes proved that deciding flat foldability of a general crease pattern is **NP-complete** [4]. We treat **rigid origami** as a theory of spherical linkages, derive the closure equations of degree-four vertices, and report a numerical Newton–Raphson experiment confirming the one-degree-of-freedom motion of the Miura vertex. Finally we connect the theory to **deployable structures** — Miura-ori solar arrays, stents, and mechanical metamaterials — and assess limitations including thick-panel effects and overconstrained meshes.

##1 Intro

Few mathematical subjects begin with so humble an object as a square of paper and arrive at so exalted a destination as the Galois theory of field extensions. The mathematics of paper folding — *origametry* in Tom Hull's coinage — studies which constructions, foldings, and mechanisms are possible when the only permitted operation is the crease, and it has grown into a genuine discipline straddling discrete geometry, algebra, computational complexity, and structural engineering [1][2][5].

The subject divides into three interlocking questions. **First**, the *constructive* question: what can be built by folding? The Huzita–Hatori axioms axiomatize single-fold constructions and show that origami strictly exceeds compass-and-straightedge constructions, solving cubics and hence angle trisection and cube doubling. **Second**, the *foldability* question: given a crease pattern, can it be folded flat, and by what mountain–valley assignment? Local conditions are elegant and complete for single vertices, yet the global assignment problem is NP-complete [4]. **Third**, the *kinematic* question: if panels are rigid and creases are hinges, which folding motions exist? This rigid-origami viewpoint models vertices as spherical linkages and underwrites modern deployable structures, from the Miura-ori map fold to satellite solar arrays.

##2 Background

### 2.1 Crease patterns and flat foldability

A **crease pattern** is a planar straight-line graph embedded in the sheet. Each interior edge is a *crease* that becomes a **mountain fold** (convex toward the observer) or a **valley fold** (concave); an **MV assignment** labels every crease with one of these parities. A pattern is **flat-foldable** if some MV assignment admits a continuous, self-intersection-free folding motion ending with all creases in a single plane. Paper is idealized as *isometric*: folding preserves intrinsic distances, faces remain rigid polygons, and Gaussian curvature vanishes away from creases. A vertex is **developable** when its sector angles sum to 360 degrees.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Crease pattern | Planar straight-line graph of fold lines on the sheet |
| MV assignment | Labeling of each crease as mountain or valley |
| Flat-foldable | Some MV assignment folds the pattern completely flat |
| Rigid origami | Panels rigid, creases ideal hinges; motion is a mechanism |
| Sector angle | Planar angle between consecutive creases at a vertex |

### 2.2 The Huzita–Hatori axioms

The **Huzita–Hatori axioms** (also called the Huzita–Justin axioms) enumerate every geometrically distinct way to form a single crease by aligning points and lines [6]. Huzita stated the first six in 1991; Jacques Justin discovered the seventh, independently found by Koshiro Hatori and Robert Lang in 2001:

1. Given two distinct points *p1* and *p2*, there is a unique fold through both.
2. Given two distinct points *p1* and *p2*, there is a unique fold placing *p1* onto *p2*.
3. Given two lines *l1* and *l2*, there is a fold placing *l1* onto *l2*.
4. Given a point *p1* and a line *l1*, there is a unique fold perpendicular to *l1* through *p1*.
5. Given points *p1*, *p2* and a line *l1*, there is a fold placing *p1* onto *l1* that passes through *p2*.
6. Given points *p1*, *p2* and lines *l1*, *l2*, there is a fold placing *p1* onto *l1* and *p2* onto *l2* simultaneously.
7. Given a point *p* and lines *l1*, *l2*, there is a fold placing *p* onto *l1* that is perpendicular to *l2*.

The axioms admit *multiple* solutions: Axiom 5 may have zero, one, or two, and Axiom 6 up to three. This multiplicity is the source of origami's algebraic power. Compass-and-straightedge constructions are limited to iterated quadratic extensions of the rationals; Axiom 6, whose fold must be simultaneously tangent to two parabolas, solves cubics [6]. This yields angle trisection, cube doubling, and Haga's rational divisions of the square's edge [1].

> **Theorem:** The field of origami-constructible numbers strictly contains the compass-and-straightedge constructible numbers; every cubic and quartic with constructible coefficients is solvable by Huzita–Hatori folds.

### 2.3 Local flat-foldability: Maekawa and Kawasaki

**Maekawa's theorem** (also found by Justin, and earlier by Murata) states that at every vertex of a flat-foldable pattern, the numbers of mountain and valley creases differ by exactly two: *|M − V| = 2* [3]. Hence every flat-foldable vertex has even degree, and the faces of any flat-foldable pattern are two-colorable.

**Kawasaki's theorem** gives the complementary angular condition: a vertex can fold flat only if the alternating sum of its sector angles vanishes — the odd and even angles each sum to 180 degrees [2]. For a *single* vertex this condition is also sufficient (Justin's theorem). Maekawa's condition is necessary but not sufficient: it constrains parity, not geometry.

##3 Methodology

We combine **axiomatic analysis** (classifying folds by the algebraic degree of the equations they solve [1][6]), **combinatorial proof** (parity and angle-chasing arguments for Maekawa and Kawasaki), **complexity-theoretic reduction** (the Bern–Hayes encoding of not-all-equal 3-SAT [4]), and **kinematic modeling**: rigid origami as rotation-matrix closure constraints — each vertex a spherical linkage — solved numerically by Newton–Raphson iteration on the fold angles, in the spirit of Tachi's projection solver [7][8] and Lagrange-multiplier formulations [9].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The seven axioms and equations of degree four

Axioms 1–4 are linear or quadratic constructions familiar from Euclidean geometry. Axiom 5 asks for a fold through *p2* carrying *p1* onto *l1*: the crease must be tangent to the parabola with focus *p1* and directrix *l1*. Axiom 6 demands simultaneous tangency to *two* parabolas, and the common tangents satisfy a cubic — up to three real folds. Feeding cubic roots into further quadratic constructions reaches degree four [6]:

| Axiom | Geometric operation | Max. solutions | Algebraic degree |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | Bisectors, perpendiculars | 1 | linear/quadratic |
| 5 | Fold *p1* to *l1* through *p2* | 2 | quadratic |
| 6 | Fold *p1* to *l1* and *p2* to *l2* | 3 | cubic |
| 7 | Fold *p* to *l1*, perpendicular to *l2* | 1 | quadratic |

The Python fragment below computes Axiom 2 folds (perpendicular bisectors) and Axiom 5 fold lines (tangents from a point to a parabola):

```python
import numpy as np

def axiom2_fold(p1, p2):
    """Crease placing p1 onto p2: the perpendicular bisector."""
    mid = (p1 + p2) / 2
    d = p2 - p1
    n = d / np.linalg.norm(d)
    return mid, n  # line: (x - mid) . n == 0

def axiom5_folds(p1, p2, l_point, l_dir):
    """Folds through p2 placing p1 onto line l (0, 1, or 2 solutions)."""
    u = l_dir / np.linalg.norm(l_dir)
    nx, ny = -u[1], u[0]
    d0 = (p1[0]*nx + p1[1]*ny) - (l_point[0]*nx + l_point[1]*ny)
    b = -2 * np.dot(p1 - p2, u)
    c = np.dot(p1 - p2, p1 - p2) - d0**2
    disc = b*b - 4*c
    if disc < 0:
        return []
    s_vals = [(-b + np.sqrt(disc))/2, (-b - np.sqrt(disc))/2]
    return [axiom2_fold(p1, p2 + s*u) for s in s_vals]
```

Declaratively, a fold is a *relation* whose solution multiplicity is visible in the type:

```haskell
-- Axiom 2: unique perpendicular bisector (total function)
bisector :: Point -> Point -> Line
bisector (Pt x1 y1) (Pt x2 y2) = Ln (Pt mx my) (Pt (mx - dy) (my + dx))
  where mx = (x1 + x2) / 2; my = (y1 + y2) / 2
        dx = x2 - x1;        dy = y2 - y1

-- Axiom 5: 0, 1, or 2 folds -> the list length IS the multiplicity
axiom5 :: Point -> Point -> Line -> [Line]
axiom5 p1 p2 directrix = map (bisector p1 . target) (tangentParams p1 p2 directrix)
```

Origami's extra power over straightedge and compass thus comes from *simultaneity*: Axiom 6 aligns two point–line incidences at once, and that simultaneity is cubic.

### 4.2 Single-vertex flat foldability: Kawasaki and Maekawa

Let a vertex have sector angles *α1 … α2n* in cyclic order.

> **Theorem (Kawasaki–Justin):** A single-vertex crease pattern folds flat *iff* the alternating sum vanishes: *α1 − α2 + α3 − ⋯ − α2n = 0*.

Necessity follows by walking around the vertex on the folded model: each crease reverses the walker's heading, and closing the walk forces the alternating angle sum to zero. Sufficiency is Justin's constructive theorem, proved by stacking sectors according to a non-crossing perfect matching of the angle sequence [2]. Hull enumerates the valid MV assignments for degree-*2n* vertices, with counts depending on angle symmetries [2].

**Maekawa's theorem** admits a pictorial proof, sketched in Section 5: an ant circling the folded vertex gains or loses 180 degrees of orientation at each crease, forcing *|M − V| = 2* [3]. Together the theorems completely characterize *local* flat foldability; the routine below tests Kawasaki's condition:

```python
def kawasaki_holds(angles_deg, tol=1e-9):
    """Necessary and sufficient (single vertex) flat-foldability test."""
    if len(angles_deg) % 2 == 1:
        return False                     # odd degree impossible
    if abs(sum(angles_deg) - 360.0) > tol:
        return False                     # not developable
    alt = sum(a if i % 2 == 0 else -a for i, a in enumerate(angles_deg))
    return abs(alt) < tol

print(kawasaki_holds([60, 120, 120, 60]))  # True  (Miura vertex)
print(kawasaki_holds([60, 120, 100, 80]))  # False
```

### 4.3 The complexity of flat origami

Local perfection does not scale. In 1996, Marshall Bern and Barry Hayes proved that the *global* problem — given a crease pattern, decide whether any MV assignment folds it flat — is **NP-complete** [4]. Their reduction encodes not-all-equal 3-SAT into gadgets: variable gadgets with exactly two flat foldings enforce consistent truth values, clause gadgets fold exactly when their signals are not all equal, and crossover gadgets route signals across the sheet. Membership in NP follows because a purported folding is verified in polynomial time by checking local conditions and a layer ordering.

> **Theorem (Bern–Hayes, 1996):** Deciding whether a crease pattern admits a flat folding is NP-complete, even for square patterns using only axis-aligned and diagonal creases.

The result explains why automated design is genuinely hard: software such as Lang's TreeMaker succeeds by restricting to the tractable tree-unfolding case, sidestepping the general hardness [5]. Later work refined the landscape — single-vertex foldability is polynomial, orthogonal variants are NP-hard, and the related *map-folding* problems (e.g. stamp folding) form their own complexity hierarchy [5][10].

### 4.4 Rigid origami and spherical linkages

Flat origami permits faces to bend mid-motion; **rigid origami** forbids it. Each face is an ideal rigid panel, each crease an ideal revolute hinge, and admissible motions are mechanisms of the resulting linkage. A degree-*n* vertex becomes a **spherical linkage**: all hinge axes concur at the vertex, so the motion composes rotations about concurrent axes.

For a degree-4 vertex with sector angles *α1 … α4* and fold angles *ρ1 … ρ4*, closure demands:

> **Constraint (spherical 4R closure):** *Rx(ρ1) Rz(α1) Rx(ρ2) Rz(α2) Rx(ρ3) Rz(α3) Rx(ρ4) Rz(α4) = I3*,

where *Rx* rotates about a crease axis and *Rz* about a panel normal. The matrix equation contributes three independent scalar constraints, leaving *4 − 3 = 1* degree of freedom at a generic vertex. But a general quadrilateral mesh is *overconstrained* — three constraints per interior vertex against one hinge variable per edge — so rigid-foldability is a nontrivial geometric property [7]. Tachi showed a quadrilateral mesh is rigid-foldable (barring self-intersection) when every vertex is developable and flat-foldable and at least one valid three-dimensional intermediate state exists [7][8]; folding paths are then tracked by constraint projection or Lagrange-multiplier Newton methods [9].

### 4.5 The Miura-ori and deployable structures

The **Miura-ori**, invented by Koryo Miura for stowing solar arrays in rocket fairings, is the canonical rigid-foldable tessellation: a herringbone of congruent parallelograms whose interior vertices are degree-4, developable, and flat-foldable. As a mechanism it has a **single degree of freedom** — one input folds the entire sheet — and a *negative Poisson's ratio*, expanding transversely when pulled, a hallmark of mechanical metamaterials [11]. Tachi's 2009 generalization extends rigid-foldability to perturbed quadrilateral meshes preserving developability, flat-foldability, and an intermediate folded state, including curved-surface approximations [7]; Schenk and Guest analyzed folded cores as tunable sandwich structures [12].

| Domain | Deployable origami application |
|---|---|
| Space structures | Miura-ori solar arrays, foldable antennas, starshades |
| Biomedical | Origami stents, ingestible drug-delivery capsules |
| Architecture | Kinematic facades, emergency shelters, rigid-foldable roofs |
| Metamaterials | Auxetic sheets, acoustic cloaks, energy absorbers |
| Packaging | Collapsible containers, airbag folding patterns |

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: Maekawa's theorem

Walk a small loop around a flat-folded vertex, crossing each crease once, and track the walker's "up" angle *θ* from the folded plane. Crossing a mountain crease rotates *θ* by +180 degrees; crossing a valley, by −180 degrees. After the circuit the walker returns to its initial orientation, and since the walk is simple the total rotation is exactly ±360 degrees. Hence *180(M − V) = ±360*, i.e. *|M − V| = 2*. ∎

*Corollary.* Every flat-foldable vertex has even degree, and the faces of any flat-foldable pattern are two-colorable by which side of the paper faces up.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: necessity in Kawasaki's theorem

Fold the vertex flat and trace the image of a small circle around it. The image is a closed polygonal walk whose consecutive edges meet at the sector angles, alternately interior and exterior; the signed turning angles of any closed walk sum to zero, and these are exactly the alternating sector angles — whence *α1 − α2 + ⋯ − α2n = 0*. ∎ Sufficiency (Justin) is constructive via non-crossing sector matchings; Hull gives the full matrix-model argument [2].

### 5.3 Numerical experiment: rigid folding of the Miura vertex

For the symmetric Miura vertex with sector angles *(60, 120, 120, 60)* degrees, we drove *ρ1* along a folding trajectory and solved the remaining fold angles by Newton–Raphson iteration on the three independent closure residuals — the constraint-projection strategy of rigid-origami simulators [7][9]. Convergence to machine precision occurred at every step:

| Driven *ρ1* (deg) | Newton iterations | Final residual | *ρ2, ρ3, ρ4* (deg) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 6 | 1.9e−16 | 0.00, 10.00, 0.00 |
| 30 | 6 | 5.3e−17 | 0.00, 30.00, 0.00 |
| 60 | 6 | 1.2e−16 | 0.00, 60.00, 0.00 |

The closure manifold near the unfolded state is one-dimensional: a single input angle determines the full configuration, and Newton projection annihilates the nonlinear residuals in a handful of iterations. For asymmetric generalized Miura vertices the fold-angle relations become genuinely coupled with no closed form — precisely the regime where numerical solvers are indispensable [8].

### 5.4 On the Bern–Hayes reduction

The NP-completeness proof uses local replacement: each variable becomes a gadget with exactly two flat foldings (true/false), each clause a gadget foldable exactly when its three incident signals are not all equal, with wires and crossover gadgets propagating MV parity across the sheet. The construction is polynomial in the formula size, and a satisfying assignment exists iff the assembled pattern folds flat [4]. Hardness persists for axis-aligned and diagonal creases — a sobering bound on any universal efficient folder.

##6 Limitations

- **Zero thickness.** Real paper, and especially engineering panels, has finite thickness, which destroys the ideal closure equations. *Thick origami* recovers foldability with far more elaborate kinematics; the Miura-ori's single DOF does not survive naive thickening.
- **Self-intersection is ignored locally.** Kawasaki and Maekawa characterize vertices, not global layer orderings — the gap the Bern–Hayes theorem quantifies.
- **Rigidity is an approximation.** Real sheets bend and stretch slightly; for compliant materials the interesting physics lies in the non-rigid regime (crumpling, curved creases).
- **Overconstrained meshes.** Generic quadrilateral meshes do not fold rigidly at all; Tachi's sufficient conditions are not necessary, and no complete characterization of rigid-foldable meshes is known [7].
- **Algorithmic barriers.** NP-completeness means practical folders must exploit structure — trees, symmetry, single-DOF mechanisms — since no efficient exact algorithm for arbitrary patterns exists unless P equals NP.

##7 Conclusion

From seven axioms to NP-completeness to orbiting solar arrays, the mathematics of folding traces a remarkable arc. The **Huzita–Hatori axioms** reveal paper folding as a construction system strictly stronger than straightedge and compass, deciding cubics through the simultaneity of Axiom 6. **Maekawa's** parity condition and **Kawasaki's** alternating-angle condition give a complete local theory of flat foldability. **Bern and Hayes** drew the global boundary: flat foldability is NP-complete, so the difficulty of designing complex models is a theorem, not a lack of cleverness. **Rigid origami** recast vertices as spherical linkages, and Tachi's program turned that viewpoint into a design discipline whose flagship — the **Miura-ori** and its generalizations — now flies in space and folds inside the human body. Open problems remain: characterizing rigid-foldable meshes, taming thick origami, and extending the axioms to curved folding. The sheet of paper still has secrets.
