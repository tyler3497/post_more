# The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm and Quantum Annealing: Adiabatic Paths, Parameter Concentration, and NISQ Limitations

## Abstract

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) [1] is a leading candidate for useful computation on noisy intermediate-scale quantum hardware. This thesis examines QAOA as a Trotterized, variational discretization of the quantum adiabatic algorithm and its relation to physical quantum annealers. We develop the ansatz—alternating cost and mixer unitaries parameterized by $2p$ angles—and its convergence to adiabatic evolution as depth grows. We analyze *parameter concentration*, the finding that optimal angles cluster across instances [8], enabling transferability that slashes classical optimization cost. We then examine shallow-depth limits: reachability deficits, locality blind spots [10], and the symmetry-protected no-go result of Bravyi et al. [3], who show Goemans–Williamson outperforming constant-depth QAOA on some MaxCut instances. The variational landscape is treated through barren plateaus [4, 5] and training hardness. Finally we contrast gate-model QAOA with D-Wave annealers—Chimera, Pegasus, and Zephyr topologies, freeze-out, chain breaks, and embedding overhead [9]—as a complementary study in near-term quantum optimization.

## 1 Introduction

Combinatorial optimization underlies logistics, chip design, finance, and machine learning, and many such problems are *NP-hard*. Quantum computing entered through two doors: the **quantum adiabatic algorithm** (QAA), which evolves a system slowly from a trivial initial ground state to the ground state encoding the solution, and gate-model variational algorithms designed for noisy hardware.

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA), proposed by Farhi, Goldstone, and Gutmann in 2014 [1], bridges these paradigms. It discretizes the continuous adiabatic path into $p$ alternating layers of unitary evolution under a *cost Hamiltonian* $H_C$ (diagonal, encoding the objective) and a *mixer Hamiltonian* $H_B$ (providing tunneling between configurations), parameterized by $2p$ angles $(\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta})$. A classical optimizer tunes these angles to optimize the expected value of $H_C$. The algorithm is *variational* (quantum-classical hybrid loop), *approximate* (finite depth $p$ yields an approximation ratio), and *adiabatic in its asymptotic limit*: as $p \to \infty$ with suitable angles, QAOA recovers the adiabatic algorithm exactly.

This thesis examines QAOA along four axes: (i) the adiabatic connection via Trotterization and optimal control; (ii) parameter concentration and transferability, which reduce the classical optimization burden; (iii) expressivity limits of shallow QAOA, including locality arguments and symmetry-protected obstacles; and (iv) the optimization landscape, dominated by barren plateaus and training hardness. We close by contrasting QAOA with D-Wave's quantum annealers, whose topologies, freeze-out dynamics, and chain-break failure modes offer a complementary case study in near-term quantum optimization.

> **Thesis statement.** QAOA is best understood as a *variational Trotterization of the adiabatic path*, whose performance is governed by a tension between three forces: the expressive power conferred by depth $p$, the structure of the parameter landscape (concentration and barren plateaus), and the locality constraints of the ansatz and hardware.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### The adiabatic theorem and quantum annealing

The quantum adiabatic theorem states that a system prepared in the ground state of $H(0)$ remains in the instantaneous ground state of $H(t)$ if the Hamiltonian varies slowly relative to the inverse squared minimum spectral gap $\Delta_{\min}$ along the path. Quantum annealing implements this directly: a transverse field $A(s)H_B$ is ramped down while the problem Hamiltonian $B(s)H_C$ is ramped up, with $s = t/T \in [0,1]$.

The catch is severe: $\Delta_{\min}$ can be exponentially small for NP-hard problems, and first-order phase transitions close the gap on typical instances too. The adiabatic runtime is governed by the gap, and no general speedup guarantee exists.

### QAOA ansatz

QAOA prepares

$$
|\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta}\rangle = \prod_{j=1}^{p} e^{-i\beta_j H_B} e^{-i\gamma_j H_C} |+\rangle^{\otimes n},
$$

with mixer $H_B = \sum_i X_i$ and initial state $|+\rangle^{\otimes n}$. The objective $F_p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta}) = \langle \boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta} | H_C | \boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta} \rangle$ is optimized classically. For MaxCut on $G=(V,E)$,

$$
H_C = \sum_{(i,j) \in E} \frac{1}{2}(1 - Z_i Z_j),
$$

so that $\langle z | H_C | z \rangle$ counts cut edges. Farhi et al. [1] proved that $p=1$ QAOA on 3-regular graphs guarantees an approximation ratio of at least $0.6924$. Performance improves monotonically with $p$ in the optimized setting, since level-$p$ parameters embed in level $p+1$ by setting $\gamma_{p+1}=\beta_{p+1}=0$ [2].

### The Quantum Alternating Operator Ansatz

Hadfield et al. [6] generalized QAOA to the **Quantum Alternating Operator Ansatz**, permitting general phase-separation and mixing unitaries—including mixers preserving feasibility subspaces, such as the XY (ring) mixer for cardinality-constrained problems. The theoretical core of this thesis applies to the transverse-field formulation, but the framework extends naturally.

### Variational quantum algorithms and barren plateaus

McClean et al. [4] showed that deep, unstructured parameterized circuits exhibit **barren plateaus**: gradient variance vanishes exponentially in qubit count when the circuit forms an approximate 2-design. Cerezo et al. [5] refined this into a cost-function-dependent statement: global cost functions are untrainable at essentially all depths, while local cost functions remain trainable up to $O(\log n)$ depth. These results bear directly on QAOA at the depths needed for classical competitiveness.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is theoretical-analytic, synthesized with published numerical evidence:

1. **Ansatz construction.** Formalize the QAOA unitary sequence and derive it from the Trotterized adiabatic path, identifying the angle-to-schedule map $(\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta}) \leftrightarrow (A(s), B(s))$.
2. **Concentration analysis.** Review parameter concentration: optimal angles for MaxCut on random regular graphs cluster around instance-independent values, with variance shrinking as an inverse polynomial in $n$ [8]. Examine transferability protocols reusing donor-instance parameters on acceptors.
3. **Limitation analysis.** Present the reachability deficit, the locality arguments of Farhi, Gamarnik, and Gutmann [10], and the symmetry-protection no-go theorem of Bravyi et al. [3].
4. **Landscape analysis.** Characterize the variational landscape via barren-plateau theory [4, 5], gradient variance scaling, and training complexity.
5. **Annealer comparison.** Compare gate-model QAOA with D-Wave annealers: hardware topologies, minor embedding and chain breaks, freeze-out, and noise modeling [9].
6. **Reference implementation.** Provide pseudocode for the full QAOA loop.

```python
def qaoa_objective(gammas, betas, edges, n):
    """Depth-p QAOA expectation of the MaxCut Hamiltonian (state vector)."""
    import numpy as np
    psi = np.ones(2**n) / np.sqrt(2**n)          # |+>^n
    for j in range(len(gammas)):
        for (u, v) in edges:                     # exp(-i gamma_j H_C)
            psi = apply_zz_rotation(psi, u, v, n, gammas[j] / 2)
        for i in range(n):                       # exp(-i beta_j H_B)
            psi = apply_x_rotation(psi, i, n, 2 * betas[j])
    return np.real(np.vdot(psi, apply_cost_hamiltonian(psi, edges, n)))
```

The outer loop minimizes $-F_p$ over $\mathbb{R}^{2p}$ (Nelder–Mead, COBYLA, or parameter-shift gradients), typically initialized via INTERP or FOURIER heuristics [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The QAOA Circuit as Variational Trotterization

For the adiabatic path $H(s) = (1-s)H_B + s H_C$, dividing total time $T$ into $p$ steps and applying first-order Trotter–Suzuki decomposition at each step yields

$$
e^{-i H(s_j)\Delta t} \approx e^{-i(1-s_j)H_B \Delta t}\, e^{-i s_j H_C \Delta t},
$$

the QAOA layer structure with $\beta_j = (1-s_j)\Delta t$, $\gamma_j = s_j \Delta t$.

> **Theorem 1.** *For any fixed annealing time $T$ and schedule $s(t)$, QAOA parameters at depth $p$ exist such that Trotterized evolution approximates continuous-time adiabatic evolution with error $O(T^2/p)$.*

Crucially, the converse direction is richer: QAOA's angle space strictly *contains* all Trotterized adiabatic schedules, since the optimizer may choose non-adiabatic "bang-bang" protocols. Pontryagin's principle from optimal control suggests bang-bang protocols are often optimal for state preparation [2], explaining why numerically optimized angles frequently deviate from smooth ramps—they exploit non-adiabatic shortcuts.

Zhou et al. [2] showed optimized angles often follow annealing-like patterns ($\gamma_j$ increasing, $\beta_j$ decreasing), but the deviations from smoothness are precisely where QAOA can outperform naive adiabatic evolution. QAOA is thus a *superset* of Trotterized annealing, not a mere approximation of it.

### 4.2 Parameter Concentration and Transferability

For MaxCut on random $d$-regular graphs, optimal angles $(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^*, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*)$ depend only weakly on the instance [8]:

- Optimal parameters concentrate in fixed regions as vertex count $n$ varies, spread shrinking as an inverse polynomial in $n$.
- Two symmetric branches of optima exist (reflection symmetry of the landscape).
- At fixed $p$, $\langle H_C \rangle$ depends on the graph only through local neighborhoods: the reverse causal cone of each edge extends at most $p$ hops, and for large-girth regular graphs this neighborhood is a *tree* independent of global structure—hence concentration.

This locality also powers classical simulation at fixed $p$: expectations decompose into tree contributions, enabling the high-depth analysis of Basso et al. on large-girth graphs [2].

**Transferability** exploits concentration operationally [8]. Optimize angles on a small *donor* instance, then reuse them directly—or as a warm start—on a large *acceptor*. Parameters transfer not only across sizes but, in some studies, across problem classes: angles optimized on bin packing transfer competitively to MaxCut, independent set, and portfolio optimization up to 42 qubits, and even improve D-Wave annealing protocols [8]. Optimal QAOA schedules appear to capture something universal about the *dynamics* of combinatorial optimization rather than instance-specific structure.

| Phenomenon | Scaling | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Parameter concentration | $\mathrm{Var}(\theta^*) \sim \mathrm{poly}^{-1}(n)$ | Optimize small, reuse large |
| Cross-problem transfer | Empirical | Cross-class warm starts |
| INTERP/FOURIER init | $O(p)$ structured params | Avoids poor local minima |
| Reverse causal cone | Radius $p$ per layer | Classical simulability at fixed $p$ |

### 4.3 Reachability Deficits, Locality, and Symmetry-Protected Obstacles

At fixed $p$, QAOA explores only a limited Hilbert-space region—the **reachability deficit**: the ansatz may contain no state with good overlap on the optimum. Two landmark results quantify this.

**Locality: QAOA must see the whole graph.** Farhi, Gamarnik, and Gutmann [10] proved that for certain instance families—Maximum Independent Set on random graphs, some MaxCut instances—any *local* algorithm, including fixed-depth QAOA, is fundamentally limited. At depth $p$, the unitary acting on a vertex depends only on its $p$-neighborhood; problems requiring global coordination of long-range correlations defeat shallow QAOA. This is an *expressivity* failure, not an optimization failure.

**Symmetry protection and the Goemans–Williamson gap.** Bravyi et al. [3] proved a version of the No Low-Energy Trivial States (NLTS) conjecture for Hamiltonians with topological order and onsite $\mathbb{Z}_2$ symmetry: shallow circuits cannot prepare even approximate low-energy states. Corollary: MaxCut instances exist where the classical Goemans–Williamson SDP *strictly outperforms* QAOA at *any constant* level $p*. The ansatz's locality and symmetry are the culprits; they proposed a *nonlocal* QAOA variant with numerical evidence of large gains on frustrated Ising models [3].

These results delineate QAOA's viable regime: problems whose solutions are visible within $p$ hops, and whose symmetries align with the ansatz rather than fighting it.

### 4.4 Barren Plateaus and the Hardness of Training

Even when good states are reachable, *finding* them is a separate battle [4, 5]:

- **McClean et al. [4]:** for circuits forming approximate 2-designs, $\mathrm{Var}[\partial_k F] \in O(2^{-n})$—exponentially many measurements are needed to resolve gradients.
- **Cerezo et al. [5]:** the phenomenon is *cost-function dependent*; global costs are untrainable at essentially all depths, while local costs (like QAOA's MaxCut Hamiltonian) remain trainable to $O(\log n)$ depth, transitioning to exponential decay beyond.

QAOA sits in the middle: its cost is local (favorable), but at the depths $p \sim 30$–$40$ needed for classical competitiveness [2], the circuit approaches 2-design-like expressivity on the relevant subspace. Training VQAs is NP-hard in general, and landscapes are swamped with poor local minima [4]. Practical mitigations:

1. **Structured initialization**: INTERP (interpolating angles from $p-1$ to $p$) and FOURIER (few Fourier modes) avoid random initialization [2].
2. **Layerwise learning**: grow $p$ incrementally, freezing earlier layers.
3. **Parameter transfer**: exploit concentration to start near the optimum [8].

### 4.5 Physical Quantum Annealing: D-Wave, Freeze-Out, and Chain Breaks

QAOA's analog sibling is the D-Wave annealer, implementing the transverse-field Ising model

$$
H(s) = -A(s)\sum_i X_i + B(s)\left(\sum_i h_i Z_i + \sum_{i<j} J_{ij} Z_i Z_j\right)
$$

in superconducting flux qubits. Three hardware-graph generations define its evolution: **Chimera** (2048 qubits, $K_{4,4}$ unit cells, degree 6), **Pegasus** (~5640 qubits, degree 15), and **Zephyr** (degree 20), each reducing embedding overhead.

Arbitrary problems require **minor embedding**: each logical variable becomes a *chain* of physical qubits coupled ferromagnetically with chain strength $J_c$. Two characteristic failure modes result [9]:

1. **Chain breaks**: fluctuations flip qubits within a chain, breaking the logical value. The break fraction grows with chain length, which scales roughly linearly with logical variable count. Majority-vote post-processing repairs single flips in odd-length chains perfectly, but even-length chains are only probabilistically repairable [9].
2. **Freeze-out**: as $A(s) \to 0$, dynamics slow dramatically; the state freezes at $s^*$ before the anneal ends, and the final distribution reflects an effective temperature at the freeze-out point rather than the true ground state—annealing's analog of the reachability deficit.

Gaussian control-error noise models predict chain-break probability as a function of chain length and strength, matching Advantage2 prototype experiments [9]. Embedding overhead is the dominant practical bottleneck: dense 100-variable problems may consume thousands of physical qubits, and the effective problem energy scale is diluted by the large $J_c$ needed to keep chains intact.

> **Key contrast.** QAOA is *digital and variational*: the schedule is a free parameter, optimized classically, and hardware need only implement local gates. Annealing is *analog and fixed-schedule*: the path is set by calibration, and the user controls only encoding and anneal time. QAOA can exploit non-adiabatic shortcuts; the annealer cannot. Conversely, the annealer's thousands of qubits exceed any near-term gate-model device—though whether this is a *computational advantage* for optimization remains contested.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Theoretical Results

Key quantitative results from the literature:

1. **Shallow-depth guarantees.** $p=1$ QAOA on 3-regular MaxCut achieves approximation ratio $\geq 0.6924$; on 2-regular graphs (rings), $0.75$ [1].
2. **Scaling with depth.** Zhou et al. [2]: for unweighted 3-regular graphs ($N \leq 22$), fractional error $1-r \propto e^{-p/p_0}$ with $p_0$ growing slowly in $N$; for weighted graphs, $1-r \propto e^{-\sqrt{p/p_0}}$. Classical-competitive performance (Goemans–Williamson) requires $p \approx 30$ [2].
3. **Scaling advantage evidence.** Shaydulin et al. demonstrated scaling advantage on a classically intractable problem (low-autocorrelation binary sequences): ground-state probability $27.3\times$ above competing approaches at $p=40$, with parameters optimized for ground-state probability differing markedly from those optimized for expected solution quality [7].
4. **High-depth analysis.** Basso et al. analyzed QAOA at high depth on large-girth regular graphs and the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model, computing infinite-size performance exactly via tree structure, with improving bounds in depth [2].
5. **Annealer benchmarks.** D-Wave Advantage systems factored $8{,}219{,}999 = 32{,}749 \times 251$ via annealing—the largest integer factored on a pure quantum device—through engineered embeddings [9]. Majority-vote chain-break mitigation recovers near-unembedded success probabilities for favorable embeddings [9].
6. **Training cost.** Monte Carlo simulations including projection noise show measurements per objective evaluation scale as $M \approx \mathrm{Var}(F_p)/\xi^2$; total runtime is dominated by the classical loop, not quantum execution [2].

| Result | Regime | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Farhi et al. [1] | $p=1$, 3-regular MaxCut | Ratio $\geq 0.6924$ |
| Zhou et al. [2] | $p \leq 20$, $N \leq 22$ | $1-r \propto e^{-p/p_0}$ (unweighted) |
| Bravyi et al. [3] | Any constant $p$ | GW beats QAOA on symmetric instances |
| Shaydulin et al. [7] | $p=40$, LABS problem | $27.3\times$ ground-state probability |
| D-Wave [9] | Pegasus, factorization | $8{,}219{,}999$ factored |

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **The depth wall.** Classical competitiveness needs $p \sim 30$–$40$ [2], but NISQ coherence and gate fidelities limit realizable depth. Each MaxCut layer needs $O(|E|)$ two-qubit gates, multiplied by SWAP overhead on sparse-connectivity hardware. Error mitigation extends reach modestly but scales exponentially in mitigated volume.
2. **Trainability at scale.** Barren plateaus [4, 5] and NP-hard training mean the classical outer loop—not the quantum circuit—is often the true bottleneck. Concentration [8] and structured initialization [2] mitigate but do not eliminate this.
3. **Locality and symmetry barriers.** The results of [3] and [10] are *fundamental*: no error correction rescues constant-depth QAOA from locality blind spots or symmetry-protected obstructions. Escapes require $p$ growing with $n$ or redesigned ansätze (nonlocal mixers [3], adaptive ansätze).
4. **Annealer limitations.** Embedding overhead, chain breaks, freeze-out, and control noise [9] bound D-Wave's effective problem size far below its physical qubit count. The analog design precludes error correction and variational schedule optimization.
5. **Benchmarking rigor.** Fair comparison with classical solvers (Gurobi, parallel tempering, GW) demands careful accounting of wall-clock time, full solution-quality distributions, and instance selection. Quantum advantage claims in optimization remain contested and instance-dependent.
6. **Open questions.** Does $p = \mathrm{polylog}(n)$ QAOA beat the best classical algorithms on any natural problem class? Can concentration be proven for general QUBO? What is the precise relation between optimized angles and optimal-control bang-bang protocols?

## 7 Conclusion

QAOA sits at the intersection of adiabatic quantum computation, variational algorithms, and near-term hardware. Its derivation as a Trotterized adiabatic path [1, 2] provides grounding and intuition: optimized angles often trace annealing-like schedules, while deviations encode non-trivial quantum shortcuts. Parameter concentration and transferability [8] transform QAOA from a per-instance variational burden into a reusable dynamical primitive, taming the classical optimization cost.

The limitations are equally sharp, and several are fundamental. Locality arguments [10] and symmetry-protected no-go theorems [3] prove shallow QAOA cannot see the whole problem and cannot beat classical algorithms on certain structured instances at constant depth. Barren plateaus [4, 5] and training hardness threaten the variational loop. Physical annealers [9] offer scale—thousands of qubits—but pay in embedding overhead, chain breaks, and freeze-out, with no variational freedom to compensate.

Progress will be coordinated, not singular: deeper, lower-noise hardware pushing $p$ into the competitive regime; ansätze respecting problem symmetries; initialization and transfer protocols taming the landscape; and rigorous benchmarking separating genuine advantage from engineering artifacts. QAOA's deepest lesson may be conceptual: the adiabatic path is not a fixed road to follow slowly, but a landscape to navigate—and the fastest route is not always the smoothest.
