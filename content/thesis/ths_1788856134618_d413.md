# The Mathematics of Neural Rendering: From the Volume Rendering Integral to 3D Gaussian Splatting and Differentiable Rasterization

## Abstract

Neural rendering has undergone a phase transition: from implicit radiance fields optimized through volumetric ray marching to explicit anisotropic primitives rasterized in real time. This thesis unifies the mathematics behind that transition. We begin with the classical volume rendering integral and its quadrature approximation, show how positional encoding overcomes the spectral bias of coordinate MLPs yet induces aliasing, and derive the integrated positional encoding that makes mip-NeRF a consistent multiscale estimator [3]. We then develop the 3D Gaussian splatting representation of Kerbl et al. [2]: anisotropic Gaussians parameterized by rotation and scale, projected to screen space through the EWA affine approximation, blended by a tile-based differentiable rasterizer with analytically derived gradients, shaded view-dependently by spherical harmonics, and optimized through an adaptive densification policy of cloning, splitting, and pruning. We analyze the convergence behavior of the resulting non-convex optimization, quantify its computational complexity against ray-marched neural fields, and close with open problems in aliasing, temporal coherence, and the geometry of the Gaussian manifold.

---

## 1. Introduction

The problem of novel view synthesis is deceivingly simple to state: given a finite set of calibrated photographs of a static scene, reproduce the radiance arriving at any virtual camera. For decades the dominant machinery was explicit geometry — meshes reconstructed by multi-view stereo and textured by reprojection. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [1] replaced this pipeline with something far more radical: a continuous five-dimensional function $F_\Theta(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \mapsto (\mathbf{c}, \sigma)$, parameterized by a multilayer perceptron, mapping spatial position $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and viewing direction $\mathbf{d} \in \mathbb{S}^2$ to emitted radiance $\mathbf{c}$ and volume density $\sigma$. Views are synthesized by numerically integrating this field along camera rays, and because every step is differentiable, the field can be fit to photographs by gradient descent with nothing but a photometric loss.

The elegance of NeRF concealed two fundamental costs. First, rendering requires hundreds of MLP evaluations per pixel, yielding frame times measured in seconds rather than milliseconds. Second, the point-sampling nature of NeRF's quadrature made it blind to scale: the same high-frequency positional features were excited regardless of the pixel footprint, producing the shimmering aliasing artifacts that mip-NeRF [3] was designed to eliminate.

*3D Gaussian Splatting* [2] resolved the performance problem by abandoning the MLP almost entirely. The scene becomes a cloud of explicit anisotropic 3D Gaussians, each carrying opacity and spherical-harmonic coefficients, projected to the image plane and composited by a purpose-built tile-based differentiable rasterizer. Optimization converges in tens of minutes; rendering exceeds 100 frames per second at 1080p. Yet the method is not merely engineering: it is a re-interpretation of volume rendering in which the quadrature nodes themselves are the learned representation, and in which gradients flow backward through sorting, projection, and blending with exact analytic formulas.

This thesis presents the full mathematical stack: the integral, its discretization, the aliasing theory that constrains it, the Gaussian re-parameterization that accelerates it, and the convergence analysis that explains why it works.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Radiative Transfer Simplification

The complete radiative transfer equation accounts for absorption, emission, in-scattering, and out-scattering. Neural rendering universally adopts the *emission–absorption* simplification, dropping scattering terms. For a camera ray $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}$ with near and far bounds $t_n, t_f$, the expected observed color is the classical volume rendering integral [1]:

$$C(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\,\sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t), \mathbf{d})\,dt,$$

where the *accumulated transmittance*

$$T(t) = \exp\left(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\,ds\right)$$

is the probability that the ray travels from $t_n$ to $t$ without being absorbed. The integrand $T(t)\sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))$ is a probability density over the ray: the probability that the ray terminates in $[t, t+dt]$. Rendering is therefore Monte-Carlo-free *expectation* of color under the termination distribution — a fact that will matter when we analyze convergence.

### 2.2 Coordinate MLPs and Spectral Bias

The field $(\sigma, \mathbf{c}) = F_\Theta(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d})$ is implemented as an 8-layer MLP. A naive MLP applied to raw coordinates suffers from *spectral bias*: gradient descent on wide networks fits low-frequency functions exponentially faster than high-frequency ones, so raw coordinates yield blurry reconstructions. NeRF's remedy is the sinusoidal *positional encoding* [1]:

$$\gamma(p) = \left(\sin(2^0\pi p), \cos(2^0\pi p), \ldots, \sin(2^{L-1}\pi p), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi p)\right),$$

applied coordinate-wise with $L=10$ for position and $L=4$ for direction. This lifts the input into a Fourier feature space where high frequencies are linearly accessible, a trick justified by the neural tangent kernel analysis of Rahaman et al.: the NTK of an MLP composed with $\gamma$ is stationary with a tunable bandwidth.

### 2.3 Elliptical Weighted Average Splatting

Projecting a 3D Gaussian to a 2D image is not new. Zwicker et al.'s *EWA splatting* (2001) showed that a 3D Gaussian covariance $\Sigma$ projects under the affine approximation of the projective map to a 2D covariance

$$\Sigma' = J\,W\,\Sigma\,W^\top J^\top,$$

where $W$ is the world-to-camera rotation and $J$ is the Jacobian of the projective transform evaluated at the Gaussian mean. Kerbl et al. [2] rediscover and differentiate this construction, making $\Sigma'$ the linchpin of their rasterizer.

### 2.4 Spherical Harmonics

View-dependent color is expanded in the real spherical-harmonic basis $Y_\ell^m$ up to degree $\ell = 3$, giving $(3+1)^2 = 16$ coefficients per color channel. The SH basis is orthonormal on the sphere and band-limited by construction, so evaluating color in direction $\mathbf{d}$ is a cheap dot product — no MLP query required.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is analytic and comparative. We formalize the NeRF rendering integral and its stratified-sampling quadrature, derive the integrated positional encoding as the Gaussian expectation of the positional encoding, and prove its anti-aliasing property. We then construct the 3D Gaussian scene representation, derive the EWA projection and the tile-based rasterizer's forward and backward passes, specify the adaptive densification controller as a discrete dynamical system, and analyze convergence using the lens of non-convex stochastic optimization with explicitly computed gradients. Empirical claims are grounded in the published evaluations of [1, 2, 3, 4], and every technical assertion is traceable to a numbered reference.

> **Theorem 1 (Quadrature as alpha compositing).** The stratified-sampling estimator of the volume rendering integral is exactly equivalent to front-to-back alpha compositing with opacities $\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)$.

*Proof.* Partition $[t_n, t_f]$ into intervals of length $\delta_i$ and assume $\sigma, \mathbf{c}$ piecewise constant. Then $T(t_i) = \prod_{j<i} \exp(-\sigma_j \delta_j) = \prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j)$, and the integral over interval $i$ contributes $\mathbf{c}_i(1-e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i})T(t_i) = \mathbf{c}_i\alpha_i\prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j)$. Summation yields the compositing formula. ∎

This single equivalence is the bridge between NeRF and Gaussian splatting: both are alpha compositing; they differ only in *where the opacities come from* — learned densities at ray samples versus learned 2D Gaussians per pixel.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Volume Rendering Integral and Its Quadrature

NeRF [1] evaluates $C(\mathbf{r})$ by stratified sampling: $[t_n, t_f]$ is divided into $N$ uniform bins and one sample drawn uniformly per bin,

$$t_i \sim \mathcal{U}\left[t_n + \tfrac{i-1}{N}(t_f-t_n),\; t_n + \tfrac{i}{N}(t_f-t_n)\right],$$

which keeps the estimator unbiased while ensuring the MLP is queried at continuously varying positions across iterations. The quadrature rule of Max [1, §5] then gives

$$\hat{C}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i\left(1 - \exp(-\sigma_i\delta_i)\right)\mathbf{c}_i,\qquad T_i = \exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\sigma_j\delta_j\right).$$

A two-stage *hierarchical* procedure follows: a coarse network's weights $w_i = T_i(1-e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i})$ define a piecewise-constant PDF along the ray, and inverse-transform sampling concentrates fine-network samples near surfaces. This is importance sampling with the proposal learned online.

The Python reference implementation below captures the full quadrature in a dozen lines:

```python
import torch

def volume_render(sigmas, colors, deltas):
    """sigmas: (N,), colors: (N,3), deltas: (N,) segment lengths."""
    alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigmas * deltas)          # opacity per segment
    trans = torch.exp(-torch.cumsum(
        torch.cat([torch.zeros(1), sigmas * deltas[:-1]]), dim=0))
    weights = trans * alpha                            # termination PDF
    rgb = (weights[:, None] * colors).sum(dim=0)
    return rgb, weights
```

> **Theorem 2 (Convergence of the modified directional estimator).** Rethinking the directional integration in NeRF — replacing the classical ordering of color–direction coupling — yields an estimator provably equivalent to classical NeRF in the ideal limit but with strictly better convergence properties under numerical quadrature [5].

This matters because every NeRF variant inherits quadrature error: the estimator converges to the true integral only as $N \to \infty$, and finite-$N$ bias interacts with network capacity in ways that are still only partially characterized [5].

### 4.2 Positional Encoding, Aliasing, and the Integrated Fix

NeRF's point samples are *infinitesimal*: each quadrature node excites the full Fourier spectrum of $\gamma$, including frequencies far above the Nyquist limit of the pixel footprint. When the camera moves or the image is downsampled, these unrepresentable frequencies fold back as shimmering aliasing — the classic failure mode visible in NeRF's distant geometry.

Mip-NeRF [3] replaces rays with *cones*. For pixel footprint radius $\dot{r}$, the cone through interval $[t_0, t_1]$ is approximated by a Gaussian with mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ (closed-form expressions in [3, §3.1]). The key innovation is the *integrated positional encoding* (IPE): the expectation of $\gamma$ under that Gaussian,

$$\gamma(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})}[\gamma(\mathbf{x})],$$

which admits the closed form [4, Eq. 1]:

$$\gamma(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} \sin(2^\ell\boldsymbol{\mu})\,\exp\!\left(-2^{2\ell-1}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\right) \\ \cos(2^\ell\boldsymbol{\mu})\,\exp\!\left(-2^{2\ell-1}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\right) \end{bmatrix} \right\}_{\ell=0}^{L-1}.$$

The exponential damping is a *low-pass filter with automatic bandwidth*: when the frustum is wide relative to the period $2^{-\ell}$, the corresponding feature collapses to zero, exactly suppressing the aliased frequencies. The scale of the observation is thereby *encoded in the features themselves*, allowing a single MLP to learn a multiscale representation — halving model size versus NeRF's coarse/fine pair while improving accuracy [3].

| Method | Sampling primitive | Encoding | Scale-aware | MLPs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NeRF [1] | Ray (point samples) | PE, fixed bandwidth | No | 2 (coarse + fine) |
| mip-NeRF [3] | Cone (Gaussian frustum) | IPE, adaptive bandwidth | Yes | 1 (multiscale) |
| mip-NeRF 360 [4] | Cone + contracted space | IPE | Yes, unbounded | 1 + proposal MLP |
| Zip-NeRF [3, cited] | Multisample cone | Grid + multisampling | Yes | Hybrid grid |

Mip-NeRF 360 [4] extends the construction to unbounded scenes via a contraction mapping $\mathbf{x} \mapsto \mathbf{x}/\|\mathbf{x}\|$ for $\|\mathbf{x}\|>1$ and adds a distortion regularizer penalizing "floaters," but the IPE mathematics is unchanged.

### 4.3 Anisotropic 3D Gaussians and the EWA Projection

3D Gaussian Splatting [2] discards the MLP and the ray integral together. The scene is a set of $M$ primitives, each defined by:

- mean $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^3$,
- covariance $\Sigma = RSS^\top R^\top$, with $R \in SO(3)$ from a unit quaternion $\mathbf{q}$ and $S = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s})$, $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^3_{>0}$,
- opacity $o \in (0,1)$ via sigmoid activation,
- SH coefficients $\mathbf{k} \in \mathbb{R}^{16 \times 3}$ for view-dependent color.

The quaternion-plus-scale parameterization guarantees $\Sigma \succ 0$ under unconstrained gradient descent — a manifold-aware trick that avoids the projection steps a raw covariance matrix would require. The 3D density is

$$G(\mathbf{x}) = \exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right).$$

Projection follows Zwicker's EWA construction: with viewing transform $W$ and projective Jacobian $J$ evaluated at $\boldsymbol{\mu}$,

$$\Sigma' = J\,W\,\Sigma\,W^\top J^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 2},$$

and the pixel-space splat is the 2D Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}', \Sigma')$ with $\boldsymbol{\mu}'$ the projected center. Dropping the third row/column after the affine map is exact for Gaussians: marginalizing a Gaussian is Gaussian.

The elegance here is differentiability *through the projection*: $\partial \Sigma'/\partial \mathbf{q}$, $\partial \Sigma'/\partial \mathbf{s}$, $\partial \boldsymbol{\mu}'/\partial \boldsymbol{\mu}$ are all available in closed form, and [2] implements them as hand-derived CUDA kernels rather than relying on autodiff, eliminating graph overhead in the inner loop.

### 4.4 The Tile-Based Differentiable Rasterizer

Per-pixel sorting of thousands of overlapping splats would be prohibitive. The rasterizer of [2] instead:

1. **Projects** all Gaussians, culling those outside the frustum.
2. **Tiles** the screen into $16 \times 16$ blocks; each Gaussian is assigned to every tile its $3\sigma$ ellipse overlaps, and per-tile (depth, splat) pairs are generated.
3. **Sorts** each tile's list by depth with a GPU radix sort — the only global synchronization point.
4. **Blends** front-to-back per pixel: $C = \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i \prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j)$, where $\alpha_i = o_i \cdot G_{2D}(\mathbf{p})$ is opacity modulated by the 2D Gaussian falloff, terminating early when transmittance drops below $10^{-4}$.

The backward pass inverts this pipeline analytically. Given $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial C$, the kernel walks the sorted list *back-to-front*, accumulating $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \alpha_i$ and the running transmittance, then applies the chain rule through the 2D Gaussian evaluation, the EWA projection, and the quaternion/scale/SH parameterizations. Sorting is piecewise-constant, so gradients flow only through the *values* of sorted entries, not the permutation — the standard straight-through treatment, justified because the permutation changes on a measure-zero set of parameter configurations.

The rasterizer's critical sections can be specified as a state machine; the per-tile blending invariant is:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE TileBlend ------------------------------
VARIABLES pixColor, transmittance, sortedSplats, cursor

BlendStep ==
    /\ cursor < Len(sortedSplats)
    /\ LET s == sortedSplats[cursor]
           a == s.opacity * Gauss2D(s, pixel)
       IN /\ pixColor' = pixColor + transmittance * a * s.color
          /\ transmittance' = transmittance * (1 - a)
          /\ cursor' = cursor + 1
          /\ UNCHANGED <<sortedSplats>>

Terminate == cursor = Len(sortedSplats) \/ transmittance < 0.0001
=============================================================================
```

Complexity per frame is $O(M \log M)$ for the sort plus $O(P \cdot \bar{K})$ for blending, where $P$ is pixel count and $\bar{K}$ the mean splats per pixel — in practice yielding 100+ FPS at 1080p where NeRF manages a fraction of a frame per second [2].

### 4.5 View Dependence and Adaptive Densification

Color is evaluated from SH coefficients as $\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{d}) = \sum_{\ell,m} k_{\ell}^{m} Y_{\ell}^{m}(\mathbf{d})$, with degree ramped $0 \to 3$ during training: the DC term captures diffuse albedo first, and higher bands progressively unlock specularities. Sixteen coefficients per channel strike the empirical balance between expressiveness and the $M \times 48$ memory footprint.

Geometry is grown by the *adaptive density controller*, a discrete-event system interleaved with SGD every 100 iterations (between iterations 500 and 15,000):

| Condition | Action | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| $\\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathcal{L}\\| > \tau_p$, $\\|\mathbf{s}\\|$ small | **Clone** the Gaussian, shift along gradient | Under-reconstruction: fill holes |
| $\\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathcal{L}\\| > \tau_p$, $\\|\mathbf{s}\\|$ large | **Split** into two, shrink scales by 1.6 | Over-reconstruction: resolve detail |
| $o < 0.005$ or world-space extent excessive | **Prune** | Remove transparent/floater mass |

Additionally, opacities are periodically reset toward zero, forcing the optimizer to re-justify every primitive's existence — a sparsity-inducing mechanism reminiscent of $\ell_0$-style reweighting. Initialization comes from the Structure-from-Motion sparse point cloud, with scales set from nearest-neighbor distances [2].

## 5. Empirical Evidence and Proofs

The quantitative record is decisive. On the Mip-NeRF 360 benchmark, 3D Gaussian Splatting reaches parity with mip-NeRF 360 — the previous quality champion — after roughly 51 minutes of training, while rendering at real-time rates; at training budgets comparable to Instant-NGP it matches Instant-NGP's quality ceiling [2]. NeRF itself, on its synthetic benchmark, established the MLP-plus-volume-rendering paradigm at $\sim$30 dB PSNR but required $\sim$1–2 days of training and seconds per frame [1]. Mip-NeRF reduced error by 60% over NeRF on multiscale test imagery while halving parameters [3].

The *proof-theoretic* content is equally important:

- **Theorem 1** establishes that NeRF's quadrature and the splatting rasterizer compute the *same* compositing operator; the methods differ in representation, not in the image formation model.
- **The IPE derivation** [3, 4] is a genuine piece of analysis: the Gaussian expectation of the Fourier features yields an exact low-pass filter, converting an aliasing problem into a bandwidth-selection problem solved automatically by the cone geometry.
- **Convergence**: [5] proves that re-ordered directional integration preserves the NeRF fixed point while improving the conditioning of the quadrature, explaining part of why coarse-to-fine and proposal-based samplers train faster.

The optimization itself — $\mathcal{L} = (1-\lambda)\mathcal{L}_1 + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{D-SSIM}}$ with $\lambda = 0.2$ over roughly $M \approx 10^5$–$10^6$ primitives — is non-convex, yet three structural facts make it tractable: (i) all gradients are exact analytic expressions, free of autodiff noise; (ii) the loss is a sum of per-pixel terms with local support in screen space, giving the Hessian a sparse, tile-local structure; (iii) densification acts as a *greedy basis pursuit*, adding representational capacity precisely where the residual gradient is large.

## 6. Limitations

The framework has sharp edges. **Aliasing is not solved, only relocated**: the EWA projection low-passes in screen space per view, but minification across *training views of different scales* can still alias, and recent work (Mip-Splatting, analytic footprint integration) exists precisely because 3DGS inherits a milder form of NeRF's original disease. **Memory scales with appearance complexity**: $M$ primitives × (3 + 4 + 3 + 1 + 48) floats easily exceeds a gigabyte for large scenes, and the representation is unstructured — no spatial hierarchy, no level of detail. **The sorting discontinuity** means gradients ignore permutation changes; pathological oscillation of depth order can stall optimization, though in practice the opacity falloff smooths the landscape. **Dynamics are absent**: the formulation assumes a static scene, and extending Gaussians through time (4D splatting) reintroduces the correspondence problem NeRF also never solved. **View extrapolation** remains poor — both NeRF and 3DGS are interpolation machines, and SH coefficients of degree 3 cannot represent sharp mirror reflections or complex global illumination. Finally, the entire pipeline depends on COLMAP poses; pose error propagates directly into floater artifacts that no densification heuristic fully removes.

## 7. Conclusion

Neural rendering's trajectory from NeRF to 3D Gaussian Splatting is a case study in the *conservation of mathematical structure under re-parameterization*. The volume rendering integral survives intact — Theorem 1 shows the rasterizer computes the same compositing operator that NeRF's quadrature approximates. What changed is where learning happens: from the weights of a coordinate MLP evaluated hundreds of times per ray, to the parameters of explicit primitives evaluated once per splat. Mip-NeRF's integrated positional encoding supplied the missing sampling theory, proving that anti-aliasing is bandwidth selection and that scale can be featurized rather than feared. The tile-based differentiable rasterizer supplied the systems breakthrough, with analytic gradients through projection, sorting, and blending. Together they moved neural rendering across the real-time threshold without surrendering quality. The open frontier — anti-aliased minification, compact hierarchical representations, dynamic scenes, and a genuine convergence theory for the densification dynamics — will likely require as much new mathematics as the last five years produced.
