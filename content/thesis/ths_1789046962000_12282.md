# Neuromorphic Computing: Spiking Neural Networks, Event-Driven Hardware Architectures, and Surrogate-Gradient Learning

## Abstract

Neuromorphic computing reframes computation around the brain's native currency — *spikes*: sparse, asynchronous, all-or-nothing events that carry information in their timing rather than in high-precision amplitudes. This thesis develops the full stack of the field, from the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron and its temporal coding schemes, through the spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) that wires biology, to the *surrogate-gradient* methods that finally made deep spiking neural networks (SNNs) trainable with gradient descent [1]. We formalize the nondifferentiability problem of the spiking nonlinearity, derive the surrogate-gradient estimator and the SuperSpike three-factor rule [2], and survey event-driven silicon — IBM TrueNorth [4], Intel Loihi and Loihi 2 [3], and the million-core SpiNNaker machine [5] — quantifying their energy efficiency against von Neumann baselines. We close with a critical analysis of the gap between simulation and deployment, the precision and latency costs of surrogate gradients, and the open question of whether spike-based computation will scale to foundation-model workloads or remain a specialist architecture for the ultra-low-power edge.

## 1 Introduction

The von Neumann architecture separates memory from processing and shuttles data between them at enormous energetic cost — training one large model can consume megawatt-hours. The mammalian brain performs roughly $10^{14}$ synaptic operations per second on about 20 watts. This efficiency gap is the founding motivation of *neuromorphic computing*: the design of hardware and algorithms that compute the way brains do — with networks of simple spiking neurons communicating through sparse, asynchronous, temporally precise events.

Three intellectual threads converge in this thesis. The first is the **neuron model**: the leaky integrate-and-fire unit, whose membrane potential integrates synaptic current and emits a spike upon crossing a threshold, yielding a dynamical system whose state is both continuous (the membrane voltage) and discrete (the spike train). The second is **learning**: from the biologically observed spike-timing-dependent plasticity rule [6], which adjusts synapses according to the relative timing of pre- and post-synaptic spikes, to the modern surrogate-gradient framework [1] that sidesteps the nondifferentiability of the spike mechanism and enables backpropagation through time in deep SNNs. The third is **hardware**: a generation of event-driven chips — TrueNorth, Loihi, SpiNNaker, and the billion-neuron Hala Point system [8] — that instantiate these principles in silicon and demonstrate energy-delay products orders of magnitude below conventional processors on suitable workloads.

> **Thesis claim:** *Surrogate-gradient learning closes the training gap between spiking and conventional networks, and event-driven hardware converts the resulting sparsity into genuine energy savings — but the composition of the two, i.e., deep SNNs deployed at scale on neuromorphic silicon, remains an engineering frontier rather than a solved problem.*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The spiking neuron

A spiking neuron is a dynamical system. Its internal state, the *membrane potential* $V(t)$, evolves under the influence of synaptic input currents and intrinsic leak, and when $V(t)$ reaches a threshold $\theta$, the neuron emits a stereotyped pulse — a *spike* — and resets. The canonical model is the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron:

$$\tau_m \frac{dV}{dt} = -(V - V_{\text{rest}}) + R\, I(t), \qquad V(t) \geq \theta \Rightarrow \text{spike, } V \leftarrow V_{\text{reset}}$$

where $\tau_m$ is the membrane time constant ($10$–$20$ ms in biology) and $I(t)$ the summed synaptic current. The LIF captures the essential primitive: **integration of evidence over time, with a discrete, all-or-nothing decision**.

### 2.2 Neural codes

Information in an SNN can be carried by several *codes*:

- **Rate coding**: the stimulus is represented by the spike count or mean firing rate in a window. Robust but slow; a rate code discards the precise timing that silicon could exploit.
- **Temporal coding**: information resides in *when* spikes occur — first-spike latency, inter-spike intervals, or full spike-time patterns. Temporally coded networks can in principle decide in a single spike per neuron, minimizing latency and energy.
- **Population coding**: a stimulus is represented across a population's distributed spike pattern, trading neuron count for temporal precision.

The choice of code interacts profoundly with learning: rate-coded networks admit conversion from trained ANNs, while temporally coded networks demand true spike-time learning rules such as SuperSpike [2].

### 2.3 The von Neumann bottleneck and the event-driven alternative

In conventional processors, every multiply-accumulate executes whether or not its inputs changed. In neuromorphic hardware, **nothing happens unless a spike arrives**: synaptic integration, the dominant operation, is performed only at active synapses, and inactive neurons consume only leakage power. This *event-driven* discipline is the source of the reported energy advantages: when network activity is sparse — as it is in sensory processing — the effective operations per joule can exceed conventional accelerators by orders of magnitude [4].

### 2.4 A note on simulation vs. silicon

Much SNN research runs in clock-driven simulators (NEST, Brian2, snnTorch) that update all neurons synchronously each $\Delta t$; true neuromorphic chips are *event-driven and asynchronous*, updating state only when spikes arrive — a distinction that matters for energy accounting and learning-rule fidelity (Section 6).

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a synthesis and critical analysis rather than a single experiment; our methodology is therefore that of the theoretical survey with quantitative grounding:

1. **Formalization.** We state the LIF dynamics, the STDP update, and the surrogate-gradient estimator as precise mathematical objects, identifying exactly where nondifferentiability enters and what the surrogate replaces.
2. **Source verification.** Every hardware figure is traced to a primary source — architecture papers, vendor announcements, or peer-reviewed reviews — and every learning result to its published paper [1][2][7].
3. **Comparative analysis.** We compare neuromorphic architectures along the axes of *neuron model flexibility*, *on-chip learning support*, *scale*, and *energy efficiency*, using a summary table (Section 4.4).
4. **Critical evaluation.** Section 6 applies an adversarial lens: we enumerate the failure modes of surrogate gradients (gradient mismatch, dead neurons, simulation-reality gaps) and the scaling objections raised against neuromorphic systems (e.g., the parallelism critique in [8]'s coverage).

All code sketches are pedagogical PyTorch-style pseudocode illustrating the surrogate-gradient mechanism, not production training scripts.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron and Temporal Codes

Discretizing with $\beta = \exp(-\Delta t/\tau_m)$ yields the form used in nearly all deep-learning SNN frameworks:

```python
# Discrete-time LIF neuron, one timestep
V = beta * V + (1 - beta) * I      # leaky integration of input current
S = (V >= theta).float()           # Heaviside spike: nondifferentiable!
V = V * (1 - S) + V_reset * S      # hard reset on spike
```

The crux of the field sits in the second line: $S = H(V - \theta)$ is a Heaviside step whose derivative is zero almost everywhere and a Dirac delta at the threshold. Standard backpropagation cannot flow through it — every SNN learning method is a strategy for coping with this line [1].

*Temporal coding* exploits spike *timing*: in a first-spike-latency code, a neuron's single spike time $t_f$ encodes the input, bounding decision latency by the earliest spikes rather than an integration window. Here SNNs can beat ANNs in *latency* as well as energy — one spike triggers one synaptic fan-out instead of hundreds of dense MACs.

> **Theorem (informal, temporal capacity):** *A network of $N$ deterministic spiking neurons with $T$ distinguishable spike-time bins can represent $T^N$ distinct patterns with at most one spike per neuron, whereas a rate code requires $\mathcal{O}(T)$ spikes per neuron to achieve comparable resolution.*

The theorem is a counting argument, not a learning guarantee — and the gap between representational capacity and *learnable* capacity is precisely what Sections 4.2–4.3 address.

### 4.2 Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity and Local Learning Rules

Long before gradients were backpropagated through spikes, biology offered its own learning rule. **Spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP)** modifies a synapse according to the relative timing $\Delta t = t_{\text{post}} - t_{\text{pre}}$ of pre- and post-synaptic spikes [6]:

$$\Delta w = \begin{cases}
A_{+} \exp(-\Delta t / \tau_{+}) & \Delta t > 0 \quad \text{(pre-before-post: potentiation)} \\
-A_{-} \exp(\Delta t / \tau_{-}) & \Delta t < 0 \quad \text{(post-before-pre: depression)}
\end{cases}$$

Bi and Poo's systematic mapping in cultured hippocampal neurons revealed a coincidence window of roughly $40$ ms with an astonishingly sharp ($\sim 1$ ms) transition between potentiation and depression at zero lag [6]. STDP is *Hebbian* ("cells that fire together wire together"), *local* (each synapse needs only its two endpoint spike times), and *unsupervised* — making it trivially implementable in neuromorphic hardware, where Loihi's microcode learning engine directly supports pairwise and triplet STDP [3].

STDP's limitation as an ML tool is fundamental: it optimizes no global objective. Pure-STDP networks learn useful *features* but fall short of supervised deep networks on demanding benchmarks — motivating the gradient-based methods of the next section.

```python
# Canonical STDP update (nearest-neighbor pairing, pseudocode)
def stdp_update(w, dt, A_plus=0.01, A_minus=0.012, tau=20e-3):
    if dt > 0:
        return w + A_plus * math.exp(-dt / tau)   # causal: strengthen
    else:
        return w - A_minus * math.exp(dt / tau)   # acausal: weaken
```

### 4.3 Surrogate-Gradient Learning and the SuperSpike Rule

The **surrogate gradient** idea [1] is disarmingly simple: keep the hard Heaviside step in the *forward* pass (so the network truly spikes), but replace its derivative with a smooth *surrogate derivative* in the *backward* pass:

$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial V} \;\approx\; \sigma'(V - \theta)$$

where $\sigma$ is any smooth function whose derivative peaks near the threshold and decays away from it. Common choices include:

| Surrogate | Derivative $\sigma'(x)$ | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| Fast sigmoid | $1 / (1 + \beta\lvert x\rvert)^2$ | Zenke & Ganguli [2] |
| Arctangent | $\alpha / (2(1 + (\pi\alpha x/2)^2))$ | Fang et al. |
| SuperSpike | $1 / (1 + \beta\lvert x\rvert)^2$ (scaled) | Zenke & Vogels |
| Triangular / boxcar | piecewise linear | Esser et al. |

Empirically, training is remarkably *robust to the choice of surrogate* [7] — the shape matters less than the fact that some gradient signal reaches subthreshold neurons. Theoretical work has since shown that surrogate gradients can be understood as gradients of a *smoothed* stochastic network, giving the heuristic a principled footing [7].

**SuperSpike** [2] applies the surrogate-gradient idea to *temporal* coding. It derives a nonlinear, voltage-based three-factor learning rule that minimizes the van Rossum distance between the network's output spike train and a target spike train:

$$\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2} \int \big( (\alpha * S_{\text{out}})(t) - (\alpha * S_{\text{target}})(t) \big)^2 dt$$

with $\alpha$ an exponential kernel. The resulting update factorizes into a presynaptic trace, a postsynaptic surrogate-derivative term, and a broadcast error signal — *local in space* despite deriving from a global objective. Random feedback (feedback alignment) suffices for simple tasks, though complex ones required symmetric weights [2].

```python
# Surrogate-gradient LIF with autograd (PyTorch-style pseudocode)
class FastSigmoid(torch.autograd.Function):
    @staticmethod
    def forward(ctx, V, theta, beta=10.0):
        ctx.save_for_backward(V, torch.tensor(theta), torch.tensor(beta))
        return (V >= theta).float()          # hard spike forward
    @staticmethod
    def backward(ctx, grad_out):
        V, theta, beta = ctx.saved_tensors
        # smooth surrogate derivative backward: the whole trick
        grad_V = grad_out / (1 + beta * (V - theta).abs()) ** 2
        return grad_V, None, None

# Training loop: unroll over time, BPTT through the surrogate
for x_seq, target in dataloader:            # x_seq: [T, B, features]
    V = torch.zeros(B, N); loss = 0
    for t in range(T):
        I = x_seq[t] @ W_in
        V = beta * V + (1 - beta) * I
        S = FastSigmoid.apply(V, theta)     # spikes, differentiable-ish
        V = V * (1 - S)                     # reset
        loss = loss + readout_loss(S, target, t)
    loss.backward(); optimizer.step()      # gradients flow via surrogate
```

This recipe — *unroll in time, substitute the surrogate derivative, backpropagate* — is the workhorse behind essentially all modern deep SNN results [1].

### 4.4 Event-Driven Silicon: TrueNorth, Loihi, SpiNNaker, and Hala Point

Algorithmic advances need substrates that convert spike sparsity into energy savings. Five systems define the hardware landscape:

- **IBM TrueNorth** (Merolla et al., *Science*, 2014) [4]: 4,096 neurosynaptic cores, $10^6$ digital neurons, $256 \times 10^6$ synapses, 5.4 billion transistors in 28 nm, consuming roughly $70$ mW — the landmark proof that million-neuron digital SNNs are feasible at hearing-aid power budgets. Its synapses are *non-plastic*, targeting inference.
- **Intel Loihi** (Davies et al., *IEEE Micro*, 2018) [3]: 128 neuromorphic cores on a $60\,\text{mm}^2$ 14 nm die, $130$K neurons and $130$M synapses, with a *microcode-programmable learning engine* per core supporting STDP variants, hierarchical connectivity, and dendritic compartments — the first fully programmable on-chip learning architecture.
- **Intel Loihi 2** (2021): fabricated on Intel 4, up to $10^6$ neurons and $123$M synapses per chip with 8,192 neurons per neurocore, graded (multi-bit) spikes, fully programmable neuron models, and sub-timestep asynchronous messaging with barrier synchronization (minimum timestep $\sim 200$ ns).
- **SpiNNaker** (Furber et al., University of Manchester) [5]: a million ARM968 cores across 57,600 nodes with 7 TB of distributed RAM, communicating via tiny (40/72-bit) spike packets over a custom fabric — a machine designed to simulate up to a billion neurons in biological real time, deliberately discarding memory coherence, synchronicity, and determinism.
- **Hala Point** (2024) [8]: 1,152 Loihi 2 chips — $1.15$ billion neurons, $128$ billion synapses — in a 6U chassis drawing $2.6$ kW, rated above $15$ trillion 8-bit ops/sec/watt on conventional DNN inference.

| System | Neurons | Synapses | Process | Learning | Power |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TrueNorth [4] | $10^6$ | $256$M | 28 nm | Inference only | $\sim 70$ mW |
| Loihi [3] | $130$K | $130$M | 14 nm | On-chip STDP | $\sim$ tens of mW–W |
| Loihi 2 | $10^6$/chip | $123$M/chip | Intel 4 | Programmable | chip-scale |
| SpiNNaker [5] | $10^9$ (target) | — | 130 nm | Software-defined | $\sim 100$ kW (full) |
| Hala Point [8] | $1.15 \times 10^9$ | $128 \times 10^9$ | Intel 4 | Research | $2.6$ kW |

A striking early result: Loihi solving LASSO sparse-coding problems achieved over *three orders of magnitude* better energy-delay product than a CPU solver — a concrete demonstration that mapping the right algorithm to event-driven dynamics yields the promised efficiency [3].

### 4.5 The Software Stack: Lava, Lava DL, and Deployment

Intel's **Lava** framework [9] provides the missing software layer with a process-based, asynchronous programming model: every component is a *Process* communicating over *Channels*, and the same application graph compiles to CPUs, GPUs, or Loihi 2 neurocores. The critical bridge is **Lava DL**, which trains SNNs offline in PyTorch with surrogate gradients and hardware-aware quantization, then exports them via the `netx` exchange format for deployment to Loihi 2 [9]. This *train-offline, deploy-event-driven* pipeline is currently the most practical route to real applications: gradient-based training where gradients are cheap (GPUs), event-driven inference where energy matters (neuromorphic silicon).

## 5 Empirical Evidence / Proofs

**Learning performance.** Surrogate-gradient SNNs now sit within a few percentage points of ANN baselines on standard benchmarks (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10, and event-based N-MNIST/CIFAR10-DVS), a regime that was unreachable before 2018 [1][7]. SuperSpike demonstrated precise learning of *arbitrary spatiotemporal spike patterns* in multilayer deterministic networks [2]. The robustness of these results to the surrogate's exact shape [7] suggests the method captures something real about credit assignment in spiking systems rather than overfitting a particular smoothing.

**Energy efficiency.** The hardware numbers are the field's strongest card. TrueNorth's $46$ billion synaptic-operations-per-second-per-watt and $63$–$70$ mW full-chip operation [4]; Loihi's three-order-of-magnitude energy-delay advantage on LASSO [3]; Hala Point's $>15$ TOPS/W on DNN inference at billion-neuron scale [8] — each is a measured datapoint, and together they establish that event-driven architectures *can* convert sparsity into efficiency, at least on workloads matched to their strengths.

**Theoretical grounding.** The surrogate gradient is no longer pure heuristic: it is exact gradient descent on a stochastically smoothed network, with the surrogate derivative emerging from the noise distribution [7].

> **Theorem (surrogate gradient as smoothed gradient, informal [7]):** *For a stochastic spiking neuron with escape noise, the expected gradient of the loss with respect to the weights equals the gradient computed with a surrogate derivative determined by the noise CDF. Deterministic surrogate-gradient descent is therefore the zero-temperature limit of exact stochastic gradient descent.*

## 6 Limitations

Intellectual honesty requires enumerating where the paradigm strains:

1. **Gradient mismatch.** The surrogate derivative is not the true derivative (which is zero almost everywhere). Surrogate-gradient descent therefore does not follow the gradient of any fixed objective in the deterministic network; pathologies — biased updates, sensitivity to the surrogate's width $\beta$ — are documented [7].
2. **The dead-neuron problem.** A neuron that never spikes receives no surrogate gradient (the derivative is evaluated at subthreshold voltages only when spikes nearly occur) and can remain silent forever, wasting capacity. Rate-coded readouts and regularization partially mitigate this.
3. **Temporal credit assignment cost.** BPTT through $T$ timesteps multiplies memory and compute by $T$ relative to an ANN forward pass; the training itself is *not* energy-efficient, even if the deployed network is. Online alternatives (e-prop, SuperSpike-style three-factor rules) trade accuracy for locality.
4. **Simulation–reality gaps.** Surrogate-gradient models are trained in clock-driven simulators with idealized neuron models; deploying to asynchronous silicon with quantized weights, limited fan-in, and device mismatch degrades accuracy. Lava DL's hardware-aware training narrows but does not close this gap [9].
5. **The parallelism critique.** As noted in coverage of Hala Point [8], neuromorphic systems process single inputs through time, whereas GPUs parallelize massively across batches; training a brain-scale model on neuromorphic hardware could take decades with current algorithms. The field's honest answer is that neuromorphic computing targets *inference at the edge* and *continual learning*, not foundation-model training.
6. **Benchmark narrowness.** Most SNN results are on small vision benchmarks; there is as yet no spiking equivalent of a large language model, and it is unclear whether the temporal-coding advantages survive at that scale.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron is a computationally potent primitive; STDP shows local spike-driven plasticity is implementable in silicon; surrogate gradients [1] and SuperSpike [2] made deep spiking networks trainable; and chips from TrueNorth [4] through Loihi [3] to Hala Point [8] prove event-driven hardware converts spike sparsity into measured energy efficiency.

The open problems are now sharper than the early hype. We need learning rules that are *both* gradient-competitive and hardware-local; simulators and chips whose numerics agree; benchmarks beyond toy vision; and an honest accounting of where spikes beat dense arithmetic (sparse, streaming, always-on sensing) and where they do not (massively parallel batch training). The most promising near-term trajectory is hybrid: train with surrogate gradients on GPUs, deploy event-driven SNNs via stacks like Lava [9] to milliwatt silicon at the edge — and let the billion-neuron machines [8] teach us what large-scale spike-based computation can ultimately become.
