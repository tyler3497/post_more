# Homomorphic Encryption: the Ring-LWE Problem, the CKKS Approximate-Arithmetic Scheme, and Bootstrapping

## Abstract

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) permits arbitrary computation on encrypted data without access to the secret key, a capability first realized by Gentry in 2009 [1]. Modern practice is dominated by schemes built on the *Ring Learning With Errors* (Ring-LWE) problem, whose worst-case to average-case reduction over ideal lattices supplies both efficiency and credible post-quantum hardness [2]. Among Ring-LWE schemes, the Cheon–Kim–Kim–Song (CKKS) scheme stands apart: instead of exact arithmetic over finite fields, it natively supports *approximate* arithmetic over real and complex numbers through a canonical-embedding encoding and a rescaling procedure that mirrors floating-point rounding [4][5]. This thesis develops the full technical arc from the Ring-LWE hardness assumption to the CKKS construction, analyzes noise growth and the rescaling invariant, and explains bootstrapping — the noise-refreshing procedure that lifts leveled schemes to full homomorphism — including the CKKS bootstrapping circuit based on homomorphic modular reduction [6][7]. We state the central correctness and security theorems with proof sketches, report published performance figures, and discuss limitations and open problems.

## 1 Introduction

In 1978, Rivest, Adleman, and Dertouzos asked whether one could compute on encrypted data without decrypting it first, introducing the notion of *privacy homomorphisms*. For three decades the question resisted a complete answer: RSA gave multiplicative homomorphism, Paillier gave additive homomorphism, and the Boneh–Goh–Nissim system gave one multiplication, but arbitrary circuits remained out of reach. Gentry's 2009 thesis changed this landscape decisively by exhibiting the first *fully* homomorphic encryption scheme, built from ideal lattices and equipped with a *bootstrapping* procedure that refreshes noisy ciphertexts by homomorphically evaluating the decryption circuit [1].

> **Theorem: (Gentry's blueprint, informal).** A *somewhat* homomorphic encryption scheme whose decryption circuit has depth strictly less than the depth of circuits the scheme can homomorphically evaluate can be converted into a fully homomorphic encryption scheme, under a circular-security assumption.

The practical story since 2009 has been one of successive generations. Real-world computation is rarely exact. Machine learning, statistics, and scientific simulation all tolerate small numerical error, and floating-point arithmetic is the universal substrate. The CKKS scheme of Cheon, Kim, Kim, and Song, introduced at ASIACRYPT 2017, is the first homomorphic encryption scheme designed from the ground up for *approximate* arithmetic [4][5]. Its central insight is to treat encryption noise not as an enemy but as *part of the approximation error*, no different in kind from floating-point rounding.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Lattices and the Learning With Errors problem

A *lattice* is a discrete additive subgroup of Euclidean space, typically specified by a basis of linearly independent vectors. Two computational problems on lattices underpin modern lattice cryptography: the *Shortest Vector Problem* (SVP) and the *Closest Vector Problem* (CVP), both believed hard even for quantum computers in their worst-case approximation forms.

Regev's *Learning With Errors* (LWE) problem distills lattice hardness into a clean average-case assumption. Given a secret vector **s** over the integers modulo *q*, the LWE distribution produces samples (**a**, *b* = ⟨**a**, **s**⟩ + *e* mod *q*), where **a** is uniform and *e* is a small error drawn from a narrow distribution. The *search* problem asks to recover **s**; the *decision* problem asks to distinguish such samples from uniform ones. Regev proved that solving LWE on average is at least as hard as approximating worst-case lattice problems via a quantum reduction — the first average-case cryptographic assumption with a worst-case hardness guarantee of this strength.

### 2.2 Ideal lattices and Ring-LWE

An *ideal lattice* is a lattice that is also an ideal in a ring — typically the ring of integers of a number field. The extra algebraic structure allows compact representations: a single ring element replaces an *n*-by-*n* matrix.

Lyubashevsky, Peikert, and Regev introduced *Ring-LWE*, the ring analogue of LWE, and proved that it inherits the same style of worst-case to average-case reduction, now relative to worst-case problems on ideal lattices [2]. Concretely, one works in a cyclotomic ring *R* = ℤ[*X*]/(*X*^*N* + 1) with *N* a power of two, and samples take the form (*a*, *b* = *a*·*s* + *e*) with multiplication in the quotient ring *R_q* = *R*/*qR*. A single multiplication in this ring, computable in quasi-linear time via the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT), replaces *n* separate LWE samples. This is the efficiency breakthrough on which all modern lattice-based FHE is built.

### 2.3 Generations of fully homomorphic encryption

It is useful to organize the field into generations:

| Generation | Representative | Hardness | Key technique |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | Gentry 2009 [1] | Ideal lattices / BDDH-like | Bootstrapping via squashed decryption |
| Second | BGV [3], BFV | (Ring-)LWE | Modulus switching, leveled FHE |
| Third | GSW, FHEW, TFHE | LWE | Approximate eigenvectors, fast gate bootstrapping |
| Approximate | CKKS [4][5] | Ring-LWE | Canonical-embedding encoding, rescaling |

### 2.4 Approximate computation as a design principle

Conventional wisdom held that encryption noise must be managed so that decryption is *exact*. CKKS inverts this: the plaintext itself is an approximation, so a small additional error from homomorphic operations is harmless provided it stays below the application's tolerance.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is analytic and constructive, in four stages: (1) formalize Ring-LWE over cyclotomic rings and its worst-case to average-case reduction [2]; (2) construct CKKS bottom-up — encoding, encryption, homomorphic operations with relinearization, and rescaling [4][5]; (3) analyze how approximation error evolves through the operations; (4) describe bootstrapping via homomorphic modular reduction [6][7]. We assume IND-CPA security throughout.

**Notation.** Throughout, *N* is a power-of-two ring dimension, *R* = ℤ[*X*]/(*X*^*N* + 1), *R_q* = *R*/*qR*, Δ is the real scaling factor, *s* the secret key (a sparse ternary polynomial), χ the error distribution (discrete Gaussian of small standard deviation σ), and ℓ the current level in a modulus chain *q_ℓ*. Vectors are bold; ⟨·,·⟩ denotes inner product; σ_can denotes the canonical embedding *R* → ℂ^*N*. Ciphertexts at level ℓ are pairs (*c_0*, *c_1*) in *R_{q_ℓ}²*.

## 4 Deep Dive

### The Ring-LWE problem and its hardness foundation

> **Definition (Ring-LWE distribution).** Let *R* be the 2*N*-th cyclotomic ring, *q* an integer modulus, and χ an error distribution over *R*. For a secret *s* ∈ *R_q*, the Ring-LWE distribution *A_{s,χ}* samples *a* ← *R_q* uniformly, *e* ← χ, and outputs (*a*, *b* = *a*·*s* + *e* mod *q*).

The *search* Ring-LWE problem asks to recover *s* from polynomially many samples; the *decision* problem asks to distinguish *A_{s,χ}* from the uniform distribution on *R_q* × *R_q*. The central hardness result is:

> **Theorem: (Lyubashevsky–Peikert–Regev [2], informal).** If there exists an efficient algorithm solving decision Ring-LWE for suitable parameters, then there exists an efficient *quantum* algorithm approximating worst-case problems (such as the shortest independent vectors problem) on ideal lattices in the corresponding cyclotomic ring, to within polynomial approximation factors.

Several aspects of this theorem deserve attention:

- **Worst-case to average-case.** The reduction starts from *any* ideal lattice in the family, so breaking the scheme requires succeeding on the hardest instances.
- **Quantum vs. classical.** The reduction is quantum; a classical reduction is known only for restricted parameters, so the hardness claim is marginally weaker than one might wish — though no quantum attack is known either.

A toy illustration of Ring-LWE sampling in Python (small parameters, *not* secure) clarifies the mechanics:

```python
import numpy as np

N, q, sigma = 8, 97, 3.2          # toy parameters only
def poly_mul(a, b):
    c = np.convolve(a, b) % q
    # reduce modulo X^N + 1: X^N = -1
    for i in range(len(c) - 1, N - 1, -1):
        c[i - N] = (c[i - N] - c[i]) % q
    return c[:N]

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
s = rng.choice([-1, 0, 1], size=N)                 # sparse ternary secret
a = rng.integers(0, q, size=N)                     # uniform ring element
e = np.round(rng.normal(0, sigma, size=N)).astype(int) % q  # small error
b = (poly_mul(a, s) + e) % q
# (a, b) is one Ring-LWE sample: b = a*s + e  (mod q, mod X^N+1)
```

### The CKKS encoding: packing complex vectors into polynomials

The most original component of CKKS is its *encoding*. Where BGV and BFV encode messages as polynomials with small integer coefficients, CKKS encodes a vector **z** ∈ ℂ^{*N*/2} of complex numbers into a single polynomial *m* ∈ *R*.

The bridge is the *canonical embedding* σ_can: *R* → ℂ^*N*, which evaluates a polynomial at the 2*N*-th roots of unity. Encoding inverts this pipeline with a scaling factor Δ:

1. Take the message vector **z** ∈ ℂ^{*N*/2} and lift it to *H* via π^{−1}.
2. Multiply by the scaling factor Δ (a power of two in practice) to shift significant digits left.
3. Apply σ_can^{−1} and *round* each coefficient to the nearest integer, obtaining *m* ∈ *R*.

Decoding reverses the steps: apply σ_can, divide by Δ, and project. The rounding error, bounded by roughly *N*/(2Δ) in the embedding norm, is the *encoding error* — the first of several small errors the scheme deliberately tolerates.

Why is this encoding homomorphic-friendly? Addition of polynomials corresponds to slot-wise addition of the complex vectors, and — crucially — multiplication of polynomials corresponds, *approximately*, to slot-wise multiplication, because σ_can is a ring homomorphism and the rounding is the only source of deviation. A single ciphertext therefore carries *N*/2 independent complex *slots*, enabling SIMD-style batching: one homomorphic operation acts on all slots at once.

### Homomorphic operations, relinearization, and rescaling

**Key generation and encryption.** The secret key is a sparse ternary polynomial *s*. The public key is a Ring-LWE sample *pk* = (*b*, *a*) with *b* = −*a*·*s* + *e* mod *q_L*. Encryption of a plaintext polynomial *m* outputs *c_0* = *r*·*b* + *m* + *e_0* and *c_1* = *r*·*a* + *e_1* for random *r* and small errors *e_0*, *e_1*, modulo *q_L*. Decryption computes *c_0* + *c_1*·*s* = *m* + (small noise) mod *q_L* and decodes.

**Addition** is component-wise and adds noise terms linearly — essentially free. **Multiplication** is the expensive operation. Given ciphertexts encrypting *m_1*, *m_2* with a common secret *s*, the tensor product (*d_0*, *d_1*, *d_2*) = (*c_0c_0′*, *c_0c_1′* + *c_1c_0′*, *c_1c_1′*) decrypts under (1, *s*, *s*²) to *m_1m_2* plus noise terms that include products of the input noises. Two follow-up steps restore a two-component ciphertext:

1. **Relinearization** converts the degree-2 term *d_2* back into a linear term using an *evaluation key* — encryptions of powers of *s*² under *s* — at the cost of a key-switching noise term.
2. **Rescaling** divides the ciphertext by Δ and rounds, dropping from modulus *q_ℓ* to *q_{ℓ−1}* = *q_ℓ*/Δ.

Rescaling is the heart of CKKS. Without it, each multiplication would square the scaling factor, and the message would overflow the modulus after a few levels. With it, the invariant is maintained: at every level, the plaintext sits at scale Δ, and the noise — although it grows — stays *relatively* small. Concretely, if input ciphertexts carry noise of magnitude *B* relative to a message of magnitude Δ·*M*, then after multiplication and rescaling the relative noise is roughly *B*·(something like the message magnitude)/Δ plus an additive rounding term of order 1. Choosing Δ ≈ 2^40 or larger makes the rounding error negligible, and the dominant term grows only *linearly* with multiplicative depth — this is why the modulus chain grows linearly with depth rather than exponentially [4].

The following table summarizes noise behavior across operations (magnitudes in the canonical embedding norm):

| Operation | Message scale | Noise growth | Modulus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh encryption | Δ | *O*(σ√*N*) | *q_L* |
| Homomorphic addition | Δ | additive: *B_1* + *B_2* | unchanged |
| Tensor multiplication | Δ² | ≈ *B_1M_2* + *B_2M_1* + *B_1B_2* | unchanged |
| Relinearization | Δ² | + key-switching term | unchanged |
| Rescaling | Δ² → Δ | ÷ Δ, + *O*(1) rounding | *q_ℓ* → *q_{ℓ−1}* |

The modulus chain is typically chosen as *q_ℓ* = (∏_{i≤ℓ} *p_i*)·*q_0* with each *p_i* ≈ Δ, implemented via the Residue Number System (RNS) so that all arithmetic uses 64-bit limbs and NTT-friendly primes.

### Bootstrapping: refreshing approximate ciphertexts

After ℓ multiplications the ciphertext sits at level 0 with modulus *q_0*; no further multiplication is possible. A *leveled* scheme must fix the maximum depth at key-generation time. *Bootstrapping* removes this restriction: given a ciphertext at the bottom level, it homomorphically evaluates the decryption circuit on an encryption of the secret key, producing a fresh ciphertext at a high level encrypting the same message with small noise.

Gentry's blueprint requires the scheme to evaluate its own decryption circuit *plus one operation* within its homomorphic capacity — the scheme is then called *bootstrappable* [1]. For exact schemes like BGV, bootstrapping digit-extracts the message modulo the plaintext modulus, an expensive but well-understood procedure. CKKS bootstrapping, introduced by Cheon et al., faces a different obstacle: the "decryption" to be evaluated homomorphically is *m* + *e* reduced modulo *q_ℓ*, where the reduction must preserve the *approximate* message while removing the modulus wrap-around [6].

The CKKS bootstrapping circuit has four stages:

1. **ModRaise.** Lift the ciphertext from modulus *q_ℓ* to a large modulus *q_L* by interpreting its coefficients as integers. The plaintext becomes *m* + *e* + *q_ℓ*·*I* for an unknown small integer polynomial *I* — the wrap-around multiples introduced by the original reduction modulo *q_ℓ*.
2. **CoeffToSlot.** Homomorphically apply the linear transform σ_can (essentially a DFT) to move polynomial coefficients into the plaintext slots, so that subsequent operations act slot-wise.
3. **EvalMod.** Approximately evaluate the modular reduction *x* ↦ *x* mod *q_ℓ* on each slot. Since modular reduction is not a polynomial, it is *approximated* by one: Cheon et al. use a scaled sine function, exploiting that a scaled sine closely tracks the sawtooth of modular reduction near multiples of *q_ℓ* and can be evaluated by repeated squaring of a Taylor approximation [6]. Later work replaced the indirect sine approximation with direct polynomial approximations of the modular reduction, minimizing error variance for a given depth [7].
4. **SlotToCoeff.** Homomorphically apply the inverse transform to return to coefficient representation.

The output is a ciphertext at a high level encrypting Δ·*m* with fresh, small noise — the computation may continue. The price is depth: bootstrapping consumes on the order of a dozen levels itself, so parameters must include a dedicated *bootstrapping budget* on top of the application's circuit depth.

A Haskell-flavored sketch of the EvalMod approximation step conveys the structure (operating on plaintext values for clarity; the real evaluation is homomorphic):

```haskell
-- Approximate modular reduction via scaled sine (Cheon et al. [6])
-- Goal: f(x) ~= x - q * round(x / q)  for x near multiples of q
evalMod :: Double -> Double -> Int -> Double
evalMod q x r = q * taylorSine r (2 * pi * x / q) / (2 * pi)
  where
    -- degree-(2r+1) Taylor polynomial of sin, evaluated by repeated squaring
    taylorSine :: Int -> Double -> Double
    taylorSine r t = foldl step t [1..r]
      where step acc k =
              let t2 = acc * acc
              in acc * (1 - t2 / fromIntegral ((2*k) * (2*k+1)))
```

In the real scheme this polynomial is evaluated homomorphically with the baby-step giant-step method to minimize multiplicative depth.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Correctness and security theorems

We now state the three central theoretical guarantees, with proof sketches.

> **Theorem: (Decryption correctness).** Let (*c_0*, *c_1*) be a CKKS ciphertext at level ℓ encrypting *m* with noise *e* satisfying the infinity-norm bound ‖*e*‖_∞ < *q_ℓ*/4 in the canonical embedding. Then decryption followed by decoding returns **z**′ with ‖**z** − **z**′‖_∞ ≤ (‖*e*‖_∞ + *N*/2)/Δ.

*Proof sketch.* Decryption computes *c_0* + *c_1s* = *m* + *e* over the integers (no wrap-around occurs because the noise bound keeps the sum inside the fundamental domain of *q_ℓ*). Decoding applies σ_can/Δ: the *m*/Δ term recovers π^{−1}(**z**) up to the encoding rounding error *N*/(2Δ), and *e*/Δ contributes the homomorphic noise scaled down. ∎

> **Theorem: (IND-CPA security).** Under the decision Ring-LWE assumption for (*N*, *q_L*, χ), the CKKS public-key encryption scheme is IND-CPA secure; i.e., no efficient adversary distinguishes encryptions of two chosen messages with non-negligible advantage [4].

*Proof sketch.* The public key (*b*, *a*) is a Ring-LWE sample, hence pseudorandom under decision Ring-LWE. Each ciphertext component *r*·*b* + *m* + *e_0* re-randomizes via the ephemeral *r*; a standard hybrid argument replaces the public key and then each ciphertext component by uniform, at which point the ciphertext is independent of the message. The argument is identical in structure to the Regev/LPR public-key constructions [2]. ∎

> **Theorem: (Precision bound [5]).** The absolute precision loss incurred by homomorphic evaluation of a circuit of multiplicative depth *D* in CKKS is at most one bit more than the precision loss of evaluating the same circuit in unencrypted floating-point arithmetic.

*Proof sketch.* Rescaling after each multiplication divides both message and noise by Δ and adds a rounding error of order 1 — exactly the model of floating-point rounding to a fixed mantissa. Induction shows the accumulated error matches the floating-point error recurrence up to a constant factor absorbed by one extra bit. ∎

### Reported performance

The original CKKS bootstrapping implementation of Cheon et al. refreshes a ciphertext encrypting 128 complex slots in **139.8 seconds** at roughly 12 bits of precision — an amortized rate of about **1.1 seconds per slot** [6], and subsequent engineering has improved on these figures:

- The full-RNS variant reformulates the modulus structure so that every operation uses 64-bit limbs and NTT-friendly primes, removing multi-precision arithmetic from the hot loop [2nd reference in §2.3 context](https://eprint.iacr.org/2018/931).
- High-precision bootstrapping via direct, error-variance-optimal polynomial approximation of the modular reduction achieves 93-bit precision within multiplicative depth 11 for the EvalMod step, versus depths 11–12 for the earlier sine-based approach at lower accuracy [7].

## 6 Limitations

Despite its power, CKKS — and the Ring-LWE paradigm generally — has significant limitations:

1. **Approximation is mandatory, not optional.** CKKS cannot compute exact integer arithmetic; applications requiring exactness (e.g., exact AES evaluation, precise financial ledgers) must use BGV/BFV/TFHE instead. The approximate decryption also complicates security proofs for *exact* functionalities.
2. **Bootstrapping remains expensive.** Even with modern improvements, bootstrapping dominates running time in deep computations, and its depth consumption forces large parameters. Gate-level bootstrapping in TFHE is orders of magnitude faster per gate, though it lacks CKKS's SIMD throughput.
3. **No natural comparisons or branching.** Like all current FHE, CKKS offers no efficient encrypted comparison; control flow on encrypted data requires polynomial approximations of step functions or conversion to TFHE-style functional bootstrapping, both costly.
4. **The quantum reduction gap.** Ring-LWE's worst-case hardness rests on a *quantum* reduction [2]; a fully classical reduction would strengthen confidence. Relatedly, the extra algebraic structure of ideal lattices, while enabling efficiency, is also the surface exploited by subfield and overstretched-NTRU-style attacks — parameters must respect the Homomorphic Encryption Standard's guardrails.
5. **Side channels and misuse.** Implementations must protect key generation and decryption against timing and power analysis; moreover, CKKS's approximate decryption can leak secret-key information if decryption results are shared carelessly — a subtlety absent from exact schemes.

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from Gentry's 2009 breakthrough [1] to the CKKS scheme traces a productive shift in perspective: from exact computation over finite structures to *approximate* computation over the reals, with encryption noise reinterpreted as ordinary numerical error. Ring-LWE [2] supplies the hardness foundation and the algebraic structure that makes the scheme efficient; the canonical-embedding encoding packs complex vectors into polynomials with SIMD parallelism; rescaling maintains a floating-point-like invariant through homomorphic multiplications [4][5]; and bootstrapping — via homomorphic modular reduction built from polynomial approximations — refreshes ciphertexts so that depth need not be bounded in advance [6][7]. The precision theorem, bounding CKKS's error to within a bit of ordinary floating point, is the formal warrant for treating encrypted computation as numerical computation.

Open problems include faster bootstrapping with smaller depth consumption, fully classical hardness reductions for Ring-LWE, and hardware acceleration bringing bootstrapping latency into the millisecond regime. We can now compute, usefully and approximately, on data we cannot see.
