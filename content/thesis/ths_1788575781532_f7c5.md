# Deterministic Record and Replay: From Software Syscall Interception in rr to Hardware-Assisted Tracing and Distributed Time-Travel Debugging

## Abstract

Deterministic record/replay transforms the fundamental economics of debugging nondeterministic software failures: record an execution once, then replay it arbitrarily many times with full reverse-execution capability. This thesis surveys and unifies the principal architectures of modern record/replay systems. We analyze Mozilla rr's lightweight user-space approach, which pins recording to the user–kernel boundary, intercepts system calls through ptrace and seccomp-bpf, buffers syscalls to control overhead, and serializes multithreaded execution onto a single core using retired-branch perf counters as deterministic scheduling points [1][2]. We contrast this with hardware-assisted tracing via Intel Processor Trace (PT), which captures compressed control-flow packets in silicon with near-zero overhead and enables forward time-travel analysis in tools such as Linux perf [4][7]. We extend the analysis to distributed systems, where deterministic process groups (dOS) and deterministic network virtualization remove internal nondeterminism as an operating-system service rather than merely recording it [6]. Finally, we formalize replay-based fault injection and chaos testing, derive quantitative overhead models validated against published benchmarks, and evaluate the user experience of time-travel debugging, concluding with an analysis of the fundamental limitations of each approach and directions for future research.

---

## 1. Introduction

*Nondeterminism is the debugging tax on modern computing.* A parallel program given identical input may produce different output on two runs, because thread interleavings, asynchronous signal delivery, address-space layout randomization, and the timing of I/O completions all inject entropy that is invisible to the source code. Traditional forward debuggers assume *reproducibility*: the user restarts the program and hopes the failure reappears at the breakpoint. When the failure has a mean time to reproduce measured in thousands of executions — the classic *Heisenbug* — this workflow collapses. Test suites at Mozilla scale run millions of tests per commit; failures at rates of 10⁻⁴ are both too frequent to ignore and too rare to reproduce [3].

Deterministic record/replay (RRR) offers a principled escape. The central insight is that a program's execution is a **deterministic function** of its initial state plus a small set of *nondeterministic inputs*: system-call results, signal timings, shared-memory interleavings, and thread-scheduling decisions. If the recorder captures exactly those inputs — and nothing else — then replay is simply re-execution under a virtualized input stream, guaranteeing instruction-for-instruction fidelity. Once replay is deterministic, *reverse execution* becomes implementable: to step backwards, restart replay from the beginning and run forward to just before the current position, or restore from a checkpoint.

This thesis makes four contributions. **First**, we give a unified architectural taxonomy of RRR systems, separating *software boundary-interception* systems (rr), *hardware-assisted* systems (Intel PT), and *deterministic execution* systems (dOS). **Second**, we dissect rr's engineering in depth — syscall buffering, single-core scheduling, perf-counter scheduling points, and the treatment of data races — because rr is the most widely deployed pure-software RRR system and its design decisions reveal the fundamental trade-offs. **Third**, we quantify overheads using published benchmarks and derive a predictive cost model. **Fourth**, we extend the analysis to distributed record/replay and to replay-based chaos testing, and we evaluate the user experience of time-travel debugging.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Sources of nondeterminism

An execution of a user-space process is determined by its initial state $S_0$ and a sequence of nondeterministic events $E = e_1, e_2, \dots, e_n$. Following the terminology of Bergan et al. [6], we distinguish two classes:

| Class | Examples | Removable? |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal nondeterminism** | Thread interleavings, shared-memory races, signal timing, scheduling decisions | Yes — can be eliminated by deterministic scheduling |
| **External nondeterminism** | User input, network packet arrivals, disk I/O timing, `gettimeofday` results | No — fundamental; must be recorded or made explicit |

> Theorem: *Separation of determinism.* If all external nondeterminism $E_{ext}$ is logged during recording, and internal nondeterminism is either logged or structurally eliminated, then replay over the recorded log is a deterministic function of $(S_0, E_{ext})$ and reproduces the original execution's user-space-visible behavior exactly.

This theorem is the correctness foundation of every system surveyed here. The systems differ only in *where they place the recording boundary* and *how they log or eliminate* the two classes.

### 2.2 Historical approaches

Record/replay has been attempted since the 1980s. Early systems required **whole-virtual-machine recording** (VMware Workstation, Simics), which is robust but heavyweight. Others required a **modified OS kernel** or **custom hardware**, both of which face deployment barriers [1]. Pervasive instrumentation (binary rewriting, dynamic binary instrumentation) achieves fine-grained control but typically incurs 2×–20× slowdown, violating what O'Callahan calls the *deployability* criterion: users will not adopt a debugger that makes the program feel sluggish [1].

The modern state of the art converges on two low-overhead strategies: (i) intercept only at the **user–kernel boundary**, since almost all external nondeterminism crosses that boundary; and (ii) exploit **hardware features designed for other purposes** (performance counters, branch-trace silicon) as the timing and scheduling substrate.

### 2.3 The rr project

rr ("record and replay") is an open-source, user-space record/replay debugger for Linux on x86-64, originally developed at Mozilla under the leadership of Robert O'Callahan [1][2][3]. It targets C, C++, and Rust programs, requires no kernel modifications, and achieves recording overhead below 2× on realistic workloads. rr is the foundation of *reverse-execution debugging*: a gdb session attached to a replay supports `reverse-continue`, `reverse-step`, and `reverse-finish`, enabling the user to travel backwards from an observed failure to its root cause.

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis is a **comparative systems study** combining three methods:

1. **Architectural dissection.** We decompose each system along five axes: recording boundary, nondeterminism model, scheduling/interleaving strategy, trace representation, and replay/debugging interface. This yields a common vocabulary for comparing software, hardware-assisted, and distributed approaches.
2. **Quantitative modeling.** We reproduce the published overhead measurements for rr [1] and Intel PT tracing [4][5] and fit them to a parametric cost model with terms for syscall interception, context switching, trace bandwidth, and scheduling-point frequency.
3. **Formal analysis.** We state the determinism and fidelity guarantees as theorems and identify the exact assumptions — e.g., absence of unrecorded nondeterminism such as unsynchronized shared-memory races — on which each system depends.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 rr: the user–kernel boundary as recording frontier

rr's central design decision is to place the recording boundary **exactly at the interface between user space and the kernel** [1][2]. The rationale is elegant: nearly every source of *external* nondeterminism — file reads, network receives, `mmap` results, `gettimeofday`, futex wakeups, signal delivery timing — is mediated by a system call or an asynchronous event the kernel delivers. By intercepting every system call and every asynchronous event, and logging their user-space-visible effects, rr captures the complete set of nondeterministic inputs without instrumenting a single instruction of application code.

Mechanically, rr uses a combination of **ptrace** and **seccomp-bpf** [1]. During recording, rr supervises the tracee via ptrace, but trapping every syscall through ptrace alone would cost two context switches per syscall — prohibitively expensive for syscall-heavy workloads. The *system-call buffering* optimization [1] installs a seccomp-bpf filter that allows most syscalls to execute natively while a user-space interception library (injected into the tracee) records their results into a shared buffer. Only syscalls that cannot be safely buffered — those with complex blocking or descheduling semantics — fall through to ptrace traps.

During replay, syscalls are **not executed at all**: the recorded user-space-visible effects are copied from the trace, and the kernel is never consulted. Asynchronous events (signals, preemptions, context switches) are delivered at precisely the recorded points, identified by hardware performance-counter values (see §4.2). ```python
# Conceptual model of rr's record/replay loop (simplified)
def record_execution(tracee):
    trace = EventLog()
    while not tracee.exited():
        event = intercept_next_boundary_event(tracee)  # syscall or async event
        if event.is_syscall():
            result = allow_syscall_natively(tracee, event)  # or via buffer
            trace.log(event.id, result.user_visible_effects)
        else:  # signal, preemption, deschedule
            trace.log(event.id, perf_counter_value(RCB))
        resume(tracee)
    return trace

def replay_execution(trace, binary):
    state = initial_state(binary)
    for event in trace.events_in_order():
        if event.is_syscall():
            apply_recorded_effects(state, event)  # kernel NOT consulted
        else:
            run_until_rcb_equals(state, event.rcb_value)  # deterministic timing
            deliver_async_event(state, event)
    return state
```

### 4.2 Single-core scheduling and perf-counter scheduling points

The hardest problem in software RRR is **multithreading**. If threads run truly in parallel on multiple cores, every shared-memory access is a potential race, and recording the exact interleaving would require instrumenting every load and store — destroying performance. rr's solution is radical: **pin all threads of the recorded process to a single CPU core** [1][2].

On a single core, threads interleave only at well-defined *scheduling points*: system calls (which may block), explicit yields, and timer/preemption interrupts. rr converts the preemption problem into a recorded event by programming the CPU's **performance monitoring unit (PMU)** to interrupt after a fixed number of *retired conditional branches*. the RCB value serves as a **logical clock**: an asynchronous event recorded at RCB value $r$ can be redelivered during replay by running until the counter again reaches $r$. This is the mechanism that makes replay of preemptions and signals deterministic — a use of performance counters far outside their intended profiling purpose.

> Theorem: *Single-core determinism.* If all threads execute on one core, context switches occur only at syscalls or PMU interrupts, and the PMU counter is a deterministic function of the executed instruction stream, then the sequence of scheduling points is a deterministic function of the recorded syscall/async-event log. Hence replay reproduces the original interleaving.

The cost of this design is that **data races are not recorded**. If two threads race on unsynchronized shared memory, the outcome depends on the exact interleaving, which rr does not log. rr's position is pragmatic: *correct* programs (race-free under the language memory model) replay deterministically; racy programs may diverge. This is a documented, principled limitation, not an oversight [1]. Pinning to one core also means rr cannot record workloads whose performance depends on true parallelism — but O'Callahan et al. show that for the interactive and test workloads that dominate debugging practice, the restriction is acceptable and recording slowdown stays below 2× [1].

### 4.3 Intel Processor Trace: tracing in silicon

Intel Processor Trace (PT) attacks the problem from the hardware side [4][5]. Starting with Broadwell, Intel CPUs include dedicated silicon that emits **highly compressed packets** describing control flow: TNT (taken/not-taken) packets for conditional branches, TIP (target IP) packets for indirect branches, plus timing (TSC, MTC) and event packets. The decoder reconstructs the *exact instruction trace* by walking the program binary alongside the packets — branches whose direction is inferable from the binary need no packets at all, which is why PT bandwidth is typically measured in hundreds of megabytes per second rather than gigabytes [4].

PT's overhead is famously low — often **under 5%** — because tracing is performed by dedicated hardware, not by instrumented code [4]. Linux `perf` integrates PT via `perf record -e intel_pt//` and `perf report --itrace` / `perf script`, supporting call-graph reconstruction, branch-stack sampling, and timeline analysis [7]. The man page documents the decoding knobs (`--itrace=i1i` for per-instruction samples, `g`/`l` for call chains and last-branch entries) that turn the raw packet stream into analyst-friendly views [7].

PT is, however, **tracing, not replay**. It records *what happened* (the control-flow path) but not the *data values* (register contents, memory state) needed to re-execute. It therefore enables *forward* time-travel analysis — "show me every call to `foo` and the branch history leading to the crash" — but cannot implement rr-style reverse execution. The two technologies are complementary: rr gives full-state replay with higher overhead and a single-core restriction; PT gives whole-system, multi-core, near-zero-overhead *observation* without state reconstruction.

| Dimension | rr (software) | Intel PT (hardware) |
|---|---|---|
| Recording boundary | User–kernel interface | CPU instruction stream |
| Overhead | < 2× typical | < 5% typical |
| Multithreading | Single core only | Full multi-core |
| Reverse execution | Yes (deterministic replay) | No (forward analysis only) |
| Data values | Reconstructed by re-execution | Not captured |
| Deployment | Stock Linux kernel, x86-64 | Intel CPU with PT support |

### 4.4 Distributed record/replay and deterministic network virtualization

Extending RRR to **distributed systems** multiplies the difficulty: nondeterminism now includes message arrival order, network delays, clock skew, and partial failure. Two philosophies exist. The *recording* philosophy logs all network I/O at each node's user–kernel boundary — feasible, but trace volume is large and the per-node logs must be aligned on a consistent global cut, which requires logical clocks and careful handling of in-flight messages.

The *deterministic execution* philosophy, exemplified by **dOS** (Bergan et al., OSDI 2010) [6], eliminates internal nondeterminism as an OS service instead of recording it. dOS introduces the *Deterministic Process Group* (DPG): all communication between threads and processes inside a DPG — including implicit communication via shared memory, pipes, signals, and the filesystem — happens deterministically. External nondeterminism (user input, network packet arrivals) cannot be eliminated, so dOS makes it **explicit and controllable**: it arrives through narrow, virtualized interfaces where it can be logged, scripted, or injected.

The consequence for debugging is profound: inside a DPG, *every run is a replay*. Testing, fault injection, and replication all become deterministic operations. Where rr says "record the nondeterminism cheaply," dOS says "remove the nondeterminism structurally and virtualize what remains." The trade-off is intrusiveness: dOS requires a modified kernel and a deterministic communication substrate, whereas rr runs on stock kernels [1][6].

### 4.5 Replay-based chaos and fault-injection testing

Once replay is deterministic, it becomes a laboratory for **adversarial testing**. Replay-based fault injection works in two phases: (i) record a golden execution; (ii) replay it while *perturbing* selected nondeterministic inputs — delay a network packet, inject an `EIO` from a `read`, deliver a signal early, simulate a node crash at a recorded scheduling point. Because the baseline is deterministic, any divergence is attributable to the injected fault, giving **reproducible chaos experiments**.

This closes the loop with modern chaos engineering: tools that randomly kill processes in production gain a deterministic counterpart in which the *same* fault schedule can be replayed under the debugger, with reverse execution available to trace the failure's propagation. The dOS lineage makes this explicit — controllable external nondeterminism *is* a fault-injection API [6] — and rr's event log is naturally editable for the same purpose [1]. Formally:

> Theorem: *Fault-injection soundness.* Let $T$ be a recorded trace and $\Phi$ a fault schedule defined as a set of perturbations to events in $T$. Replaying $T$ under $\Phi$ yields a deterministic execution $E(T, \Phi)$; repeated replays of $(T, \Phi)$ are identical, so injected faults are reproducible by construction.

The practical limitation is *coverage*: the fault schedule explores perturbations of one recorded path, not the space of all paths. Combining replay-based injection with schedule fuzzing — systematically varying the recorded interleaving within race-freedom constraints — is an active research direction.

```rust
// Sketch: deterministic fault-injection harness over a recorded trace
struct FaultSchedule {
    perturbations: Vec<(EventId, Fault)>,
}

enum Fault { Delay(u64), IoError(i32), Crash, Signal(i32) }

fn replay_with_faults(trace: &Trace, sched: &FaultSchedule) -> Outcome {
    let mut state = initial_state();
    for event in trace.events() {
        if let Some(fault) = sched.lookup(event.id) {
            apply_fault(&mut state, fault);   // perturbed replay
        } else {
            apply_recorded(&mut state, event); // faithful replay
        }
    }
    state.outcome()
}
```

## 5. Empirical Results and Formal Analysis

### 5.1 Overhead model

We model recording overhead as the sum of four terms:

$$O_{total} = O_{trap} + O_{buffer} + O_{sched} + O_{log}$$

where $O_{trap}$ is ptrace/seccomp interception cost per syscall, $O_{buffer}$ the (amortized, small) cost of the syscall-buffering machinery, $O_{sched}$ the cost of PMU-interrupt-driven context switches on the single core, and $O_{log}$ the trace I/O bandwidth cost. Published rr measurements on realistic workloads (Firefox test suites, Samba, LibreOffice) show total recording slowdown **below 2×**, with replay typically *faster* than recording because syscalls are elided [1]. Intel PT tracing overhead is dominated by memory bandwidth for the packet stream and is typically **below 5%** [4].

| Workload class | rr record | rr replay | Intel PT trace |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interactive / test suites | 1.2–1.9× | 0.5–1.0× of record | ~1.01–1.05× |
| Syscall-heavy (compile) | up to ~2× | faster than record | ~1.02× |
| Parallel numeric (multi-core) | unsupported (single core) | n/a | ~1.05× |

### 5.2 Fidelity guarantees

We summarize the formal guarantees as a hierarchy:

1. **rr**: bit-exact reproduction of user-space-visible behavior for race-free programs; divergence possible under data races [1].
2. **Intel PT**: exact control-flow reconstruction; no data-state fidelity by itself [4][7].
3. **dOS/DPGs**: determinism *by construction* for internal communication; external inputs explicit and replayable [6].
4. **Replay + fault injection**: reproducibility of perturbed executions; coverage limited to perturbations of recorded paths (§4.5).

### 5.3 The deployability criterion

O'Callahan et al. argue that a debugging technology succeeds only if its *adoption cost* is near zero: no kernel patches, no recompilation, no perceptible slowdown during recording [1]. rr satisfies this on stock Linux/x86-64; Intel PT satisfies it wherever the silicon exists. dOS's kernel modifications explain its research-prototype status despite stronger guarantees [6]. The ACM Queue retrospective [3] adds the human factor: rr succeeded at Mozilla because it integrated with *existing* workflows (gdb, test harnesses) rather than demanding new ones — a lesson for every future RRR system.

## 6. Limitations

**rr's single-core restriction** is the most consequential: programs that need true parallelism cannot be recorded, and the restriction is structural, not incidental [1]. **Data races** are silently unrecorded; a racy program may replay differently, and rr cannot warn reliably. **Nondeterministic instructions** (`RDTSC`, `RDRAND`) must be trapped and emulated — rr handles `RDTSC` via prctl and emulates `RDRAND`, but each new nondeterministic ISA extension is a porting burden. **Intel PT** captures control flow but not data, so it cannot drive reverse execution alone, and decoding multi-gigabyte traces is computationally expensive [4][7]. **Distributed RRR** faces the *consistent global snapshot* problem: per-node logs must be aligned on a consistent cut, and clock skew makes "same time" ill-defined without logical clocks. **dOS-style determinism** requires kernel and sometimes application cooperation, limiting deployability [6]. Finally, **trace size** remains a practical bound: long-running server recordings can produce terabytes, requiring streaming, compression, and retention policies that current tools handle only crudely.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Deterministic record/replay has matured from a research curiosity into deployed infrastructure. rr demonstrated that *software-only* RRR on stock kernels is practical by exploiting the user–kernel boundary, syscall buffering, single-core scheduling, and PMU-based logical clocks [1][2]. Intel PT demonstrated that *hardware* can capture control flow at near-zero cost, enabling whole-system, multi-core observation at the price of data fidelity [4][5]. dOS demonstrated that nondeterminism can be *eliminated* rather than recorded, at the price of deployability [6]. Replay-based fault injection turns determinism into a testing superpower: reproducible chaos.

The open problems are now compositional: How do we combine rr-style replay with PT-style multi-core tracing to get reverse execution *without* the single-core restriction? How do we scale deterministic replay to datacenter-scale distributed systems without dOS's kernel invasiveness? And how do we make traces small enough, and tooling familiar enough, that time-travel debugging becomes the default rather than the exception? The trajectory of the field — from heavyweight VM recorders to rr's deployability to silicon tracing — suggests the answer will come from co-designing hardware, OS, and debugger around a single principle: *nondeterminism should be explicit, cheap to capture, and reproducible by construction.*
