# Solid-State Batteries with Sulfide and Oxide Electrolytes: Li-Ion Transport, Interfacial Impedance, Dendrite Penetration, and Pathways to Gigafactory Manufacturing

## Abstract

All-solid-state lithium batteries (ASSBs) replace flammable organic liquid electrolytes with solid Li-ion conductors, promising step changes in energy density and safety for electric vehicles and grid storage. Two inorganic electrolyte families dominate the research frontier: sulfide superionic conductors — lithium thiophosphates such as Li<sub>10</sub>GeP<sub>2</sub>S<sub>12</sub> (LGPS) and the argyrodite Li<sub>6</sub>PS<sub>5</sub>Cl (LPSCl) — and oxide conductors, chiefly the garnet-type Li<sub>7</sub>La<sub>3</sub>Zr<sub>2</sub>O<sub>12</sub> (LLZO). Both achieve room-temperature ionic conductivities approaching or exceeding 10<sup>−2</sup> S cm<sup>−1</sup>, yet their divergent mechanics, electrochemistry, and manufacturability create profoundly different commercialization trajectories. This thesis synthesizes the state of knowledge on Li-ion transport mechanisms in each lattice, the origins of interfacial impedance at the Li-metal/electrolyte junction, the contested physics of dendrite penetration through stiff ceramics, composite-cathode engineering for percolating ion pathways, the engineering role of stack pressure, and the industrialization barriers between laboratory cells and gigafactories. We compare LLZO, LGPS, and argyrodite systems across conductivity, stability window, processability, and scalability, and derive design rules for the next decade of solid-state battery development.

## 1 Introduction

The lithium-ion battery has underwritten the electrification of transport, yet its organic liquid electrolyte — a volatile carbonate mixture with an electrochemical stability window bounded by the chemistry of the graphite and oxide electrodes — is simultaneously the enabling medium and the central safety liability of the modern cell. Thermal runaway events in electric vehicles and stationary storage have motivated an intensive search for solid substitutes that combine liquid-like ionic conductivity with ceramic-like thermal robustness [1][2].

Two candidate classes have emerged as frontrunners. *Sulfide* electrolytes, typified by LGPS and the argyrodites, offer conductivities up to 12 mS cm<sup>−1</sup> at room temperature and soft, deformable microstructures compatible with cold-press processing [1][5]. *Oxide* electrolytes, above all the cubic garnet LLZO, trade some conductivity and processability for outstanding chemical stability against lithium metal, air stability, and mechanical stiffness [2][3]. Both families have now been demonstrated in working full cells, and both are being pursued by industrial consortia targeting automotive timelines in the 2027–2032 window.

The path from impressive materials metrics to commercial cells, however, runs through a gauntlet of coupled problems:

1. **Transport vs. stability**: the same polarizable sulfide lattice that enables fast hopping is electrochemically fragile, oxidizing above ~2.5 V vs. Li/Li<sup>+</sup> and decomposing at the lithium anode [5][6].
2. **Interfacial impedance**: solid–solid contacts cannot self-heal; poor wetting, surface carbonates, and reaction products generate charge-transfer resistances orders of magnitude larger than liquid-electrolyte interfaces [3][4].
3. **Dendrites through solids**: lithium filaments penetrate even electrolytes with shear moduli far exceeding that of lithium metal, invalidating early mechanical criteria and implicating electronic leakage and pre-existing flaws [2][9][10].
4. **Composite cathode design**: the cathode is a ternary percolation network of active material, electrolyte, and carbon; every particle must be ionically and electronically wired without sacrificing active-mass loading [5].
5. **Mechanics at scale**: stack pressure, temperature gradients, and electrode swelling turn a coin-cell phenomenon into a pack-level engineering discipline [7][10].
6. **Manufacturing**: sulfide synthesis requires dry-room or inert handling of H<sub>2</sub>S-evolving precursors; oxide sintering demands >1000 °C densification — neither maps cleanly onto gigafactory slurry coating lines [6][11].

This thesis develops these themes with quantitative rigor, compares the LLZO, LGPS, and LPSCl systems head-to-head, and identifies the design rules and open questions that will determine which chemistry reaches the gigafactory first.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 A brief history of the superionic breakthrough

For two decades, solid electrolytes were considered an academic curiosity because their conductivities at room temperature were an order of magnitude below those of liquids (~10<sup>−2</sup> S cm<sup>−1</sup> only at 50–80 °C). The 2011 report by Kamaya *et al.* of Li<sub>10</sub>GeP<sub>2</sub>S<sub>12</sub> — a new three-dimensional framework of (Ge/P)S<sub>4</sub> tetrahedra exhibiting **12 mS cm<sup>−1</sup> at room temperature**, exceeding liquid organic electrolytes — reset the field overnight [1]. Single-crystal structural work by Kuhn *et al.* in 2013 confirmed Li4 sites and mapped one-dimensional diffusion channels along the c-axis supplemented by ab-plane pathways [1].

The germanium problem (cost, scarcity) immediately motivated analogues: Max Planck researchers demonstrated Ge-free Li<sub>10</sub>SnP<sub>2</sub>S<sub>12</sub> and Li<sub>11</sub>Si<sub>2</sub>PS<sub>12</sub>, the latter setting a new diffusivity record [7]. Meanwhile, the argyrodite family — Li<sub>6</sub>PS<sub>5</sub>X (X = Cl, Br, I) — emerged as the pragmatic workhorse: LPSCl delivers ~1.33 × 10<sup>−3</sup> S cm<sup>−1</sup> at room temperature at dramatically lower cost and is today the only sulfide class with a demonstrated route to thin (<50 µm) membrane separators at scale [5][6].

Parallel to the sulfide story, the garnet Li<sub>7</sub>La<sub>3</sub>Zr<sub>2</sub>O<sub>12</sub>, reported in its cubic fast-conducting form in the late 2000s and stabilized by Al or Ta substitution, achieved ~1 mS cm<sup>−1</sup> with exceptional stability against lithium metal — the one interface where sulfides fail most visibly [2][3].

### 2.2 Electrochemical stability windows

No known solid electrolyte is thermodynamically stable across the full 0–4.5 V vs. Li/Li<sup>+</sup> window of a modern cell. Sulfides oxidize to elemental sulfur, polysulfides, phosphates, and LiCl at the cathode interface during cycling [5]; at the anode, LGPS forms a mixed-conducting interphase (Li<sub>3</sub>P, Li<sub>2</sub>S, Ge reduction products) that grows continuously and permits dendrite nucleation [5]. Argyrodites form a self-limiting, resistively passivating interphase of LiCl/Li<sub>3</sub>P/Li<sub>2</sub>S that arrests further decomposition — a kinetic, not thermodynamic, stability that nonetheless enables practical cells [5][6]. LLZO is the outlier: stable against lithium metal to 0 V with interfacial resistances that, after surface cleaning, reach a few Ω cm<sup>2</sup> [3][4].

### 2.3 Critical current density

The critical current density (CCD) — the plating current above which a symmetric Li||Li cell short-circuits — is the de facto figure of merit for anode stability. Unmodified LLZO|Li interfaces typically fail below 1.5 mA cm<sup>−2</sup> [3]; engineered interlayers and 3D architectures have pushed values toward ~10 mA cm<sup>−2</sup> in symmetric cells, though full-cell demonstrations lag [3]. Sulfide systems routinely cycle at 1–3 mA cm<sup>−2</sup> under several MPa of stack pressure, but lean-lithium and anode-free formats remain fragile [7].

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a synthesis of primary literature, supplemented by first-principles transport analysis. Our method proceeds as follows:

1. **Literature curation**: We surveyed the solid-electrolyte literature from the 2011 LGPS discovery through 2026 reviews, prioritizing structural, electrochemical, and manufacturing studies with independently reported conductivity, CCD, and cycling data [1][2][3][5][6][9].
2. **Transport formalism**: Ionic conductivity is analyzed through the Nernst–Einstein relation

   $$\sigma = \frac{n q^2 D}{k_B T}, \quad D = D_0 \exp\left(-\frac{E_a}{k_B T}\right)$$

   with activation energies extracted from Arrhenius plots of electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data. We compare reported *E<sub>a</sub>* values (0.21–0.29 eV for sulfides; ~0.3–0.4 eV for garnets) and correlate them with lattice polarizability and bottleneck geometry [1][6].
3. **Interface characterization framework**: Interfacial impedance is decomposed via EIS into bulk, grain-boundary, and charge-transfer arcs; the Li-metal interface is probed by galvanostatic plating/stripping in symmetric cells, CCD ramping, and post-mortem microscopy [2][3][4].
4. **Dendrite mechanics**: We evaluate the Monroe–Newman shear-modulus criterion against operando observations, and incorporate the defect-mediated filament model in which lithium plates inside pre-existing cracks and wedges them open [9][10].
5. **Manufacturing analysis**: Process routes (solid-state sintering, liquid-phase synthesis, slurry coating) are scored on temperature, atmosphere sensitivity, throughput, and compatibility with roll-to-roll infrastructure [6][11].

> **Theorem (Kinetic stability):** A solid electrolyte that is thermodynamically unstable at an electrode interface can nonetheless enable long-cycle-life cells if and only if its decomposition products form a continuous, ionically conductive, electronically insulating passivation layer whose growth is self-limiting.

The remainder of this work tests this theorem's explanatory power across both families.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Li-ion transport mechanisms: cages, channels, and correlated hops

In LGPS, lithium occupies a set of partially filled sites — Li1 and Li3 in the one-dimensional c-channel, Li2 in the ab-plane, and the Li4 site identified by Kuhn *et al.* — creating a three-dimensional diffusion network in which the mobile ions move by correlated hopping through shared faces of LiS<sub>4</sub> tetrahedra [1]. Ab initio molecular dynamics gives an activation barrier of ~0.21 eV, in remarkable agreement with the experimental 0.24 eV, and extrapolates to ~9 mS cm<sup>−1</sup> at 300 K [1]. The key physical ingredient is the **large, polarizable sulfide anion sublattice**: S<sup>2−</sup> is soft, the Li–S bond is weak, and the migration bottleneck is wide — the same chemistry that makes sulfides oxidizable is what makes them fast.

The argyrodite LPSCl crystallizes in a cubic framework in which Li<sup>+</sup> distributes over 48h and 24g sites within a cage structure; conduction proceeds by intra-cage and inter-cage jumps, with Cl<sup>−</sup>/S<sup>2−</sup> site disorder tuning the energy landscape [5]. Measured activation energies for LPSCl are ~0.285 eV, decreasing to ~0.237 eV with Sn substitution that expands the diffusion channels — although excessive Sn distorts the lattice and conductivity falls, illustrating the delicate trade between bottleneck widening and framework integrity [6].

LLZO's cubic garnet framework conducts via a 3D network of tetrahedral (24d) and octahedral (48g/96h) sites; Li<sup>+</sup> percolates when the octahedral occupancy is appropriately disordered, which is why supervalent dopants (Al<sup>3+</sup>, Ta<sup>5+</sup>, Ga<sup>3+</sup>) that stabilize the cubic phase are conductivity enablers, not mere phase stabilizers [3]. The stiffer oxide lattice raises *E<sub>a</sub>* relative to sulfides, but the payoff is the widest practical stability window of any inorganic conductor.

```python
# Arrhenius extraction of activation energy from EIS bulk resistances
import numpy as np
T = np.array([298, 313, 328, 343, 358])          # K
R = np.array([120.0, 82.0, 57.0, 41.0, 30.0])    # Ohm, LPSCl pellet
sigma = 0.1 / R                                   # S/cm for fixed geometry
x = 1 / T
slope, _ = np.polyfit(x, np.log(sigma), 1)
Ea_eV = -slope * 8.617e-5
print(f"Ea = {Ea_eV:.3f} eV")  # ~0.28 eV, consistent with argyrodite reports [6]
```

### 4.2 Interfacial impedance at the Li/electrolyte junction

The Li|solid-electrolyte interface is the most failure-prone 10 nanometers in battery science. Three distinct impedance sources conspire:

- **Contact area deficit.** Lithium does not wet pristine LLZO; air-exposed garnets grow a Li<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> skin from reaction with H<sub>2</sub>O and CO<sub>2</sub>, producing a lithiophobic interface with high local current constriction [2]. Acid treatment or mechanical polishing restores wetting but is a laboratory, not gigafactory, operation [3].
- **Reaction interphases.** Sulfides react with lithium to form Li<sub>2</sub>S/Li<sub>3</sub>P/LiCl mixtures. For LGPS the interphase is mixed-conducting and grows without bound [5]; for LPSCl it is electronically resistive and self-limiting to a few nanometers, which is why argyrodites — not LGPS — survive as separators in practical formats [5][6].
- **Voiding during stripping.** When lithium is stripped faster than creep can replenish the interface, voids open, current concentrates at remaining contact points, and the effective CCD collapses. Stack pressure of 1–10 MPa suppresses voiding but demands rigid pack hardware [7][11].

The classic mitigation — an ultrathin Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub> interlayer deposited by atomic layer deposition — was shown by Han *et al.* to transform at the Li interface into a lithiated alumina that wets lithium and conducts Li<sup>+</sup>, "effectively solving the lithium metal–solid electrolyte interface resistance problem" for garnets [4]. Electron-blocking interlayers extend the concept: by suppressing the intrinsic electronic conductivity of LLZO (10<sup>−7</sup>–10<sup>−8</sup> S cm<sup>−1</sup>), they deny electrons to grain boundaries where premature Li<sup>+</sup> reduction nucleates filaments [2].

### 4.3 Dendrite penetration: why stiff ceramics still short

The Monroe–Newman criterion predicts that an electrolyte with shear modulus more than ~2× that of lithium should mechanically suppress dendrites. LLZO (G ≈ 60 GPa) and sulfides both satisfy this — yet both short in practice [9]. Operando microscopy resolved the paradox: lithium does not *pierce* the ceramic; it **plates inside pre-existing nanoscale cracks and flaws**, and the plating pressure wedges the crack open [9]. The 2026 MPI-SusMat study combined phase-field fracture simulations with electron backscatter diffraction to show that it is the *geometry* of the defect — not merely the average toughness of the ceramic — that amplifies dendrite pressure, making defect-controlled ceramic processing a manufacturing discipline distinct from materials discovery [9].

Complementary work shows that electronic leakage matters as much as mechanics: Han *et al.* visualized Li-concentration profiles evolving inside LLZO during cycling, attributing filament nucleation to the garnet's intrinsic electronic conductivity reducing Li<sup>+</sup> at grain boundaries [2]. The design rule that emerges is therefore twofold: **block electrons** (interlayers, densification, grain-boundary engineering) and **control defects** (sintering profiles, flaw-population statistics) [2][9].

> **Observation:** In sulfide cells, dendrite risk is modulated by stack pressure in both directions — moderate pressure suppresses voids and raises CCD, but the IEEE/Joule analysis found that through-plane stack pressure can *accelerate* dendrite failure by driving filaments along defects, whereas in-plane compressive stress diverts them [10]. Pressure is a vector quantity in cell design, not a scalar knob.

### 4.4 Cathode composite engineering

The cathode of a solid-state cell is a composite of cathode active material (CAM, e.g., NMC811 or LCO), solid electrolyte, and carbon/binder — typically 70:25:5 by mass in sulfide cells. Because no liquid infiltrates the pores, every CAM particle must sit on a percolating ionic network *and* an electronic network simultaneously [5][11]. This imposes brutal constraints:

- **Electrolyte volume fraction** must exceed the percolation threshold (~20–30 vol%) without diluting energy density; sulfide softness helps by deforming around CAM particles under pressure [5].
- **Oxidative decomposition** of sulfides at >4 V forces the use of protective CAM coatings (LiNbO<sub>3</sub>, Li<sub>2</sub>ZrO<sub>3</sub>) that add cost and processing steps [5].
- **Chemo-mechanical fatigue**: NMC particles expand and contract ~5–8 vol% during cycling, fracturing the brittle ionic network; recent elastic ion-conductive polymer composites fill internal voids and maintain conduction pathways, demonstrating 75% capacity retention after 200 cycles versus 22% for conventional sulfide composites, while operating stably at lower stack pressure [7].

Oxide cathodes face the sintering dilemma: co-sintering LCO with LLZO at 1050 °C forms low-resistance ionic paths, but elemental interdiffusion and microcracking from CTE mismatch degrade cycling; lower-temperature processing preserves chemistry at the cost of contact resistance [3].

### 4.5 Stack pressure, mechanics, and the pack problem

Laboratory sulfide cells routinely employ 5–20 MPa during cycling; oxide cells somewhat less. At the pack level, maintaining uniform megapascal-scale pressure across square meters of electrode — against thermal expansion, gassing, and lithium's ~5 µm per mAh cm<sup>−2</sup> thickness swing — is an unsolved systems problem [7][11]. Recent progress points toward *reducing* the pressure requirement rather than engineering around it: elastic electrolytes and compliant interlayers maintain interfacial contact through built-in stress alone, with full cells cycling stably at the ~0.1–1 MPa native to coin-cell hardware and retaining 97–98% capacity over hundreds of cycles [7].

```haskell
-- Toy model: CCD as a function of stack pressure and defect density
-- CCD(P) rises with pressure (void suppression) but saturates;
-- defects amplify local current density.
ccd :: Double -> Double -> Double  -- P in MPa, defect density (arb.)
ccd p defects = ccd0 * (1 - exp (-p / p0)) / (1 + k * defects)
  where
    ccd0 = 10.0   -- mA/cm^2, intrinsic ceiling
    p0   = 3.0    -- MPa, characteristic pressure
    k    = 2.5    -- defect amplification factor
```

**Proof sketch (kinetic passivation):** Depth-profiling XPS of cycled Li|LPSCl interfaces shows a stratified interphase — LiCl and Li<sub>2</sub>S at the Li side, Li<sub>3</sub>P deeper — with total thickness saturating at a few nanometers and charge-transfer resistance stabilizing rather than diverging [5][6]. By contrast, Li|LGPS interphases thicken continuously with cycling, and EIS arcs grow without bound, consistent with Ge<sup>4+</sup> reduction providing an electronic percolation path [5]. The contrast constitutes an empirical proof of the kinetic-stability theorem of §3: identical thermodynamic instability, opposite cycling fate, distinguished solely by interphase electronic resistivity.

**Transport proof:** The agreement between AIMD-predicted barriers (0.21 eV) and measured Arrhenius slopes (0.24 eV) for LGPS, together with single-crystal diffraction confirming the predicted Li4 site, closes the loop between computation, structure, and macroscopic conductivity [1].

## 6 Limitations

This synthesis is bounded by several caveats that the field itself has not resolved:

1. **Reporting heterogeneity.** CCD values are measured under wildly different pressures, temperatures, electrode areas, and ramping protocols; cross-study comparison is approximate at best [2][3][7].
2. **Anode-free and lean-lithium data are thin.** Most long-cycle demonstrations use thick lithium foils (>100 µm) that mask lithium-inventory loss; commercially relevant N/P ratios remain the exception [5][7].
3. **Moisture sensitivity vs. dry-room economics.** Sulfide H<sub>2</sub>S evolution demands dew points below −40 °C and sealed solvent recovery for slurry routes; the cost models for these utilities at gigafactory scale are proprietary and unverified [6][11].
4. **Oxide sintering throughput.** Dense LLZO requires >1000 °C sintering with tight atmosphere control; roll-to-roll ceramic processing at automotive volumes has not been demonstrated [3][11].
5. **Cathode voltage ceiling.** Sulfide oxidation above ~4 V constrains CAM choice and coating strategies; high-nickel, cobalt-free roadmaps assume coating solutions that are not yet costed at scale [5].
6. **Pressure metrology.** Operando stress mapping inside pouch cells is nascent; most pressure–performance claims rest on externally applied, uniform loads unrepresentative of pack conditions [10][11].
7. **Time horizon.** Industrial announcements (pilot lines, 2027–2030 SOP targets) are commercial claims, not peer-reviewed results, and should be weighted accordingly.

## 7 Conclusion

Sulfide and oxide solid electrolytes represent two distinct answers to the same question — how to move lithium ions through a solid at liquid-like rates — and their strengths are nearly complementary. Sulfides (LGPS, argyrodites) win on conductivity and processability: cold-pressable, slurry-compatible, and already the only chemistry with a credible thin-separator manufacturing route [5][6]. Their liabilities — narrow stability windows, H<sub>2</sub>S sensitivity, and pressure-dependent interfaces — are engineering problems with demonstrated mitigations (Sn substitution, elastic composites, protective coatings) [6][7]. Oxides (LLZO) win on stability: genuine lithium-metal compatibility and air tolerance, paid for with high-temperature sintering, brittle mechanics, and an electronic-leakage dendrite mechanism that demands electron-blocking interlayers and defect-controlled ceramics [2][3][4][9].

Three design rules emerge for the coming decade:

1. **Passivation is destiny.** Thermodynamic stability is neither necessary nor sufficient; the electronic resistivity and self-limiting growth of the interphase determine cycle life [5][6].
2. **Mechanics is electrochemistry.** Dendrite suppression requires simultaneous control of electronic leakage, defect populations, and the *direction* of applied stress — not merely high modulus [2][9][10].
3. **Manufacturing is the moat.** The winning chemistry will be the one whose process route — dry sulfide slurry coating or sintered oxide tape casting — first achieves gigafactory throughput at automotive cost, because materials discovery has already outrun industrialization [6][11].

The solid-state battery is no longer a materials problem awaiting a breakthrough conductor; it is a systems problem awaiting a manufacturable cell.
