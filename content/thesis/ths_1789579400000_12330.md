# Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types: Semilattice Joins, Strong Eventual Consistency, and the State–Operation–Delta Design Space

## Abstract

Replicating mutable state across an asynchronous, partition-prone network without coordination forces a fundamental trade-off between availability and consistency. Conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs) resolve it by restricting replica divergence to a mathematically disciplined envelope: under *strong eventual consistency* (SEC), any two replicas that have received the same set of updates converge to identical states, regardless of delivery order. This article develops the CRDT design space from first principles. We formalize the semilattice foundations of state-based (convergent) designs, the commutativity and causal-delivery requirements of operation-based (commutative) designs, and the delta-state synthesis that combines small messages with unreliable dissemination. We analyze the observed-remove set and causal dot stores as the canonical solution to add–remove anomalies, survey the specification and verification frameworks that made CRDT semantics precise, and sketch the convergence proofs that underpin the theory. We close with open problems: composition of non-monotone types, Byzantine-tolerant convergence, and the verification gap for production composite types.

## 1 Introduction

Availability under network partition is the defining tension of wide-area and edge replication. Optimistic replication lets every replica accept writes without remote coordination, but it leaves the hard problem of reconciling divergent states to application logic, historically handled by ad hoc conflict resolution that is error-prone and difficult to audit [5]. Conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs) attack this problem at the datatype level: by constraining the algebra of updates, replicas converge deterministically to identical states once they have observed the same updates, with no rollback and no consensus [1].

The central guarantee is *strong eventual consistency* (SEC): in addition to the usual eventual-delivery requirement of eventual consistency, SEC demands *strong convergence* — replicas that have delivered the same set of updates hold the same state, irrespective of delivery order. A datatype satisfying SEC's sufficient conditions is conflict-free. The contribution of this article is a unified, proof-oriented treatment of the three families that realize SEC — state-based, operation-based, and delta-state designs — grounded in the semilattice theory that makes them work, the causal-order machinery they depend on, and the verification frameworks that finally gave them precise semantics [3].

## 2 Background

The lineage of CRDTs runs through optimistic replication [5], where replicas apply updates locally and reconcile later, and through the formalization of causality in distributed execution [8]. Lamport's happens-before relation supplies the ordering on which causal delivery is built: an operation causally precedes another if knowledge of the first could have reached the replica issuing the second, and delivery layers must respect this order for several CRDT designs to remain correct.

SEC refines eventual consistency. Eventual consistency promises only that, absent new updates, replicas eventually agree on *some* state; it says nothing about *which* state, and different conflict policies may produce different results. SEC strengthens the liveness claim with a safety claim: replicas that have received the same updates converge to the *same* state, because the state is a pure function of the update set [1]. This distinction matters operationally: it turns convergence from an emergent property of a deployment into a theorem about a datatype.

The two classical families are CvRDTs (convergent, state-based) and CmRDTs (commutative, operation-based). In the state-based approach, replicas gossip full states and merge them with an idempotent, commutative, associative *join*; in the operation-based approach, replicas disseminate individual operations, requiring commutativity of concurrent operations and a reliable causal broadcast. Both are instances of a deeper algebraic pattern — monotone evolution over a partial order — that Section 4 makes explicit [1], [2].

## 3 Methodology

This article follows an analytic-survey methodology with formal reconstruction. We re-derive the convergence arguments for each family from their algebraic preconditions, present executable specifications (in Haskell and TLA+) for the canonical examples, and organize the comparison around four axes: dissemination requirement, metadata cost, delivery assumption, and compositionality. Claims about verification and optimality follow the event-based specification framework of Burckhardt and colleagues [3]; claims about delta-state semantics follow the delta-CRDT framework [4]; design details for the observed-remove set follow the optimized dot-based construction [7]; and the pure operation-based reformulation follows the tagged-causal-broadcast treatment [6]. No new theorem is claimed; the novelty is in unifying the three families under one semilattice narrative with complete, checkable proof sketches.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 State-Based CRDTs: the Join Semilattice

A state-based CRDT models replica state as an element of a *join semilattice*: a partial order equipped with a least upper bound (join) for every pair of states. Updates are *inflations* — they only move the state upward in the order — and merging two states computes their join. Because the join is idempotent, commutative, and associative, merging the same state any number of times, in any order, yields the same least upper bound; this is precisely why state-based CRDTs tolerate duplication, reordering, and gossip over unreliable channels, and why anti-entropy can be as simple as periodic pairwise exchange [1].

The canonical example is the grow-only counter (G-Counter): the state is a vector with one entry per replica, a local increment advances only the local entry, and the merge is pointwise maximum. The counter's value is the sum of entries. Since entries never decrease and pointwise maximum is a join, all replicas that have exchanged states converge. The positive–negative counter (PN-Counter) composes two G-Counters, one for increments and one for decrements, illustrating datatype composition: CRDTs compose when their joins compose [1].

```haskell
-- G-Counter: a join semilattice of per-replica vectors
type ReplicaId = Int
type GCounter  = [(ReplicaId, Int)]

merge :: GCounter -> GCounter -> GCounter
merge xs ys = [(r, max (lookup' r xs) (lookup' r ys))
              | r <- nub (map fst xs ++ map fst ys)]
  where lookup' r v = maybe 0 id (lookup r v)

increment :: ReplicaId -> GCounter -> GCounter
increment r v = [(r', if r' == r then n + 1 else n) | (r', n) <- v]

query :: GCounter -> Int
query = sum . map snd
```

The price of this elegance is payload size: gossiping the full state on every round can be prohibitive when the state is large relative to the update rate, which motivates the delta-state refinement of Section 4.3 [4]. Moreover, not every useful datatype admits a natural monotone embedding; sets supporting both add and remove require the causal machinery of Section 4.4.

### 4.2 Operation-Based CRDTs: Commutativity under Causal Delivery

Operation-based CRDTs transmit *operations* rather than states. Correctness rests on two conditions: concurrent operations must commute — applying them in either order yields the same result — and delivery must be causal and exactly-once. Where state-based designs lean on the algebra of the state, operation-based designs lean on the algebra of the operations plus a stronger dissemination layer, typically a reliable causal broadcast [1].

The classic reformulation problem was that the original CmRDT specifications split each operation into a side-effect-free *prepare* phase (executed at the source, possibly generating metadata) and an *effect* phase (applied downstream). Baquero, Almeida, and Shoker showed how to make operation-based CRDTs genuinely operation-based by replacing this split with a *tagged reliable causal broadcast*: each operation carries its causal context as a tag, the datatype is designed so that operations commute by construction given the tag, and a notion of *causal stability* — the point at which an operation is known to have been delivered everywhere — permits garbage collection of the metadata that tags accumulate [6].

The operational trade-off is sharp. CmRDT messages are small (one operation), and state need not be shipped at all; but the dissemination layer must guarantee no loss and no duplication, a much stronger requirement than state-based gossip tolerates. In practice this pushes operation-based designs toward datacenter deployments with reliable messaging, while state-based designs dominate edge and peer-to-peer settings [2].

### 4.3 Delta-State CRDTs: Small Messages on Unreliable Channels

Delta-state CRDTs, introduced by Almeida, Shoker, and Baquero, achieve the best of both worlds: incrementally small messages like operation-based designs, disseminated over unreliable channels like state-based designs [4]. The trick is a *delta-mutator*: instead of mutating the state and returning nothing, the mutator returns a *delta* — a fragment of the state lattice, typically far smaller than the full state — that encodes exactly the effect of the mutation. The local state absorbs the delta by join, and the delta itself is what gets disseminated; remote replicas simply join received deltas into their own states.

Because join remains idempotent, commutative, and associative, delta dissemination inherits the robustness of state-based gossip: deltas may be duplicated, reordered, or lost, and convergence still follows as long as every delta produced is eventually joined at every replica. The subtlety is causal consistency. A delta alone does not record what it causally depends on, so the framework introduces *delta-intervals* — joins of contiguous sequences of deltas — and a *causal delta-merging condition* that a receiver checks before joining, guaranteeing that causally related deltas are merged in causal order. With this condition, a delta-CRDT provides causal consistency; without it, the basic anti-entropy algorithm still provides eventual convergence [4].

The engineering consequence is a tunable synchronization protocol: instead of shipping the entire state or relying on exactly-once delivery, a replica ships small deltas, batches them into intervals, and lets the lattice absorb network chaos. Evaluations in the delta-CRDT literature report payload reductions of orders of magnitude relative to full-state gossip for update-heavy workloads, since each round ships only what changed [4].

### 4.4 The Observed-Remove Set and Causal Dot Stores

Sets with both add and remove expose the fundamental anomaly of naive replication: a remove concurrent with an add cannot be resolved by either operation alone without losing one of them. The *observed-remove set* (OR-Set) resolves the anomaly with a principled policy — *add wins*: a remove cancels only the adds it has observed, so a concurrent add survives [1]. The mechanism is *causal dots*: each add generates a globally unique dot (a replica identifier paired with a monotonically increasing counter), the set state records which dots are present, and a remove records the set of dots it observed as a *causal context*. Merging two states unions the present dots and the contexts; an element is in the set if any of its dots is present and not covered by the context.

The optimized OR-Set refines this into *dot stores* with compact causal contexts, typically version-vector-like structures, so that metadata grows with the number of replicas rather than the number of operations [7]. This construction generalizes: dot stores underlie efficient maps, multi-value registers, and other composite types, and they demonstrate the broader lesson that CRDT metadata is fundamentally about tracking causality, not about the payload [6], [7].

```tla
---- MODULE ORSet ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Replicas, Elems
VARIABLES dots, ctx   \* dots: present (elem, dot) pairs; ctx: observed dots

Add(e, r, n) == dots' = dots \cup {<<e, <<r, n>>>>}
Remove(e)    == /\ ctx'  = ctx \cup {d \in dots : d[1] = e}
                /\ dots' = {d \in dots : d[1] /= e}
Merge(S, T)  == [dots |-> S.dots \cup T.dots,
                  ctx  |-> S.ctx \cup T.ctx]
====
```

The TLA+ sketch above captures the essence: adds introduce fresh dots, removes move observed dots into the context, and merge is a component-wise union — a join on the product lattice — which is why replicas converge.

### 4.5 Specification, Verification, and Optimality

For years CRDT correctness rested on informal arguments about commutativity and monotonicity. Burckhardt, Gotsman, Yang, and Zawirski supplied the missing foundation: a framework for specifying replicated data types as *relations over events* — visibility, arbitration, and returns-value relations — together with a proof technique, *replication-aware simulation*, for verifying that an implementation refines its specification [3]. This move is significant because it treats the specification as a property of the abstract execution (the set of operations and their causal and arbitration structure) rather than of any particular message pattern, which lets state-based, operation-based, and delta-state implementations be verified against the *same* specification.

The same work introduces *optimality* results: lower bounds on the metadata any implementation of a given datatype must maintain, and characterizations of when an implementation is optimal. For example, tracking causality for an OR-Set inherently requires metadata proportional to concurrency, and the dot-store construction meets that bound [3], [7]. This closes a loop that the early CRDT literature left open: not only do we know designs that converge, we know for several types that no design can do with less bookkeeping.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

The theoretical core of the field is a convergence theorem with a short proof. We state it for state-based designs.

> **Theorem (SEC for state-based CRDTs).** Let the state space be a join semilattice, let every update be an inflation, and let merge be the lattice join. Then any two replicas that have joined the same set of updates hold identical states.

> *Proof.* Each update moves the local state upward; each join computes the least upper bound of its arguments. The join of a fixed finite set of updates is the least upper bound of all of them, independent of the order or multiplicity of joins, by idempotence, commutativity, and associativity. Both replicas compute this same least upper bound. ∎

An analogous theorem holds for operation-based designs under reliable causal broadcast and commutativity of concurrent operations [1], and for delta-state designs under the causal delta-merging condition [4]. The table below summarizes the design space.

| Dimension | State-based (CvRDT) | Operation-based (CmRDT) | Delta-state (δ-CRDT) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disseminated unit | Full state | Single operation | Delta fragment |
| Delivery requirement | Unreliable, duplicates OK | Exactly-once, causal | Unreliable, duplicates OK |
| Metadata | State-sized | Causal context per op | Delta buffer plus intervals |
| Convergence mechanism | Lattice join | Commuting operations | Lattice join of deltas |
| Causal consistency | Via state merge | Via causal broadcast | Via delta-merging condition |
| Best-fit deployment | Edge, peer-to-peer | Datacenter messaging | Update-heavy, large state |

Empirically, the delta-state literature reports that shipping deltas instead of full states cuts synchronization payloads dramatically for workloads where updates are small relative to state size — the common case for counters, sets, and maps under continuous mutation — while retaining the at-least-once, out-of-order tolerance of gossip [4]. On the verification side, the replication-aware simulation technique has been applied to multiple existing implementations of registers, counters, sets, and maps, confirming that their advertised semantics match their specifications and exposing the exact metadata lower bounds [3].

## 6 Limitations

The theory's guarantees are real but bounded. First, SEC is a convergence property, not a consistency property in the linearizability sense: replicas may expose stale or causally inconsistent intermediate states, and applications that need read-your-writes or stronger invariants must layer additional protocols on top. Second, convergence assumes a benign failure model; Byzantine replicas can poison a lattice (a single replica can inflate a G-Counter entry arbitrarily, and joins cannot undo it), and Byzantine-tolerant CRDTs remain an open research area with only partial results. Third, metadata is the permanent tax: dot stores, causal contexts, and delta buffers grow with concurrency and replication degree, and while causal stability permits garbage collection [6], highly dynamic membership still stresses the bookkeeping. Fourth, composition is not free: monotone types compose cleanly, but composing a CRDT with a non-monotone invariant (for example, a uniqueness constraint) can silently violate SEC, and there is no general decision procedure for safe composition. Finally, the verification story, while rigorous [3], has been applied to a handful of core types; production CRDT libraries contain dozens of composite types whose specifications remain informal [2].

## 7 Conclusion

CRDTs convert the hardest problem of optimistic replication — reconciling divergent replicas — into an algebraic design discipline. State-based designs exploit the join semilattice; operation-based designs exploit commutativity under causal delivery, purified by the tagged-broadcast reformulation; delta-state designs recover small messages without surrendering unreliable dissemination. The observed-remove set shows how causal dots tame the add–remove anomaly, and the specification framework of Burckhardt and colleagues shows how to prove these designs correct and optimal rather than merely plausible. Open directions include Byzantine-resilient convergence, verified composition of non-monotone types, and closing the gap between the verified core and the sprawling composite libraries in production use. The field's trajectory is clear: from ad hoc reconciliation, to disciplined convergence, to verified optimality — with the lattice join doing the quiet heavy lifting throughout.
