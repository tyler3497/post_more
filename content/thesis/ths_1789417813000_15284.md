# Gradient Flows in Metric Spaces: The Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto Scheme, Evolution Variational Inequalities, and the Wasserstein Geometry of Entropy

## Abstract

We develop the theory of **gradient flows in metric spaces** and its flagship application: nonlinear diffusion equations as gradient flows of entropy-type functionals on the **Wasserstein space** of probability measures. We begin with the abstract metric theory of Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré [1]: the metric slope, curves of maximal slope, and the **Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI)** formulation that replaces differential equations with a comparison principle yielding contractivity and uniqueness for free. We then present the **Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto (JKO) minimizing-movement scheme**, the canonical time discretization that turns the heat equation into steepest descent of entropy and the Fokker–Planck equation into steepest descent of free energy [6][7]. The formal Riemannian geometry of the Wasserstein space — Otto calculus — is derived, including displacement convexity of entropy and its equivalence to the log-Sobolev and Poincaré inequalities. Finally we trace the theory into practice: neural JKO solvers [4][5], the composite-optimization view of Langevin sampling [2], the implicit bias of the JKO integrator [3], and mean-field dynamics [9].

## 1 Intro

The gradient flow is among the oldest and most robust ideas in analysis: follow the direction of steepest descent of an energy until equilibrium is reached. In a smooth Hilbert space this is elementary — differentiate the energy, solve an ordinary differential equation, and obtain an energy-dissipation identity essentially for free. The decisive generalization, due to De Giorgi and developed systematically by Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré [1], removes the linear structure entirely: a gradient flow can be defined in any **complete metric space** using only the distance function. The price of this abstraction is that the differential equation is replaced by a variational characterization, and the reward is enormous: the same formalism covers the heat equation, the Fokker–Planck equation, porous-medium diffusion, interacting particle systems, and — most consequentially for modern machine learning — the sampling algorithms that drive Bayesian computation and the mean-field dynamics that describe infinitely wide neural networks.

The pivotal discovery was made by Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto [6]: the **Fokker–Planck equation is the gradient flow of the relative entropy** (more precisely, the free energy) on the space of probability measures equipped with the 2-Wasserstein distance, and the **heat equation is the gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy** itself. Otto further supplied the formal Riemannian picture — the so-called **Otto calculus** — in which the tangent space at a measure is identified with gradient vector fields and the Wasserstein metric becomes an infinite-dimensional Riemannian metric. In this picture, computing the gradient of entropy yields precisely the velocity field that drives diffusion, and classical functional inequalities (log-Sobolev, Poincaré, Talagrand) acquire a geometric meaning as convexity and gradient-domination conditions on the Wasserstein manifold [2].

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 builds the metric-space foundations: absolutely continuous curves, the metric slope, curves of maximal slope, and the EVI formulation. Section 3 constructs the JKO minimizing-movement scheme and surveys its modern numerical realizations, including neural discretizations. Section 4 contains the deep dive: EVI contractivity, Otto calculus in full, entropy gradient flows for the classical diffusion PDEs, the second-order implicit bias of the JKO integrator, and the bridge to sampling and mean-field dynamics. Section 5 states the principal theorems and reports the empirical record of these methods. Section 6 confronts the limitations honestly, and Section 7 concludes with the outlook for the field.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Gradient flows without a linear structure

Let (X, d) be a complete metric space and φ: X → (−∞, +∞] a proper, lower semicontinuous functional. In the absence of gradients, De Giorgi's idea is to characterize descent variationally. The **metric slope** of φ at u is

|φ|(u) := lim sup_{v→u} (φ(u) − φ(v))⁺ / d(u, v).

An absolutely continuous curve u: (0, ∞) → X is a **curve of maximal slope** for φ if it satisfies the energy-dissipation inequality φ(u(t)) + (1/2)∫₀ᵗ |u̇|² + (1/2)∫₀ᵗ |φ|²(u) ≤ φ(u(0)) in the appropriate metric sense [1]. The decisive refinement is the **Evolution Variational Inequality**: for λ ∈ ℝ, a curve u satisfies EVI_λ if

(1/2)(d/dt) d²(u(t), v) + (λ/2) d²(u(t), v) + φ(u(t)) ≤ φ(v)

for every test point v ∈ X and almost every t. When φ is λ-geodesically convex, solutions of EVI_λ exist, are unique, and form a λ-contractive semigroup: d(u(t), ũ(t)) ≤ e^{−λt} d(u(0), ũ(0)) [1]. This single inequality replaces the entire PDE: it encodes the equation, stability, and long-time behavior simultaneously.

> Theorem: Let φ be λ-geodesically convex on a complete metric space (X, d). Then for every initial datum u₀ ∈ dom(φ) there exists at most one curve of maximal slope satisfying EVI_λ, and any two such solutions satisfy the exponential contractivity estimate above. In particular, the gradient flow is unique and asymptotically stable, with equilibrium characterized as the unique minimizer of φ when λ > 0.

### 2.2 The Wasserstein space and optimal transport

The model space is 𝒫₂(ℝᵈ), the set of Borel probability measures with finite second moment, metrized by the **2-Wasserstein distance**

W₂²(μ, ν) := inf_{π ∈ Π(μ,ν)} ∫ |x − y|² dπ(x, y),

the infimum over couplings. Absolutely continuous curves μₜ in 𝒫₂ are characterized by the **continuity equation** ∂ₜμₜ + ∇·(vₜμₜ) = 0 for a velocity field vₜ, and the metric speed is the minimal L²(μₜ)-norm of admissible velocities [1][7]. Otto's formal calculus [6] promotes this to a Riemannian structure: the tangent space at μ is identified with gradient vector fields ∇ψ, and the Wasserstein inner product is ⟨∇ψ₁, ∇ψ₂⟩_μ := ∫ ⟨∇ψ₁, ∇ψ₂⟩ dμ.

For a functional ℱ(μ) = ∫ f(μ) dx with density f, the **Wasserstein gradient** is

grad_μ ℱ = ∇[f′(μ)],

the ordinary gradient of the variational derivative. Three canonical examples, already implicit in the original discovery and made rigorous subsequently, are [6][7]:

| Functional ℱ(μ) | Gradient grad_μ ℱ | Flow ∂ₜμ = −∇·(μ grad_μ ℱ) |
|---|---|---|
| Boltzmann entropy Ent(μ) = ∫ μ log μ dx | ∇ log μ | **Heat equation** ∂ₜμ = Δμ |
| Rényi entropy ℛ_p(μ) = (1/(p−1)) ∫ μᵖ dx | (p/(p−1)) ∇μ^{p−1} | **Porous medium** ∂ₜμ = Δμᵖ |
| Free energy ℱ(μ) = Ent(μ) + ∫ V dμ + (1/2)∬ W(x−y) dμdμ | ∇(log μ + V + W ∗ μ) | **Fokker–Planck / granular media** |

The entropy is **displacement convex** along Wasserstein geodesics (a consequence of the underlying convexity of (x, μ) ↦ μ log μ), which yields the EVI₀ formulation for the heat flow and EVI_λ for Fokker–Planck under a λ-convex potential V [1][7]. Displacement convexity is equivalent, via the Otto–Villani theorem, to the **log-Sobolev inequality**: it is the gradient-domination condition (a Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality on the infinite-dimensional manifold) that forces exponential convergence of the continuous flow [2].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The JKO minimizing-movement scheme

The JKO scheme is the **backward Euler method on the Wasserstein manifold**. Given a time step τ > 0 and current iterate ρₖ, the next iterate solves the proximal problem

ρ_{k+1} := arg min_{ρ ∈ 𝒫₂} { ℱ(ρ) + (1/(2τ)) W₂²(ρ, ρₖ) }.

This is the metric-space analogue of the proximal-point algorithm; iterating it and sending τ → 0 with kτ → t produces a curve of maximal slope — the gradient flow — via De Giorgi's minimizing-movement construction [1][5][6]. The scheme enjoys **unconditional stability** for λ-geodesically convex ℱ and dissipates energy at every step: ℱ(ρ_{k+1}) + (1/(2τ))W₂²(ρ_{k+1}, ρₖ) ≤ ℱ(ρₖ). It is first-order accurate in τ, and its remarkable structural properties — energy dissipation, contractivity, preservation of convexity — are not shared by generic explicit integrators [3].

A minimal particle-flavored pseudocode for the JKO step applied to the free energy illustrates the structure:

```python
import torch

def jko_step(particles, tau, potential, n_inner=200):
    """One JKO step for F(mu) = E_{mu}[V] + Ent(mu) via particles."""
    x = particles.clone().detach().requires_grad_(True)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([x], lr=1e-3)
    x0 = particles.detach()
    for _ in range(n_inner):
        opt.zero_grad()
        entropy = 0.5 * x.pow(2).sum(dim=1).mean()  # proxy for Ent via kernel estimate
        loss = potential(x).mean() + entropy \
               + (1.0 / (2 * tau)) * ((x - x0) ** 2).sum(dim=1).mean()
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
    return x.detach()
```

The Wasserstein penalty (1/(2τ))W₂² is the source of both the power and the difficulty: evaluating it exactly requires solving an optimal transport problem at every step.

### 3.2 Numerical realizations: neural and particle discretizations

Classical finite-volume or Lagrangian discretizations of the JKO step suffer the curse of dimensionality. Three modern routes have emerged [4][5]:

1. **Input-convex neural networks (ICNNs).** Parametrize the Kantorovich potential of each JKO step by an ICNN, converting the transport problem into a stochastic optimization amenable to SGD; densities and samples are then directly available at every time step without mesh generation [4].
2. **Deep minimizing movements.** Represent the evolving density itself by a neural network and minimize the JKO functional over network parameters, achieving mesh-free, high-dimensional PDE approximation [5].
3. **Particle / forward-flow splittings.** For the composite free energy (potential plus entropy), split the JKO step into an explicit spatial gradient step on V (forward flow) and the exact heat flow (implemented by adding Gaussian noise) — this is precisely the unadjusted Langevin algorithm, as clarified by Wibisono's composite-optimization analysis [2].

The abstract machine underlying all of these is De Giorgi's minimizing movement. The following TLA+ sketch captures the iteration invariant that any correct implementation must preserve:

```tla+
---- MODULE JKOScheme ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
VARIABLES rho, tau, energy, step
Init == /\ rho \in ProbMeasures
        /\ tau \in Real /\ tau > 0
        /\ energy = F(rho)
        /\ step = 0
Next == \E rhoNext \in ProbMeasures :
          /\ rhoNext \in ArgMin({r \in ProbMeasures :
                F(r) + W2sq(r, rho) / (2 * tau)})
          /\ energy' = F(rhoNext) + W2sq(rhoNext, rho) / (2 * tau)
          /\ energy' <= energy          \* dissipation invariant
          /\ rho' = rhoNext
          /\ step' = step + 1
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<rho, tau, energy, step>>
THEOREM Dissipation == Spec => [](energy' <= energy)
====
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The EVI formulation: a comparison principle in place of a PDE

The Evolution Variational Inequality is the single most important conceptual export of the metric theory [1]. In a Hilbert space, the gradient flow u̇ = −∇φ(u) is equivalent, for convex φ, to the family of scalar inequalities (1/2)(d/dt)|u(t) − v|² + φ(u(t)) ≤ φ(v) for all v — the standard EVI₀. The metric generalization replaces the Hilbert norm by the distance d and adds the curvature correction (λ/2)d². Why does this matter? Because **existence and uniqueness follow from the inequality alone**: given two candidate flows, evaluate EVI for each against the other and add; the cross terms telescope into the contractivity estimate. No PDE regularity, no chain rule in infinite dimensions, no strong solutions are required.

For the heat flow on 𝒫₂, the entropy is displacement convex (λ = 0), so the heat flow satisfies EVI₀; for the Fokker–Planck flow with λ-convex potential V, the free energy is λ-displacement convex and the flow satisfies EVI_λ with exponential contractivity e^{−λt} [1][7]. This explains, in one stroke, the celebrated exponential convergence of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process and its generalizations.

### 4.2 Otto calculus: Riemannian geometry of the Wasserstein space

Otto calculus [6] formalizes the identification T_μ𝒫₂ ≅ {∇ψ : ψ smooth} with metric tensor ⟨∇ψ₁, ∇ψ₂⟩_μ = ∫⟨∇ψ₁,∇ψ₂⟩dμ. The continuity equation ∂ₜμ + ∇·(μ∇ψ) = 0 declares ∇ψ to be the velocity of the curve; the gradient of a functional ℱ is the tangent vector satisfying (d/dt)|₀ ℱ(μₜ) = ⟨grad_μℱ, ∇ψ⟩_μ for all curves. For internal energies ℱ(μ) = ∫f(μ)dx, differentiating under the continuity equation and integrating by parts yields grad_μℱ = ∇[f′(μ)] — the formula displayed in Section 2. The heat equation then falls out as ∂ₜμ = ∇·(μ∇log μ) = Δμ, exactly the gradient flow ∂ₜμ = −∇·(μ grad_μEnt) [6][7].

Higher-order Otto calculus pushes this further: higher Wasserstein differentials of internal energies follow the finite-dimensional Faà di Bruno chain rules, yielding exact expressions for higher time-derivatives of entropy along the heat flow — a program recently used to attack the Gaussian completely monotone conjecture [8]. The formalism is not merely aesthetic: it is the engine that converts geometric intuition (curvature of the Wasserstein manifold underlies displacement convexity) into analytic inequalities (log-Sobolev, Talagrand, HWI).

### 4.3 The classical diffusion equations as entropy gradient flows

The correspondence is now rigorous far beyond the formal level. The heat equation on Riemannian manifolds is the gradient flow of the relative entropy with respect to the volume measure in the Wasserstein space over the manifold [7]; the Fokker–Planck equation with potential V is the gradient flow of the free energy ℱ(μ) = Ent(μ|π) relative to the Gibbs measure π ∝ e^{−V}; the porous-medium equation is the gradient flow of the Rényi entropy; and the granular-media (McKean–Vlasov) equation is the gradient flow of the free energy with interaction kernel W [6]. Each of these flows satisfies the EVI with λ given by the convexity modulus of the driving functional, and each inherits the metric contractivity of the abstract theory.

The log-Sobolev inequality Ent(μ|π) ≤ (1/(2ρ)) I(μ|π), where I is the Fisher information, is precisely the **gradient-domination (Polyak–Łojasiewicz) inequality** for the relative entropy on the Wasserstein manifold: |grad_μEnt(⋅|π)|²_μ ≥ 2ρ Ent(μ|π) [2]. Under LSI the continuous-time Langevin dynamics converges exponentially in relative entropy — the infinite-dimensional analogue of gradient flow converging exponentially under gradient domination in ℝᵈ. This single observation is the bridge from PDE theory to sampling algorithms.

### 4.4 The implicit bias of the JKO integrator

First-order accuracy is not the whole story of the JKO scheme. Halmos and Hanin [3] characterize the **second-order implicit bias** of the JKO integrator: the discrete iterates ρₖ^τ are approximated to order τ² by the exact Wasserstein gradient flow of a *modified* energy

J^τ(ρ) = J(ρ) − (τ/4) ∫ ‖∇(δJ/δρ)(ρ)‖² ρ(dx),

that is, the original energy minus a quarter-step-weighted Fisher-information-type correction.

### 4.5 From gradient flows to sampling and mean-field dynamics

The modern payoff of the theory is algorithmic.

In machine learning, MFLD describes the training dynamics of infinitely wide neural networks: the parameter distribution evolves as the Wasserstein gradient flow of an entropy-regularized risk functional, with propagation of chaos justifying the finite-particle approximation [9].

## 5 Theoretical Analysis and Empirical Results

### 5.1 Principal theorems

> Theorem: Let ℱ be λ-geodesically convex and lower semicontinuous on 𝒫₂(ℝᵈ), bounded below. Then the JKO iterates ρ_{k+1} = arg min {ℱ(ρ) + (1/(2τ))W₂²(ρ, ρₖ)} are well defined, satisfy the dissipation inequality ℱ(ρ_{k+1}) + (1/(2τ))W₂²(ρ_{k+1},ρₖ) ≤ ℱ(ρₖ), and the piecewise-constant interpolants converge as τ → 0 to the unique EVI_λ gradient flow of ℱ. The limit curve satisfies (1/2)(d/dt)W₂²(μₜ,ν) + (λ/2)W₂²(μₜ,ν) + ℱ(μₜ) ≤ ℱ(ν) for all ν.

> Theorem: (Otto–Villani, metric form) If the target π satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality with constant ρ, then the relative entropy is ρ-gradient-dominated on (𝒫₂, W₂), and the Fokker–Planck / Langevin gradient flow satisfies Ent(μₜ|π) ≤ e^{−2ρt} Ent(μ₀|π). Conversely, gradient domination of the entropy implies LSI. The JKO discretization inherits exponential contractivity up to the modified-energy correction of [3].

### 5.2 Empirical and numerical evidence

The numerical record confirms the theory across regimes:

- **ICNN-based JKO** [4]: large-scale Wasserstein gradient flows computed without meshes or particles; Fokker–Planck diffusions, unnormalized density sampling, and nonlinear filtering all reproduced with accurate densities.
- **Deep minimizing movements** [5]: neural parametrizations of the JKO step approximate PDE solutions accurately even in high dimensions, finding the steepest-descent direction of the functional where grid methods fail.
- **Composite splitting analysis** [2]: for Gaussian targets, ULA's bias is computed exactly and shown to persist at arbitrarily small fixed step size, while the proposed symmetrized (forward–backward) splitting is consistent — a clean experimental separation of splitting bias from time-discretization error.
- **Data-driven gradient flows** [10]: primal-dual JKO implementations assimilate data into porous-medium and drift-diffusion-aggregation flows by changing only the driving functional, demonstrating the modularity of the variational formulation.
- **Tuning-free sampling** [9]: the unified gradient-flow discretization framework recovers ULA, MFLD, SVGD, and barycenter solvers as special cases, with convergence guarantees inherited from the EVI theory.

The following table summarizes the discretization landscape for the Langevin/entropy flow:

| Method | Splitting | Bias | Implementable step |
|---|---|---|---|
| ULA (forward flow) | explicit V + exact heat flow | biased, persists as τ→0 (fixed) | gradient step + Gaussian noise [2] |
| SLA (forward–backward) | forward V + backward heat flow | consistent for Gaussians | proximal step in space [2] |
| Exact JKO | fully implicit proximal | none (up to modified energy) | ICNN / deep MM [3][4][5] |
| Proximal sampler | alternating Gibbs on joint law | none | tractable for log-concave [9] |

## 6 Limitations

The theory is powerful but not universal, and the gaps matter.

1. **Convexity is load-bearing.** Existence, uniqueness, and contractivity via EVI require λ-geodesic convexity of the functional. For nonconvex potentials — multimodal targets, neural-network risks — the metric theory provides no global guarantee; the field relies on LSI-type conditions that are hard to verify and often fail, or on purely local analyses [2][9].
2. **The Wasserstein distance is computationally brutal.** Each JKO step is an optimal-transport problem. ICNN and particle approximations [4][5] scale better than grids but introduce their own approximation errors, and rigorous error analysis for neural JKO discretizations remains incomplete.
3. **Weak solutions and regularity.** Curves of maximal slope are weak objects; identifying them with classical PDE solutions requires substantial extra regularity theory, especially on manifolds or with degenerate diffusion (porous medium) [7].
4. **Splitting bias in sampling.** As Wibisono showed [2], the most widely used sampler (ULA) is *not* a gradient-descent discretization of the entropy flow but a forward-flow splitting with persistent bias. Practitioners who believe they are running "Wasserstein gradient descent" are running something subtly different.
5. **Mean-field limits are asymptotic.** Propagation of chaos justifies particle approximations only as N → ∞; finite-particle fluctuations in nonconvex landscapes can dominate and are poorly quantified [9].
6. **Curvature of the base space.** Displacement convexity of entropy depends on nonnegative Ricci curvature of the underlying manifold; on negatively curved spaces the clean picture breaks and the EVI formulation must be substantially reworked [7].

## 7 Conclusion

The Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto scheme did more than supply a numerical method: it revealed that diffusion *is* steepest descent, once the geometry is right. The metric theory of Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré [1] turned that revelation into a complete analytical framework — metric slope, curves of maximal slope, and the EVI comparison principle — that proves existence, uniqueness, contractivity, and exponential convergence from a single inequality. Otto calculus [6][7][8] made the geometry explicit and computable, and the last decade has converted the theory into algorithms: neural JKO solvers [4][5], a composite-optimization understanding of Langevin sampling [2], the second-order implicit bias of the JKO integrator [3], and a unified, tuning-free framework for sampling and mean-field dynamics [9].

Extending rigorous EVI-type guarantees beyond displacement convexity — to the genuinely nonconvex objectives of modern machine learning — remains the central open problem. So does closing the gap between the implicit JKO step and the biased-but-practical explicit splittings that practitioners actually run. If history is a guide, the next breakthrough will again come from taking the geometry seriously: the right metric makes the hard problem easy, and the Wasserstein space has not yet finished teaching us what steepest descent means.
