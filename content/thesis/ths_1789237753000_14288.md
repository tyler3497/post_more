# Algebraic Effects and Handlers: From Plotkin and Power's Theory to Koka, Eff, and Effect-Typed Languages

## Abstract

Algebraic effects and handlers constitute the most significant reformulation of effectful programming since Moggi's monadic semantics. This thesis traces the arc from Plotkin and Power's algebraic theory of computational effects — in which effects are presented by *operations and equations* whose free model induces the computational monad — to Plotkin and Pretnar's handlers, which interpret operations as *models* of the theory and handling as the *universal homomorphism* out of the free model. We develop the theory in full: algebraic operations and generic effects, the free monad as the canonical handler carrier, and the correspondence between handlers and multi-prompt delimited continuations. We survey the language-design response: Eff, Koka's row-polymorphic effect types with scoped labels, Frank's ambient abilities, and Multicore OCaml's retrofitted handlers. We analyze compilation strategies — Leijen's type-directed selective CPS translation and the evidence-passing scheme of Xie et al. — with their measured performance, examine effect inference, scoped effects, and the connection between effect capabilities and linear logic, and close with open problems in effect-based concurrency and multi-shot continuation safety.

## 1 Introduction

In 1991 Eugenio Moggi proposed that *notions of computation* be modelled uniformly by monads: computations returning values in *X* are elements of *TX* for a suitable monad *T* [14]. Yet monads answer *"what is the shape of an effectful computation?"* while leaving a deeper question untouched: *what primitive operations generate the effect, and what equations govern them?*

Plotkin and Power's decisive move, developed across [1] and [2], was to take **operations and equations as primitive**. State is not the state monad *S → (A × S)*; it is the operations `get` and `put` together with the familiar equations (`get` after `put` returns the stored value, and so on). The monad is then *derived*: it is the free-model monad of the equational theory. This shift of focus — from monads to algebraic theories — is what makes effects *modular*: Hyland, Plotkin, and Power showed that theories of combined effects are often built from theories of individual effects by simple operations such as disjoint sum and tensor [2].

Handlers, introduced by Plotkin and Pretnar [3][4], complete the picture by giving the operations a *programming construct*: a general handler gives meaning to *every* operation of an algebraic theory, with access to the delimited continuation of the handled computation. Handling is not an ad hoc control operator — it is the unique homomorphism induced by the universal property of the free model, unifying previously unrelated constructs (CCS relabelling and restriction, timeout, rollback, stream redirection) under one semantic umbrella [3].

This thesis is organized as follows. §2 recalls the monadic background and the modularity crisis that motivated the algebraic turn. §3 describes our method: a close reading of the primary papers, reconstruction of the key constructions, and a comparative analysis of implementations. §4 develops the theory in depth across five subsections: the algebraic presentation itself, handlers as models, the delimited-continuation and free-monad connection, effect rows and inference, and compilation. §5 presents formal arguments and empirical measurements from the literature. §6 states the limitations of the framework honestly. §7 concludes with open problems.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Moggi's monads and the transformer problem

Moggi's computational λ-calculus interprets a computation of type *A* as an element of *TA*, where *T* is a strong monad. The approach is uniform but not compositional: combining the state and exception monads requires a *monad transformer*, and transformer stacks commit the programmer to a fixed order of effects with observable semantic consequences.

> **Definition (Algebraic operation).** Let *T* be a strong monad on a category with finite products. An *n*-ary *algebraic operation* is a family of maps *op_X : (TX)^n → TX*, natural in *X*, such that for every Kleisli arrow *f : X → TY*,
>
> *op_X(t₁, …, tₙ) ⋆ f = op_Y(t₁ ⋆ f, …, tₙ ⋆ f)*,
>
> i.e., the operation commutes with sequential composition [1].

This *algebraicity law* is the crux: an operation is algebraic when it can be performed *before* the rest of the computation is known, with the continuation distributed over each argument. `raise`, `get`, `choose` are algebraic; `callcc` is not — the continuations monad famously admits no algebraic presentation [2], a fact that already hints at why handlers and continuations stand in a subtle, non-identical relation (§4.3).

### 2.2 Plotkin and Power's algebraic theory

In [1], Plotkin and Power define, for a strong monad *T*, the equivalent notions of **algebraic operation** and **generic effect**: to give an *n*-ary algebraic operation is equivalent to giving a *generic effect*, an element of *T(n)* (a computation over an *n*-element set of "continuation variables"). In [2] they go further: a *notion of computation* — a signature Σ of operation symbols with arities, plus equations *E* — *determines* a monad, namely the free-model monad *T_{Σ,E} = U ∘ F_{Σ,E}*, where *F_{Σ,E}(X)* is the free *(Σ,E)*-algebra on *X*. Global and local state, exceptions, nondeterminism, and I/O all arise this way. The slogan:

* **Effects are specified by operations and equations; the monad is a theorem, not an axiom.**

Our method is threefold. First, we reconstruct the core constructions of [1][2][3] — algebraic operations, generic effects, free models, and the handling construct — checking each claim against the published papers and Pretnar's tutorial exposition [5]. Second, we compare the principal implementations (Eff [5], Koka [6][7], Frank [11], Multicore OCaml [9], evidence-passing Haskell libraries [8]) along *typing discipline*, *handler expressiveness*, and *compilation strategy*, using their published semantics and benchmarks. Third, we map the boundary of the theory — what is *not* algebraic and where the open problems lie — drawing on scoped effects [12], effect inference [13], and delimited control [14]. Every factual claim about a published result is cited inline; the bibliography contains only verified works with arXiv identifiers or DOIs.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Algebraic Theories of Effects and the Free-Model Monad

An *effect signature* is a set of operation symbols, each with a parameter type *P* and an arity type *A* (in the finitary case, a natural number *n*). A computation is a *tree*: leaves are `return x` for *x ∈ X*, and internal nodes are operations applied to a parameter and a family of continuations, one per arity branch. Equations *E* quotient these trees. The free model *F_{Σ,E}(X)* is exactly this quotiented tree type, and it carries a canonical monad structure: `return` is the leaf constructor and bind grafts trees.

In Haskell, the *free monad* over a signature functor captures this idea directly [12]:

```haskell
data Free f a = Pure a | Free (f (Free f a))

foldFree :: Functor f => (a -> b) -> (f b -> b) -> Free f a -> b
foldFree gen alg (Pure x)  = gen x
foldFree gen alg (Free fx) = alg (fmap (foldFree gen alg) fx)
```

Here `gen` interprets values and `alg` interprets operation nodes — and `foldFree` is *already* a handler in embryo: a handler is a fold over the free structure, where each operation clause supplies the algebra and the `val` clause supplies the generator. The equations *E* become the handler's proof obligations: a handler is *correct* when its clauses satisfy the equations, in which case the fold factors through the quotient — the semantic content of Plotkin and Pretnar's treatment [3].

> **Theorem (informal; Plotkin–Pretnar [3]).** Let *(Σ, E)* be an effect theory and *M* a model of it with carrier *C*. For every map *g : X → C* there exists a *unique* homomorphism *ĝ : F_{Σ,E}(X) → C* extending *g*. The handling construct denotes exactly this homomorphism.

The uniqueness is what makes handlers *modular interpreters*: the programmer supplies the model (the clauses) and the return clause, and the universal property threads the delimited continuation — the homomorphism applied to each continuation subtree.

Standard theories illustrate the range. **Exceptions** are presented by nullary operations `raise_e` with no equations. **Global state** is presented by `get : 1 → S` (arity *S*) and `put : S → 1` (arity 1) with the four standard interaction equations. **Nondeterminism** is presented by binary `choose` with semilattice equations (associativity, commutativity, idempotence). **I/O** is presented by `read` and `write` with no equations. Each yields, via the free construction, exactly Moggi's monad for the effect — modulo the expected forgetful functor [2][3].

### 4.2 Handlers as Models: The Plotkin–Pretnar Handling Construct

The 2009 ESOP paper [4] and its 2013 journal version [3] introduce the *handler* as a programming abstraction. A handler for an effect theory has a **return clause** `val x ↦ M` interpreting pure values, and an **operation clause** `op(p; k) ↦ M` for each operation, where *p* binds the parameter and *k* binds the *delimited continuation* — the rest of the handled computation, itself wrapped in the handler.

The handling construct `with h handle c` evaluates *c*; when *c* performs `op(v)`, control transfers to the clause with *k* bound to *λy. with h handle E[y]*, where *E* is the captured evaluation context up to the handler. Crucially, the clause may invoke *k* zero, one, or many times — yielding exceptions (zero), state threading (one), and backtracking search (many) from the same mechanism.

The Eff language of Bauer and Pretnar [5] was the first designed around this construct, with effects declared as operations and handlers first-class:

```ocaml
(* Eff: a handler collecting nondeterministic choices *)
let choose_all = handler
  | val x -> [x]
  | #choose () k -> (k true) @ (k false)
  | finally x -> x
```

Here the continuation *k* is invoked *twice* — the clause is literally the semilattice algebra for `choose`. Other examples from [3] show the breadth: **rollback** (state composed with exception handling, discarding the state continuation on `raise`), **stream redirection**, **timeout**, and Milner's CCS **relabelling and restriction** — all instances of the same universal construction.

Handlers also *compose*: nested handlers correspond to composed homomorphisms, and nesting order determines effect interaction — e.g., state handled inside nondeterminism yields per-branch state, the reverse yields global state. This recovers algebraically what monad transformer stacks encode operationally [5][12].

### 4.3 Delimited Continuations and the Free Monad

There is a precise operational correspondence between handlers and *delimited continuations*. The handling rule `handle_h (E[perform op(v)]) ⟶ clause_op(v, λy. handle_h (E[y]))` is exactly the rule of a *multi-prompt* delimited-control operator, where each handler prompt delimits the captured continuation [7]. Leijen's operational semantics for Koka makes the correspondence explicit by defining handling over evaluation contexts `H^l` indexed by effect labels, directly parallel to `shift`/`reset` [7].

Kiselyov's `delimcc` library and the `shift`/`reset` tutorial literature [14] provide the other direction: any handler can be macro-expressed with multi-prompt delimited continuations, and any (algebraic) use of delimited continuations can be expressed with handlers. The relationship is not an identity, however, and the differences matter:

| Aspect | Delimited continuations (`shift`/`reset`) | Algebraic effect handlers |
|---|---|---|
| Typing of the captured context | Answer-type polymorphism or effect systems | The handler's own clauses; continuation type fixed by operation signature |
| Multi-shot use | Unrestricted (with `shift`) | Unrestricted in theory; often restricted to one-shot in practice (OCaml) |
| Reasoning principle | CPS reasoning, control stacks | Universal property of the free model |
| Non-algebraic effects | Expressible (e.g., `callcc`) | Not expressible: continuations are not algebraic [2] |

The last row is the fundamental boundary: because the continuations monad has no presentation by operations and equations, there is no effect theory whose handlers *are* first-class continuations. Handlers capture *delimited* continuations only insofar as the delimiter structure mirrors handler nesting — a fact with real consequences for effect-based concurrency (§6).

### 4.4 Effect Rows, Row Polymorphism, and Inference

A handler calculus is only half a language: the other half is the *type system* that tracks which effects a computation may perform. Koka, by Daan Leijen [6][7], pioneered **row-polymorphic effect types**. A function type carries an *effect row*:

```text
map : (a -> <e> b, list<a>) -> <e> list<b>
```

where `<e>` is a row variable: `map` works for *any* effects its argument may have. Rows are built from labels with *scoped* semantics — duplicate labels are meaningful, so `<exn, exn | μ>` differs from `<exn | μ>` — giving precise typing of effect *elimination*: handling `exn` removes exactly one occurrence. Koka's inference extends Hindley–Milner with row unification, proved sound and complete [7]. The `total` effect (empty row) marks pure functions, and divergence `div` is tracked explicitly.

Pretnar's "Inferring Algebraic Effects" [13] gives the complementary account for Eff-style languages: constraint-based inference collecting operation requirements and generalizing over them. The common lesson is that *effect polymorphism* — not effect monomorphism — is what makes handlers usable: without row variables, every higher-order combinator would need a closed effect annotation, and the annotation burden would defeat the abstraction.

Frank [11], by Lindley and McBride, takes a different route: handlers are not a separate construct but a generalization of function application, with operators handling the effects of their arguments via pattern matching on *requests*, using the call-by-push-value value/computation distinction and an "ambient ability" for effect polymorphism.

### 4.5 Compilation: Selective CPS and Evidence Passing

Naive handler implementations pay a tax: the operational rule searches outward at runtime for the innermost handler of the performed operation — a *linear* walk up the handler stack [8]. Two compilation strategies from the literature eliminate or amortize this cost.

**Type-directed selective CPS (Leijen [7]).** Koka's compiler uses the effect rows *at compile time*: functions whose rows are `total` compile to direct style, while effectful functions compile to continuation-passing style, with the translation *selective* — only the effectful parts pay for CPS. Because handlers delimit continuations statically, the translation can further optimize: *tail-resumptive* operations (where the clause invokes the continuation exactly once in tail position — the overwhelmingly common case: state, exceptions, I/O) compile to plain state-passing loops with no continuation allocation at all. Koka additionally compiles to C via a novel memory-management scheme, demonstrating that handlers need not imply GC-heavy runtime machinery.

**Evidence passing (Xie et al. [8]).** "Effect Handlers, Evidently" (ICFP 2020) replaces the handler search with *passing evidence*: each handler site builds a runtime record (the *evidence vector*) mapping operation labels to implementations, passed as an extra argument to effectful functions — a dictionary-passing translation for effects, analogous to type-class dictionaries. Performing an operation becomes a constant-time vector lookup. The paper proves the translation correct and reports performance competitive with hand-written monadic code [8].

**Retrofitting (Sivaramakrishnan et al. [9]).** Multicore OCaml took the hardest path: adding handlers to an existing industrial language *without* effect types and without regressing existing code. The implementation represents each handler scope as a *fiber* (a heap-allocated stack segment); performing an effect captures the fiber prefix as a continuation object, resumed by `continue`/`discontinue`. Continuations are *one-shot*, permitting an efficient representation without persistent stacks. The measured results: a mean **1% overhead** on a macro benchmark suite that does not use handlers, DWARF-unwind-table compatibility, and a concurrent web server on handlers performing on par with optimized monadic concurrency libraries [9][10].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

The literature offers both theorems and measurements; we summarize the load-bearing ones.

**Formal results.** (i) Plotkin and Power [1]: giving an algebraic operation is *equivalent* to giving a generic effect, and the monadic semantics of the computational λ-calculus extends uniformly with generic effects. (ii) Plotkin and Power [2]: notions of computation determine monads, covering global/local state, with adequacy results. (iii) Plotkin and Pretnar [3]: handlers denote models, handling denotes the unique homomorphism, conservatively extending exception handling. (iv) Leijen [7]: row-based effect inference for Koka is sound and complete; selective CPS is type-preserving. (v) Xie et al. [8]: the evidence-passing translation is semantics-preserving. (vi) Wu, Schrijvers, and Hinze [12]: plain algebraic handlers cannot express *scoped* operations without higher-order syntax — a precise negative result delimiting the framework.

**Empirical results.** (i) Multicore OCaml [9]: 1% mean overhead on non-handler code; handler-based concurrency matches monadic libraries. (ii) Koka [7]: selective CPS keeps pure code at direct-style speed; tail-resumptive handlers compile to loops. (iii) Evidently [8]: evidence passing brings handler dispatch to near-monadic performance. (iv) Dolan et al. [10]: a highly concurrent web server written in direct style with handlers shows no penalty versus callback-driven code.

Taken together, the formal results say handlers are *canonical* — forced by the universal property, not invented — and the empirical results say they are *affordable*.

## 6 Limitations

Honesty requires stating what the framework does *not* do.

1. **Non-algebraic effects are out of scope.** Continuations, and any effect whose monad is not ranked (not presented by operations and equations), cannot be given as an algebraic theory [2]. Handlers therefore cannot macro-express `callcc`; the delimited continuations they capture are always delimited by handler structure.

2. **Scoped operations need more machinery.** As Wu et al. show [12], operations whose semantics depends on a *scope* (exception handlers themselves, `once`, local state with dynamic extent) are not algebraic in the plain sense; they require higher-order syntax or explicit scope delimiters. The interaction of scoping with handler *ordering* remains delicate.

3. **One-shot vs. multi-shot.** Efficient implementations (notably OCaml [9]) restrict continuations to one-shot use. Multi-shot continuations — needed for backtracking search and probabilistic programming — require persistent or copied stacks, with attendant costs and linearity discipline; mainstream type systems do not track resumption multiplicity.

4. **Effect polymorphism vs. abstraction.** Row polymorphism [6][7] solves the annotation problem but leaks effect information into types: a module's inferred row exposes which effects its internals use. Masking and sealing mechanisms exist but are not yet uniform across languages.

5. **Reasoning about handler *composition*.** The order-dependence of nested handlers (state-inside-choice vs. choice-inside-state) is semantically principled but operationally subtle; there is no widely adopted logic for proving equivalences of handler stacks, though Kammar and Plotkin's work on effect-dependent optimizations is a start.

6. **Concurrency semantics.** Effect-based concurrency (as in OCaml's Eio or Koka's structured asynchrony) still lacks a canonical formal semantics: the interaction of handlers with parallelism, cancellation, and resource lifetimes is defined by implementation rather than theory.

---

## 7 Conclusion

The trajectory from Plotkin and Power to the present is a rare case of theory *preceding* and *shaping* practice without dilution. The algebraic insight — effects as operations and equations, monads as derived free constructions [1][2] — gave handlers their semantic anchor: a handler is a model, handling is the unique homomorphism [3][4]. Language designers then answered the engineering questions the theory leaves open — how to *type* effects (rows [6][7], ambient abilities [11], or nothing at all [9]), how to *infer* them [13], and how to *compile* them (selective CPS [7], evidence passing [8], fibers [9]) — each with measured success.

The open problems are now the interesting ones. Can we give a canonical semantics — and a logic — for effect-based concurrency matching the canonicity of the Plotkin–Pretnar construction? Can type systems track resumption multiplicity and scope, recovering multi-shot safety without giving up OCaml-grade performance? Can the capability reading of effects be made precise through linear logic? And can effect-dependent optimization be put on the same algebraic footing as the effects themselves? The algebraic theory of effects began by asking what operations *generate* a monad; its next chapter will ask what operations generate a *system*.
