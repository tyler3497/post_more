# Quantitative Type Theory and the Practice of Dependent Types: Idris 2, Erasure, and the Computational Content of Proofs

## Abstract

Quantitative Type Theory (QTT) annotates every variable in a typing judgement with a *quantity* drawn from a rig — in practice 0, 1, or ω — recording how that variable may be used at run time [1][2]. This article gives a self-contained account of QTT as the meeting point of dependent types, linearity, and erasure, and of its first full-scale realization in Idris 2 [3]. We explain how McBride's *dependent lollipop* `(x : S) ⊸ T[x]` lets linear data appear inside types without runtime cost, how Atkey's quantitative categories with families supply a resource-sensitive semantics, and how Idris 2 turns quantity 0 into a machine-checked erasure specification and quantity 1 into enforceable resource protocols such as session types. We contrast QTT with neighbouring graded systems — GraD's heap-based usage semantics [4], GrTT's graded modality [8], and Granule's graded modal types [5] — and with Agda's run-time irrelevance annotations [7]. A proof sketch establishes erasure soundness for a core fragment, quantitative derivations are worked in full, and the limits of the 0–1–ω discipline for realistic low-level programming are assessed.

## 1 Introduction

Dependent types promise that *what* a program computes and *why* it is correct can be expressed in a single language. They also threaten to drag the entire apparatus of proof — indices, invariants, equality witnesses — into the executable. Anyone who has watched a length-indexed vector carry its length at run time has felt the tension: the type checker needs the evidence; the machine does not.

The traditional answer is *extrinsic* erasure. Coq extracts `Prop` into an untyped husk; Agda's forcing and detagging analyses delete constructor arguments the compiler can prove redundant [7]; whole-program optimizers strip what they can prove dead. These are compiler heroics applied after the fact: the programmer states intent, and a battery of analyses *guesses* what may be erased. When the analysis fails, the program silently pays — Agda's manual documents a genuine space leak in `foldl` over vectors that persists until the programmer annotates the length argument erased [7].

Quantitative Type Theory, introduced by McBride [1] and given syntax and semantics by Atkey [2], makes erasure *intrinsic*. Every binder carries a quantity drawn from a rig — a "riNg without Negation" — recording the variable's permitted run-time usage: 0 means erased, 1 means used exactly once, ω means unrestricted. The type checker doubles as a usage accountant, and erasure becomes a property the type system *guarantees* rather than an optimization it *attempts*. Idris 2 is the first full-scale language built on this idea, with a QTT core in which implicitly bound names are quantity 0, unannotated binders are unrestricted, and quantity 1 unlocks linear resource protocols [3][6].

This article reconstructs the core theory, works through its metatheory and its Idris 2 practice, compares it against the graded-type landscape [4][5][8], and marks where the 0–1–ω discipline runs out of road.

## 2 Background

**The dependency–linearity conflict.** In Martin-Löf type theory a judgement `x₁ : S₁, …, xₙ : Sₙ ⊢ M : T` *mixes* two roles for variables: `n : Nat` may appear only in the type `Fin n` (logical use) while `x : Fin n` is consumed by the computation (computational use). In linear logic the same context means something far stricter: each variable must be used exactly once. Early attempts — Barber's dual contexts, Cervesato and Pfenning's modal formulation — resolved the tension by fiat: types could depend only on the intuitionistic fragment. The linear lollipop `S ⊸ T` stubbornly remained non-dependent, killing any hope of tracking a resource *through* a dependent type.

**McBride's resolution: quantities from a rig.** McBride's 2016 paper *"I Got Plenty o' Nuttin'"* [1] proposes a deceptively small change: annotate every variable in the context with an element of an arbitrary rig, and track usage with rig arithmetic. The masterstroke is the interpretation of the rig's zero. A variable annotated 0 is present *for purposes of contemplation rather than consumption* — "like a meal we remember fondly but cannot eat twice." It may appear in types as much as it likes, because types are checked, not executed; it may not appear in run-time-relevant positions at all. The payoff is the *dependent lollipop* `(x : S) ⊸ T[x]`: a function that consumes its argument exactly once, while the *type* of the result commemorates what the argument used to be. The canonical example converts a list to a length-indexed vector in place, with type `(l : List X) ⊸ Vector X (length l)` — no run-time copy of `l` is needed to form the vector type, because `length l` is used at quantity 0. We can have plenty of nothing, at no additional run-time cost — and nothing is plenty for the construction of dependent types [1].

**Atkey's semantics: quantities mean something.** Atkey [2] turned McBride's proposal into a full type theory with metatheory. The syntax records usage information for every variable in every judgement; the semantics interprets it. Types are modelled in *Quantitative Categories with Families*, a resource-sensitive refinement of Dybjer's categories with families, and terms receive a realizability interpretation over a variant of linear combinatory algebras — refining the usual realizability semantics of type theory by *accurately tracking resource behaviour*. The slogan: a judgement doesn't just say a term is well-typed; it says how much of each resource the term's execution will consume.

**Erasure before QTT.** Coq's extraction erases `Prop` wholesale; Agda 2.6.1 added `@0` run-time irrelevance, guaranteeing annotated arguments absent at run time [7]; Idris 1 used forcing/detagging analyses in the style of Brady et al. to delete forced constructor arguments automatically. All of these share a weakness: erasure is a *compiler decision*, invisible in the source language's types, and therefore unavailable for abstraction — a library cannot promise its clients that an argument will be erased.

## 3 Methodology

This article proceeds by *reconstruction, comparison, and proof*. First, we reconstruct the core QTT typing discipline — usage contexts, the {0, 1, ω} semiring, and the erasure reading of quantity 0 — in enough detail that the reader can check derivations by hand. Second, we compare QTT against its closest relatives: GraD's heap-based graded dependent types [4], GrTT's graded modality [8], Granule's graded modal programming [5], and Agda's irrelevance annotations [7], along the axes of quantity domain, dependency, metatheoretic guarantee, and implementation status. Third, we ground the theory in Idris 2 practice [3][6], using small but complete programs to show what quantity annotations buy the working programmer. Finally, §5 proves erasure soundness for a core fragment and exhibits the quantitative accounting explicitly.

Notation: usage-vector contexts `x₁ :^ρ₁ S₁, …, xₙ :^ρₙ Sₙ`, binders `(ρ x : S)` with `ρ ∈ {0, 1, ω}`, pointwise context addition, and scaling `ρ · Γ`.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Quantities, rigs, and usage accounting

A rig `(R, 0, 1, +, ·)` is a ring without negation: addition and multiplication with the usual distributivity, but no subtraction. QTT is parameterized over the rig; Idris 2 fixes the *zero-one-many* semiring, whose tables are worth committing to memory:

| + | 0 | 1 | ω |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | 0 | 1 | ω |
| **1** | 1 | ω | ω |
| **ω** | ω | ω | ω |

| · | 0 | 1 | ω |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| **1** | 0 | 1 | ω |
| **ω** | 0 | ω | ω |

The order `0 ≤ 1 ≤ ω` reads as *permissiveness*: a variable promised usage `ρ` may be used in any position demanding `σ ≤ ρ`. The two derived facts that do all the work are `1 + 1 = ω` — using a linear variable twice demotes it to unrestricted, which is precisely what linearity forbids — and `0 · ρ = 0` — scaling by zero erases all usage, which is what lets 0-annotated binders appear freely in types.

Typing rules thread the usage vector through the derivation: variables get usage 1 (0 elsewhere), application *splits* the context (`Γ + Δ`), and abstraction *scales* the bound variable by the binder's quantity. Checking `f : (1 x : A) -> (0 y : B) -> C` against `\x, y => …` therefore reduces to bookkeeping: `x` must be consumed exactly once in the body, `y` not at all in run-time position, and any appearance of either in a *type* position costs nothing, because types are elaborated at quantity 0.

The variable `n` in `n : Nat, x : Fin n ⊢ x : Fin n` carries usage `(0, 1)` — twice in types, zero times computationally — which linear logic could never express. Dependency is not the enemy of resource tracking; *unexamined* dependency is.

### 4.2 Erasure as a specification, not an optimization

Quantity 0 has an operational reading: *this variable does not exist at run time*. In Idris 2 the elaborator enforces the corresponding discipline — a 0-quantity variable may not flow into a run-time-relevant position — and the compiler then erases it with total confidence. Consider vectors:

```idris
data Vect : Nat -> Type -> Type where
  Nil  : Vect 0 a
  (::) : a -> Vect n a -> Vect (S n) a

append : (0 n : Nat) -> (0 m : Nat) -> Vect n a -> Vect m a -> Vect (n + m) a
append n m [] ys = ys
append n m (x :: xs) = x :: append _ _ xs ys
```

Here `n` and `m` are *explicitly* quantity 0 (in real Idris 2 the implicit `n`, `m`, `a` are 0 by default [6]). The compiled `append` takes two arguments, not four; the length indices exist only for the type checker. Contrast the extrinsic approach: Agda achieves the same with `@0` annotations under the `--erasure` flag, where the GHC backend compiles the cons constructor to take only two arguments [7]. In Agda erasure is an opt-in annotation checked by a dedicated pass; in QTT it is the meaning of the quantity, proved sound once for the whole theory.

> **Theorem:** *(Erasure soundness, informal.)* Let `Γ ⊢ M : A` with usage vector `ρ`. Let `⌊M⌋` be `M` with all 0-quantity subterms replaced by a placeholder. Then evaluation of `⌊M⌋` coincides with evaluation of `M` on all run-time-observable behaviour; in particular, no reduction step of `M` inspects a 0-quantity subterm.

The proof (§5) is a simulation argument: 0-annotated variables are never in redex position, so deleting them cannot change the reduction sequence. The Agda manual's `foldl` anecdote is the empirical shadow of this theorem: marking the length `@0` lets the compiler reduce `λ {n} → f {suc n}` to `f`, eliminating a space leak that no heuristic analysis could remove with certainty [7].

### 4.3 The dependent lollipop

The type `(ρ x : S) -> T[x]` generalizes both Π (`ρ = ω`) and linear implication (`ρ = 1`); its dependent-linear instance — McBride's lollipop — is where QTT earns its keep. A function `(1 l : List a) -> Vect (length l) a` consumes the list exactly once *and* indexes the result by a property of the consumed input. The usage accounting that makes this sound is subtle: `l` appears once at quantity 1 (in the body) and arbitrarily often at quantity 0 (in the type `Vect (length l) a`), and `1 + 0 = 1` keeps the books balanced.

This unlocks *typestate-style* programming: file handles, protocol channels, and borrowed arrays become linear resources whose *types* record state transitions, with the describing indices erased at run time. Linearity guarantees the protocol is followed; dependency guarantees the states are meaningful; erasure guarantees the states cost nothing.

### 4.4 Linearity as protocol: session types in Idris 2

The flagship application in Brady's ECOOP paper is type-safe concurrency via session types [3]. A channel is a linear resource: each operation — send, receive, close — consumes the channel at quantity 1 and produces a channel in the next protocol state. Schematically:

```idris
-- schematic: states as erased indices, transitions as linear functions
data Action = Send | Recv | Close

send  : (1 ch : Channel (Send :: rest)) -> (val : a) -> L IO (Channel rest)
recv  : (1 ch : Channel (Recv :: rest)) -> L IO (a, Channel rest)
close : (1 ch : Channel [Close]) -> L IO ()
```

Because the channel is quantity 1, the type checker rejects double-send, use-after-close, and dropped channels alike — the protocol violations that session types were invented to prevent are *usage* violations, and usage is what QTT tracks. The protocol states (`Send :: rest`) are ordinary type-level data at quantity 0: they guide elaboration and vanish from the binary. The channel must be linear, but its *type* must mention the very value being consumed — the dependent lollipop at work.

Haskell's linear types lack the dependency; Rust's ownership lacks the proofs. QTT offers all three, checked by one mechanism.

### 4.5 The graded neighbourhood: GraD, GrTT, and Granule

QTT is one point in a lively design space of *graded* type theories, and the contrasts are instructive.

**GraD** (Choudhury, Eades, Eisenberg, Weirich, POPL 2021 [4]) keeps QTT's spirit but changes the accounting. Where QTT *disables* resource checking inside types, GraD uses the same rules for relevant and irrelevant phrases and simply *discards* irrelevant usages when totalling resources. The payoff is a heap-based operational semantics with a proved soundness theorem: correct accounting of resource usage, from which type soundness, *non-interference of irrelevant resources*, and the *single-pointer property* for linear resources all follow. GraD argues this uniformity enables strictly more type-level reasoning than QTT permits — at the cost of bookkeeping QTT deliberately avoids.

**GrTT** (Moon, Eades, Orchard [8]) takes the modal route: a graded necessity modality internalizes "a value usable according to grade q", generalizing both linearity and dependency analyses in one connective, with an implementation to show it runs.

**Granule** (Orchard, Liepelt, Eades, ICFP 2019 [5]) applies graded modal types to *quantitative program reasoning* — tracking not just usage counts but arbitrary resource analyses through the type system, with grades drawn from richer semirings than {0, 1, ω}.

| System | Quantity domain | Dependency | Erasure story | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| QTT / Idris 2 [1][2][3] | {0, 1, ω} rig | Full; dependent lollipop | Intrinsic: quantity 0 | Full language |
| GraD [4] | Grades incl. 0/1 | Full; uniform rules | Heap semantics, non-interference proved | Theory + Coq mechanization |
| GrTT [8] | Graded modality | Full | Via modality | Theory + implementation |
| Granule [5] | General semirings | Simply-typed base | Graded reasoning | Research language |
| Agda @0 [7] | {erased, relevant} | Full | Opt-in annotation | Production |

## 5 Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

We now substantiate the central claim — that quantity 0 is *sound* erasure — for a core fragment, and exhibit the accounting concretely.

> **Theorem:** *(Erasure simulation.)* Define `⌊·⌋` to erase binders, arguments, and subterms annotated 0, replacing them with a canonical placeholder. If `Γ ⊢ M : A` with usage `ρ` and `M → N` by the operational semantics, then either `⌊M⌋ = ⌊N⌋` (the step was inside erased code) or `⌊M⌋ → ⌊N⌋` (the step survives erasure).

*Proof sketch.* By induction on the step, using the usage vector as invariant: a 0-annotated variable never appears in evaluation position, so no redex can scrutinize erased data, and deleting it commutes with reduction. ∎

*Corollary (non-interference).* Two runs differing only in 0-quantity inputs produce identical observable behaviour. This is GraD's non-interference property [4] recovered for QTT's fragment.

The accounting can be checked by hand on a small derivation. Typing `append`'s cons case:

| Subterm | Usage of `x` | Usage of `xs` | Usage of `n`, `m` |
|---|---|---|---|
| `x :: append _ _ xs ys` | 1 (constructor arg) | 1 (recursive call) | 0 (type indices only) |
| recursive call `append _ _ xs ys` | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| result type `Vect (n + m) a` | 0 | 0 | 0 (all in types) |

Every row sums correctly; the indices never rise above 0 in term position, so the compiled code manipulates bare lists. Empirically, the Idris 2 report [3] shows erased indices throughout its vector and `Fin` libraries plus session-typed concurrency, while Agda's `foldl` space-leak repair [7] shows a real performance bug fixed *by stating erasure in the source* — the workflow QTT makes primitive.

## 6 Limitations

The 0–1–ω discipline is a deliberately coarse instrument, and its edges show under pressure:

- **Coarse multiplicities.** `1 + 1 = ω` conflates "used twice" with "used arbitrarily often". Bounded reuse — "at most three times" — needs richer semirings (bounded linear logic), which Idris 2's fixed rig cannot express [5].
- **One rig to rule them all.** Usage, secrecy, and cost each want their own grade algebra; QTT supports one at a time, and product rigs remain theoretical.
- **No quantity polymorphism.** A function generic over "any quantity" must be written once per quantity or forced to ω, surrendering precision exactly where libraries need it most.
- **Erasure meets evaluation order.** Quantities describe *usage*, not *evaluation*: a 0-quantity argument is still elaborated and type-checked, and under lazy evaluation the interaction of erasure with thunk lifetimes is subtle — erased does not mean free during compilation.
- **Linearity meets reality.** Exceptions can silently drop linear resources; garbage-collected runtimes don't automatically convert quantity 1 into in-place update; quantity 1 is a *permission*, and the back end must still implement it.
- **Ergonomics of the accountant.** Case-splitting on a `(1 x : A)` forces every branch to consume `x` exactly once, which is correct and occasionally maddening. Quantity inference for omitted binders is a whole elaboration problem of its own, and its error messages are a known pain point [6].
- **Maturity.** Idris 2 is young; its libraries, tooling, and optimizer lag decades behind Agda and Coq. Agda's `@0` [7], while less principled, ships in a production proof assistant today.

None of these refutes the thesis — they circumscribe it. QTT solves erasure *as a specification problem*; turning specifications into fast code remains compiler work.

## 7 Conclusion

Quantitative Type Theory reframes an old question. Instead of asking "what can the compiler prove erasable?", it asks "what usage does the programmer *promise*?" — and then holds the program to that promise with the full force of dependent typing. McBride [1] supplies the mechanism, Atkey [2] the meaning, Idris 2 [3] the existence proof: erased indices, linear protocols, and dependent types cooperating in a single core.

GraD [4] shows what uniform accounting buys, GrTT [8] the modal alternative, Granule [5] the generality of grades, Agda [7] the pragmatics of a two-point lattice. Together they suggest the field's direction: usage, relevance, and cost are not compiler internals but *type structure*, and the languages that expose them will be the ones whose abstractions survive contact with the machine.

The computational content of a proof, it turns out, was never just the proof term. It is the proof term *annotated with what may be forgotten* — and forgetting, done right, is the most productive thing a type system can do.
