# Superfluid Turbulence in Helium II: Quantized Vortices, the Vortex Filament Model, and Kolmogorov Spectra

## Abstract

Liquid helium-4 below the lambda transition (2.17 K) is a two-fluid system whose superfluid component can sustain only *quantized* vorticity, giving turbulence in He II a discrete, topological character absent from classical fluids. This thesis synthesizes the physical foundations and modern quantitative theory of superfluid turbulence: Onsager and Feynman's prediction of quantized circulation, Landau's two-fluid hydrodynamics, the mutual-friction experiments of Hall, Vinen, and Donnelly that first revealed the vortex tangle, and K. W. Schwarz's vortex filament model, whose 1985–1988 direct numerical simulations established the algorithmic foundation for all subsequent vortex-dynamics studies. We examine the dual nature of quantum turbulence — the Kolmogorov-type "quasiclassical" regime recovered at finite temperature by Maurer and Tabeling's von Kármán measurements and modern coflow experiments, versus the "ultraquantum" regime exemplified by thermal counterflow — and the role of vortex reconnections, Kelvin-wave cascades, and mutual friction as effective dissipation channels. The synthesis closes with open problems: the closure of the Vinen equation, the bottleneck at the intervortex scale, and effective viscosity in the zero-temperature limit.

## 1 Intro

Few states of matter challenge the conventional picture of turbulence as thoroughly as superfluid helium II. In a classical incompressible fluid, vorticity is a continuous field, viscous diffusion smooths velocity gradients, and the Kolmogorov cascade transfers energy from integral scales to the dissipation range where viscosity converts it to heat. In He II, by contrast, the superfluid component is an inviscid, irrotational quantum fluid whose circulation is quantized in units of κ = h/m₄ ≈ 9.97 × 10⁻⁴ cm²/s [10][1]. Rotation and turbulence in the superfluid component can therefore be carried *only* by topological defects — quantized vortex lines with atomic-scale cores — and any turbulent state of the superfluid is necessarily a tangle of such lines [2][3].

This topological constraint has profound dynamical consequences. Because the circulation of each filament is fixed, vortex lines cannot diffuse or cancel by viscosity; they evolve by their self-induced Biot–Savart velocity field, stretch and fold like classical vortices, and — crucially — *reconnect* when they approach within a core radius of one another, emitting phonons and Kelvin waves [6][8]. The resulting "quantum turbulence" is a tangle of discrete lines whose statistics determine the macroscopic mutual friction between the superfluid and the normal fluid, the only dissipation mechanism available at millikelvin temperatures.

The experimental lineage of this subject begins with the discovery that supercritical counterflow in He II becomes dissipative. Gorter and Mellink (1949) attributed the dissipation to mutual friction [8]; Feynman (1955) proposed that the turbulent superfluid state consists of a tangle of quantized vortices [2]; and Hall and Vinen (1956–1957), through second-sound attenuation in rotating helium and vibrating-wire measurements of the circulation quantum, confirmed both the existence of quantized vortices and the mechanism of mutual friction [9][2]. The modern computational era was inaugurated by K. W. Schwarz's vortex filament simulations of 1985–1988, which reproduced the measured vortex line density, anisotropy parameters, and friction coefficients from microscopic vortex dynamics with remarkable fidelity [4][10].

Two further discoveries reshaped the field. First, Maurer and Tabeling (1998) demonstrated that at scales large compared with the intervortex spacing ℓ, He II turbulence driven by a von Kármán flow exhibits a classical Kolmogorov −5/3 energy spectrum [5][7], implying that the two fluids are locked by mutual friction and the tangle behaves as a coarse-grained classical eddy field. Second, Walmsley and Golov (2008) showed that freely decaying turbulence in He II comes in two flavors: *quasiclassical* turbulence with L ∝ t⁻³ᐟ² and *ultraquantum* (Vinen) turbulence with L ∝ t⁻¹, distinguished by the presence or absence of energy-containing large-scale eddies [5][11]. Understanding the relationship between these regimes — and the quantum mechanisms that terminate the classical cascade — is the central theme of this thesis.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The two-fluid model and Landau's criterion

Below Tλ = 2.17 K, He II is described by Landau's phenomenological two-fluid model: an inviscid superfluid component of density ρs with velocity vs, and a viscous normal fluid of density ρn carrying the entropy, with total density ρ = ρs + ρn [6][8]. The superfluid velocity is the gradient of the phase of the macroscopic condensate wavefunction, vs = (ℏ/m₄)∇φ, and hence is irrotational except at singularities where the phase is undefined — the cores of quantized vortices. The normal fluid consists of thermal excitations (phonons and rotons) and behaves as an ordinary viscous fluid with viscosity ηn.

Mutual friction arises when thermal excitations scatter off the velocity field of vortex lines [9].

> **Theorem (Hall–Vinen mutual friction).** For a uniform array of quantized vortices with mean vorticity ω̂ω, the force on the superfluid per unit volume is **F**ns = (Bρsρn/ρ) ω̂ × (ω × **v**ns) + (B′ρsρn/ρ) ω̂ × **v**ns, where **v**ns = **v**n − **v**s is the counterflow velocity and B, B′ are temperature-dependent phenomenological coefficients [9].

### 2.2 Quantized circulation: Onsager, Feynman, Vinen

The quantization condition ∮**v**s · d**l** = nκ follows from single-valuedness of the condensate phase [2][3]. Onsager suggested it in an unpublished remark; Feynman (1955) gave the first physical picture, proposing vortex filaments of atomic core radius a₀ ≈ 1 Å carrying one quantum of circulation each [2]. Vinen's vibrating-wire experiments in rotating He II (1961) provided the first direct measurement of κ and demonstrated that dissipation in rotating and counterflowing He II is carried by quantized vortices interacting with the normal fluid [9][2].

The second-sound technique — measuring the attenuation of the entropy wave in which the two fluids oscillate in antiphase — provides a non-invasive probe of the vortex line density L (length per unit volume), since vortices scatter second sound [2][3]. The attenuation coefficient is proportional to L, making second-sound resonators the workhorse diagnostic of counterflow turbulence for over half a century [4][5].

### 2.3 Thermal counterflow as the canonical quantum turbulence

When heat is injected into He II, the normal fluid carries entropy away from the heater while the superfluid flows toward it, producing a counterflow **v**ns whose magnitude is proportional to the heat flux. Above a critical velocity, the superflow becomes dissipative: the measured thermal resistance rises sharply because a vortex tangle grows and mutual friction converts the counterflow kinetic energy into heat [8][2]. Vinen proposed the phenomenological evolution equation for the line density [10]:

```
dL/dt = α χ₁ |v_ns| L^(3/2) − α χ₂ κ L²        (Vinen equation)
```

where χ₁, χ₂ are dimensionless coefficients of order unity and α is the mutual friction parameter. This equation, though not derived from first principles, has described counterflow data for nearly seventy years, and recent experiments continue to test its validity and proposed generalizations [10].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The vortex filament model

Schwarz's decisive contribution was to represent each quantized vortex as a space curve **s**(ξ, t) of infinitesimal thickness carrying circulation κ, moving with the local superfluid velocity [4]. The velocity of a filament point decomposes into a self-induced part given by the Biot–Savart integral over all filaments plus any imposed background flow:

> **Theorem (Biot–Savart law for quantized filaments).** At zero temperature the velocity of a point **s** on a vortex filament is **v**s(**s**) = (κ/4π) ∮ (**s**₁ − **s**) × d**s**₁ / |**s**₁ − **s**|³, the integral running over the entire vortex configuration [4][6].

The singular kernel is regularized by the core cutoff a₀. In practice the integral is split into a local contribution — the *local induction approximation* (LIA), which depends on the local curvature **s**′ × **s**″ ln(ℓ/a₀) — and a nonlocal remainder computed numerically, often with a Barnes–Hut tree method for efficiency [8].

At finite temperature, the filament moves under the balance of the Magnus force and the mutual friction drag, yielding Schwarz's equation of motion [4]:

```
ds/dt = v_s + α s' × (v_ns − v_s) − α' s' × [s' × (v_ns − v_s)]
```

```python
# Schwarz equation of motion for one filament discretization point
import numpy as np

def schwarz_velocity(s, sp, vs, vn, alpha, alpha_p):
    vns = vn - vs                                  # counterflow velocity
    t1 = sp / np.linalg.norm(sp)                   # unit tangent
    term = alpha * np.cross(t1, vns - vs)          # transverse friction
    term -= alpha_p * np.cross(t1, np.cross(t1, vns - vs))  # longitudinal friction
    return vs + term
```

### 3.2 Algorithmic reconnection

The Biot–Savart dynamics alone cannot change the topology of the tangle. Schwarz's key algorithmic insight was to *reconnect* filaments artificially whenever two segments approach within the discretization spacing, since the true core-scale physics (governed by the Gross–Pitaevskii equation) inevitably reconnects them [4][6]. The Gross–Pitaevskii equation,

iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = −(ℏ²/2m)∇²ψ + g|ψ|²ψ − μψ,

reproduces reconnection events with emission of sound pulses, and the filament model inherits their large-scale consequences while discarding the core scale ξ [6]. Subsequent studies showed that macroscopic statistics — steady-state L, anisotropy, and friction coefficients — are surprisingly insensitive to the reconnection criterion, validating Schwarz's pragmatic approach [8].

### 3.3 Diagnostics: line density, anisotropy, spectra

From simulations one extracts the vortex line density L, the anisotropy parameters I∥, I⊥ (measuring the mean orientation of **s**′ relative to the counterflow direction), and the curvature parameter c₂ [4][10]. In experiments, L is obtained from second-sound attenuation; velocity spectra are measured with Pitot tubes (Maurer & Tabeling) and pressure probes in modern wind-tunnel facilities [5][7]. The comparison between these diagnostics and the predictions of the filament model — most notably Schwarz's reproduction of the steady-state line density L¹ᐟ² = γ(|**v**ns| − v₀) with the correct temperature-dependent γ — is the empirical core of the field [4][10].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The two regimes of decay: quasiclassical versus ultraquantum

Walmsley and Golov's decisive experiments (2008) injected turbulence into He II with an impulsive spin-down of a container and observed that the late-time decay of L follows one of two laws depending on the initial conditions [11]. When the forcing generates large-scale eddies, the decay follows the classical-like law L ∝ t⁻³ᐟ² with a Kolmogorov spectrum extending from the integral scale down to ℓ; this is *quasiclassical* (Kolmogorov) turbulence, in which the two fluids are locked by mutual friction and the tangle behaves as a classical vorticity field at large scales [5][11]. When forcing produces no large-scale flow, the tangle is random on all scales and decays as L ∝ t⁻¹, the *ultraquantum* or Vinen regime, with no inertial range [10][11]. The two regimes are connected: ultraquantum turbulence can evolve toward the quasiclassical form if large eddies develop [5].

### 4.2 Kolmogorov spectra and the Maurer–Tabeling legacy

In the von Kármán apparatus, two counter-rotating disks stir He II and a Pitot tube measures local velocity fluctuations. Maurer and Tabeling (1998) found the frequency spectrum E(f) ∝ f⁻⁵ᐟ³, consistent with the Kolmogorov spectrum under Taylor's frozen-turbulence hypothesis [5][7]. Subsequent experiments in superfluid wind tunnels (Roche et al., the Grenoble group) confirmed the −5/3 scaling over wider ranges, measured the 4/5 law for third-order structure functions, and demonstrated that the energy injected at large scales is identical in He I and He II [7]. At finite temperature the normal fluid is also turbulent, and mutual friction couples the two components; numerical two-fluid simulations (Salort, Lévêque, Roche) show the Kolmogorov cascade persisting at large scales while, at low temperatures, small scales evolve toward energy equipartition among spectral modes [7].

### 4.3 Reconnection and the Kelvin-wave cascade

Reconnection is the elementary dissipative event of quantum turbulence. When two filaments cross, they exchange strands, and the sharp cusps formed at the reconnection site relax by emitting Kelvin waves — helical distortions propagating along the filaments [6][8]. Nonlinear interaction of Kelvin waves drives a wave-turbulence cascade to ever smaller wavelengths, where at finite temperature mutual friction damps them, and at zero temperature they radiate phonons [6]. Competing theories predict E(k) ∝ k⁻⁵ᐟ³ (Kozik–Svistunov weak wave turbulence) or k⁻⁷ᐟ⁵ (L'vov–Nazarenko); the cascade carries the classical energy flux across the intervortex scale, bridging the Kolmogorov range and the dissipative range [6][8]. A related phenomenon, the *bottleneck* accumulation of energy near kℓ ~ 1, arises from the reduced efficiency of the cascade at the crossover [8].

### 4.4 The Vinen equation and its closure problem

Differentiating the total vortex length in the filament model yields an exact evolution equation for L that involves the mean curvature ⟨|**s**″|⟩ and the anisotropy of the tangle [10]. Schwarz closed this hierarchy with empirical relations measured in his simulations, recovering Vinen's equation with computed coefficients χ₁, χ₂ [4][10]. Recent high-statistics counterflow experiments (Varga, Gao, Guo, Vinen) find that for sparse tangles all proposed forms of the Vinen equation describe the growth of L equally well, while for dense tangles (L ≳ 10⁵ cm⁻²) none is satisfactory — the growth rate slows significantly near steady state, and agreement is recovered only if Schwarz's geometric parameter c₂ is allowed to vary with L [10]. The closure problem — finding a tractable set of macroscopic variables for an arbitrary inhomogeneous tangle in general flows — remains open [10].

### 4.5 Counterflow versus coflow: why the tangle differs

Thermal counterflow has no classical analogue: the normal and superfluid components move in *opposite* directions on average, so the tangle cannot be polarized into large eddies and remains statistically isotropic and random [2][10]. Mechanical coflow (grids, propellers, wind tunnels), by contrast, drives both fluids in the same direction, permitting partial polarization of the vortex lines and the formation of a classical-like energy cascade [5][7]. This distinction explains why counterflow experiments dominated the early literature yet could never exhibit Kolmogorov spectra: they probe Vinen turbulence, whereas coflow probes quasiclassical turbulence [8][11].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Schwarz's quantitative triumph

1. **Steady-state line density.** L¹ᐟ² = γ(|**v**ns| − v₀) with γ(T) matching the measurements of Childers–Tough and others across 1.3–2.1 K [4][10].
2. **Anisotropy.** The parameters I∥ ≈ 0.8, I⊥ ≈ 0.6 for counterflow along ẑ, quantifying the preferential alignment of vortex lines perpendicular to the flow [4].
3. **Friction coefficients.** The simulated mutual friction force per unit volume agrees with the measured Gorter–Mellink coefficient A(T) [4][9].

### 5.2 The Kolmogorov spectrum in He II

- *Maurer & Tabeling (1998):* f⁻⁵ᐟ³ velocity spectrum in von Kármán He II flow [5].
- *Roche et al. (Grenoble wind tunnel):* k⁻⁵ᐟ³ spectrum and 4/5-law verification in coflow [7].
- *SHREK pipe-flow calorimetry:* identical energy injection at large scales in He I and He II; friction coefficients resembling classical flows [7].
- *Oregon grid-turbulence (Niemela, Sreenivasan, Donnelly):* classical decay laws in He II [8].

### 5.3 The Kelvin-wave cascade as dissipation channel

> **Theorem (Decay dichotomy).** For freely decaying He II turbulence, the vortex line density obeys L(t) ∝ t⁻³ᐟ² if the initial state contains energy at scales ≫ ℓ (quasiclassical), and L(t) ∝ t⁻¹ if the tangle is random on all scales (ultraquantum/Vinen), with the effective viscosity νeff setting the prefactor in the former case [11][5].

## 6 Limitations

The vortex filament model, for all its successes, discards the core scale and with it the microscopic physics of reconnection: the emission spectrum of sound, the nucleation of vortices at boundaries and ions, and the detailed structure of the reconnection cusp are all imposed algorithmically rather than derived [6][8]. At scales below ℓ the model cannot describe the Kelvin-wave cascade quantitatively, and different reconnection criteria, while yielding similar large-scale statistics, differ in their small-scale predictions [8].

The Vinen equation's closure problem is unresolved: no finite set of macroscopic variables has been shown to govern an inhomogeneous, anisotropic tangle in general flows, and recent experiments indicate that even the steady-state coefficients drift with L [10]. The phenomenological coefficients B, B′, α, α′ are measured, not computed from first principles, though microscopic theories of phonon–roton scattering give partial accounts [9].

Experimentally, velocity statistics below the intervortex scale remain largely inaccessible: tracer particles (hydrogen flakes, excimers) are larger than ℓ in most regimes, and the interpretation of their motion — whether they follow the normal fluid, get trapped on vortices, or sample both — is itself an active research area [5][6]. The bottleneck at kℓ ~ 1, intermittency corrections in the coupled two-fluid system, and the precise temperature dependence of the decoupling scale are open quantitative questions [8][7].

## 7 Conclusion

Superfluid turbulence in He II is the clearest example in physics of turbulence built from discrete topological defects. From Onsager's and Feynman's quantization hypothesis, through the mutual-friction experiments of Hall, Vinen, and Donnelly, to Schwarz's vortex filament simulations — which remain the most quantitatively successful microscopic theory of any turbulent flow — the field has repeatedly shown that the tangle of quantized vortices is not a metaphor but the actual dynamical object [2][4][9]. The discovery of Kolmogorov spectra at large scales (Maurer–Tabeling and successors) revealed that quantization is compatible with classical cascade phenomenology once the tangle is coarse-grained, while the ultraquantum regime and the Kelvin-wave cascade exhibit genuinely quantum dissipation mechanisms with no classical counterpart [5][6][11].

The outstanding challenges — closing the Vinen hierarchy, computing the effective viscosity from vortex dynamics, resolving the bottleneck and intermittency at the quantum scale, and extending the theory to ³He-B and atomic condensates — ensure that superfluid turbulence will remain a meeting ground of low-temperature physics, nonlinear dynamics, and statistical field theory for years to come [6][8][10].
