# De Novo Genome Assembly with de Bruijn Graphs: k-mer Error Correction, Repeat Resolution via Paired-End Scaffolding, String Graph Overlap-Layout-Consensus, and Long-Read Assembly with HiFi and Nanopore

## Abstract

De novo genome assembly reconstructs an unknown genome from billions of short, error-prone sequencing reads without a reference template. This article develops the full theoretical and practical stack of modern assembly: the reduction of fragment assembly to an Eulerian path problem in de Bruijn graphs [1], statistical k-mer spectrum methods for error correction [5], topological simplification of the assembly graph via tip, bubble, and bulge removal [2], repeat resolution through paired-end scaffolding and mate-pair constraints, the string graph formulation of overlap–layout–consensus with linear-time transitive reduction [3], and the long-read revolution driven by PacBio HiFi and Oxford Nanopore technologies [6]. We prove the Eulerian characterization underlying de Bruijn assembly, analyze the complexity and failure modes of each stage, benchmark representative assemblers, and formalize the evaluation metrics — N50, NG50, and BUSCO — that quantify contiguity, completeness, and correctness [8][9]. The synthesis shows that no single graph paradigm dominates; hybrid pipelines that combine the error tolerance of de Bruijn graphs with the repeat-spanning power of long reads now define the state of the art.

---

## 1. Introduction

The genome assembly problem is reconstruction: given a multiset of substrings (reads) sampled approximately uniformly from an unknown string (the genome), recover the original. In *de novo* assembly no reference guides placement; adjacency, order, and orientation must be inferred from read overlaps alone. Three difficulties dominate: *scale* (~3 × 10⁹ bases, billions of reads for mammals), *errors* (substitutions and indels whose spurious k-mers can outnumber true ones), and *repeats* (segmental duplications, transposons, and tandem repeats longer than any read, collapsing distinct loci into ambiguity).

For two decades the field followed *overlap–layout–consensus* (OLC): compute all pairwise overlaps, find a Hamiltonian-path layout (NP-complete), and derive a consensus [4]. Pevzner, Tang, and Waterman's 2001 breakthrough recast assembly as an Eulerian path problem in a de Bruijn graph — linear-time solvable, and treating repeats as first-class structure rather than masked nuisance [1]. Together with short-read sequencing, this made large-eukaryote shotgun assembly routine. A second revolution came with single-molecule long reads (10 kb to megabases) spanning most repeats at higher error rates; modern assemblers — hifiasm, Flye, Shasta, Canu — blend both worlds in hybrid pipelines [6][9].

This article unifies these threads: the mathematics of de Bruijn assembly, the statistics of error correction, the topology of graph simplification, the combinatorics of scaffolding, the string graph alternative to OLC, the algorithms of long-read assembly, and the metrology of assembly quality.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The shotgun model and Lander–Waterman statistics

In whole-genome shotgun sequencing, reads of length *L* are sampled from a genome of length *G* at coverage *c = NL/G*, where *N* is the number of reads. Under the Lander–Waterman model, read starts follow a Poisson process: coverage at a base is Poisson(*c*), and the probability a base is uncovered is *e^(−c)*. Coverage gaps fragment the assembly into contigs even in a repeat-free, error-free world, and mean contig length grows exponentially with *c* — which is why deep coverage was the first prerequisite of assembly.

### 2.2 Overlap graphs and the Hamiltonian barrier

Classical OLC builds an *overlap graph*: each read is a vertex, and a directed edge connects reads whose suffix–prefix overlap exceeds a threshold. A layout consistent with all overlaps is a path visiting every vertex exactly once — a Hamiltonian path. Since Hamiltonian path is NP-complete, assemblers resort to greedy heuristics (as in the Celera assembler), which are vulnerable to repeats: a repeat shared by two loci creates a dense tangle of false adjacencies that greedy extension cannot resolve [4].

### 2.3 de Bruijn graphs and the Eulerian turn

Fix an integer *k*. Every read is decomposed into its constituent *k*-mers. In the formulation of Pevzner et al. [1] and Idury–Waterman, the de Bruijn graph has one vertex per (*k*−1)-mer and one directed edge per *k*-mer, connecting its prefix (*k*−1)-mer to its suffix (*k*−1)-mer. Identical *k*-mers from different reads are *glued* into a single edge, so repeats collapse into shared vertices and edges rather than exploding into dense overlap cliques. The assembly problem becomes: find a walk using every edge exactly once — an Eulerian path.

> **Theorem (Euler–Hierholzer characterization).** A directed graph admits an Eulerian trail using every edge exactly once if and only if (i) every vertex with nonzero degree belongs to a single weakly connected component of the underlying undirected graph, and (ii) every vertex is *balanced* (in-degree equals out-degree), or exactly one vertex has out-degree − in-degree = 1 (the start) and one has in-degree − out-degree = 1 (the end).

Eulerian trails are found in linear time *O(|E|)* by Hierholzer's algorithm. This is the central algorithmic miracle of the de Bruijn approach: the hard combinatorial core of assembly collapses from NP-complete to linear time [1][2].

### 2.4 The k-mer spectrum

Let *S(R)* be the multiset of all *k*-mers in the read set *R*, with multiplicities. In an ideal error-free dataset at coverage *c*, each genomic *k*-mer appears approximately *c·(L−k+1)/L* times, so the multiplicity histogram is a mixture: a sharp peak near the coverage depth (true *k*-mers), repeats at integer multiples of it, and a vast tail of multiplicity-1 *k*-mers generated by sequencing errors. A single substitution corrupts up to *k* distinct *k*-mers, so the erroneous fraction is roughly *kε* — over 40% at *k* = 55, ε = 1%. Error correction is therefore a precondition for graph construction, not a polishing step.

## 3. Methodology

A modern de Bruijn assembly pipeline proceeds through five stages [4]:

1. **Error correction.** Estimate the *k*-mer spectrum, classify *k*-mers as trusted or erroneous, and correct reads — via spectrum methods (Quake, QuorUM [10]) or Hamming-graph clustering (Hammer, BayesHammer [5]) for non-uniform coverage.
2. **Graph construction.** Build the de Bruijn graph from corrected reads using compressed representations — *unitigs* (maximal non-branching paths) and succinct structures (Bloom filters, FM-index-backed graphs) — since the naive graph is far too large.
3. **Graph simplification.** Remove topological artifacts induced by residual errors and polymorphism: *tips* (dead-end paths), *bubbles* (parallel paths that diverge and reconverge), and *bulges* (short detours). See §4.2.
4. **Scaffolding.** Use paired-end and mate-pair reads of known insert size to order and orient contigs across repeats.
5. **Consensus and output.** Traverse the simplified graph to emit contigs; map reads back to polish the consensus sequence.

The choice of *k* is a fundamental tradeoff [4]: small *k* gains sensitivity but collapses repeats of length ≥ *k*; large *k* resolves repeats but fragments the graph where coverage dips. Multi-*k* strategies (SPAdes) build and merge graphs at several *k* values.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 k-mer Error Correction as a Statistical Decision Problem

Error correction in the *k*-mer spectrum framework is a binary classification task on *k*-mers, followed by a projection of corrections onto reads. Let *m(x)* be the observed multiplicity of *k*-mer *x*. Under uniform coverage, *m(x)* for a true genomic *k*-mer follows approximately Poisson(λ) with λ ≈ *c*, while an erroneous *k*-mer typically has *m(x)* = 1 or 2. A multiplicity threshold τ separates *trusted* from *untrusted* *k*-mers.

**Quake** [10] adds base quality scores, correcting reads by maximum likelihood under a trusted-*k*-mer mixture; **QuorUM** shows aggressive trimming minimizes false *k*-mers only at the cost of discarding true ones, fragmenting assemblies [10].

Under non-uniform coverage (single-cell MDA), thresholds fail. **Hammer** [5] builds a *Hamming graph* on *k*-mers and elects a consensus center per connected component; **BayesHammer** adds Bayesian subclustering to split components spanning near-identical repeats, making single-cell *E. coli* assembly feasible where ~97% of *k*-mers were non-genomic [5].

```python
def trusted_kmers(counts, tau):
    """Partition a k-mer count dict into trusted / erroneous sets."""
    trusted = {k: c for k, c in counts.items() if c >= tau}
    erroneous = {k: c for k, c in counts.items() if c < tau}
    return trusted, erroneous

def correct_read(read, k, trusted):
    """Greedy single-pass correction: rewrite each untrusted k-mer
    to the trusted k-mer at minimum Hamming distance (Quake-style)."""
    kmers = [read[i:i+k] for i in range(len(read) - k + 1)]
    out = list(read)
    for i, x in enumerate(kmers):
        if x not in trusted:
            cands = [t for t in trusted if sum(a != b for a, b in zip(x, t)) <= 2]
            if cands:
                best = min(cands, key=lambda t: sum(a != b for a, b in zip(x, t)))
                for j, b in enumerate(best):
                    out[i + j] = b
    return "".join(out)
```

Error correction is *information recovery*, not filtering: every discarded true *k*-mer risks a contig break, every retained false one a false branch.

### 4.2 Graph Simplification: Tips, Bubbles, and Bulges

Even after correction, the raw de Bruijn graph is unusable: residual errors, heterozygosity, and repeats create millions of spurious branches. Simplification applies local topological rewrite rules [2]:

- **Tips** are short dead-end paths — a vertex chain of length < 2*k* with out-degree 0 (or in-degree 0). *Tip clipping* removes tips below a coverage-scaled length threshold — at the risk of deleting true low-coverage sequence, which is indistinguishable from error.
- **Bubbles** are pairs of vertex-disjoint paths that start and end at the same vertices (*source* and *sink*), arising from SNPs, small indels, or near-identical repeat copies. *Bubble popping* (Velvet's Tour Bus) merges the two paths via bounded BFS from each branching vertex, keeping the higher-coverage allele.
- **Bulges** are short detours rejoining the main path within a few vertices — the signature of a single sequencing error (Compeau–Pevzner–Tesler's canonical *TGGCGTG*/*TGGAGTG* example) [2]; removing the lower-coverage detour, iterated to convergence, yields a graph admitting clean Eulerian traversal.

*Figure 2. Topological simplification of the de Bruijn graph: tip clipping, bubble popping, and bulge removal, each shown before and after the rewrite.*

> **Theorem (Idury–Waterman graph statistics).** Under uniform coverage *a*, read length *L*, and per-base error rate *r*, the expected numbers of vertices, singleton (tip) vertices, and fork vertices in the de Bruijn graph admit closed forms in *a*, *L*, *r*, and *k*. In particular, the expected number of false (*k*−1)-mers grows linearly with the error rate while true vertices saturate at genome size — quantifying exactly why simplification is mandatory, not optional.

Simplification is confluent but not canonical: operation order decides which haplotype survives — which diploid-aware assemblers exploit by *preserving* bubbles as heterozygous variants.

### 4.3 Repeat Resolution via Paired-End Scaffolding

A repeat of length ≥ *k* collapses into a single high-coverage vertex (or unitig) with multiple in- and out-edges — the graph cannot locally decide which incoming edge pairs with which outgoing edge. Paired-end reads rescue the situation with *long-range linking information*: the two ends of a pair are known to originate ~*d* bases apart (insert size *d* ± σ), even though the sequence between them is unknown.

**Scaffolding** treats contigs as nodes in a *scaffold graph*; each pair spanning two contigs votes for their order, orientation, and gap distance — a constrained linear-ordering problem solved heuristically (SSPACE, Bambus, ALLPATHS-LG). Mate-pair libraries with large inserts (2–40 kb) form a hierarchy of jumping distances, while EULER-DB threads mate-pairs as *super-paths* directly through repeat vertices during Eulerian traversal [1].

The fundamental limit is information-theoretic: a repeat longer than the largest available insert size (or read length) is *unresolvable* — no algorithm can determine the correct traversal without additional data. This is precisely the barrier long reads demolish.

### 4.4 The String Graph and Overlap–Layout–Consensus

Myers' *string graph* [3] reformulates OLC to eliminate its redundancies while preserving read coherence (the full sequence of each read, which de Bruijn graphs discard when shredding into *k*-mers). Construction:

1. Discard reads contained in another read (~40% carry no new information).
2. Create two vertices per remaining read (its ends); each suffix–prefix overlap becomes labeled directed edges carrying the non-overlapping overhang.
3. Apply **transitive reduction**: if read *A* overlaps *B*, *B* overlaps *C*, and *A* overlaps *C* with the overhang consistent with going through *B*, the edge *A→C* is *reducible* and removed. Myers gave a linear-expected-time transitive reduction algorithm using overlap intervals [3].
4. Collapse non-branching chains into unitigs.

Every path in the reduced string graph is a valid sequence hypothesis; contigs are maximal non-branching paths. Because overlaps are computed at read granularity with approximate matching, string graphs tolerate the 5–15% error of raw Nanopore reads where *k*-mer shredding would drown in false *k*-mers — the foundation of Canu, Flye, miniasm, and hifiasm [6][9].

The de Bruijn vs. string graph debate resolves pragmatically: de Bruijn graphs excel when reads are short and accurate (errors are correctable, *k*-mers are trustworthy); string graphs excel when reads are long (overlaps are informative, *k*-mers are not). Hybrid assemblers use both.

### 4.5 Long-Read Assembly: HiFi and Nanopore

Two single-molecule technologies redefined the possible:

- **PacBio HiFi** (circular consensus sequencing) yields 10–25 kb reads at >99.9% accuracy, eliminating the error-correction stage and — critically — letting true heterozygous variants be distinguished from errors. **hifiasm** builds a phased string graph for haplotype-resolved assembly; **hifiasm-UL** aligns ONT ultra-long reads (>100 kb) to the HiFi graph, encodes them as integer unitig-ID sequences, assembles a second "double" graph, and merges the two, reaching telomere-to-telomere contiguity on diploid and polyploid genomes [6].
- **Oxford Nanopore** produces megabase-scale reads at historically 3–10% error. **Shasta** assembles a human genome in under 6 hours on one node via a noise-robust marker graph, polished to Q30 by MarginPolish/HELEN; eleven human genomes were assembled in nine days at median NG50 18.5 Mb [9].

A comprehensive benchmark of 11 HiFi assemblers on complex eukaryotes confirms the hierarchy: hifiasm-class phased string-graph assemblers lead on contiguity and phasing accuracy, de Bruijn-based HiFi assemblers (LJA) trade some contiguity for speed, and all benefit monotonically from adding ultra-long reads [9].

## 5. Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

### 5.1 Complexity results

| Problem | Complexity | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Eulerian trail (de Bruijn assembly core) | *O(\|E\|)* linear | Hierholzer's algorithm [1] |
| Hamiltonian path (naive OLC layout) | NP-complete | Motivates Eulerian reformulation [4] |
| Transitive reduction of string graph | Linear expected time | Myers' interval-marking algorithm [3] |
| de Bruijn superwalk (shortest walk consistent with read paths) | NP-hard | Medvedev et al.; real graphs need heuristics |
| Optimal scaffolding (linear ordering) | NP-hard | Heuristics + mate-pair hierarchy in practice |

### 5.2 Assembler benchmark landscape

Representative assemblers on human-scale data (typical published values, not one controlled experiment) [6][9]:

| Assembler | Read type | Graph paradigm | Typical human contig NG50 | Consensus QV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| hifiasm (+UL) | HiFi (+ ONT ultra-long) | Phased string graph, double graph | 50–150 Mb (T2T in combination) | Q50+ |
| LJA | HiFi | de Bruijn (multiplex) | 20–40 Mb | Q50 |
| Flye | ONT / HiFi | Repeat (string) graph | 15–40 Mb | Q30–Q40 |
| Shasta | ONT | Marker graph + polish | ~18 Mb median | Q30 |
| Canu / HiCanu | ONT / HiFi | Overlap–layout–consensus | 10–30 Mb | Q40–Q50 |
| SPAdes | Illumina | Multisized de Bruijn | 0.05–0.2 Mb (human) | Q40 |

The pattern is unmistakable: contiguity scales with read length and with the assembler's ability to *phase* rather than *collapse* heterozygosity.

### 5.3 Computing the metrics

```python
def nx_stats(lengths, genome_size=None):
    """Return N50/NG50 and L50/LG50 for a list of contig lengths."""
    s = sorted(lengths, reverse=True)
    total = genome_size if genome_size else sum(s)
    acc, l50 = 0, 0
    for L in s:
        acc += L
        l50 += 1
        if acc >= total / 2:
            return {"Nx": L, "Lx": l50, "total": sum(s), "n": len(s)}
```

> **Definition (N50 / NG50).** Sort contig lengths decreasingly: *l₁ ≥ l₂ ≥ … ≥ lₙ*. The **N50** is the length *lᵢ* of the shortest contig such that contigs of length ≥ *lᵢ* cover at least 50% of the *assembly* length; **L50** is the corresponding count *i*. **NG50/LG50** replace assembly length with the estimated *genome* size — penalizing assemblies that inflate N50 by discarding sequence [8].

**BUSCO** measures *completeness* orthogonally to contiguity: it searches the assembly for curated near-universal single-copy genes of the organism's lineage (e.g., 3,354 diptera genes in odb10) via HMM profiles, classifying each as complete, duplicated, fragmented, or missing [8].

Modern best practice reports all three axes — contiguity (auNG/Nx curves), completeness (BUSCO, *k*-mer completeness via Merqury), and correctness (QV from *k*-mer survival, structural validation via Hi-C or optical maps) — because each is gameable in isolation: N50 rises when sequence is discarded or misjoined, BUSCO is blind to collapsed repeats, and QV says nothing about missing sequence [8].

## 6. Limitations

1. **The repeat barrier is fundamental, not algorithmic.** Any repeat longer than the longest read or insert is unresolvable from the data alone; >100 kb segmental duplications and centromeric satellites remain holdouts even for ultra-long reads.
2. **Diploidy vs. collapse.** Collapsing haplotypes destroys heterozygosity; full phasing doubles compute and fragments homozygous regions, worse in polyploids [6].
3. **Error profiles still bite.** Nanopore homopolymer errors survive polishing, and de Bruijn graphs on raw ONT reads remain impractical — hence marker sketches and string graphs.
4. **Metrics mislead.** N50 rewards discarding sequence and tolerates misjoins; BUSCO covers only conserved gene space and misses collapsed duplications; reference-free QV assumes reads are truth.
5. **Computational cost.** Mammalian de Bruijn graphs hold billions of *k*-mers (hundreds of GB even succinctly), and all-vs-all overlap is quadratic without minimizer/MinHash seeding.
6. **Non-uniform coverage** (single-cell, metagenomic) violates the Poisson assumptions behind correction and simplification, requiring specialized tools [5].

## 7. Conclusion

Assembly has traversed three eras: Hamiltonian OLC, limited by NP-completeness; Eulerian de Bruijn graphs, which made short-read assembly tractable via linear-time algorithms, statistical error correction, and topological simplification [1][2][4]; and the long-read era, where phased string graphs and hybrid HiFi/ultra-long pipelines resolve previously unresolvable repeats [3][6][9]. The enduring lessons: match the graph to the data's error profile, correct errors *before* building structure, treat repeats as signal, and evaluate on orthogonal axes — contiguity, completeness, correctness — since any single metric can be gamed [8].
