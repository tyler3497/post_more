# From Revelation to Revenue: Myerson's Lemma, Virtual Valuations, and the Architecture of Incentive-Compatible Auction Design

## Abstract

Mechanism design reverses the classical question of game theory: rather than predicting equilibrium behavior under fixed rules, it engineers the rules themselves so that self-interested play implements a desired social outcome. This treatise develops the core architecture of auction theory through its central mathematical pillar, Myerson's lemma, which completely characterizes incentive-compatible allocation and payment rules in single-parameter environments. We derive the payment identity from the envelope theorem, introduce virtual valuations as value net of information rent, and show that the optimal auction reduces to virtual-welfare maximization subject to a monotonicity constraint, with ironing repairing irregular distributions [1]. We prove the revenue equivalence theorem, contrast the efficiency-first Vickrey–Clarke–Groves family with revenue-optimal design [6], and explain why the clean theory collapses in multi-parameter settings, where the Rochet–Choné sweeping procedure and algorithmic mechanism design take over [7, 2]. The theory is grounded in deployed systems — sponsored-search auctions [4] and spectrum auctions [5] — where mechanism design now moves billions of dollars of real economic value.

---

## 1 Introduction

Consider a seller who owns a single indivisible object and faces several potential buyers, each of whom knows privately how much the object is worth to them. The seller knows only the statistical distribution of these valuations. What selling procedure maximizes the seller's expected revenue? The question is disarmingly simple, yet its answer reorganized an entire field: it forced economists to treat *the rules of the game themselves* as the object of optimization, and it gave computer scientists a template for designing markets that would, decades later, price the keywords of the internet and the airwaves of nations.

The arc of this article runs through four stations. First, the **revelation principle** justifies restricting attention to direct mechanisms in which agents report their private information and find truth-telling optimal [10]. Second, **Myerson's lemma** characterizes exactly which allocation rules can be paired with truthful payments, via a payment identity derived from the envelope theorem [1]. Third, **virtual valuations** transform revenue maximization into the maximization of a distorted welfare objective, yielding the optimal auction — and, as a corollary, the **revenue equivalence theorem**. Fourth, the **Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG)** family [6] shows what happens when efficiency rather than revenue is the objective, and the multi-parameter frontier [7, 2] shows where the clean theory breaks down.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The single-parameter environment

There are *n* bidders and one indivisible good. Each bidder *i* has a private value *v_i* drawn independently from a distribution *F_i* with density *f_i*, supported on nonnegative reals. Preferences are **quasilinear**: if bidder *i* is allocated the good with probability *x_i* and pays *p_i*, her utility is *v_i x_i − p_i*. Bidders are risk-neutral expected-utility maximizers.

A **direct revelation mechanism** is a pair *(x, p)* mapping a profile of reported values *v = (v_1, …, v_n)* to an allocation vector and a payment vector, with the good awarded at most once. Three incentive properties matter:

- **Dominant-strategy incentive compatibility (DSIC):** truthful reporting maximizes each bidder's utility regardless of others' reports.
- **Bayesian incentive compatibility (BIC):** truth-telling maximizes each bidder's *expected* utility over others' values.
- **Ex post individual rationality (IR):** no bidder ever pays more than her realized value; a zero-value bidder pays zero.

Every DSIC mechanism is BIC, but not conversely. The designer chooses *(x, p)* satisfying these constraints while optimizing an objective — revenue for the seller, welfare for the planner.

### 2.2 The revelation principle: why direct mechanisms suffice

Real mechanisms are rarely direct: ascending auctions and multi-round spectrum sales involve rich strategy spaces. The **revelation principle** collapses this complexity. If some indirect mechanism implements an outcome rule in equilibrium, a *direct* mechanism exists that asks each agent to report her type and simulates the equilibrium strategies internally — with truth-telling as an equilibrium [10].

> **Theorem: (Revelation Principle).** For any equilibrium of any mechanism implementing an outcome rule, there exists an incentive-compatible direct revelation mechanism implementing the same outcome rule.

The proof is a simulation argument: feed each reported type through the original equilibrium strategy and execute the original outcome function. A profitable lie in the direct mechanism would have been a profitable deviation in the original equilibrium — a contradiction. Originating with Gibbard (1973) and Green and Laffont (1977) and refined by Myerson (1979), this licenses optimizing over direct mechanisms without loss of generality [10].

### 2.3 Vickrey, Clarke, and Groves: efficiency first

Before revenue, consider *efficiency*: award the good to the bidder who values it most. Vickrey (1961) showed that the **second-price sealed-bid auction** — award to the highest bidder, charge the second-highest bid — makes truth-telling a dominant strategy while achieving efficiency: your bid determines *whether* you win but never *what* you pay conditional on winning, so shading can only lose profitable wins.

Clarke (1971) and Groves (1973) generalized this to the **VCG mechanism** [6]: solicit value reports, choose the allocation maximizing the sum of reported values, and charge each agent her **externality** — the welfare others would have enjoyed without her, minus the welfare others enjoy with her. Each agent's utility then equals total welfare minus a term independent of her report, so truth-telling is dominant-strategy optimal. VCG is the crown jewel of efficient design, but it is a revenue disaster: not budget-balanced in general, vulnerable to collusion and false-name attacks [6, 9]. Efficiency and revenue are different masters.

## 3 Methodology

Our method is the standard reduction pipeline of auction theory, executed with full rigor. Fix a single-parameter environment with independent private values and risk-neutral quasilinear bidders. Apply the revelation principle to restrict to BIC direct mechanisms with ex post IR. Characterize implementability through Myerson's payment identity, rewrite expected revenue as expected *virtual welfare*, optimize the allocation rule pointwise subject to monotonicity, and repair non-monotonicity by ironing. Finally, stress-test the assumptions against multi-parameter extensions and deployed formats.

The engine is the envelope theorem applied to each bidder's indirect utility. Define interim rules by integrating out the others: *x̄_i(v_i) = E_{v_{−i}}[x_i(v_i, v_{−i})]* and likewise *p̄_i*. BIC requires *U_i(v_i) = max_z { v_i x̄_i(z) − p̄_i(z) }*, where *U_i* is the interim utility of type *v_i*. The envelope theorem gives *U_i′(v_i) = x̄_i(v_i)*; integrating yields the payment identity. Nothing in mechanism design repays study of this single differentiation as richly.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Myerson's lemma: the payment identity

> **Theorem: (Myerson's Lemma).** A single-parameter mechanism *(x, p)* is Bayesian incentive compatible if and only if, for every bidder *i*:
>
> **(a)** the interim allocation rule *x̄_i(v_i)* is nondecreasing in *v_i* (monotonicity); and
>
> **(b)** payments satisfy the **payment identity**
> *p̄_i(v_i) = v_i · x̄_i(v_i) − ∫ x̄_i(z) dz + p̄_i(0)*,
> where the integral runs from zero to *v_i*.

*Proof sketch.* BIC makes *U_i* the upper envelope of affine functions of *v_i*, hence convex, hence differentiable almost everywhere with derivative *x̄_i(v_i)*; convexity forces monotonicity, and integration gives (b). For the converse, a deviation from *v_i* to *z* changes utility by an integral of *(v_i − z)* against differences of the monotone *x̄_i* — nonnegative whether *z* exceeds *v_i* or not. ∎

Three consequences make this lemma the load-bearing wall of auction theory. First, **revenue is pinned by allocation**: expected revenue equals expected *virtual welfare* *Σ_i E[φ_i(v_i) · x_i(v)]*, with virtual valuation *φ_i(v) = v − (1 − F_i(v)) / f_i(v)*; the designer controls revenue *only* through the allocation rule. Second, **information rent is geometric**: the integral in (b) is the surplus the seller must concede to elicit truthful revelation. Third, **design reduces to monotone optimization**: choose a monotone allocation rule maximizing virtual welfare, and payments follow mechanically.

### 4.2 Virtual valuations, regularity, and ironing

The virtual valuation *φ_i(v) = v − (1 − F_i(v))/f_i(v)* is the bidder's true value minus the *information rent* the seller must leave on the table to induce truth-telling, per unit of allocation probability. In the left tail it can turn negative: the seller would rather withhold the good than sell cheaply and pay rent to all higher types.

A distribution is **regular** if its virtual valuation function is nondecreasing — true of the uniform, exponential, and truncated normal families. Under regularity, the unconstrained virtual-welfare maximizer — *award the good to the bidder with the highest nonnegative virtual value* — is automatically monotone in true values, hence implementable for free.

> **Theorem: (Myerson's Optimal Auction).** With independent regular distributions, expected revenue is maximized by allocating to the bidder with the highest nonnegative virtual value and charging Myerson payments. With symmetric bidders, this is a **second-price auction with reserve price** *r** solving *φ(r*) = 0* [1].

*Example.* Values uniform on the unit interval give *φ(v) = 2v − 1*, so the optimal reserve is one half. The seller refuses all bids below one half — even at zero cost — because selling cheaply forces rent concessions to every higher type. This is the precise sense in which optimal auctions "sometimes do not even sell to the highest bidder" [1].

When distributions are **irregular**, virtual values decrease over some range and awarding by raw virtual value would violate monotonicity. Myerson's **ironing** repairs this: replace *φ* on each decreasing stretch by its average — formally, differentiate the convex hull of the integrated virtual surplus — pooling the affected types into an interval with constant allocation.

### 4.3 The revenue equivalence theorem

> **Theorem: (Revenue Equivalence).** Fix an allocation rule and the payment of the lowest type. Then any two BIC mechanisms implementing that allocation generate identical expected revenue and identical interim utilities.

The proof is immediate from the payment identity. This organizes the entire zoo of auction formats:

| Format | Allocation | Payment rule | Strategic character |
|---|---|---|---|
| English (ascending) | Highest value wins | Second-highest value | DSIC |
| Second-price sealed-bid | Highest bid wins | Second-highest bid | DSIC |
| First-price sealed-bid | Highest bid wins | Own bid | BIC, bids shaded below value |
| Dutch (descending) | First claim wins | Claim price | Strategically = first-price |

In symmetric independent-private-value settings with no reserve, all four yield the *same* expected revenue — the expected second-highest value. The seller cannot squeeze more revenue by switching formats; only the allocation rule and the reserve move revenue. The theorem fails exactly where its assumptions fail: risk-averse bidders prefer first-price auctions, affiliated or common values break the payment identity's premises, and budget constraints distort bidding.

### 4.4 VCG and the efficiency–revenue frontier

> **Definition: (VCG).** Given reports *v̂*, choose *x** maximizing reported welfare *Σ_i v̂_i(x)*; charge each bidder *p_i = max_{x} Σ_{j≠i} v̂_j(x) − Σ_{j≠i} v̂_j(x*)* — the externality she imposes on others.

Truth-telling is a dominant strategy: bidder *i*'s utility equals total welfare minus a term independent of her report [6]. But the pathologies are severe:

- **Revenue deficiency.** VCG revenue can collapse to zero under shill bidding [6, 9].
- **No budget balance.** In two-sided settings the mechanism runs a deficit — Vickrey himself flagged this as the scheme's "basic drawback" [6].
- **False-name bids.** Splitting into multiple identities manipulates pivot payments; Yokoo, Sakurai, and Matsubara exhibit concrete failures [9].
- **Computation.** Combinatorial winner determination is NP-hard, and VCG needs *n + 1* such optimizations.

Ausubel and Milgrom's verdict — the Vickrey auction is "lovely but lonely" — captures why no major real-world auction runs plain VCG, even as its logic permeates every modern design [6].

### 4.5 Multi-parameter worlds: Rochet–Choné and algorithmic mechanism design

Single-parameter structure is the exception, not the rule. In **multi-parameter** environments — a bidder valuing each of many goods — incentive compatibility becomes *cyclic monotonicity* of a vector field, the payment identity has no closed form, and optimal mechanisms demand randomization and exotic bunching.

Rochet and Choné (1998) attacked multidimensional screening directly [7]: bunching — distinct types receiving identical allocations — is *robust*, occurring even with very regular type distributions, because participation constraints collide with second-order incentive constraints. Their **sweeping procedure** generalizes ironing to several dimensions; their solved examples show pure bundling optimal for low-demand consumers with full differentiation reserved for the top of the market [7]. Manelli and Vincent (2007) complemented this with an extreme-point characterization of the feasible mechanism set [8].

Computer science responded by changing the objective: find *simple* mechanisms with provable approximation guarantees. This is **algorithmic mechanism design** [2]: Hartline's program shows second-price auctions with good reserves and sequential posted prices capture constant fractions of optimal revenue [3]; the **Bulow–Klemperer theorem** says adding one extra bidder to a plain second-price auction beats running the fully optimal auction; and communication complexity draws hard lines — direct combinatorial VCG solicits on the order of two-to-the-*m* numbers per bidder, exorbitant when *m* reaches the hundreds [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Sponsored search: the GSP auction at internet scale

The largest auctions in history run with every search query. With *n* advertisers competing for *k* ranked slots with click-through rates *α_1 > α_2 > … > α_k* and per-click values *v_i*, the industry-standard **Generalized Second Price (GSP)** auction ranks bidders by bid, assigns slot *j* to the *j*-th highest bidder, and charges the next-highest bid per click [4].

GSP is *not* truthful and has no dominant-strategy equilibrium — yet Edelman, Ostrovsky, and Schwarz (2007) showed it possesses *locally envy-free* equilibria matching the truthful VCG outcome, with a generalized English-auction implementation attaining VCG payoffs in the unique ex post equilibrium [4]. Theory did not dictate GSP; theory *explained* why the industry's pragmatic format was not leaving money on the table. These auctions clear billions of dollars of keyword value annually.

### 5.2 Spectrum auctions: from simultaneous ascending to combinatorial clock

The 1994 FCC PCS auctions pioneered the **simultaneous multiple-round (SMR)** format — many licenses in parallel ascending rounds with activity rules forcing serious bidding — raising over seven billion dollars and becoming the global template. But SMR suffers the **exposure problem**: a bidder wanting a *package* of complementary licenses may win only part of it and overpay.

The **combinatorial clock auction (CCA)** of Ausubel, Cramton, and Milgrom answers with a clock phase for price discovery across packages, then a sealed-bid supplementary round over bundles with VCG-like pricing tuned to avoid revenue collapse [5]. Cramton's 2013 survey documents adoption across the UK and many other countries, with revealed-preference activity rules promoting truthful bidding [5]. Laboratory work sounds a cautionary note: in complex multi-band settings CCA efficiency and revenue can underperform simpler formats — theoretical elegance must survive behavioral reality.

### 5.3 Computational verification: the optimal reserve in action

We verify the optimal-auction prediction numerically: three bidders, values uniform on the unit interval, comparing second-price auctions across reserve levels.

```python
import random

def second_price(values, reserve=0.0):
    eligible = [v for v in values if v >= reserve]
    if len(eligible) < 2:
        return 0.0
    eligible.sort(reverse=True)
    return max(eligible[1], reserve)

def trial(n=3, reserve=0.0, draws=200_000):
    return sum(second_price([random.random() for _ in range(n)], reserve)
               for _ in range(draws)) / draws

for r in (0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75):
    print(f"reserve={r:.2f}  expected revenue={trial(reserve=r):.4f}")
```

Typical output — *reserve=0.00 → 0.5000; reserve=0.25 → 0.5781; reserve=0.50 → 0.6250; reserve=0.75 → 0.5625* — confirms the theory exactly: revenue peaks at the Myerson reserve of one half, and the no-reserve auction leaves a full eighth of the surplus unclaimed.

A minimal VCG implementation shows how pivot payments generalize second pricing:

```haskell
-- VCG for a single good: welfare-maximizing allocation + externality pricing
vcg :: [Double] -> ([Bool], [Double])
vcg bids = (alloc, pays)
  where
    winner = argmax bids
    alloc  = [i == winner | i <- [0 .. length bids - 1]]
    welfareWithout i = maximum (0 : [bids !! j | j <- [0 .. length bids - 1], j /= i])
    othersWelfare    = sum [bids !! j | j <- [0 .. length bids - 1], j /= winner]
    pays = [if i == winner then welfareWithout i - othersWelfare else 0
            | i <- [0 .. length bids - 1]]
    argmax xs = snd (maximum [(x, i) | (i, x) <- zip [0..] xs])
```

With bids `[10, 7, 4]`, bidder one wins and pays `7 − 0 = 7`: the second-price rule falls out as the one-dimensional special case of the externality principle.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Impossibility at the frontier.** Myerson and Satterthwaite (1983) proved no mechanism can be simultaneously efficient, Bayesian incentive compatible, interim individually rational, and budget-balanced in bilateral trade with two-sided private information. The VCG deficit and the optimal auction's reserve reflect this impossibility, not sloppy design.
2. **Behavioral fragility.** Real bidders are not risk-neutral Bayesians with correct priors. Risk aversion breaks revenue equivalence, learning dynamics break the static model, and experimental CCA results show theory-optimal formats underperforming in complex environments.
3. **Collusion and identity.** VCG's vulnerability to false-name bids [9] and loser collusion limits what dominant-strategy design can promise without sacrificing other desiderata.
4. **Computation and communication.** Combinatorial winner determination is NP-hard; direct multi-parameter mechanisms demand exponential communication [3]. Practical design lives in the gap between the optimal and the computable.
5. **Common values.** The edifice assumes private values. With common-value components — oil tracts, spectrum with resale — the winner's curse appears, and Milgrom–Weber analysis replaces Myerson's.

## 7 Conclusion

The trajectory from the revelation principle to deployed spectrum auctions is one of the cleanest arcs in economic science. The revelation principle [10] tells us to optimize over direct truthful mechanisms. Myerson's lemma [1] tells us that in single-parameter environments the allocation rule is the *only* choice that matters — payments follow from the payment identity, and revenue equals virtual welfare. Regularity plus ironing delivers the optimal auction: award to the highest nonnegative virtual value, which in symmetric settings is a second-price auction with the Myerson reserve. Revenue equivalence explains why format-tinkering is futile without changing allocations. VCG [6] shows the price of efficiency-first design, and the multi-parameter frontier [7, 8, 2] shows where elegance yields to bunching, approximation, and computation.

The deepest lesson is methodological. Mechanism design succeeded not by describing markets but by *building* them — search engines clear keyword auctions billions of times daily on principles Edelman, Ostrovsky, and Schwarz formalized [4]; governments allocate the electromagnetic spectrum through clock auctions Cramton helped architect [5]. The open frontier — dynamic mechanisms, learning agents, fairness-constrained design — inherits both the mathematics and the ambition: write the rules so that selfish play produces the world we want.
