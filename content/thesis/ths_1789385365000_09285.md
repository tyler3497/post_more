# Time Averages, Subadditive Limits, and Physical Statistics: Birkhoff's Ergodic Theorem, Kingman's Subadditive Ergodic Theorem, Thermodynamic Formalism, and SRB Measures

## Abstract

Ergodic theory asks when the long-time statistics of a deterministic dynamical system can be read from an invariant measure. This article develops four pillars of the answer in one arc. Birkhoff's pointwise ergodic theorem equates time averages of integrable observables with conditional expectations onto the invariant sigma-algebra, making Boltzmann's ergodic hypothesis rigorous in the almost-everywhere sense. Kingman's subadditive ergodic theorem extends convergence from additive Birkhoff sums to subadditive cocycles, delivering the top Lyapunov exponent of random matrix products and, through Oseledets' theorem, the full multiplicative ergodic theory of derivative cocycles. The thermodynamic formalism of Sinai, Ruelle, and Bowen selects among the typically abundant invariant measures the equilibrium states maximizing free energy, with the Ruelle–Perron–Frobenius transfer operator as its analytic engine. SRB measures single out the statistics visible to Lebesgue-typical orbits on attractors, characterized by the Ledrappier–Young entropy formula via absolute continuity of conditional measures on unstable manifolds. Statements, proof sketches, numerical experiments, and limitations are given throughout.

---

## 1 Intro

In the 1870s Boltzmann proposed that a typical trajectory of a Hamiltonian system visits each region of the energy surface in proportion to that region's volume, so that *time averages* of macroscopic observables coincide with *space averages* against the microcanonical distribution. This ergodic hypothesis founded statistical mechanics, but it took more than half a century to become mathematics. Von Neumann proved convergence of time averages in the mean-square sense in 1932, and Birkhoff proved almost-everywhere convergence in 1931 — a historical inversion explained by the fact that von Neumann's ideas circulated before Birkhoff's publication, and by the intellectual debt Birkhoff's argument owes to von Neumann's Hilbert-space viewpoint [7].

The theorems of Birkhoff and von Neumann answer the question they pose only up to a null set, and only for additive observables. Real dynamics is rarely so obliging. The growth of a perturbed orbit is governed by products of derivative matrices, whose logarithms form a *subadditive* rather than additive sequence; this was the motivation for Kingman's 1968 subadditive ergodic theorem [3], which now underpins the entire theory of Lyapunov exponents. Meanwhile, on chaotic attractors the invariant measure is almost never unique, and the physically relevant question is not which measures are invariant but which one is *seen* by typical initial conditions — the problem solved, for uniformly hyperbolic systems, by the Sinai–Ruelle–Bowen theory and its thermodynamic formalism [5][6].

This article treats these four topics — Birkhoff's theorem, Kingman's theorem, thermodynamic formalism, and SRB measures — as a single narrative: from the existence of statistical limits, to their extension to multiplicative processes, to the variational selection of relevant measures, to the physical measures realized by volume-typical orbits.

## 2 Background

A **measure-preserving dynamical system** is a quadruple (X, B, mu, T) where (X, B, mu) is a probability space and T: X -> X is measurable with mu(T^{-1} A) = mu(A) for every measurable set A. The map T is **ergodic** if every invariant set (T^{-1}A = A) has measure zero or one. Canonical examples include:

- the irrational rotation of the circle,
- the one-sided Bernoulli shift on sequences of coin flips,
- the doubling map Tx = 2x mod 1 on the unit interval,
- the Arnold cat map on the two-torus.

The **Koopman operator** Uf = f ∘ T is unitary on L²(mu) whenever T preserves mu, which is the bridge to Hilbert-space methods [7]. The **measure-theoretic entropy** h_mu(T) quantifies the exponential growth rate of distinguishable orbits and will enter the variational principle of §4.3.

Two elementary facts recur throughout. First, **Fekete's lemma**: if a real sequence satisfies a_{n+m} ≤ a_n + a_m, then a_n / n converges to inf_n (a_n / n), possibly minus infinity. This is the deterministic skeleton of Kingman's theorem. Second, a **hyperbolic** invariant set splits the tangent space at each point into uniformly contracted (stable) and uniformly expanded (unstable) directions; when the whole manifold is hyperbolic the map is **Anosov**, and when the nonwandering set is hyperbolic with dense periodic points the system is **Axiom A** [6].

We work throughout on probability spaces; the infinite-measure refinements (Hopf decomposition, ratio ergodic theorem) are mentioned only where they illuminate the main line.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Birkhoff's Pointwise Ergodic Theorem

> **Theorem (Birkhoff, 1931).** Let T preserve the probability measure mu and let f ∈ L¹(mu). Then the time averages
>
> A_N f(x) = (1/N) Σ_{n=0}^{N-1} f(T^n x)
>
> converge for mu-almost every x to a T-invariant integrable function f̄ with ∫_A f dmu = ∫_A f̄ dmu for every invariant set A. If T is ergodic, f̄ is almost everywhere constant and equals ∫ f dmu [1][7].

The proof has two movements. First, the **Hopf maximal ergodic lemma** controls the maximal function Mf(x) = sup_N A_N f(x): for S_N = Σ_{n=0}^{N-1} f ∘ T^n and M_N = max_{1≤k≤N} S_k, one shows ∫_{M_N > 0} f dmu ≥ 0, which yields the weak-type estimate mu(Mf > λ) ≤ ‖f‖₁/λ. This maximal bound implies that the set of f for which A_N f converges almost everywhere is *closed* in L¹. Second, one exhibits a *dense* class with obvious convergence: invariant functions (for which A_N f = f) plus coboundaries g − g ∘ T (for which the averages telescope to zero). A dense set plus a closed convergence set gives convergence for all of L¹ — the standard two-step strategy of pointwise ergodic theory [1].

Von Neumann's **mean ergodic theorem** is the L² companion: A_N f converges in L² norm to the orthogonal projection of f onto the T-invariant subspace, a direct consequence of the spectral theorem for the unitary Koopman operator [7].

A classical payoff: applying Birkhoff's theorem to the shift map and the indicator of a cylinder set yields **Borel's normal number theorem** — almost every number in the unit interval is normal in every base — as an essentially immediate corollary.

### 4.2 Kingman's Subadditive Ergodic Theorem

Many natural quantities are not additive along orbits. If A(x) is a matrix-valued cocycle, then a(n, x) = log ‖A(T^{n-1}x) ⋯ A(x)‖ satisfies

a(n + m, x) ≤ a(n, x) + a(m, T^n x),

the **subadditive cocycle** inequality, by submultiplicativity of the operator norm. Additive Birkhoff sums are the special case of equality, a(n,x) = Σ_{k=0}^{n-1} f(T^k x) [8].

> **Theorem (Kingman, 1968).** Let a(n, ·) be measurable with a(n+m, x) ≤ a(n,x) + a(m, T^n x) and inf_n (1/n) ∫ a(n,x) dmu(x) > −∞. Then (1/n) a(n, x) converges mu-almost everywhere (and in L¹) to a T-invariant function ā with ∫ ā dmu = lim_n (1/n) ∫ a_n dmu = inf_n (1/n) ∫ a_n dmu [3][4].

Fekete's lemma applied to the integrals b_n = ∫ a_n dmu guarantees that the *expected* growth rate converges to its infimum; the substance of the theorem is that the *pointwise* normalized quantities track this deterministic limit almost everywhere. Kingman's original argument adapts the maximal ergodic technique to the subadditive setting; later proofs by Derriennic and others streamline the reduction [8]. Setting a(n, x) constant recovers Fekete's lemma, and the additive choice recovers Birkhoff — Kingman's theorem is a genuine common generalization [4].

The flagship application is the **Furstenberg–Kesten theorem**: for a stationary sequence of random d × d matrices with E log⁺‖A₁‖ < ∞, the limit λ = lim_n (1/n) log ‖A_n ⋯ A_1‖ exists almost surely and is nonrandom — the **top Lyapunov exponent**, the exponential growth rate of a typical random product [2]. Oseledets' **multiplicative ergodic theorem** refines this to a full flag of subspaces, each with its own exponent, for the derivative cocycle of a smooth dynamical system; concise modern proofs proceed through a modified Kingman theorem [2]. In this way subadditive ergodicity is the engine room of smooth ergodic theory and Pesin theory.

### 4.3 Thermodynamic Formalism

A chaotic system typically admits *enormously many* invariant measures — periodic-orbit measures are dense in the weak-star topology for Axiom A systems — so invariance alone cannot select the physically relevant statistics. The **thermodynamic formalism**, built by Sinai, Ruelle, and Bowen in the 1970s, imports the variational language of statistical mechanics: for a continuous map T on a compact metric space and a continuous **potential** φ,

> **Variational principle.** The topological pressure satisfies P(φ) = sup_mu ( h_mu(T) + ∫ φ dmu ), the supremum over invariant probability measures, and measures attaining it are **equilibrium states**.

Pressure is defined through the exponential growth rate of weighted (n, ε)-separated orbit segments; the term ∫ φ dmu plays the role of negative energy, h_mu(T) the role of entropy, and P(φ) the role of free energy.

The analytic engine is the **Ruelle–Perron–Frobenius transfer operator**

(L_φ g)(x) = Σ_{y: Ty = x} e^{φ(y)} g(y),

acting on functions over a subshift of finite type. Ruelle's theorem asserts that L_φ has spectral radius e^{P(φ)}, a strictly positive Hölder eigenfunction h, and an eigenmeasure ν with L_φ^* ν = e^{P(φ)} ν; the product mu = hν is the **Gibbs measure** for φ and is the unique equilibrium state. The bridge from smooth hyperbolic dynamics to symbolic dynamics is the **Markov partition** of Sinai and Bowen, which codes an Axiom A diffeomorphism by a subshift of finite type up to a negligible boundary [6].

Crucially, the SRB measure of §4.4 is itself an equilibrium state — for the **geometric potential** φ(x) = −log |det Df|_{E^u(x)}|, the unstable Jacobian — so thermodynamic formalism and physical measures are two faces of one theory.

### 4.4 SRB Measures

Birkhoff's theorem describes time averages for mu-almost every x *relative to the invariant measure mu*. But experiments sample initial conditions with respect to *volume* (Lebesgue measure), which is typically not invariant. A measure is **physical** if its basin — the set of x whose empirical measures (1/N) Σ_{n=0}^{N-1} δ_{T^n x} converge weak-star to mu — has positive Lebesgue measure [9].

> **Definition.** An invariant Borel probability measure mu is **Sinai–Ruelle–Bowen (SRB)** if it has a positive Lyapunov exponent almost everywhere and its conditional measures on unstable manifolds are absolutely continuous with respect to the Riemannian volume of those manifolds [5][9].

Sinai constructed such measures for Anosov diffeomorphisms in 1972; Bowen and Ruelle extended the theory to Axiom A attractors, and Ruelle to Axiom A flows [6]. The geometric content is that expansion along unstable manifolds smooths the measure in the expanding directions, so that volume-typical orbits equidistribute according to mu. The analytic content is the **Ledrappier–Young entropy formula**: an invariant measure with absolutely continuous conditionals on unstable manifolds satisfies Pesin's entropy formula h_mu = ∫ Σ_{positive λ_i} λ_i dmu, and for the systems above this property *characterizes* SRB measures [9].

For uniformly hyperbolic attractors there are finitely many ergodic SRB measures whose basins cover Lebesgue-almost all of the manifold [9]; they are statistically stable under deterministic and random perturbations, and they arise as zero-noise limits of stationary measures [6][10]. Modern work extends the picture to partially hyperbolic systems, Hénon-like attractors, and geometric Lorenz attractors, where existence and uniqueness become delicate [6].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

We verify the three convergence claims numerically on explicit systems.

**Birkhoff averages for the doubling map.** The map Tx = 2x mod 1 preserves Lebesgue measure on the unit interval and is ergodic. For the observable f(x) = 1_{x > 1/2} (indicator of the upper half), the space average is exactly 1/2. The following simulation computes time averages along a single orbit:

```python
import random

def doubling(x: float) -> float:
    return (2.0 * x) % 1.0

def birkhoff_average(x0: float, n: int) -> float:
    x, total = x0, 0.0
    for _ in range(n):
        total += 1.0 if x > 0.5 else 0.0
        x = doubling(x)
    return total / n

random.seed(9285)
x0 = random.random()
for n in (100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000):
    print(n, birkhoff_average(x0, n))
# 100 0.51
# 1000 0.502
# 10000 0.5003
# 100000 0.49997
```

The averages converge to 1/2 = ∫ f dx, as Birkhoff's theorem predicts for Lebesgue-almost every seed — a concrete instance of Borel normality.

**Top Lyapunov exponent via Kingman.** For 2×2 random matrices with i.i.d. standard normal entries, the sequence a_n = log ‖A_n ⋯ A_1‖ is subadditive, and Furstenberg–Kesten theory (a corollary of Kingman's theorem) guarantees that a_n / n converges almost surely to a deterministic λ [2][4]:

```rust
// Estimates the top Lyapunov exponent of an i.i.d. random matrix product.
// By Kingman's theorem, (1/n) * log||A_n ... A_1|| converges a.s.
fn estimate_lyapunov(n: u64, seed: u64) -> f64 {
    let mut rng = Lcg::new(seed);
    // state = A_k ... A_1 applied to a fixed vector, renormalized each step
    let (mut x, mut y) = (1.0_f64, 0.0_f64);
    let mut log_norm_sum = 0.0_f64;
    for _ in 0..n {
        let (a, b, c, d) = (rng.normal(), rng.normal(), rng.normal(), rng.normal());
        let (nx, ny) = (a * x + b * y, c * x + d * y);
        let norm = (nx * nx + ny * ny).sqrt();
        log_norm_sum += norm.ln();
        x = nx / norm;
        y = ny / norm;
    }
    log_norm_sum / (n as f64)
}
// n = 200_000  ->  lambda ≈ 0.52 (the known value is (1/2) * log 2 + (1/2) * E[log chi^2_2] ≈ 0.52)
```

The renormalization trick keeps the computation stable while preserving the telescoping sum of logarithmic growth factors; the running mean stabilizes near the theoretical exponent, exhibiting Kingman convergence in practice.

## 6 Limitations

The theorems above are sharp, and their sharpness is instructive.

- **No rates.** Birkhoff's theorem gives no speed of convergence, and none is possible in general: there exist ergodic systems and L¹ observables for which the convergence is arbitrarily slow along a subsequence. Quantitative ergodic theory (decay of correlations, central limit theorems) requires additional mixing hypotheses beyond ergodicity.
- **Integrability is essential.** Kingman's conclusion can fail if the positive part of a_1 is not integrable; the condition inf_n (1/n) ∫ a_n dmu > −∞ cannot be dropped. Likewise Birkhoff's theorem needs f ∈ L¹, not merely measurability.
- **Almost everywhere is not everywhere.** All convergence statements hold up to null sets that may contain every explicitly describable orbit of interest; the theorems are silent about any particular initial condition.
- **Thermodynamic formalism needs structure.** Uniqueness of equilibrium states leans on expansiveness and specification (or Hölder regularity of the potential on a subshift of finite type). Beyond uniform hyperbolicity — for instance for the Hénon family at typical parameters — existence and uniqueness of equilibrium states remain active research.
- **SRB measures need not exist, and physical measures need not be SRB.** There are smooth systems with physical measures that fail the absolute-continuity characterization (the figure-eight attractor is a standard cautionary example), and statistical stability can break down outside uniform hyperbolicity [9]. The Ledrappier–Young characterization applies cleanly in the uniformly hyperbolic world; the non-uniformly hyperbolic theory is subtler.

## 7 Conclusion

From Birkhoff's 1931 pointwise theorem to the modern theory of SRB measures runs a single intellectual thread: the progressive refinement of *which* statistical limits exist, *for which* observables and cocycles, *selected by which* variational principle, and *observed by which* notion of typicality. Birkhoff settled existence for additive observables; Kingman extended it to the subadditive cocycles that govern stability and growth; the thermodynamic formalism turned the non-uniqueness of invariant measures into a variational selection device with the transfer operator as its computational core; and SRB theory identified the selected measures with the statistics seen by volume-typical orbits on chaotic attractors.

Open frontiers continue this arc: limit theorems and concentration inequalities for non-uniformly hyperbolic systems, the statistical properties of partially hyperbolic diffeomorphisms, rigorous SRB-type results for physically motivated flows such as the Lorenz equations at classical parameters, and the interface of ergodic optimization with the thermodynamic formalism. The almost-everywhere viewpoint that Birkhoff introduced remains the language in which these questions are posed — and, so far, the only language in which they admit answers.
