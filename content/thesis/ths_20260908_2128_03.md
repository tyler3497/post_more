# Byzantine-Robust Federated Learning: Threat Models, Robust Aggregation Rules, Certified Defenses, and the Adaptive-Attack Frontier

## Abstract

Federated learning (FL) trains a global model from gradients computed on mutually distrustful clients, exposing the aggregation step to *Byzantine* failures: compromised clients may submit arbitrary vectors to derail convergence or implant backdoors. This thesis surveys the modern defensive stack for Byzantine-robust FL. We formalize threat models spanning data poisoning, model poisoning, and targeted backdoor attacks, and give precise definitions of robust aggregation, including $(\alpha, f)$-Byzantine resilience. We analyze the classical selection-based rules (Krum, Multi-Krum, Bulyan), coordinate-wise statistics (trimmed mean, median, geometric median), and norm-clipping-based certified defenses, quantifying breakdown points, per-round complexity, and statistical error rates. We then study the attacker side: model-replacement backdoors, optimization-driven local model poisoning of the Fang style, and adaptive adversaries that optimize directly against a known aggregator. Finally, we examine the tension between privacy mechanisms — secure aggregation and differential privacy — and robustness, closing with empirical evaluations, impossibility results, and open problems.

## 1. Introduction

Federated learning (FL) promises to train high-capacity models from data that never leaves the user's device. In the standard parameter-server architecture, each round begins with the server broadcasting the current global model $w_t$; a cohort of clients computes local updates (gradients or model deltas) on their private data and returns them; the server aggregates these vectors into a new model $w_{t+1}$. This design is intrinsically vulnerable: the server, *by design*, has no visibility into how client updates are generated [6]. A compromised client may therefore submit an *arbitrary* vector — a Byzantine failure in the sense of distributed systems — with consequences ranging from degraded accuracy to complete model hijack.

The naive rule, *Federated Averaging* (FedAvg), computes the arithmetic mean — catastrophically non-robust: one Byzantine client can dominate the mean and steer the model arbitrarily. Blanchard et al. [1] proved that *no* linear-combination rule tolerates even a single Byzantine failure, motivating a principled theory of *Byzantine-robust aggregation*.

The field has since developed three complementary lines of defense:

1. **Robust aggregation rules** — selection rules such as Krum [1] and Bulyan [3], coordinate-wise trimmed statistics [2], and the geometric median — replacing the mean with nonlinear statistics.
2. **Certified and bounded-influence defenses** that clip update norms and bound per-client influence.
3. **Privacy-preserving aggregation** via secure multiparty computation [4], which shields individual updates — and thereby conflicts with the inspection robustness requires.

Yet each defensive advance has been met by an adaptive offensive advance. The Bulyan paper showed that provable *convergence* is insufficient: in high dimension, an adversary retains an $\Omega(\sqrt{d})$ *leeway* that suffices to steer the model to ineffective minima [3]. Fang et al. [5] formulated *local model poisoning* as an optimization problem and broke aggregation rules previously claimed robust; Bagdasaryan et al. [6] showed that a single malicious participant, selected only once, can implant a backdoor with 100% attack accuracy via model replacement, while evading anomaly detection.

This thesis contributes a unified treatment of this co-evolution: §2 formalizes threat models and resilience definitions; §3 taxonomizes aggregation rules; §4 deep-dives into threats, rules, adaptive attacks, and privacy interplay; §5 evaluates and proves; §6 maps limitations and open problems; §7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The Distributed Learning Model

We adopt the parameter-server model of [1]: $n$ workers, at most $f$ Byzantine. Each honest worker $i$ submits $g_t^{(i)} \sim G_t$ with $\mathbb{E}[G_t] = \nabla Q(w_t)$; Byzantine workers submit *arbitrary* vectors, potentially coordinated by an omniscient adversary observing all honest updates. The server applies $F: (\mathbb{R}^d)^n \to \mathbb{R}^d$ and updates

$$w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta_t \, F(g_t^{(1)}, \dots, g_t^{(n)}).$$

The fundamental pathology of linear aggregation is captured by the following impossibility result:

> **Theorem: (Impossibility of Linear Aggregation [1]).** Let $F_{\text{lin}}(v_1,\dots,v_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i v_i$ with all $\lambda_i \neq 0$. For any target vector $U \in \mathbb{R}^d$, a single Byzantine worker can force $F_{\text{lin}} = U$ by submitting $v_n = \tfrac{1}{\lambda_n}U - \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \tfrac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_n} v_i$.

Hence the mean is out; only *nonlinear* rules stand a chance. The standard positive definition is:

> **Theorem: ($(\alpha, f)$-Byzantine Resilience [1]).** A rule $F$ is $(\alpha, f)$-Byzantine resilient for $\alpha \in [0, \pi/2]$ if, for any $f$ Byzantine inputs, (i) $\langle \mathbb{E}[F], \nabla Q(w_t) \rangle \ge (1 - \sin\alpha)\,\|\nabla Q(w_t)\|^2 > 0$, and (ii) the moments $\mathbb{E}[\|F\|^r]$ for $r \in \{2,3,4\}$ are bounded by a linear combination of the moments of $\|G_t\|$.

Condition (i) guarantees the aggregated step remains a *descent* direction in expectation; condition (ii) controls the stochastic noise so SGD analysis carries through.

### 2.2 Threat Models

We distinguish three attack surfaces, increasing in attacker power:

- **Data poisoning.** The attacker corrupts or fabricates training samples on compromised clients; the poisoned data induces a poisoned local update through honest local training. Limited power, but cheap and hard to attribute.
- **Model poisoning.** The attacker bypasses training and submits *crafted* update vectors directly. Because a client in FL controls its entire local computation, it can optimize its submission against the known aggregation rule [5].
- **Backdoor attacks.** A targeted form of model poisoning: the attacker seeks a model that behaves normally on clean inputs but outputs an attacker-chosen label on inputs containing a trigger pattern. Bagdasaryan et al. [6] introduced *model replacement* with a *constrain-and-scale* objective, and showed a single attacker, selected in a single round, can reach 100% backdoor accuracy.

Attacks are **targeted** (backdoors preserving main-task accuracy) or **untargeted** (maximizing global error). Classical rules were evaluated against untargeted, non-adaptive attacks — the mismatch with adaptive adversaries is the subject of §4.4.

### 2.3 Statistical Robustness

Yin et al. [2] recast the problem as *robust mean estimation in high dimension*: with $m$ workers, $n$ samples each, and Byzantine fraction $\alpha$, what is the minimax statistical error? Their coordinate-wise median and trimmed-mean gradient-descent rules achieve order-optimal rates for strongly convex losses.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is comparative and adversarial: we taxonomize aggregation rules along four axes — **breakdown point** (maximum tolerable Byzantine fraction), **computational complexity**, **statistical rate**, and **adversarial assumptions** (non-adaptive vs. adaptive). Table 1 (see §5) summarizes the quantitative comparison.

### 3.1 Selection-Based Rules

Selection rules choose one or a few submitted vectors and discard the rest:

- **Krum** [1]: select $\arg\min_i KR(i)$ with $KR(i) = \sum_{i \to j} \|v_i - v_j\|^2$ over the $n - f - 2$ nearest neighbors of $v_i$. Cost $O(n^2(d + \log n))$; requires $2f+2 < n$; provably $(\alpha, f)$-resilient.
- **Multi-Krum**: select the $m$ vectors with smallest Krum scores and average them — interpolating between Krum ($m=1$) and FedAvg ($m=n$).
- **Bulyan** [3]: a meta-rule. Iteratively apply a base rule (e.g., Krum) to select $\theta = n - 2f$ vectors, then per coordinate average the $2f$ values closest to the median (coordinate-wise trimmed mean). Requires $n \ge 4f + 3$.

### 3.2 Coordinate-Wise Statistical Rules

Coordinate-wise rules aggregate each dimension independently [2]:

- **Coordinate-wise median**: $j$-th output coordinate is $\text{median}(v_1[j], \dots, v_n[j])$.
- **Coordinate-wise trimmed mean**: sort each coordinate, discard the $\beta$-fraction smallest and largest, average the rest.
- **Geometric median**: $\arg\min_y \sum_{i=1}^n \|v_i - y\|_2$, the $L_1$ minimizer over vectors (not coordinate-wise). Solved approximately via Weiszfeld's algorithm.

### 3.3 Bounded-Influence and Certified Defenses

Norm clipping, $\text{clip}(v) = v \cdot \min(1, \tau/\|v\|_2)$, bounds each client's influence to $\tau$ — the shared substrate of differentially private SGD and defenses like FLTrust, which additionally bootstraps *trust* from a small server-held clean dataset. Certified defenses bound worst-case damage under restricted attack budgets.

### 3.4 Evaluation Protocol

We evaluate defenses against *adaptive* adversaries [5]: the Fang-style methodology (§4.4) maximizes deviation of the aggregated output from the benign aggregate, subject to evading the rule's selection criterion.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Adversarial Landscape: From Data to Backdoors

The attacker's power in FL is *asymmetric*: an honest client computes $\Delta_i = \text{SGD}(w_t, D_i)$, but a compromised client may return *any* function of $w_t$, the honest cohort's updates (under the omniscient threat model), and its malicious objective. Three canonical attack families illustrate the escalation:

1. **Untargeted data poisoning** (weakest): label-flipping or sample injection; the poisoned update stays within the reachable set of honest training and is largely absorbed by robust aggregators.
2. **Untargeted model poisoning**: the attacker bypasses training and submits *crafted* vectors, optimizing them against the known aggregation rule [5].
3. **Backdoor model replacement** (strongest targeted attack) [6]: the attacker trains a backdoored model on poisoned data with a *constrain-and-scale* loss that simultaneously optimizes the backdoor and evades anomaly detection, then submits a scaled update that *replaces* the global model in a single FedAvg round. The crucial insight of [6] is that secure aggregation — designed for confidentiality — simultaneously *blinds* the server to anomaly detection.

> **Theorem: (Backdoor Replacement Power [6]).** Under FedAvg with secure aggregation, a single attacker selected once suffices to install a backdoor with $\approx 100\%$ attack accuracy while preserving main-task accuracy, because the server observes only the final sum.

### 4.2 Selection Rules: Krum, Multi-Krum, and Bulyan

Krum selects the vector minimizing the sum of squared distances to its nearest neighbors — a *majority-vote-by-proximity* heuristic. When honest gradients concentrate, Byzantine vectors must either hide inside the honest cluster (limiting damage) or stand out and be ignored.

The fragility, however, is high-dimensional. El Mhamdi et al. [3] proved that Krum, geometric-median, and Brute selection leave an attacker *leeway* of $\Omega(\sqrt{d})$: a coordinated adversary can add a per-coordinate perturbation of order $1$ to *each* of $d$ coordinates — each too small to trigger detection — whose *total* Euclidean displacement $\sqrt{d}$ suffices to trap SGD in ineffective basins of the non-convex loss. Convergence theorems are therefore necessary but not sufficient; what matters is the *attacker's residual influence*.

Bulyan answers by composing selection with coordinate-wise trimming:

```
Bulyan(v_1..v_n, f):
    S = ∅
    repeat (n − 2f) times:  S ← S ∪ {Krum-select among remaining}
    for each coordinate j:
        m_j = median { v_i[j] : i ∈ S }
        take 2f values of {v_i[j]} closest to m_j
        output[j] = mean of those 2f values
```

Bulyan collapses the leeway to $O(1/\sqrt{d})$ [3] and matches clean-data convergence on MNIST and CIFAR-10 under attack — at the price of $n \ge 4f+3$, $O(n^2 d)$ cost, and remaining vulnerability to *adaptive* attacks tuned against Bulyan itself [5].

### 4.3 Coordinate-Wise Statistics: Trimmed Mean, Median, Geometric Median

Yin et al. [2] established statistical optimality. Key results for strongly convex losses, with $m$ workers, $n$ samples each, and Byzantine fraction $\alpha$:

> **Theorem: (Optimal Rates [2]).** Coordinate-wise trimmed-mean gradient descent achieves statistical error $\tilde{O}\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{n}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{nm}}\right)$, which is *order-optimal*: no algorithm can do better. Coordinate-wise median achieves $\tilde{O}\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{n}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{nm}} + \frac{1}{n}\right)$ under weaker (non-sub-exponential) assumptions.

The implementation is strikingly simple:

```python
import numpy as np

def coordinate_trimmed_mean(updates, beta):
    """updates: (n, d) array of client vectors; beta: trim fraction."""
    stacked = np.stack(updates)            # shape (n, d)
    k = int(beta * stacked.shape[0])
    ordered = np.sort(stacked, axis=0)     # per-coordinate sort
    trimmed = ordered[k:stacked.shape[0] - k]
    return trimmed.mean(axis=0)

def coordinate_median(updates):
    return np.median(np.stack(updates), axis=0)
```

```haskell
-- Coordinate-wise median over client update vectors
coordMedian :: [[Double]] -> [Double]
coordMedian vecs = map median (transpose vecs)
  where
    median xs = let s = sort xs; n = length s
                in (s !! (n `div` 2) + s !! ((n-1) `div` 2)) / 2
```

Norm clipping in Rust:

```rust
/// Clip a gradient to Euclidean norm at most `tau`, in place.
pub fn clip_l2(grad: &mut [f32], tau: f32) {
    let norm: f32 = grad.iter().map(|x| x * x).sum::<f32>().sqrt();
    if norm > tau {
        let scale = tau / norm;
        grad.iter_mut().for_each(|x| *x *= scale);
    }
}
```

Coordinate-wise rules enjoy breakdown approaching $1/2$ at $O(nd \log n)$ cost, but discard *vector* structure: every coordinate may lie within the honest range while the *joint* direction is adversarial — the per-coordinate disagreement attack of [3]. The geometric median respects vector structure but inherits $\Omega(\sqrt{d})$ leeway [3].

### 4.4 Fang-Style Adaptive Attacks: Optimizing Against the Aggregator

Fang et al. [5] treated attack design as *constrained optimization against a known defense*. Under the omniscient model, the attacker observes honest updates $\{v_i\}_{i \in \mathcal{H}}$ and crafts $f$ Byzantine vectors $\{b_j\}$ maximizing

$$\max_{\{b_j\}} \; \big\|F(\{v_i\} \cup \{b_j\}) - \bar{v}_{\mathcal{H}}\big\|_2
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad \text{evasion constraints of } F,$$

where $\bar{v}_{\mathcal{H}}$ is the benign mean. For Krum, the evasion constraint is that a crafted vector be *selected* — i.e., lie among the dense cluster while pulling the selection toward the attacker's target direction $\mathbf{s} = -\bar{v}_{\mathcal{H}}$ (the *inverse* of the benign update direction). Fang et al. instantiate this per rule:

- **Against Krum/Multi-Krum**: place Byzantine vectors at $\bar{v}_{\mathcal{H}} - \lambda \mathbf{s}$ for a tuned $\lambda$, balancing detectability (distance to honest vectors) against influence.
- **Against trimmed mean/median**: perturb each coordinate by the maximum the trim tolerates — the optimal deviation follows in closed form from the order statistics.
- **Against Bulyan**: attack the two stages separately, exploiting that the trimmed-mean stage inherits coordinate-wise weaknesses.

Empirically, the optimized attacks raised test error rates of Krum, Bulyan, trimmed mean, and median to near undefended levels [5]; generalized data-poisoning defenses helped only partially.

The deeper lesson is *methodological*: evaluating a robust aggregator against fixed, non-adaptive attacks measures only the attacker's laziness. The correct evaluation is a *Stackelberg game* — the defender commits to $F$, the attacker best-responds — which Fang et al. first instantiated systematically for FL, shifting the frontier toward certified defenses with *provable* worst-case bounds.

### 4.5 Privacy Interplay: Secure Aggregation, Clipping, and Certified Defense

Secure aggregation (SecAgg) [4] is the canonical FL privacy mechanism: clients mask updates with pairwise random seeds from Diffie–Hellman key exchange, so the server learns only the *sum*. It is communication-efficient ($1.73\times$ expansion for $2^{10}$ users and $2^{20}$-dimensional vectors) and dropout-tolerant, with security proven in honest-but-curious and malicious models [4].

But SecAgg creates a *fundamental tension* with robustness:

| Capability | Secure Aggregation | Robust Aggregation |
|---|---|---|
| Server sees individual updates | No (only sums) | Required |
| Anomaly / backdoor detection | Impossible by design | Core mechanism |
| Dropout tolerance | Yes (threshold $t$-of-$n$) | Not applicable |
| Byzantine resilience | None (mean under the masks) | Up to breakdown point |

Bagdasaryan et al. [6] weaponize this tension: because SecAgg hides individual contributions, the attacker embeds defense-evasion in the local training loss with impunity. Robust rules therefore *require* abandoning full secure aggregation, or restricting it to a shuffler model that hides identities but exposes magnitudes.

A TLA+ specification of a single SecAgg round exposes the masking structure that robustness must pierce:

```tla
---- MODULE SecAggRound ----
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Clients, Prime
VARIABLES masked, dropped
Init == masked = [c \in Clients |-> 0] /\ dropped = {}
Round(c, x, maskIn, maskOut) ==
    /\ c \notin dropped
    /\ masked' = [masked EXCEPT ![c] = (x + maskIn - maskOut) % Prime]
Unmask ==  \* masks cancel pairwise: server learns only the sum
    Sum({masked[c] : c \in Clients \ dropped}) % Prime = Sum({x[c] : c \in Clients \ dropped}) % Prime
====
```

Norm clipping bridges the two worlds: applied *client-side before masking*, it bounds each contribution to $\tau$ without revealing the vector, and underpins differentially private FL (clip-then-add-noise) and certified defenses. The emerging consensus is a *layered* architecture: clipping $\to$ (partial) secure aggregation $\to$ robust statistics on the revealed aggregate $\to$ DP noise.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Comparative Table

| Rule | Breakdown point | Per-round cost | Statistical error (strongly convex) | Byzantine model |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FedAvg (mean) | 0 | $O(nd)$ | Optimal (no adversary) | None |
| Krum [1] | $< n/2 - 1$ | $O(n^2(d + \log n))$ | Convergent (non-convex SGD) | Non-adaptive, $2f+2<n$ |
| Multi-Krum [1] | $< n/2 - 1$ | $O(n^2(d + \log n))$ | Convergent | Non-adaptive |
| Bulyan [3] | $\le (n-3)/4$ | $O(n^2 d)$ | Convergent; leeway $O(1/\sqrt{d})$ | Non-adaptive |
| Coord. trimmed mean [2] | $< 1/2$ | $O(nd \log n)$ | $\tilde{O}(\alpha/\sqrt{n} + 1/\sqrt{nm})$ (optimal) | Non-adaptive |
| Coord. median [2] | $< 1/2$ | $O(nd \log n)$ | $\tilde{O}(\alpha/\sqrt{n} + 1/\sqrt{nm} + 1/n)$ | Non-adaptive |
| Geometric median | $< 1/2$ | $O(nd \log(1/\epsilon))$ (Weiszfeld) | Convergent; leeway $\Omega(\sqrt{d})$ [3] | Non-adaptive |
| Clipping + DP | Tunable via $\tau$ | $O(nd)$ | DP-utility tradeoff | Bounded influence |

### 5.2 Key Theoretical Results

> **Theorem: (Krum is $(\alpha,f)$-resilient [1]).** If $2f+2 < n$ and honest gradients satisfy standard moment bounds, Krum is $(\alpha, f)$-Byzantine resilient for some $\alpha < \pi/2$, and distributed SGD converges almost surely.

> **Theorem: (Lower bound [2]).** For strongly convex losses, *no* aggregation rule achieves statistical error below $\tilde{\Omega}(\alpha/\sqrt{n} + 1/\sqrt{nm})$ under regular conditions — the trimmed-mean rate of §4.3 is therefore unimprovable.

> **Theorem: (Leeway collapse under Bulyan [3]).** While Krum and geometric-median leave an attacker leeway of $\Omega(\sqrt{d})$, Bulyan reduces it to $O(1/\sqrt{d})$, at the cost of requiring $n \ge 4f+3$.

### 5.3 Empirical Findings from the Literature

- **Bulyan under the $\Omega(\sqrt{d})$ attack [3]**: on MNIST ($d \approx 8\times10^4$) and CIFAR-10, Krum, geometric median, and Brute collapse to near-random accuracy within 50 epochs under the coordinated attack; Bulyan tracks the no-attack baseline.
- **Fang attacks [5]**: optimized local model poisoning raises test error on four datasets for Krum, Bulyan, trimmed mean, and median to near undefended levels; generalized data-poisoning defenses help only partially.
- **Backdoor model replacement [6]**: a single attacker selected once achieves $\approx 100\%$ backdoor accuracy on CIFAR-10 and Reddit next-word prediction, preserving main-task accuracy and evading anomaly detection via constrain-and-scale.
- **Secure aggregation overhead [4]**: $1.73\times$–$1.98\times$ communication expansion at $2^{10}$–$2^{14}$ users — cheap enough that privacy, not cost, binds the SecAgg–robustness combination.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1. **Adaptive attacks vs. certified guarantees.** Every classical rule falls to an attacker that optimizes against it [5]; certified defenses bound damage only under *restricted* budgets, and no practical rule certifies robustness against an *unrestricted* omniscient adversary in non-convex deep learning.

2. **The non-IID confound.** In cross-device FL, honest updates are already heterogeneous: the distance between an honest-but-skewed client and a Byzantine client can be smaller than between two honest clients, and no current theory cleanly separates the two.

3. **Privacy–robustness incompatibility.** Secure aggregation [4] and robust aggregation are architecturally opposed: one hides individual updates, the other inspects them. Deployments must choose a point on this tradeoff; per-client clipping before masking is a partial bridge, but fine-grained robust statistics remain incompatible with full SecAgg.

4. **Breakdown-point economics.** Bulyan's $n \ge 4f+3$ caps the Byzantine fraction near 25%; in open cross-device cohorts the *effective* Byzantine fraction is unknown and possibly unbounded, voiding fixed-$f$ guarantees.

5. **Backdoor persistence.** Backdoors implanted via model replacement [6] persist across many honest rounds; "unlearning" them without a trusted clean dataset is unsolved, and FLTrust-style defenses require precisely such a dataset.

6. **Evaluation methodology.** The field lacks a standardized adaptive-attack benchmark; until defenses are evaluated against best-response attackers, claims of "Byzantine robustness" will overstate real security.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Byzantine-robust federated learning has matured from the impossibility of linear aggregation [1] through selection rules (Krum, Bulyan [3]) and optimal coordinate-wise statistics [2] to the sobering realization that adaptive attackers [5] and single-shot backdoors [6] defeat every fixed defense. The mathematical core is now clear: robustness is *robust mean estimation under an adversarial minority*, with an unavoidable $\tilde{\Omega}(\alpha/\sqrt{n} + 1/\sqrt{nm})$ price [2], $\Omega(\sqrt{d})$ high-dimensional leeway for naive rules [3], and a structural conflict between update-hiding privacy and inspection-based security [4][6]. The frontier is therefore not another aggregator but *certified, composable defenses* — clipping, trust bootstrapping, differential privacy — evaluated against the strongest adaptive adversary the theory can describe. Convergence was the first milestone; it is no longer the goal. The goal is a learning protocol whose worst case is *proven*, not merely hoped for.
