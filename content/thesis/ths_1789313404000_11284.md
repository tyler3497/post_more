# From Paxos to Raft: A Unified Theory of Distributed Consensus, Impossibility, and Machine-Checked Correctness

## Abstract

Distributed consensus — getting a set of unreliable processes to agree on a single value — is the fundamental primitive beneath replicated state machines, fault-tolerant databases, and modern coordination services. This thesis develops a unified account of the major algorithmic responses to that impossibility. We reconstruct Lamport's Paxos as a ballot-numbered, quorum-intersection protocol with rigorous phase structure [1], derive Multi-Paxos and its stable-leader optimization, contrast Ongaro and Ousterhout's Raft with its randomized leader election and log-matching invariants [2], and analyze Egalitarian Paxos (EPaxos), the first leaderless protocol to commit commands in a single wide-area round trip while balancing load uniformly [4]. We then formalize the boundary itself: Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson's bivalency argument [3], and the Chandra–Toueg failure-detector hierarchy culminating in the weakest detector sufficient for consensus [5][6]. Finally, we survey machine-checked verification of these protocols in TLA+ and TLAPS, including mechanically verified Multi-Paxos and automatically inferred inductive invariants [7][8][9], and we characterize the enduring liveness-versus-safety tradeoffs these systems embody.

## 1 Introduction

Consensus is the agreement problem from which nearly all of fault-tolerant distributed computing derives. A set of *processes* communicates over unreliable networks, processes may fail by *crashing* (halting without warning), and the survivors must eventually agree on exactly one value — the "chosen" decree, the next log entry, the new configuration. The required properties are conventionally split:

- **Safety**: (i) only a proposed value may be chosen, (ii) at most one value is ever chosen, and (iii) a process learns a value only if it was genuinely chosen.
- **Liveness**: every correct process eventually decides (or learns) some value.

This thesis makes three contributions. First, it gives a *comparative mechanics* of the four landmark protocol families — single-decree Paxos, Multi-Paxos, Raft, and EPaxos — on common ground, exposing how each rebalances quorum size, leadership, and ordering discipline. Second, it states the *limits* precisely: the FLP impossibility theorem and the failure-detector hierarchy that turns "enough synchrony" into a measurable commodity. Third, it examines *machine-checked certainty*: how TLA+ specifications, the TLC model checker, and TLAPS-checked proofs have moved these notoriously error-prone algorithms into the realm of verified software [7][9].

> **Theorem:** (Informal consensus specification). Any correct consensus protocol must satisfy *validity*, *agreement*, and *termination*; in asynchronous systems with crash failures, termination cannot be guaranteed deterministically for all runs [3].

The rest of the thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the asynchronous crash-failure model and the state-machine-replication reduction that makes consensus the load-bearing primitive. Section 3 describes our methodology: comparative protocol reconstruction plus verification evidence. Section 4 is the deep dive — Paxos, Multi-Paxos, Raft, EPaxos, and the FLP/failure-detector boundary. Section 5 presents the machine-checked verification results. Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclude.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 System model

We adopt the standard *asynchronous, message-passing, crash-failure* model [3][5]:

1. A finite set of *n* deterministic processes, pairwise connected by reliable (non-duplicating, eventually-delivered, uncorrupted) channels with **no upper bound on delay**.
2. No synchronized clocks and no bound on the relative speed of processes.
3. Processes fail only by **crashing**; a crashed process performs no further steps, and failures are permanent.
4. At most *f* processes crash, and we require *n ≥ 2f + 1* so that a **majority quorum** of correct processes always exists.

The crucial operational fact is that in this model a crashed process is *indistinguishable from a slow one* — any observation is consistent with the process being arbitrarily delayed. This single indistinguishability drives both the impossibility result and the failure-detector research program.

### 2.2 State machine replication

Consensus matters because it reduces to — and is reduced by — *state machine replication* (SMR): if every replica starts from the same state and applies the same totally ordered log of commands, all replicas remain consistent despite failures. Paxos chooses a single value; **Multi-Paxos** and **Raft** choose an infinite sequence of log entries by running the single-value protocol per slot [1][2].

### 2.3 Quorums: the algebraic heart

A *quorum system* is a family of subsets of processes such that any two quorums **intersect**. Majority quorums — any ⌊n/2⌋ + 1 processes — are the canonical example. Quorum intersection is the algebraic engine of every protocol below: if value *v* was *chosen* (accepted by some quorum Q₁), then any later quorum Q₂ shares at least one member with Q₁, so any later protocol phase that consults Q₂ cannot escape knowledge of *v*.

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a *synthetic reconstruction* rather than an experiment report. Its method has three legs:

1. **Comparative protocol analysis.** We restate Paxos, Multi-Paxos, Raft, and EPaxos in uniform notation — ballot/term numbers, prepare/request-vote phases, accept/append phases, quorum rules — and compare them on latency (round trips per commit), leadership structure, ordering discipline, and failure handling.
2. **Formal verification survey.** We summarize the TLA+ specification tradition for consensus, the TLC explicit-state model checker for finding counterexamples in small instances, and TLAPS, the proof system that *machine-checks* human-written proofs of safety [7][8]. We report verification scales: lines of specification, proof size, and checking time.
3. **Impossibility and hierarchy analysis.** We sketch the FLP bivalency argument [3] and the Chandra–Toueg taxonomy of unreliable failure detectors along two axes — *completeness* (every crashed process is eventually suspected) and *accuracy* (correct processes are not wrongly suspected) — culminating in the weakest detector for consensus [5][6][11].

Facts, dates, and attributions were cross-checked against the primary sources cited throughout.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Single-Decree Paxos: the anatomy of a ballot

Lamport's 1998 "The Part-Time Parliament" introduced Paxos through the allegory of legislators wandering in and out of the chamber [1]. Stripped of metaphor, **single-decree (Basic) Paxos** chooses one value among roles called *proposers*, *acceptors*, and *learners*. Each proposer attempt is a *ballot* with a unique, totally ordered ballot number. A ballot has two phases:

- **Phase 1 (Prepare/Promise).** The proposer sends `Prepare(b)` to a majority; each acceptor that has not promised a higher ballot promises not to accept ballots below *b* and returns the highest-numbered value it has already accepted (if any).
- **Phase 2 (Accept/Accepted).** The proposer must propose the value returned in Phase 1 (or any value if none was returned), sending `Accept(b, v)` to a majority. An acceptor accepts unless it has promised a higher ballot. When a majority accepts, *v* is *chosen*.

Two constraints make this safe. First, **B1/B2**: ballot numbers are unique, and quorums intersect [1]. Second, the *inheritance rule*: a proposer may only propose the value carried by the highest accepted ballot it saw in Phase 1. Suppose *v* is chosen at ballot *b* via quorum Q₁. Any later ballot *b′ > b* consults a quorum Q₂ that intersects Q₁, so it hears about *v* from some acceptor that accepted it, and inheritance forces it to re-propose *v*.

The protocol's notorious subtlety is that *safety is unconditional* (it holds even during network partitions), while *liveness* requires a distinguished proposer and a sufficiently long failure-free period for its two phases to complete — the price FLP extracts, as we shall see.

### 4.2 Multi-Paxos: from one decree to a log

Choosing one value does not replicate a state machine; we need an unbounded *sequence* of values. **Multi-Paxos** runs one instance of single-decree Paxos per log slot (decree number). Its central optimization is the **stable leader**: a single proposer that, once elected, executes Phase 1 *once* for all future slots, then pipelines Phase 2 for each new command [1][12]. This collapses the steady-state cost to one round trip per command and is precisely how systems like Chubby and Spanner descend from Paxos.

The deconstruction literature shows Multi-Paxos is not monolithic: it can be factored into composable modules (single-decree instances, a leader-election oracle, message channels) with refinement proofs showing implementations preserve the abstract specification [12]. The main engineering burdens are leader election itself, log compaction (garbage-collecting chosen slots via snapshots), and reconfiguration — all topics Raft later made first-class.

### 4.3 Raft: understandability as a design criterion

Ongaro and Ousterhout's **Raft** (USENIX ATC 2014, Best Paper) "produces a result equivalent to (multi-)Paxos" but restructures the protocol around *understandability*: it separates leader election, log replication, and safety, and enforces a stronger degree of coherency to shrink the state space a reader must hold in mind [2].

Key mechanisms:

1. **Strong leader with terms.** Time is divided into *terms*, numbered monotonically. Each term begins with a randomized-timeout election: a candidate requests votes, wins with a majority, and becomes leader for the term. Crucially, *log entries flow only from leader to followers* — unlike Paxos, where any proposer may inject values into any slot.
2. **Log replication via AppendEntries.** The leader accepts client commands, appends them to its log, and replicates via `AppendEntries` RPCs carrying the previous entry's term and index. A follower rejects the RPC if its log disagrees — the **Log Matching property**: if two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, all preceding entries are identical [2].
3. **Election Safety and Leader Completeness.** At most one leader per term; and a leader's log contains every entry committed in prior terms. The latter follows from the *election restriction*: a candidate's log must be at least as up-to-date as a majority's before it can win, so no committed entry is ever lost across terms.

Raft's celebrated contribution beyond mechanics is *membership change*: a two-phase **joint consensus** using overlapping majorities, so the cluster never operates under two disjoint majorities simultaneously [2]. The paper's user study — 43 students at two universities, 33 of whom answered Raft questions better than Paxos questions — made "understandability" an empirically measured property rather than an aesthetic claim [2].

| Feature | Paxos / Multi-Paxos | Raft | EPaxos |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steady-state leader | Stable proposer (optional) | Strong leader per term | None (egalitarian) |
| Commit latency (common case) | 1 RTT (Multi-Paxos) | 1 RTT | 1 RTT (fast path) |
| Ordering discipline | Per-slot independent | Leader-serialized log | Dependency graph |
| Leader election | External to core protocol | Randomized timeouts, first-class | Not needed |
| Membership change | Ad hoc | Joint consensus | Via reconfiguration protocol |
| Quorum size | Majority | Majority | Majority (fast quorum 3f+1 of 2f+1 for 1-fault case) |

### 4.4 EPaxos: egalitarian, leaderless consensus

Multi-Paxos and Raft both concentrate work and latency on a leader — a bottleneck under wide-area latency and a single point of failover interruption. **Egalitarian Paxos (EPaxos)** [4] removes the leader entirely. Any replica may be the *command leader* for a command it receives (typically the nearest replica to the client). Instead of competing for ordered *slots*, each command carries a set of **dependencies**: other commands it must be ordered after.

EPaxos commits a command in **one round trip** in the common case (the *fast path*, using a fast quorum) and at most two rounds in all cases, with message counts linear in the number of replicas [4].

EPaxos is an instance of **generalized consensus**: non-interfering (non-conflicting) commands may commit in any relative order, so only genuine conflicts force coordination. This insight — order only what must be ordered — is the deepest efficiency idea in the modern consensus literature.

### 4.5 The boundary: FLP impossibility and the failure-detector hierarchy

All of the above guarantees *safety* unconditionally and *liveness* only under favorable conditions. Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson proved this asymmetry is fundamental [3]:

> **Theorem:** (FLP, 1985). No deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus among n ≥ 2 processes in an asynchronous system if even one process may crash [3].

The proof is the celebrated **bivalency argument**. A configuration is *0-valent* (only decision 0 reachable), *1-valent*, or *bivalent* (both reachable). A bivalent initial configuration exists; from any bivalent configuration and any applicable step, another bivalent configuration is reachable — so an adversarial scheduler can keep the system bivalent forever, deferring decision indefinitely.

**Circumventing FLP** therefore means weakening a hypothesis: add randomness (Ben-Or style probabilistic termination), partial synchrony (timeouts that eventually hold), or *failure detectors*. Chandra and Toueg formalized the last route: an **unreliable failure detector** is an oracle, per process, listing suspected crashed processes — allowed to make mistakes [5]. Detectors are classified by two properties:

- **Completeness**: *strong* (every crashed process is eventually permanently suspected by every correct process) vs. *weak* (every crashed process is eventually permanently suspected by *some* correct process), each in *permanent* or *eventual* (◇) form.
- **Accuracy**: *strong* (no correct process is ever suspected), *weak* (some correct process is never suspected), and their eventual variants.

This yields eight classes; the weakest class that still solves consensus is the **eventually weak detector ◇W** — equivalently, the eventual leader oracle **Ω** [6][11]. The two landmark theorems frame all practice: consensus is solvable with ◇S (eventually strong) plus a majority of correct processes, and (Chandra–Hadzilacos–Toueg) nothing weaker than Ω suffices [6].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 TLA+ specifications and the TLC model checker

Lamport's **TLA+** (Temporal Logic of Actions) is the lingua franca for specifying these protocols: a specification is a formula describing initial states and the next-state relation, and safety properties are invariants checked over all reachable states. The **TLC model checker** exhaustively explores finite instances (small numbers of processes, ballots, values), and has found real bugs in published consensus protocols before deployment.

A minimal TLA+ fragment for single-decree Paxos illustrates the style:

```tla
------------------------------ MODULE SimplePaxos ------------------------------
EXTENDS Integers, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Acceptors, Quorum, Ballots, Values
VARIABLES promised, accepted, chosen

TypeOK ==
    /\ promised \in [Acceptors -> Ballots \cup {-1}]
    /\ accepted  \in [Acceptors -> Ballots \cup {-1}]
    /\ chosen    \in [Values \cup {None}]

Safety == chosen # None => \A v \in Values : (chosen = v) => ~(\E w \in Values : w # v /\ chosen = w)

Prepare(a, b) ==
    /\ b > promised[a]
    /\ promised' = [promised EXCEPT ![a] = b]
    /\ UNCHANGED <<accepted, chosen>>

Accept(a, b, v) ==
    /\ b >= promised[a]
    /\ accepted' = [accepted EXCEPT ![a] = <<b, v>>]
    /\ UNCHANGED <<promised, chosen>>

Learn(Q, b, v) ==
    /\ Q \in Quorum
    /\ \A a \in Q : accepted[a] = <<b, v>>
    /\ chosen' = v
    /\ UNCHANGED <<promised, accepted>>
=============================================================================
```

### 5.2 TLAPS-checked proofs of Multi-Paxos

**TLAPS**, the TLA+ Proof System, machine-checks hierarchical, human-written proofs. Chand, Liu, and Stoller produced a formal TLA+ specification of Multi-Paxos with a TLAPS-checked safety proof, deliberately minimizing the difference from the earlier Basic Paxos proof to create a reusable proof pattern for Paxos variants [7]. A follow-up using *message history variables* cut the specifications and proofs substantially — about half as many manually written invariants — with Multi-Paxos proofs checking within 1.5 minutes where prior proofs failed to check at all [8].

### 5.3 Automatically inferred invariants

The frontier is *automatic* verification. IC3PO, a novel model-checking algorithm exploiting the spatial and temporal regularity of Paxos's specification, inferred an inductive invariant for Lamport's original Paxos specification that **identically matches the human-written invariant** previously derived with significant manual effort in interactive theorem proving — the first automatically inferred inductive invariant for the original Paxos spec [9].

### 5.4 Byzantine extension by refinement

Lamport's "Byzantizing Paxos by Refinement" gives TLA+ specifications of both ordinary Paxos and the Castro–Liskov PBFT algorithm with a TLAPS-checked proof that the Byzantine-tolerant algorithm *refines* ordinary Paxos — 2f+1 non-faulty processes effectively running Paxos amid f malicious ones [10]. Refinement, rather than re-proof, is the scalable verification strategy: prove the simple protocol once, then show the complex one implements it.

The following Python sketch demonstrates the quorum-intersection property that underlies every safety proof above, by brute force over small configurations:

```python
from itertools import combinations

def majority_quorums(n):
    k = n // 2 + 1
    return list(combinations(range(n), k))

def check_intersection(n):
    qs = majority_quorums(n)
    return all(set(a) & set(b) for a in qs for b in qs)

for n in (3, 5, 7, 9):
    assert check_intersection(n), f"quorum intersection failed for n={n}"
print("Majority quorums intersect for all tested n: safety's algebraic base holds.")
```

## 6 Limitations

Every protocol surveyed pays FLP's tax somewhere; the differences are *where*:

1. **Liveness is always conditional.** Paxos needs a distinguished proposer and a quiescent window; Raft needs timely heartbeats and randomized elections to converge; EPaxos's fast path degrades under contention, falling back to the slow path. None terminates in all asynchronous runs — that is precisely what [3] forbids.
2. **Quorum cost.** Majority quorums tolerate f failures with 2f+1 processes, but wide-area deployments pay cross-region latency on every quorum round. EPaxos mitigates this with one-round commits [4]; Raft's leader can at least place the leader near the workload.
3. **Reconfiguration and log growth.** Membership change remains the most bug-prone area in practice — Raft's joint consensus is a protocol in its own right [2], and log compaction via snapshotting is where production incidents cluster.
4. **Verification gaps.** TLAPS proofs cover *safety*, not liveness, for most Paxos variants; model checking covers only small instances; and refinement proofs like [10] assume the TLA+ specification faithfully models the implementation — a gap implementation-verification projects (IronFleet, Verdi) address at 30,000–50,000 lines of proof [9].
5. **Byzantine failures.** Everything here assumes crash failures. Byzantine-tolerant variants (PBFT via refinement [10]) need 3f+1 processes and substantially more complex quorum arguments; the FLP and failure-detector results also change character under Byzantine faults.

## 7 Conclusion

Distributed consensus is the rare subject where theory and practice advanced in lockstep. Lamport's Paxos [1] proved safety could be had unconditionally in asynchrony; Multi-Paxos showed a stable leader makes it fast; Raft [2] showed understandability is an engineering property worth optimizing; EPaxos [4] showed the leader itself was optional. Beneath them all, FLP [3] draws the hard line — deterministic liveness is unachievable — and the failure-detector hierarchy [5][6] measures exactly how much synchrony each protocol smuggles in through timeouts and leader oracles. Machine-checked verification in TLA+ and TLAPS [7][8][9] is converting these subtle protocols from folklore into certified artifacts, with automatic invariant inference beginning to close the gap between human insight and machine proof. The enduring lesson is architectural: **make wrong suspicions safe and merely slow, guarantee safety always, and earn liveness from the environment** — a discipline every reliable distributed system still follows.
