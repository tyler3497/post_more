# Quantum Illumination and Quantum Radar: Entanglement-Enhanced Detection from Lloyd's Protocol to Experimental Realizations

## Abstract

Quantum illumination (QI) is a quantum-enhanced target-detection protocol in which one member of an entangled photon pair — the *signal* — probes a region where a target may be present, while its partner — the *idler* — is retained for a joint measurement with the returning light. The central surprise, established by Seth Lloyd in 2008 and sharpened into a Gaussian-state theory by Tan *et al.*, is that entanglement improves detection *even though the lossy, noisy round trip destroys it*: residual signal–idler correlations survive entanglement breaking and beat every classical probe of equal transmitted energy [1][2]. In the canonical regime — low-brightness signal, weak reflectivity, bright background, large time-bandwidth product — two-mode squeezed vacuum (TMSV) illumination improves the quantum Chernoff bound error exponent by a factor of four, a **6 dB advantage**, over the optimal coherent-state strategy. This thesis reconstructs the protocol from first principles, derives the advantage, surveys the receiver hierarchy from the realizable optical parametric amplifier (OPA) receiver to the optimal feed-forward sum-frequency-generation (FF-SFG) receiver, and traces the experimental arc from the first optical demonstration to cryogenic microwave quantum radar. We examine idler-storage strategies, radar-equation integration, jamming resilience, and the formidable gap between a 6 dB error-exponent gain and an operational quantum radar.

## 1 Introduction

Radar is, at its mathematical core, a problem of *quantum channel discrimination*: a transmitter injects a probe into a black box that either contains a target or does not, and the receiver must decide which. Classical radar solves this with bright coherent pulses and ever more clever signal processing. Yet classical performance is bounded by the statistics of the probe state itself, and in the photon-starved, noise-dominated regime those statistics are unforgiving. **Quantum illumination** asks whether entanglement — prepared before transmission, partially destroyed by the channel, and exploited in joint measurement — can push detection performance past every classical limit at fixed transmitted energy.

The idea entered the literature in 2008 when Lloyd showed that entangled illumination could raise the effective signal-to-noise ratio of photodetection exponentially in the number of entanglement bits, albeit in an idealized low-noise analysis [1]. The modern, experimentally relevant theory arrived months later: Tan, Erkmen, Giovannetti, Guha, Lloyd, Maccone, Pirandola, and Shapiro formulated **Gaussian-state quantum illumination**, in which a continuous-wave spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) source emits a train of low-brightness two-mode squeezed vacuum mode pairs, and proved a 6 dB advantage in the quantum Chernoff bound over the optimal classical (coherent-state) illumination of identical energy [2]. That result — an advantage *in the high-noise regime where classical radars actually struggle* — launched a research program that now spans receiver theory, microwave implementations, integrated photonics, and radar-systems analysis [3][9].

> **Thesis statement:** Quantum illumination provides a rigorously quantified, entanglement-enabled 6 dB improvement in the asymptotic error exponent for detecting a weakly reflecting target in bright thermal noise; the advantage is achievable in principle with an optimal receiver and has been partially demonstrated in the laboratory, but idler storage, nonlinear-optics efficiency, and the harsh arithmetic of the radar range equation keep an operational quantum radar firmly in the research domain.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 frames target detection as binary quantum hypothesis testing. Section 3 develops the Gaussian QI model: the TMSV transmitter, the lossy thermal channel, and the Chernoff machinery. Section 4 is the deep dive: the 6 dB derivation, the receiver hierarchy (OPA, phase-conjugate, FF-SFG), microwave realizations with Josephson parametric amplifiers, and the analog-versus-digital idler-storage debate. Section 5 surveys experimental evidence, Section 6 catalogues limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background: Target Detection as Quantum Channel Discrimination

Detecting a target is a **binary hypothesis test**. Let hypothesis $H_0$ denote *target absent* and $H_1$ denote *target present*, with equal *a priori* probabilities in the Bayesian formulation. The receiver observes a quantum state — $\hat{\rho}_0$ under $H_0$, $\hat{\rho}_1$ under $H_1$ — and applies a two-outcome measurement to minimize the average error probability $P_e$. The minimum is the **Helstrom limit**,

$$P_e^{\mathrm{Hel}} = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\lVert\hat{\rho}_1 - \hat{\rho}_0\rVert_1\right),$$

but for the multi-copy states $\hat{\rho}_0^{\otimes M}$, $\hat{\rho}_1^{\otimes M}$ encountered in radar (where $M = TW \gg 1$ is the time-bandwidth product), the operationally vital quantity is the **quantum Chernoff bound** [2][3]:

$$P_e \le \frac{1}{2}\min_{0\le s\le 1}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\hat{\rho}_0^{\,s}\,\hat{\rho}_1^{\,1-s}\right)^M \equiv \frac{1}{2}Q_s^{\,M}.$$

The exponent $-\ln Q_s$ governs how fast errors vanish as the integration time (mode count $M$) grows.

Two regimes matter. In the **good regime** (low loss, dim background), distinguishing signal from noise is easy and entanglement's edge is modest.

The fair **classical benchmark** is the best classical transmitter of equal energy: a coherent-state probe with the quantum-optimal (Helstrom) receiver.

## 3 Theoretical Methodology: The Gaussian Quantum Illumination Model

### 3.1 The TMSV transmitter

The Gaussian QI transmitter is a continuous-wave SPDC source producing $M$ independent signal–idler mode pairs in the **two-mode squeezed vacuum**,

$$|\psi\rangle_{SI} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{N_S^{\,n}}{(N_S+1)^{\,n+1}}}\,|n\rangle_S|n\rangle_I,$$

with $N_S \ll 1$ photons per mode. The signal modes are transmitted toward the region of interest; the idler modes are retained at the receiver.

### 3.2 The lossy thermal channel

Under $H_0$ (target absent), each returned mode is pure thermal background with mean photon number $N_B \gg 1$, uncorrelated with the idler.

> **Channel model.** With $\hat{a}_B$ a thermal mode of brightness $N_B$ and $0 < \kappa \ll 1$ the target's power reflectivity,
> $$H_0:\quad \hat{a}_R = \hat{a}_B, \qquad H_1:\quad \hat{a}_R = \sqrt{\kappa}\,\hat{a}_S + \sqrt{1-\kappa}\,\hat{a}_B.$$

The round trip is **entanglement-breaking**: under $H_1$, the signal–idler entanglement is destroyed by the overwhelming admixture of thermal noise.

### 3.3 Decision-theoretic framing

Target detection is thus discrimination between the $M$-fold tensor products $\hat{\rho}_0^{\otimes M}$ and $\hat{\rho}_1^{\otimes M}$ of zero-mean Gaussian states.

```python
import numpy as np

kappa = 1e-3      # target power reflectivity (weak)
N_S   = 0.01      # signal photons per mode (dim probe)
N_B   = 100.0     # background photons per mode (bright noise)
M     = 10**6     # time-bandwidth product

# Asymptotic per-mode Chernoff exponents
xi_cl = kappa * N_S / (4.0 * N_B)   # coherent-state benchmark
xi_qi = kappa * N_S / N_B           # TMSV quantum illumination

P_e_cl = 0.5 * np.exp(-M * xi_cl)
P_e_qi = 0.5 * np.exp(-M * xi_qi)
print(f"classical exponent: {xi_cl:.3e} -> P_e ~ {P_e_cl:.3e}")
print(f"quantum exponent:   {xi_qi:.3e} -> P_e ~ {P_e_qi:.3e}")
print(f"exponent ratio: {xi_qi/xi_cl:.2f} = 10*log10(4) = 6.02 dB")
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The transmitter, the channel, and why the idler matters

Picture the canonical QI apparatus: an SPDC crystal pumped by a laser emits correlated signal–idler pairs. A dichroic element routes the signal outward through the transmit antenna while the idler is diverted into a storage subsystem. The signal propagates, scatters off the (possibly absent) target with reflectivity $\kappa$, accumulates thermal background $N_B$, and returns to a **joint measurement** with the idler. Three facts make this architecture non-classical:

1. **The probe is dimmer than the noise it hides in.** With $N_S \ll 1 \ll N_B$, the signal is buried tens of dB below the background per mode; detection is possible only through the idler correlation.
2. **The idler never touches the target.** All target information enters through the fragile cross-covariance, which is why idler storage fidelity is the protocol's central engineering bottleneck (Section 4.5).
3. **Energy is the currency of fairness.** Every comparison holds the transmitted energy $MN_S$ fixed; the advantage is in *how* that energy's quantum statistics are arranged, not in spending more of it [2][8].

### 4.2 The 6 dB advantage: quantum versus classical error exponents

The headline result of Tan *et al.* is a pair of asymptotic Chernoff bounds [2]:

> **Theorem (Tan *et al.*, 2008).** In the regime $N_S \ll 1 \ll N_B$, $0 < \kappa \ll 1$, $M \gg 1$, the quantum Chernoff quantities satisfy
> $$Q_{\mathrm{QI}} \;\approx\; 1 - \frac{\kappa N_S}{N_B}, \qquad Q_{\mathrm{CS}} \;\approx\; 1 - \frac{\kappa N_S}{4N_B},$$
> so that $P_e \le \tfrac{1}{2}Q^M$ gives a **fourfold (6 dB) improvement in the error exponent** for TMSV illumination over the optimal coherent-state classical strategy.

*Interpretation.* Because $Q \approx 1 - \xi$ with $\xi \ll 1$, the bound behaves as $P_e \lesssim \tfrac{1}{2}e^{-M\xi}$; quadrupling $\xi$ is equivalent to quadrupling the integration time, the transmitted energy, or — in radar language — gaining 6 dB of effective SNR.

Subsequent work proved this 6 dB is **fundamental, not merely a TMSV artifact**.

| Probe | Receiver | Chernoff exponent | Gain over classical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coherent state | Helstrom (optimal) | $\kappa N_S/4N_B$ | 0 dB (benchmark) |
| TMSV | OPA receiver | $\approx \kappa N_S/2N_B$ | ~3 dB |
| TMSV | Phase-conjugate receiver | $\approx \kappa N_S/2N_B$ | ~3 dB |
| TMSV | FF-SFG receiver | $\kappa N_S/N_B$ | **6 dB (optimal)** |

### 4.3 Receivers: from the OPA to the FF-SFG

The Chernoff bound is a promise; the receiver is the price.

- **OPA receiver (Guha–Erkmen).** The return and idler are combined in a low-gain optical parametric amplifier and the output photon-counted. This realizable design captures roughly half the advantage — about **3 dB** — and remains the workhorse of near-term experiments [3].
- **Phase-conjugate receiver.** Phase-conjugating the idler and mixing it with the return on a balanced beam splitter converts the fragile phase-sensitive correlation into an intensity signature. It matches the OPA's ~3 dB with simpler optics [3].
- **FF-SFG receiver (Zhuang–Zhang–Shapiro).** The conceptual optimum. Each returning signal–idler pair undergoes **sum-frequency generation** in the same crystal type used for SPDC, coherently fusing the pair into a single pump-frequency photon; a **feed-forward** circuit, inspired by the Dolinar receiver, applies cycle-by-cycle displacements conditioned on photon-counting outcomes. The resulting minimum-error receiver attains the full 6 dB Chernoff bound and yields QI's true ROC [4].

The FF-SFG analysis is as important for its *caveat* as for its optimality: it assumes **100%-efficient single-photon-pair SFG**, which is far beyond the current capability of nonlinear optics [3].

### 4.4 Microwave quantum illumination: Josephson parametric amplifiers and the cryogenic frontier

QI's most natural habitat may be the **microwave band**.

Barzanjeh *et al.* proposed the first microwave QI architecture in 2015: **Josephson parametric amplifiers (JPAs)** generate two-mode squeezed microwave fields; one mode probes the target region while the other is retained; electro-optomechanical converters were envisioned to bridge microwave and optical domains [6].

The experimental state of the art followed two tracks:

1. **Analog microwave QI.** Using JPAs and analog correlation receivers, experiments demonstrated quantum-enhanced covariances against a *symmetric classical noise radar* — an important but weaker benchmark than the optimal coherent-state strategy [7][9].
2. **Digital-receiver microwave QI.** Assouly *et al.* (2020) digitized the quadratures of both the return and the idler and performed the phase-conjugated correlation *in software*.

The digital approach also reframes the idler problem: instead of preserving a fragile quantum state in hardware, one measures it immediately and stores *classical data*.

### 4.5 Idler storage: analog delay versus digital correlation

The idler must survive, in usable form, for the round-trip time of the signal.

- **Analog storage** keeps the idler as a quantum state: optical fiber delay spools, free-space delay lines, or true quantum memories (rare-earth crystals, atomic ensembles, optomechanical resonators). Every decibel of idler loss directly erodes the cross-correlation and hence the advantage; microwave idlers additionally demand millikelvin confinement. Analog storage preserves the full quantum measurement, including the option of a future optimal receiver — but it is bulky, lossy, and range-limiting [3][9].
- **Digital correlation** measures the idler immediately (typically by heterodyne) and stores the classical outcomes. The quantum advantage then resides in the *classical correlation record* between the measured idler quadratures and the return. This eliminates the quantum memory entirely and enabled the 2020 microwave demonstration — at a cost: the measurement chosen up front may not be the optimal one, and phase stability (or active phase tracking) between the idler record and the return becomes the new bottleneck [7].

Sanz *et al.*'s optimality theorem sharpens the stakes: **without access to a quantum memory, coherent states are already optimal probes** [8].

## 5 Empirical Results and Experimental Evidence

The experimental record, now spanning more than a decade, confirms QI's physics while underscoring its engineering immaturity:

- **First optical realization (Lopaeva *et al.*, 2013).** Using SPDC photon pairs and photon-counting detection, the INRIM collaboration implemented Lloyd's protocol in a lossy, noisy environment and demonstrated the protocol's striking **robustness to noise and loss** — the quantum advantage persisted where naive intuition said quantum coherence should have perished [5].
- **Quantum-enhanced noise radar (2019).** A JPA-based microwave source with an analog receiver outperformed a symmetric classical noise radar, bringing QI techniques to radar-like continuous-wave operation [9].
- **Digital-receiver microwave QI (Assouly *et al.*, 2020).** The landmark system-level test: entangled microwaves from a JPA, a target region, and a digital correlation receiver — beating the optimal classical homodyne benchmark with a phase-stable target [7].
- **Radar-equation integration.** Recent systems analyses have begun embedding the QI error exponent into the classical **radar range equation**, translating the 6 dB exponent gain into maximum-range predictions and clarifying a sobering fact: because the advantage is in the *exponent* at low per-mode SNR, it extends range only modestly once transmit power, antenna gain, and integration time are accounted for [9].
- **Jamming and spoofing resilience.** QI's low-brightness probe is inherently low-probability-of-intercept, and entanglement monogamy denies an adversary the ability to replicate signal–idler correlations — but an intercept-resend spoofer that *breaks* the entanglement collapses the advantage, and the community has begun formalizing spoofing-aware QI security analyses [3].

> **Theorem (operational reading).** *Quantum illumination does not make a better radar by transmitting more effectively; it makes a better detector by correlating more cleverly. Every demonstrated advantage to date is a detection-SNR or error-exponent advantage at fixed transmitted energy — never a transmit-power advantage.*

Integrated photonics is the emerging hardware path: chip-scale SPDC in silicon nitride and thin-film lithium niobate now produces the entangled mode pairs QI needs in a footprint compatible with fieldable sensors, and recent demonstrations of entanglement-enhanced sensing on photonic chips suggest the transmitter — at least — can be miniaturized [9].

## 6 Limitations: The Long Road from 6 dB Theory to Radar Practice

Intellectual honesty requires enumerating what the 6 dB does *not* imply:

1. **The receiver gap.** The only receiver attaining the full 6 dB (FF-SFG) needs unit-efficiency single-pair sum-frequency generation — nonexistent technology [4]. Practical receivers (OPA, phase-conjugate, digital) capture roughly half the gain [3][7].
2. **Idler storage at range.** Analog idler storage loss scales with delay; digital correlation needs phase stability. Long-range radar implies long idler retention, precisely where both approaches degrade [8][9].
3. **The benchmark subtlety.** QI beats the optimal *equal-energy* classical probe. A classical radar that simply transmits *more* energy — or uses bright-pulse homodyne detection — can outperform QI in absolute terms [7]. The advantage is efficiency of information per photon, not raw sensitivity per dollar.
4. **Exponent versus SNR.** The 6 dB is a gain in the *error exponent* in the asymptotic low-SNR regime. Translating exponent gains into detection-range gains through the $R^{-4}$ radar equation yields far less dramatic operational improvements [9].
5. **Target modeling.** The theory assumes a weakly reflecting, phase-stable, non-fluctuating point target in stationary thermal noise. Real targets fluctuate (Swerling models), scintillate, and move; Doppler and speckle effects on the fragile cross-correlation remain under-studied.
6. **Noise-regime mismatch.** Optical QI operates where backgrounds can be made small (undermining the $N_B \gg 1$ assumption), while microwave QI enjoys huge $N_B$ but pays in cryogenics and antenna physics. Each band gets the advantage only by paying a different tax.

## 7 Conclusion

Quantum illumination is that rare quantum technology whose central claim — a 6 dB error-exponent advantage for detecting a weakly reflecting target in bright noise — is simultaneously **rigorously proven, fundamentally optimal, and experimentally corroborated in its physics** [1][2][4][8]. From Lloyd's 2008 insight that entanglement could aid photodetection [1], through Tan *et al.*'s Gaussian theory locating the advantage in the high-noise regime [2], through the receiver hierarchy culminating in the optimal FF-SFG design [4], to JPA-based microwave experiments that beat the strongest classical benchmarks [6][7], the field has converted a paradox — *entanglement helps even after the channel destroys it* — into a quantitative engineering science.

What remains is the harder conversion: from error exponents to radar range, from cryogenic demonstrators to fieldable sensors, from unit-efficiency nonlinear optics that does not exist to receivers that do. The next decade of quantum radar will be won not by discovering a larger advantage — Sanz *et al.* proved 6 dB is the ceiling [8] — but by *keeping* the advantage through real channels: better idler handling, integrated-photonic transmitters, spoofing-aware protocols, and honest radar-equation accounting [9]. If that program succeeds, quantum illumination will not replace the radar — it will teach the radar to listen for correlations that classical physics cannot hear.
