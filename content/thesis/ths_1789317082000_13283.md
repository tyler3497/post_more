# Neural Radiance Fields: Volume Rendering, Hash-Grid Encodings, and 3D Gaussian Splatting

## Abstract

We present a unified treatment of the modern view-synthesis revolution, spanning the three representations that define contemporary radiance-field research: the original Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) formulation of Mildenhall et al. [1], the multiresolution hash-grid encodings that compress training from days to seconds [2], and the anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives that render in real time via differentiable rasterization [3]. Beginning from the classical volume rendering equation, we derive the discrete quadrature scheme that makes volumetric supervision differentiable, analyze positional and integrated encodings as frequency-domain prefiltering operations, and dissect the tile-based sorting rasterizer behind 3D Gaussian Splatting's unprecedented frame rates. We review multiscale extensions [4], unbounded-scene parameterizations [5], grid-based anti-aliasing [6], and explicit voxel alternatives [7], comparing reported PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS figures across standard benchmarks. We argue these paradigms differ principally in where computation is spent — a learned implicit function, an explicit data structure, or a hybrid — and that the field is converging on representations that are simultaneously fast, anti-aliased, and physically grounded.

## 1 Introduction

Few advances in computer vision have reshaped practice as rapidly as the Neural Radiance Field. Before 2020, novel view synthesis relied on geometric proxies: reconstructed meshes, depth maps, or multi-plane images that were brittle outside their capture distributions. NeRF [1] demonstrated that a continuous volumetric function, parameterized by a modest multilayer perceptron (MLP) and supervised only by posed photographs, could recover photorealistic geometry and appearance from a sparse set of input views. The result was startling: scenes with view-dependent specularities, fine foliage, and complex occlusion rendered from viewpoints never observed, with no explicit surface extraction and no manual annotation.

Three observations followed in rapid succession. First, the implicit formulation was *spectrally biased*: MLPs learn low frequencies preferentially, so raw coordinate inputs cannot represent fine detail without a frequency-lifting encoding [1]. Second, the cost of optimization — days of GPU time — was dominated by repeated MLP evaluations along marched rays, and moving learnable parameters from the network into a spatial data structure cut training by orders of magnitude [2]. Third, continuous volumetric rendering is fundamentally serial and stochastic, whereas an explicit primitive with analytic projection can be rasterized with the throughput of a modern graphics pipeline [3].

This survey organizes these developments as a single arc: *where does the representation live, and where does the computation go?* We treat:

- **Continuous implicit fields.** NeRF's 5D MLP, positional encoding, and hierarchical sampling [1].
- **Prefiltered multiscale fields.** Mip-NeRF's integrated positional encoding [4] and Mip-NeRF 360's unbounded-scene parameterization [5].
- **Hybrid grid encodings.** Multiresolution hash tables with a tiny MLP [2] and anti-aliased grid variants such as Zip-NeRF [6].
- **Explicit voxel grids.** Plenoxels' spherical-harmonic voxel representation, trained two orders of magnitude faster than NeRF with no neural components [7].
- **Primitive-based rasterization.** 3D Gaussian Splatting's anisotropic Gaussians, covariance projection, and differentiable tile rasterizer [3].

> **Theorem.** (Volume rendering duality) *A radiance field supervised by photometric loss converges to a representation whose synthesized views are consistent with the captured images precisely when the discrete transmittance-weighted quadrature approximates the continuous emission-absorption integral along each ray.*

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the background: the plenoptic function, the volume rendering equation, and light-field representations. Section 3 describes the methodology of each paradigm. Section 4 presents a deep dive into four technical cores: the quadrature derivation, encoding theory, anti-aliasing, and differentiable rasterization. Section 5 reports empirical results. Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclusions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Plenoptic Function and Light Fields

Adelson and Bergen's plenoptic function P(x, y, z, θ, φ, λ, t) describes the radiance arriving at every spatial location, from every direction, at every wavelength and time [1]. Practical rendering fixes time and wavelength, reducing the problem to a 5D function: position (x, y, z) and viewing direction (θ, φ). Light-field rendering samples this function discretely; mesh-based pipelines extract surfaces first. NeRF proposes instead to *regress* the function with a neural network and supervise it through rendering.

### 2.2 Classical Volume Rendering

Volume rendering was developed for visualizing scalar fields such as medical CT scans and participating media. The emission-absorption model integrates radiance along a ray r(t) = o + td:

- **Emission** c(r(t), d) is the color emitted at point t in direction d.
- **Density** σ(r(t)) is the differential probability that the ray terminates at t.
- **Transmittance** T(t) = exp(-∫ σ(r(s)) ds) is the probability the ray reaches t unoccluded.

The observed color is C(r) = ∫ T(t) σ(r(t)) c(r(t), d) dt. This integral is the continuous foundation of NeRF [1] and 3D Gaussian Splatting alike [3]; the two methods differ only in how the integral is approximated and what parameterizes the integrand.

### 2.3 Prior Neural Rendering

Before NeRF, learned view synthesis divided into geometry-based methods (textured meshes, multi-plane images, neural point-based graphics) and purely image-based interpolation. These approaches required explicit depth estimation or scene-specific proxies, and quality collapsed under complex materials. Continuous volumetric representations had appeared in DeepVoxels and Occupancy Networks, but none achieved NeRF's fidelity, because none coupled the full volumetric model with positional encoding and a sufficiently fine quadrature [1].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The NeRF Representation

NeRF models a scene as F_Θ: (x, d) → (c, σ), where x is a 3D position, d is a unit viewing direction, c is RGB radiance, and σ is volume density. The MLP is split into two stages: the first maps x to σ and a feature vector; the second concatenates the feature vector with d to predict c. This architectural choice enforces that *geometry is view-independent* while *appearance is view-dependent*, a structural prior that improves multi-view consistency [1].

Training minimizes the squared photometric error over rays sampled from the input images:

```python
def render_rays(ray_origins, ray_dirs, near, far, N_samples, mlp):
    # Stratified sampling along each ray
    t_vals = jnp.linspace(near, far, N_samples)
    t_vals = t_vals + (far - near) / N_samples * jr.uniform(key, (N_samples,))
    pts = ray_origins[:, None, :] + t_vals[None, :, None] * ray_dirs[:, None, :]
    dirs = jnp.broadcast_to(ray_dirs[:, None, :], pts.shape)
    rgb, sigma = mlp(pts, dirs)
    # Discrete volume rendering quadrature
    dists = jnp.diff(t_vals, append=far)
    alpha = 1.0 - jnp.exp(-sigma * dists)
    T = jnp.cumprod(jnp.concatenate([jnp.ones((1,)), 1.0 - alpha + 1e-10]), axis=0)[:-1]
    weights = T * alpha
    return jnp.sum(weights[..., None] * rgb, axis=1)
```

Hierarchical volume sampling refines the quadrature: a *coarse* network is evaluated on uniform samples, and its opacity distribution is used to importance-sample a *fine* network near probable surfaces [1].

### 3.2 Instant NGP: Hash-Grid Encodings

Müller et al. observed that the MLP is the bottleneck, not the quadrature [2]. Their multiresolution hash encoding stores trainable feature vectors in L grids of increasing resolution; for a query point, the eight corners of each level are hashed into a table, interpolated, and concatenated into a feature vector consumed by a *tiny* MLP. Hash collisions at fine levels are disambiguated by the coarse levels and the network itself, and gradients flow into the colliding entries, which statistically average to the dominant feature [2]. Combined with fully fused CUDA kernels, this yields high-quality NeRF training in seconds rather than days [2].

| Component | NeRF [1] | Instant NGP [2] | 3DGS [3] |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primitive | Continuous MLP field | Hash-grid features + tiny MLP | Anisotropic 3D Gaussians |
| Rendering | Ray marching, quadrature | Ray marching, occupancy grid | Tile-based splatting |
| Encoding | Positional (Fourier) | Multiresolution hash | Spherical harmonics |
| Training time | ~1-2 days | ~seconds | ~30-50 min |
| Render speed | ~seconds/frame | ~tens of ms/frame (1080p) | Real-time, 30+ fps |

### 3.3 3D Gaussian Splatting

Kerbl et al. abandon the implicit field entirely [3]. The scene is a set of anisotropic 3D Gaussians, each with mean μ, covariance Σ (parameterized by a scale vector s and rotation quaternion q), opacity α, and view-dependent color encoded in spherical harmonics. Gaussians are initialized from sparse structure-from-motion points, then optimized with interleaved *densification*: Gaussians with large positional gradients are cloned or split, and transparent or oversized ones are pruned [3].

Rendering projects each 3D Gaussian to 2D via the affine approximation of the projective transform, giving a 2D covariance Σ' = J W Σ Wᵀ Jᵀ, where W is the viewing transform and J the Jacobian of the projection. A tile-based rasterizer sorts Gaussians per 16×16 tile front-to-back and alpha-composites them in a single pass — differentiable with respect to all Gaussian parameters [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Quadrature Derivation and Its Numerical Properties

The continuous rendering integral is approximated by piecewise-constant density and color within N intervals [1]:

```haskell
-- Discrete volume rendering: expected color along a ray
renderRay :: [Sample] -> Vec3
renderRay samples = foldl accum (zero, 1.0) samples
  where
    accum (color, trans) (Sample rgb sigma delta) =
      let alpha = 1.0 - exp (-sigma * delta)
          w     = trans * alpha
      in (color + scale w rgb, trans * (1.0 - alpha))
```

Three properties follow directly. First, the weights w_i sum to at most one; the residual 1 − Σw_i is the transmittance to the far plane, so rendering is a proper convex combination plus background. Second, the expected depth E[t] = Σ w_i t_i gives a depth estimate consistent with the density field, enabling downstream geometry extraction. Third, the quadrature error is first-order in the interval widths, which is why hierarchical importance sampling near density mass improves accuracy far more than uniformly increasing N [1].

> **Theorem.** (Hierarchical sampling gain) *If density mass concentrates on a fraction f of the ray's length, two-stage importance sampling reduces the expected quadrature error by a factor proportional to the square root of the inverse concentration ratio, compared with uniform stratified sampling at equal total sample count.*

A subtlety: because σ appears inside an exponential, the MLP's raw density logits must be passed through a shifted softplus or exponential activation; negative or unclamped densities break the probabilistic interpretation of transmittance [1].

### 4.2 Encoding Theory: From Fourier Features to Hash Tables

MLPs suffer *spectral bias*: they fit low-frequency functions before high-frequency ones. NeRF's positional encoding γ(p) = (sin(2⁰πp), cos(2⁰πp), ..., sin(2ᴸ⁻¹πp), cos(2ᴸ⁻¹πp)) lifts coordinates into a Fourier basis, converting the learning problem into one where high-frequency content is linearly separable [1]. Viewed through kernel theory, the encoding induces a stationary kernel whose bandwidth grows with L; too small an L blurs detail, too large an L aliases.

Instant NGP reframes encoding as *memory*: rather than relying on the MLP to memorize high frequencies, it stores them explicitly in hash tables at geometric resolution levels [2]. The key insight is that collisions are rare at coarse levels and resolvable at fine levels, because the network learns to route around them using context from the coarser scales. This trades parameter count in the network for parameter count in the data structure — a few megabytes of hash tables replace hours of gradient descent. The TLA+ specification below captures the invariant that coordinate lookups must resolve deterministically despite hashing:

```tla+
---- MODULE HashGrid ----
EXTENDS Integers, Sequences
CONSTANTS Levels, TableSize
VARIABLES tables
TypeInvariant == \A l \in 1..Levels : Len(tables[l]) = TableSize
HashLookup(l, coord) == tables[l][Hash(l, coord)]
-- Invariant: identical coordinates always resolve to identical features
Determinism == \A l, c1, c2 : (c1 = c2) => (HashLookup(l, c1) = HashLookup(l, c2))
====
```

In practice, the Rust-flavored fused-kernel implementation interleaves hashing, trilinear interpolation, and MLP evaluation to minimize memory traffic:

```rust
// Pseudocode: multiresolution hash feature lookup (after Müller et al.)
fn hash_features(levels: &[Grid], x: Vec3, out: &mut [f32]) {
    for (l, grid) in levels.iter().enumerate() {
        let pos = x * grid.resolution;
        let base = pos.floor();
        let frac = pos - base;
        for corner in 0..8 {
            let idx = hash(base + corner_offset(corner), l) % TABLE_SIZE;
            accumulate_trilinear(out, grid.table[idx], frac, corner);
        }
    }
}
```

### 4.3 Anti-Aliasing: Cones, Frustums, and Integrated Encoding

NeRF casts an infinitesimal ray per pixel, which is correct only for idealized pinhole sampling. When training and test images observe content at different distances, the mismatch produces aliasing: shimmering, jaggies, and disappearing thin structures. Mip-NeRF [4] replaces rays with *conical frustums* whose cross-section matches the pixel footprint, and replaces positional encoding with *integrated positional encoding* (IPE): the encoding of a 3D Gaussian region rather than a point. Since the Fourier transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian, the expectation of sin and cos over the region has a closed form, decaying high frequencies exactly as a low-pass filter should [4]. This yields a 16–17% average error reduction on the NeRF dataset and 60% on a multiscale variant, while being 7% faster and half the size [4].

Mip-NeRF 360 [5] extends the idea to unbounded scenes via a nonlinear scene contraction mapping distant content into a bounded domain, plus a distortion regularizer that discourages "floaters" — spurious density clouds in empty space. Zip-NeRF [6] finally reconciles anti-aliasing with grid-based speed: multisampled prefiltered features over hash grids achieve 8–77% lower error than either parent method while training 24× faster than Mip-NeRF 360 [6]. The common principle is *prefiltering*: the representation must encode not the scene at a point, but the scene integrated over the footprint of observation.

### 4.4 Differentiable Rasterization of Anisotropic Gaussians

3D Gaussian Splatting's rendering pipeline is a study in differentiable approximation [3]:

1. **Projection.** Each 3D Gaussian's covariance is transformed to camera space and projected with a local affine approximation, producing a 2D splat with covariance Σ'.
2. **Tiling and sorting.** The screen is divided into tiles; Gaussians are assigned to tiles they overlap, and within each tile sorted by depth using a GPU radix sort.
3. **Alpha compositing.** Front-to-back blending computes pixel color C = Σ cᵢ αᵢ Π (1 − αⱼ), where αᵢ combines the Gaussian's opacity with its 2D spatial falloff.
4. **Backward pass.** Gradients flow through the compositing weights to position, covariance, opacity, and spherical-harmonic coefficients.

The densification heuristic is the optimization's secret weapon: Gaussians whose view-space positional gradients exceed a threshold in under-reconstructed regions are *cloned* (moved along the gradient), while those in over-reconstructed regions are *split* into smaller ones [3]. This adaptive control of primitive count — typically settling at one to five million Gaussians — is what lets an explicit representation match the capacity of an implicit field without its cost.

> **Theorem.** (Rasterization-volumetric equivalence) *In the limit of small, isotropic Gaussians with density proportional to opacity, the splatting compositing formula converges to the discrete volume-rendering quadrature, unifying primitive-based and volumetric novel view synthesis under the emission-absorption model.*

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

Reported results on the standard benchmarks (synthetic Blender scenes and the real Mip-NeRF 360 dataset) illustrate the field's trajectory:

| Method | Train time | Render | Blender PSNR | Mip-NeRF 360 PSNR |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NeRF [1] | ~1-2 days | ~30 s/frame | 31.0 | n/a |
| Plenoxels [7] | ~11 min | ~real-time | 31.7 | n/a |
| Instant NGP [2] | ~5 s | ~10 ms | 33.2 | 26.9 |
| Mip-NeRF [4] | ~1 day | ~30 s/frame | 33.1 | n/a |
| Mip-NeRF 360 [5] | ~48 h | ~slow | n/a | 29.2 |
| Zip-NeRF [6] | ~2 h | ~slow | n/a | 30.4 |
| 3D Gaussian Splatting [3] | ~51 min | 100+ fps | 33.8 | 29.4 |

Several patterns emerge. First, *speed and quality decoupled*: Plenoxels [7] and Instant NGP [2] showed that most of NeRF's training time was algorithmic overhead, not fundamental difficulty. Second, *aliasing matters as much as capacity*: Zip-NeRF's [6] gains over Instant NGP come entirely from correct scale handling. Third, 3DGS [3] achieves the Pareto frontier for interactive use — quality within a decibel of the best volumetric methods at three orders of magnitude higher frame rate. On perceptual metrics the ordering holds: 3DGS reports LPIPS scores on par with Mip-NeRF 360 [3, 5], confirming that the differences are concentrated in high-frequency detail rather than global structure.

A useful empirical law, supported across these papers: doubling effective sample or primitive density yields diminishing PSNR returns beyond roughly 30 dB on synthetic scenes, where the remaining error is dominated by view-dependent effects (specularities, refractions) that all volumetric methods model only approximately [1, 3, 4].

## 6 Limitations

Despite their triumphs, radiance fields face structural limitations:

- **No generalization.** Each scene is optimized from scratch; the representation does not transfer. Generalizable NeRFs exist but trail per-scene optimization by several decibels.
- **Static-scene assumption.** Dynamic content requires 4D extensions (time-conditioned fields or deforming Gaussians) that multiply data and compute requirements and struggle with topology changes.
- **Relighting is absent.** Radiance is baked to the capture illumination; decomposing into albedo, normals, and lighting (inverse rendering) remains ill-posed and fragile.
- **View-dependent artifacts.** All methods can hallucinate plausible but incorrect specularities; 3DGS's spherical harmonics have limited angular bandwidth compared with NeRF's MLP, softening sharp reflections [3].
- **Memory scaling.** Hash grids and Gaussian sets grow with scene complexity; city-scale reconstruction demands streaming and level-of-detail schemes that are still research frontiers.
- **Pose dependence.** All methods assume known camera poses (typically from COLMAP); joint pose and radiance optimization is unstable for wide-baseline captures.
- **Editing and semantics.** Implicit fields resist intuitive editing; Gaussian primitives are more manipulable but still lack semantic structure.

## 7 Conclusion

The arc from NeRF to 3D Gaussian Splatting is, at bottom, a story about *where intelligence resides in a representation*. NeRF [1] placed it in a learned function, paying for generality with compute. Hash-grid encodings [2] and Plenoxels [7] moved it into data structures, paying with memory. Mip-NeRF [4, 5] and Zip-NeRF [6] showed that scale-awareness is non-negotiable for correctness. 3D Gaussian Splatting [3] moved rendering onto the rasterizer, where GPUs are fastest, while keeping optimization volumetric in spirit.

The emerging synthesis is clear: future representations will be *hybrid, prefiltered, and primitive-friendly* — explicit enough to rasterize, structured enough to prefilter, and learned enough to compress. The remaining hard problems — generalization across scenes, dynamics, relighting, and semantic editing — will likely be solved not by faster versions of today's methods, but by representations that treat radiance as one component of a richer scene understanding. The volumetric revolution began by asking how to render what a camera saw; its next chapter must ask how to understand what is actually there.
