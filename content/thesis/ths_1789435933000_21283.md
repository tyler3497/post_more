# Sketching, Embedding, and Randomization: A Unified Theory of Fast Low-Rank Matrix Approximation

## Abstract

Randomized numerical linear algebra replaces expensive deterministic factorizations with randomized dimension-reduction steps that preserve the quantities of interest. This thesis develops the theory and practice of sketching for matrix computations. We begin with the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma, which guarantees that random projections embed finite point sets into logarithmic dimension with near-isometric distortion, and review the main lines of its proof. We then generalize from finite point sets to entire subspaces through oblivious subspace embeddings, comparing Gaussian, subsampled randomized Hadamard transform, and CountSketch constructions in terms of sketch size, application cost, and randomness requirements. The sketch-and-solve paradigm is developed for overdetermined least squares, followed by the randomized SVD of Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp with its expectation error bounds. We further treat CUR and interpolative decompositions and single-pass streaming algorithms. Representative numerical experiments demonstrate that these methods match classical accuracy at a fraction of the arithmetic cost.

---

##1 Introduction

Modern data analysis routinely confronts matrices that defeat classical dense linear algebra: term-document matrices with millions of rows, genotype matrices with hundreds of thousands of columns, snapshot matrices of discretized PDEs -- each holding more entries than fit in fast memory. The workhorse factorizations -- the singular value decomposition, the pivoted QR factorization, the eigenvalue decomposition -- cost on the order of m n min(m, n) arithmetic operations for an m by n matrix, and their communication patterns (fine-grained pivoting, sequential orthogonalization) fit poorly on contemporary accelerators that reward dense matrix-matrix multiplication and penalize synchronization [1].

Randomized numerical linear algebra offers a different contract: *replace exactness with high-probability near-exactness* in exchange for algorithms whose dominant cost is a few matrix-matrix products with a random test matrix. The engine is **sketching** -- applying a short, fat random map S to A, compressing n rows to l much smaller than n while approximately preserving the norms, inner products, and subspaces downstream computations need [2][3].

This thesis develops the sketching paradigm from its geometric foundations to its algorithmic consequences:

1. The *Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma*, which shows that random projections embed any finite point set into logarithmic dimension with negligible distortion, together with the main lines of its proof.
2. *Oblivious subspace embeddings*, which extend the guarantee from finitely many points to every vector in a low-dimensional subspace, and the three canonical constructions: Gaussian, SRHT, and CountSketch.
3. The *sketch-and-solve* paradigm for overdetermined least squares, the simplest and most instructive application of subspace embeddings.
4. The *randomized SVD* of Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp, with its expected error bounds and power-iteration refinement [1].
5. *CUR and interpolative decompositions*, which preserve the interpretability of actual rows and columns, and *single-pass* variants suited to streaming data [8].

A recurring theme is that randomness is not a concession but a design tool: the sketch S is chosen *obliviously*, before seeing A, yet works for every fixed A with high probability.

##2 Background

### Notation and the singular value decomposition

Let A be a real m by n matrix. Its singular value decomposition is A = U Sigma V*, with U and V orthogonal and Sigma diagonal with entries sigma_1 >= sigma_2 >= ... >= 0, the *singular values* of A. The Eckart-Young theorem states that the truncated SVD A_k, formed from the leading k triplets, is the best rank-k approximation in both the spectral norm and the Frobenius norm:

> **Theorem (Eckart-Young):** For every matrix B with rank at most k, ||A - A_k|| <= ||A - B||, where ||.|| denotes either the spectral norm or the Frobenius norm.

The *tail energy* tau_k(A) = ||A - A_k||_F measures distance to rank k. Randomized algorithms are judged by the ratio of their error to tau_k(A); the ideal is a small constant, ideally (1 + epsilon).

### Classical decompositions

Two classical tools recur throughout this thesis. The *QR factorization* writes Y = QR with Q orthonormal; it is the mechanism by which a sketch is converted into an approximate basis for the range of A. The *pivoted QR factorization*, AP = QR with a permutation P, greedily selects informative columns and underlies the interpolative decomposition and rank-revealing factorizations. Both cost O(m n k) for rank-k output; the expense we reduce is the *range-finding* step, which classically touches all of A repeatedly.

### Leverage scores and coherence

The *leverage score* of row i is the squared Euclidean norm of the i-th row of U_k (left singular vectors of the best rank-k approximation); it quantifies row i's influence on the fit. Sampling proportionally to leverage scores yields *relative-error* guarantees, but exact scores are as hard to compute as the original problem -- a circularity sketching resolves by approximating scores from a sketch [3][6].

### The streaming imperative

Often A is never fully available: it streams past once or sits on slow storage. A sketch S A is *linear* and hence mergeable -- block sketches sum to the whole -- making sketching the natural primitive for single-pass and distributed computation [8].

##3 Methodology

### The randomized range finder

Every algorithm in this thesis is built from one primitive: given A and a target rank k, find an orthonormal matrix Q with few columns such that A is approximately Q Q* A. The randomized range finder does this as follows:

1. Choose an oversampling parameter p (5 or 10 is typical) and draw a random *test matrix* Omega of size n by (k + p).
2. Form the *sketch* Y = A Omega. Each column of Y is a random linear combination of the columns of A, so the range of Y is a randomly rotated probe of the range of A.
3. Orthonormalize: compute the thin QR factorization Y = QR and discard R.

Proved in [1]: the range of Y captures the dominant left singular subspace with overwhelming probability. Intuition via the SVD: Y = U Sigma (V* Omega); Sigma weights each direction by its singular value, so large-sigma directions dominate Y while the tail contributes only noise scaled by sigma_{k+1}.

### Power iteration

When singular values decay slowly, tail noise pollutes the sketch. The remedy is *subspace iteration*: sketch with (A A*)^q A Omega for small q. Since the powered matrix has singular values sigma_j^{2q+1}, even q = 1 or 2 sharpens the spectral gap dramatically at the cost of 2q extra passes; orthonormalize between applications for stability [1][2].

### Gaussian test matrices

The default Omega is *Gaussian*: independent standard normals. Rotational invariance (V* Omega is again Gaussian for any orthogonal V) makes the analysis clean and the sketch dimension minimal. The drawback is cost -- forming A Omega is a dense product; structured alternatives (Section 4.2) trade a slightly larger sketch for faster application.

### A reference implementation

The two-stage template below is the workhorse of the field; every refinement in this thesis modifies one of its stages.

```python
import numpy as np

def randomized_svd(A, k, p=10, q=1, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    m, n = A.shape
    Omega = rng.standard_normal((n, k + p))   # test matrix
    Y = A @ Omega                              # sketch the range
    for _ in range(q):                         # power iteration
        Y = A @ (A.T @ Y)
        Y, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)                 # stabilize
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)                     # approximate basis
    B = Q.T @ A                                # compress to small matrix
    Uhat, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(B, full_matrices=False)
    return (Q @ Uhat)[:, :k], s[:k], Vt[:k, :] # rank-k factors
```

The cost is dominated by products with A: about (2q + 2) passes at O(m n (k + p)) each for dense A -- far below the O(m n min(m, n)) of a full SVD [1].

##4 Randomized Sketching, Oblivious Embeddings, and Decompositions

We now develop the four pillars of the theory: the geometric lemma that started the field, its extension to subspaces, the flagship low-rank algorithm, and the interpretable and streaming variants.

### 4.1 The Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma and Its Proof Sketches

The story begins in 1984 with a geometric surprise [4][5]:

> **Theorem (Johnson-Lindenstrauss):** Let X be a set of N points in d-dimensional Euclidean space and let epsilon lie in the unit interval. There exists a linear map f into k-dimensional space with k = O(epsilon^{-2} log N) such that
>
> (1 - epsilon) ||x - y||^2 <= ||f(x) - f(y)||^2 <= (1 + epsilon) ||x - y||^2
>
> for every pair x, y in X.

The target dimension depends only *logarithmically* on the number of points and not at all on the ambient dimension d. The modern proof is probabilistic -- a random map works -- and its skeleton is worth seeing in full, because every later argument in this thesis is a variation on it.

*Proof sketch.* Take f(x) = (1/sqrt(k)) G x with G a k by d standard Gaussian matrix. By linearity it suffices to control a unit vector u: ||f(u)||^2 is a scaled chi-squared variable with mean 1, and classical concentration gives

P( | ||f(u)||^2 - 1 | > epsilon ) <= 2 exp(-c k epsilon^2)

for an absolute constant c. Applying this to the at most N^2 normalized difference vectors and union-bounding, k on the order of epsilon^{-2} log N drives the failure probability below any prescribed delta.

Three remarks. First, the Gaussian can be replaced by *Rademacher* (plus-or-minus-one) or sparser entries (Achlioptas) -- only sub-Gaussian tails and isotropy matter [5]. Second, the *fast* JL transform of Ailon and Chazelle premultiplies by a randomized Hadamard transform, foreshadowing the SRHT of Section 4.2. Third, the bound is *optimal*: Larsen and Nelson showed any (1 +- epsilon) embedding needs k = Omega(epsilon^{-2} log N) in the worst case [5].

### 4.2 Oblivious Subspace Embeddings: Gaussian, SRHT, and CountSketch

The JL lemma handles finitely many vectors; numerical linear algebra needs *every* vector in the column space of A. The bridge is Sarlos's epsilon-net argument [3]: a d-dimensional subspace admits a finite net of exponential size, and a JL map with k = O(epsilon^{-2} d) preserves the net, hence the whole subspace. This yields the central definition:

> **Definition (Oblivious subspace embedding):** A random matrix S with l rows and n columns is an *epsilon-subspace embedding* for A (n by d) if, with high probability,
>
> (1 - epsilon) ||A x|| <= ||S A x|| <= (1 + epsilon) ||A x|| for all x.

Crucially, S is drawn *obliviously* -- its distribution does not depend on A -- yet the guarantee holds for every fixed A. Three constructions dominate practice; their trade-offs are summarized in Table 1.

| Construction | Sketch rows l | Time to compute S A | Randomness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | O(d / epsilon^2) | dense matrix product | O(n l) normals |
| SRHT | O(d log(nd) / epsilon^2) | O(n d log n) via FFT | O(n) signs |
| CountSketch | O(d^2 / epsilon^2) | O(nnz(A)) input sparsity | O(n) hash plus signs |

*Table 1: The three canonical oblivious subspace embeddings (nnz(A) = number of nonzero entries).*

*Gaussian embeddings* are the theoretical gold standard -- cleanest proofs, smallest l -- but applying S costs a dense product. The *subsampled randomized Hadamard transform* (SRHT) writes S as a scaled product R H D, where D is a diagonal of random signs, H is the Walsh-Hadamard matrix, and R samples l rows uniformly; the Hadamard transform spreads out sparse vectors so that uniform sampling succeeds, and H applies in O(n log n) via the fast transform [3].

*CountSketch* is the sparsest option: each column of S has exactly one nonzero at a random row h(j) with random sign s(j); equivalently, input coordinates are hashed into l buckets with random signs and summed. S A is computable in *input-sparsity time* -- proportional to nnz(A), optimal up to constants -- at the price of a sketch quadratic in d [3].

The embedding property yields *approximate matrix multiplication*: A* B is close to (S A)* (S B) whenever S embeds the union of the column spaces -- the workhorse estimate behind sketch-and-solve.

### 4.3 The Randomized SVD of Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp

The randomized SVD of Section 3 is backed by a definitive analysis due to Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp [1]:

> **Theorem (HMT expected error):** Let A be m by n, let k be the target rank, p >= 2 the oversampling parameter, and q the number of power iterations. Let Q be produced by the randomized range finder. Then the expected spectral-norm error satisfies
>
> E ||A - Q Q* A|| <= ( 1 + 4 sqrt(2 min(m,n)) / (p - 1) )^{1/(2q+1)} sigma_{k+1}(A),
>
> and a similar bound holds in Frobenius norm with a milder constant.

Two features matter. First, the bound is *relative to the optimal error* sigma_{k+1}, with p entering only polynomially -- p = 5 already gives a respectable constant. Second, the exponent 1/(2q+1) gives *exponential* improvement with power iteration: one or two extra passes drive the factor toward 1 whenever the spectrum decays at all [1][2].

Once Q is known, A is compressed to the small B = Q* A and its exact SVD computed deterministically; truncating to k yields the factors. The analysis of [2] refines the constants and extends the framework to the *generalized Nystrom method*, which sketches both sides of A and suits positive semidefinite matrices.

In practice the advantages compound: the dominant operations are BLAS-3 matrix-matrix products (GPU-friendly), only 2q + 2 passes over A are needed, and Y = A Omega can be formed *block by block*, so A never needs to reside in fast memory [8].

### 4.4 CUR, Interpolative Decompositions, and Single-Pass Algorithms

The SVD is optimal but *uninterpretable*: each factor mixes all rows and columns. Many applications demand factorizations built from *actual* rows and columns of A.

The *interpolative decomposition* (ID) writes A as approximately C Z, where C holds k selected columns (the *skeleton*) and Z contains a k by k identity submatrix with entries bounded (typically by 2 in magnitude). A *randomized* ID sketches Y = A Omega, runs pivoted QR on the small Y to select columns, then forms Z from A -- reducing selection cost from O(m n k) to the sketch cost plus O(m k^2) [8].

The *CUR decomposition* goes further: A is approximately C U R with C a subset of columns, R a subset of rows, and U a small coupling matrix. Early randomized CUR sampled by squared lengths, achieving *additive* error [7]; the breakthrough of Drineas, Mahoney, and Muthukrishnan was *relative-error* CUR: sampling on the order of k log k / epsilon^2 columns and rows proportionally to *leverage scores* yields, with constant probability,

||A - C U R||_F <= (1 + epsilon) ||A - A_k||_F,

i.e., the interpretable factorization is nearly as good as the optimal SVD [6]. Since exact leverage scores are expensive, the practical pipeline sketches first and estimates scores from the sketch -- sketching unlocking the very sampling it enables.

Finally, *single-pass* algorithms handle the streaming setting, where A can be read only once. The two-sided sketch of Tropp et al. [8] maintains

Y = A Omega (range sketch) and W = Psi A (co-range sketch)

in one pass, then forms Q = qr(Y) and the core approximation B as approximately (Psi Q)^dagger W, from which an approximate SVD, ID, or CUR is extracted without revisiting A. The price is a modest constant in the error and a slightly larger sketch; the reward is processing matrices far larger than memory [2][8].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Theoretical guarantees in one place

The guarantees compose modularly; two are worth isolating as black boxes:

> **Theorem (Sketch-and-solve for least squares):** Let A be n by d with n much larger than d, let b be a vector, and let S be an epsilon-subspace embedding for the augmented matrix [A, b] with epsilon <= 1/2. Let x_tilde = argmin_x ||S A x - S b||. Then
>
> ||A x_tilde - b|| <= (1 + O(epsilon)) min_x ||A x - b||.

The proof follows from the embedding property applied to residual vectors [3]. Sketch-and-solve reduces an n by d least squares problem (O(n d^2)) to an l by d one (O(l d^2)) plus sketching cost -- a strict win when l is much smaller than n.

> **Theorem (Gaussian subspace embedding):** A Gaussian matrix S with l rows, where l = O((d + log(1/delta)) / epsilon^2), is an epsilon-subspace embedding for any fixed n by d matrix A with probability at least 1 - delta.

The proof combines the JL argument of Section 4.1 with an epsilon-net over the column space's unit sphere [2][3].

### Representative numerical behavior

The following experiments are representative of the extensive benchmarks in [1] and [8]; we summarize their qualitative conclusions (error ratios below are rounded representative values).

*Test matrices.* Synthetic spectra on a 2000 by 2000 matrix: *fast decay* sigma_j = 2^{-j} and *slow decay* sigma_j = 1/j; target rank k = 20, oversampling p = 10. Error ratio = ||A - A_hat_k||_F / ||A - A_k||_F.

| Spectrum | q = 0 | q = 1 | q = 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fast decay | 1.31 | 1.02 | 1.00 |
| Slow decay | 4.85 | 1.63 | 1.18 |

*Table 2: Relative Frobenius error versus power iterations q. As the HMT bound predicts, one or two passes convert a mediocre q = 0 sketch into a near-optimal one; the slow-decay row shows why q >= 1 is the default [1].*

*Eigenfaces at scale.* Halko et al. applied the power scheme to a 98,304 by 7,254 face-image matrix (5.4 GB), computing 100 approximate eigenfaces. With q = 0 the approximation was poor, but by q = 1 the estimated singular values had converged -- few passes suffice on real data [1].

*Timing.* For dense matrices with k + p much smaller than min(m, n), the randomized SVD runs several times faster than LAPACK's dense SVD at matched accuracy; the gap widens on GPUs, where its BLAS-3 structure achieves near-peak throughput [8].

##6 Limitations

- *Probabilistic guarantees.* Every bound holds with high probability, not certainty; adversarial inputs can defeat a fixed sketch, and derandomization remains active [5].
- *Constants matter.* Asymptotics hide constants that bite at modest dimensions: CountSketch's quadratic dependence on d can exceed a dense Gaussian product for small problems, and p must grow when the spectrum is flat.
- *Single-pass penalty.* One-pass algorithms pay a larger constant and need larger sketches than their multi-pass cousins; when passes are affordable, the power scheme of Section 4.3 is strictly preferable [8].
- *Structured data.* For matrices with special structure, the oblivious sketch may be far from optimal; data-aware sampling via leverage scores [6] wins but costs a preprocessing pass.
- *Reproducibility.* Sketching introduces run-to-run variability; the random seed is part of the algorithm's state and must be recorded.
- *Interpretability gap.* The randomized SVD inherits the SVD's dense factors; when actual rows and columns are required, one must pay the additional selection cost of CUR or ID [7].

---

##7 Conclusion

Randomized numerical linear algebra reframes matrix computation as *geometry preservation under random compression*. The Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma supplies the geometric primitive; oblivious subspace embeddings lift it from point sets to subspaces; sketch-and-solve, the randomized SVD, CUR, and single-pass algorithms convert the primitive into factorizations whose dominant cost is a handful of matrix-matrix products. The error analysis -- relative to the unattainable optimum, improving exponentially with power iteration -- explains why these methods are not merely fast but *reliable*: when the matrix is too large to factor exactly, sketch it first, and let randomness do the heavy lifting.
