# Billion-Scale Approximate Nearest-Neighbor Search: Hierarchical Graphs, Disk-Resident Indices, Product Quantization, and the Recall–Latency Frontier

## Abstract

Approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) search over billion-vector corpora is the computational backbone of retrieval-augmented language models, large-scale recommendation, and semantic search, yet exact search is provably intractable at this scale and in-memory indices collapse under terabyte-scale embeddings. This thesis unifies the three dominant algorithmic families — graph-based navigation (HNSW, Vamana/DiskANN), disk-resident hybrid indices, and compression-driven quantization (IVF-PQ) — into a single analytical framework, deriving the memory, latency, and quantization-error bounds that govern billion-scale design. We present the probabilistic layering of HNSW, the beam-search traversal and leader-set design of DiskANN's Vamana graph, and the distortion theory of product quantization including asymmetric distance computation, with quantitative results on the SIFT1B and DEEP1B benchmarks and the NeurIPS'21 Big-ANN challenge. We characterize the recall–latency Pareto frontier, quantify quantization error propagation, and derive serving-cost models for dense LLM embeddings at 1B–100B scale.

## 1 Introduction

The modern retrieval stack has a single, dominant primitive: given a query vector *q ∈ ℝᵈ* and a corpus *X = {x₁, …, xₙ}* with *n* on the order of 10⁹, return the *k* vectors minimizing *d(q, xᵢ)*. When *d* = 768 and vectors are single-precision floats, the corpus alone occupies **3 TB**; with *d* = 4096 (contemporary LLM embeddings), it occupies **16 TB**. A brute-force scan costs *O(nd)* floating-point operations per query — roughly **0.75 TFLOP** for a billion 768-dimensional vectors — far beyond interactive latency budgets even on flagship accelerators. Classical space-partitioning indices (kd-trees, ball trees) degrade to near-linear search above a few dozen dimensions [1].

Approximate nearest-neighbor search abandons exactness for orders-of-magnitude efficiency, accepting a recall penalty *ρ = |returned ∩ true top-k| / k* typically in the 0.9–0.999 range. Three architectural families have survived the winnowing of a decade of research and now divide the production landscape:

1. **Proximity graphs** — Hierarchical Navigable Small Worlds (HNSW) [2] and the Vamana graph underlying DiskANN [3] dominate the recall–latency frontier on DRAM-resident corpora.
2. **Disk-resident hybrid indices** — DiskANN [3] and its successors place compressed vectors in RAM and full-precision vectors on NVMe SSDs, reaching billion scale on a *single commodity node*.
3. **Compression indices** — Inverted-file product quantization (IVF-PQ) [4] reduces vectors to 8–64 *bytes* each, enabling billion-scale indices in tens of gigabytes with GPU-exhaustive search [5].

These families embody a fundamental tension: graph traversal minimizes *distance computations per query* (latency), while quantization minimizes *bytes per vector* (memory). At billion scale the two interact — graphs need vectors in RAM, quantization introduces distortion that propagates through graph routing — and the frontier is no longer separable. This thesis analyzes all three, their interaction, and their deployment in serving LLM embeddings.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Problem formulation

Given corpus *X ⊂ ℝᵈ*, |*X*| = *n*, and metric *d(·,·)* (typically squared Euclidean *ℓ₂* or inner product), the *k*-ANN problem asks for *S ⊆ X*, |*S*| = *k*, such that *S* approximates the true *k* nearest neighbors *T(q)*. Recall is measured as *R@k = |S ∩ T(q)| / k* and, following the Big-ANN protocol [6], as *R@1* and *R@100* for the top-1 and top-100 queries. Latency is reported as mean or p99 milliseconds per query, and throughput as queries per second (QPS) under a fixed recall floor — the standard is to sweep the recall–latency curve rather than report a single point.

### 2.2 The curse and the budget

The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma guarantees that pairwise distances survive random projection to *O(log n / ε²)* dimensions, but *n = 10⁹* still demands *d ≈ 200+* for ε = 0.1 — the regime where tree indices fail. The fundamental resource identity at billion scale is bytes-per-vector:

| Method | Bytes / vector | 1B vectors |
|---|---|---|
| Float32 raw | 512 (d=128) | 512 GB |
| PQ, 8 subquantizers | 8 | 8 GB |
| PQ, 64 subquantizers | 64 | 64 GB |
| Binary codes | 16 | 16 GB |
| OPQ + ADC rerank | 8 + 512 (disk) | 8 GB RAM + 512 GB SSD |

This table is the entire motivation for the field: raw storage exceeds commodity RAM by an order of magnitude, while 8-byte codes fit comfortably. The question is how much *accuracy* 8 bytes buy — and how to route a query to the right 8 bytes efficiently.

### 2.3 Small-world navigability

Kleinberg's analysis of navigable small worlds showed that greedy routing succeeds in *O(log² n)* hops when edge probabilities decay as *r⁻ᵈ* with dimensionality — the theoretical seed from which all modern proximity graphs grow. The HNSW construction [2] replaces explicit distance-decay wiring with a *hierarchical* decomposition: links are separated by characteristic distance scale, and search descends from sparse long-range layers to the dense base layer, mirroring the skip-list's probabilistic tower structure.

> **Theorem 1.** *(Malkov–Yashunin layering.)* If each element is assigned to layer *L* with *P(L = ℓ) = (1 − m_L) m_L^ℓ* for normalization constant *m_L*, then the expected number of layers is *O(log n)*, the expected degree per layer is bounded, and greedy search from the top-layer entry point terminates at the true nearest neighbor's neighborhood in *O(log n)* expected hops under the navigable-small-world wiring.

The skip-list analogy is not cosmetic: it yields the same logarithmic height distribution and, crucially, suggests straightforward balanced distributed implementations [2].

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is a comparative-analytical study: we reconstruct each index family's construction and query algorithms, derive their asymptotic resource costs, and calibrate the constants against published billion-scale measurements from the SIFT1B/DEEP1B benchmarks, the GPU similarity-search literature [5], and the NeurIPS'21 Big-ANN challenge [6].

**Corpus accounting.** For a graph index with out-degree *M* and bidirectional edges, per-node overhead is approximately *2·M·4 + 4* bytes for adjacency plus the vector payload; for *n = 10⁹*, *d = 128*, *M = 16*, this is ~640 GB — the figure that forces either quantization or disk. For IVF-PQ with *nlist* coarse cells and *m*-byte codes, index memory is *nlist·d·4 + n·m + n·4* bytes (centroids + codes + ids).

**Query accounting.** Graph search cost is dominated by distance computations: *C ≈ L·B̄·d* where *L* is candidate-list size (*ef* or beam width) and *B̄* the mean visited-neighborhood expansion. IVF-PQ cost is *nlist_visited·d* coarse comparisons plus *nprobe·(n/nlist)·m* table lookups — the *m* lookups per candidate are the celebrated asymmetry that makes PQ fast.

**Error accounting.** We decompose total error into *quantization distortion* (codec-induced), *routing error* (greedy search missing the true neighborhood), and *pruning error* (aggressive degree caps). Section 4.4 formalizes how these compose.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 HNSW: hierarchical graph construction and the neighbor-selection heuristic

HNSW builds a stack of navigable-small-world layers *G₀ ⊇ G₁ ⊇ … ⊇ G_max*, where *G₀* contains every element and membership thins exponentially upward. Insertion of a new element *q* proceeds top-down: at each layer *ℓ ≤ L(q)* a greedy search from the current entry point collects *efConstruction* candidates, and the *M* (or *2M* at layer 0) nearest are linked bidirectionally. The decisive innovation over flat NSW is the **neighbor-selection heuristic**: rather than connecting the *M* nearest candidates, HNSW keeps a candidate *e* only if no already-selected neighbor *v* satisfies *d(e,v) < d(e,q)* — i.e., it maximizes *directional diversity*, preventing the clumping that traps greedy search in highly clustered data [2].

```python
def hnsw_search(layer_graphs, q, k, ef):
    # greedy descent from top layer
    ep = top_entry_point
    for lc in range(Lmax, 0, -1):
        ep = greedy_move(layer_graphs[lc], q, ep)   # 1-NN at this scale
    # beam search at layer 0
    W = beam_search(layer_graphs[0], q, ep, ef)     # ef-sized candidate pool
    return top_k(W, k)

def greedy_move(G, q, ep):
    while True:
        nxt = argmin(G.neighbors(ep), key=lambda v: dist(q, v))
        if dist(q, nxt) >= dist(q, ep):
            return ep
        ep = nxt
```

The three tuning knobs — *M* (connectivity), *efConstruction* (build-time candidate pool), and *ef* (query-time beam width) — trace the recall–latency curve. Larger *ef* widens the frontier: each doubling of *ef* buys roughly a fixed recall increment at near-linear latency cost. Empirically, *M = 16–32* and *efConstruction = 200–500* are the production standard for 10⁹-scale corpora where memory permits [5, 6].

The Achilles' heel is well documented: random insertion order can create *disconnected regions* and *local optima*, and the advertised *O(n log n)* construction is fragile in high dimensions, where exhaustive per-layer traversal inflates build cost dramatically. Recent work (e.g., LID-aware insertion schemes, HNSW-ASU variants) attacks precisely these weaknesses, reporting 2.5–3.3× QPS gains at 98%+ recall with 75% less graph memory — evidence that the classical construction leaves substantial headroom.

### 4.2 DiskANN: the Vamana graph and single-node billion-scale serving

DiskANN [3] answers the memory question differently: keep the *graph navigable from SSD*. Its Vamana graph is flat (single layer) with fixed out-degree *R* (typically 32–64), and its query algorithm is **beam search with compressed distances**: full-precision vectors live on NVMe SSD, while *product-quantized* vectors and a small set of entry points ("leader set", ~1% of nodes) reside in RAM. A query expands a beam of *L* candidates using PQ-estimated distances (pure RAM traffic), then **reranks** the finalist set with full-precision vectors fetched from SSD — a handful of random 4 KB reads per query.

The headline result remains striking: **>5,000 QPS at <3 ms mean latency with 95%+ 1-recall@1 on SIFT1B (1B × 128-d), on a single 64 GB / 16-core box with one SSD** [3]. The Vamana pruning rule generalizes HNSW's diversity heuristic with an explicit distance-dominance parameter *α*: neighbor *e* is retained iff *d(e, q) < α · min_{v selected} d(e, v)*, a relaxation that trades construction-time graph quality against degree.

> **Theorem 2.** *(DiskANN beam-search correctness sketch.)* Under the Vamana degree bound *R* and α-pruning, the greedy beam search with beam width *L* visits a monotone-decreasing distance sequence from the leader set; if *L* exceeds the graph's expansion diameter, the returned set contains the true nearest neighbor. Reranking with exact vectors then restores *R@1* to within the PQ distortion floor.

DiskANN's deployment record — Microsoft Bing semantic search and Azure Cognitive Search, with ports into Milvus and pgvector — makes it the reference disk-resident design. Its successors (FreshDiskANN for streaming inserts, Starling, SPANN with clustering-based posting lists) refine the memory/disk split but preserve the two-phase compressed-search + exact-rerank skeleton.

```rust
// Asymmetric distance computation: the PQ fast path used by DiskANN's beam search
fn adc(query: &[f32], code: &[u8], tables: &[&[f32]]) -> f32 {
    let mut acc = 0.0f32;
    for (m, &idx) in code.iter().enumerate() {
        let sub = &query[m * DSUB .. (m + 1) * DSUB];
        // tables[m][idx] precomputes ||q_m - c_{m,idx}||^2
        acc += tables[m][idx as usize];
    }
    acc // m lookups + m adds per candidate: ~8 flops per vector at m=8
}
```

### 4.3 IVF-PQ: inverted files meet product quantization

Product quantization [4] decomposes *ℝᵈ* into a Cartesian product of *m* subspaces *ℝ^{d/m}* and learns, per subspace, a codebook of *k* = 256 centroids (8-bit indices). A vector is encoded as *m* bytes; its reconstruction *q(x)* is the concatenation of the chosen centroids. The masterstroke is **asymmetric distance computation (ADC)**: distances *‖q − q(xᵢ)‖²* are estimated via per-subspace lookup tables built once per query in *O(d·k)* time, then each database vector costs only *m* table lookups and additions — no multiplies. Symmetric distance (SDC) is cheaper to precompute but measurably less accurate, and the literature has settled on ADC [4, 5].

The inverted file (IVFADC) adds a coarse quantizer with *nlist* cells (typically 2¹⁶–2²⁰ at billion scale): each vector is assigned to its nearest centroid, and the database stores per-cell posting lists of PQ codes. Querying visits *nprobe* cells. The recall dials are *(nlist, nprobe, m)*: more cells localize the search, more probes recover recall, more bytes shrink distortion.

A canonical billion-scale configuration from the RobustiQ study [5]:

| Codebook | R@1 | R@10 | R@100 | Query time (GPU) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Faiss, 8 B | 0.138 | 0.443 | 0.798 | 0.31 ms |
| Faiss, 16 B (2 GPUs) | 0.318 | 0.783 | 0.962 | 0.28 ms |
| RobustiQ, 8 B | 0.173 | 0.540 | 0.860 | 0.10 ms |
| RobustiQ, 16 B (2 GPUs) | 0.404 | 0.797 | 0.947 | 0.10 ms |

Two observations matter: (i) doubling code bytes buys ~15–17 points of R@1; (ii) GPU-exhaustive PQ search reaches **0.03–0.10 ms** per query at billion scale — the latency floor against which graph indices compete [5].

```haskell
-- PQ encoding as a product of subquantizers (conceptual)
encode :: [Codebook] -> Vector -> Code
encode codebooks x = map (\(cb, sub) -> argmin cb sub) (zip codebooks (split x))

-- total distortion decomposes additively over subspaces
distortion :: [Codebook] -> [Vector] -> Double
distortion cbs xs = sum [ sum [ (norm (sub - nearest cb sub))^2
                             | (cb, sub) <- zip cbs (split x) ] | x <- xs ]
```

### 4.4 Quantization error analysis

PQ distortion *D = E[‖x − q(x)‖²]* decomposes additively over subspaces, and ADC's distance *estimation error* is bounded by the triangle inequality: *|‖q−x‖ − ‖q−q(x)‖| ≤ ‖x − q(x)‖*. Hence recall degradation is controlled by *per-subspace* distortion — which is why **optimized PQ (OPQ)** learns an orthonormal rotation *R* minimizing *D* before quantization, decorrelating dimensions so the naive subspace split is near-optimal. Locally optimized variants (LOPQ) go further, learning per-cell rotations.

The error *composes* with graph routing: DiskANN's beam search ranks candidates by distorted distances, so a vector whose true distance is small but whose distortion is large can be pruned before reranking. The analysis gives a clean bound: if beam width *L* satisfies *L ≥ k + #{x : ‖q−x‖ − ‖q−q(x)‖ > δ}* for distortion radius *δ*, then no true top-*k* element is pruned by distortion alone. In practice this is why DiskANN pairs *aggressive* PQ (few bytes, RAM-cheap) with a *generous* beam and exact rerank — the beam absorbs the distortion, and the SSD pass corrects it. The Inverted Multi-Index (IMI) pushes the same logic further, partitioning the space into a Cartesian product of coarse cells to reduce memory below 64 GB for 1B × 128-d vectors, at the cost of ~60% R@1 — a vivid illustration of the memory–accuracy exchange rate.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation

**Benchmarks.** SIFT1B (1B × 128-d SIFT descriptors, 10k queries) and DEEP1B (1B × 96-d deep descriptors, 10k queries) remain the canonical billion-scale datasets, introduced with the re-ranking framework of Jégou et al. The **NeurIPS'21 Big-ANN challenge** [6] standardized evaluation across three hardware tracks: T1 (limited DRAM, FAISS baseline), T2 (DRAM + inexpensive SSD, DiskANN baseline), and T3 (unrestricted hardware) — the first consensus comparison of graph, quantization, and GPU-exhaustive approaches at billion scale on cost-normalized hardware.

**Graph vs. quantization, head to head.** On DRAM-rich hardware, HNSW variants own the high-recall frontier (R@100 > 0.99 at single-digit ms); on DRAM-constrained hardware, DiskANN's SSD design matches HNSW-class recall with two orders of magnitude less RAM; on GPU hardware, exhaustive IVF-PQ wins raw QPS by avoiding graph irregularity entirely [5, 6]. No family dominates all three tracks — the frontier is a *surface* over (RAM, SSD, GPU) hardware axes.

**Serving LLM embeddings.** Production RAG corpora use 768–4096-d embeddings where 1B vectors occupy 3–16 TB raw. The viable serving recipes are: (i) DiskANN-style hybrid indices with OPQ-compressed RAM vectors and full-precision SSD rerank; (ii) sharded HNSW over GPU/DRAM fleets (the OpenSearch vector-acceleration benchmark indexed 1B × 1024-d vectors with 0.93 R@100 at ~26 ms p50 using GPU build acceleration); (iii) IVF-PQ with multi-GPU exhaustive scan for the tightest latency SLOs. Filtering (metadata predicates) and multi-tenancy remain the open engineering frontier — most published numbers are for *unfiltered* search.

**The Pareto frontier, quantitatively.** Across published billion-scale results, the frontier is well approximated by *latency ∝ 1/(1 − ρ)^γ* with *γ ≈ 1.5–2* near the top: the last two points of recall cost more than the first ninety. This super-linearity is the practitioner's rule of thumb: specify *ρ = 0.95* when 0.99 is unaffordable, because the marginal cost curve is steepest exactly where intuition says accuracy is "almost free."

## 6 Limitations

1. **Dynamic corpora.** HNSW supports incremental insertion but deletions leave tombstones and degrade graph quality; DiskANN's FreshDiskANN and PipeANN/OdinANN address streaming insert/delete on SSD at the cost of background consolidation and temporarily elevated recall variance.
2. **Distribution shift.** All three families assume the query distribution resembles the corpus distribution. Out-of-distribution queries (e.g., cross-lingual or cross-modal) defeat both coarse quantizers and graph entry points; entry-point selection is the least-studied failure mode.
3. **Quantization bias.** PQ distortion is data-dependent: rare subpopulations quantize worse, so recall is systematically lower for tail queries — a fairness concern in retrieval over skewed corpora.
4. **Dimensional scaling.** At *d = 4096*, even 64-byte PQ codes (64 subquantizers × 8 bits) give coarse reconstructions; the memory math forces either aggressive compression with rerank or graph indices with terabyte RAM. Billion-scale *d > 2048* remains an open efficiency problem.
5. **Filtered search.** Predicate-filtered ANN (the production norm) invalidates precomputed posting lists and graph neighborhoods; current solutions (filtered beam search, partitioned indices) lack the maturity of the unfiltered frontier.
6. **Theoretical gaps.** No tight lower bound connects *bytes-per-vector* to achievable *(recall, latency)*; the Pareto surface is mapped empirically, not derived.

## 7 Conclusion

Billion-scale ANN search is a solved-in-practice, open-in-theory discipline. The three families — hierarchical graphs, disk-resident hybrids, and compression indices — are not competitors but *points on a single resource surface* parameterized by RAM, SSD, and accelerator budgets. HNSW's probabilistic layering gives the best recall per distance computation; DiskANN's Vamana graph with PQ-compressed beam search and SSD rerank gives the best recall per dollar of RAM; IVF-PQ gives the best recall per byte. The unifying principle is the distortion budget: quantization error must be absorbed by search redundancy (beam width, probes) and corrected by an exact final pass. For LLM-embedding serving at 1B+ scale, the practical prescription is a hybrid index — compressed vectors in RAM, full precision on fast storage, exact rerank — with the recall dial set by the super-linear cost of the final points. The frontier's next movement will come from filtered search, streaming updates, and quantization schemes co-designed with the embedding models themselves.
