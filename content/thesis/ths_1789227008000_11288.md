# Privacy Accounting for Differential Privacy: Rényi Divergence, the Moments Accountant, and Tight Composition for Private Deep Learning

## Abstract

Training deep neural networks on sensitive data creates a fundamental tension: thousands of gradient steps each leak information, and naive privacy accounting renders the cumulative guarantee vacuous. This thesis develops the modern theory of *privacy accounting* — the quantitative discipline of tracking cumulative privacy loss under composition. We present Rényi differential privacy (RDP) [1] and zero-concentrated differential privacy (zCDP) [2] as divergence-based relaxations of differential privacy that compose additively and characterize the Gaussian mechanism exactly. We reconstruct the moments accountant of Abadi et al. [3] as a special case of RDP accounting, derive the closed-form Rényi analysis of the sampled Gaussian mechanism [4], and explain how privacy amplification by subsampling converts linear sampling rates into quadratic privacy gains. Explicit conversion theorems translate RDP and zCDP budgets back into classical $(\epsilon, \delta)$-guarantees, and a reference Python implementation computes an end-to-end DP-SGD budget. We close by surveying limitations — lossy conversions, adaptive composition, hyperparameter-tuning costs, and finite-precision caveats — and next-generation accountants based on Gaussian differential privacy [5] and privacy-loss distributions.

## 1 Introduction

Differential privacy (DP) has become the *de facto* mathematical standard for quantifying the privacy risk of data analyses [8]. A randomized mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\epsilon, \delta)$-differential privacy if, for every pair of neighboring datasets $x, x'$ differing in a single record and every measurable event $S$,

$$\Pr[\mathcal{M}(x) \in S] \;\le\; e^{\epsilon}\,\Pr[\mathcal{M}(x') \in S] + \delta.$$

The parameter $\epsilon$ bounds the worst-case multiplicative change in output likelihood, while $\delta$ permits a small probability of catastrophic failure. When $\delta = 0$, we speak of *pure* DP.

In practice, real workloads are rarely single-shot. Training a neural network invokes a noisy gradient mechanism *thousands of times*, each step consuming part of a global privacy budget. The central question of **privacy accounting** is: *given the per-step privacy cost, what is the total privacy cost of the composite algorithm?* This question is far from academic — it determines whether private deep learning is feasible at all.

The earliest answers were discouraging. *Basic composition* gives $(\sum_i \epsilon_i, \sum_i \delta_i)$ — linear degradation that makes 10,000 training steps look like $\epsilon = 1000$. *Advanced composition* [9] improved this to roughly $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{k}\,\epsilon)$ via concentration, but with loose constants and no use of Gaussian structure.

The breakthrough came from a change of perspective: instead of tracking the *worst-case* privacy loss, track its *entire distribution* through the lens of information-theoretic divergences. Three ideas define the modern accounting toolkit:

- **Rényi differential privacy (RDP)** [1]: bound the Rényi divergence of order $\alpha > 1$ between output distributions on neighboring inputs. RDP composes by *addition* and captures the Gaussian mechanism in closed form.
- **Zero-concentrated differential privacy (zCDP)** [2]: demand the Rényi divergence grow at most *linearly* in the order $\alpha$, i.e. $D_\alpha \le \rho\,\alpha$. A single parameter $\rho$ then governs arbitrarily many compositions.
- **The moments accountant** [3]: track the log moment-generating function of the *privacy loss random variable*, yielding the tight analysis that made differentially private deep learning (DP-SGD) practical.

This thesis unifies these strands. We develop the divergence-based accounting framework from first principles, give the precise Rényi analysis of the *sampled* Gaussian mechanism at the heart of DP-SGD [4], explain privacy amplification by subsampling as the engine of its power, and present a working accountant implementation. We further discuss conversion back to $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP, empirical results on standard benchmarks, and the limitations that motivate the next generation of accountants [5].

## 2 Background

**Neighboring datasets and sensitivity.** Two datasets $x, x' \in \mathcal{X}^n$ are *neighbors* (written $x \sim x'$) if they differ in exactly one record — by addition, removal, or substitution, depending on convention. The $\ell_2$-sensitivity of a function $f : \mathcal{X}^n \to \mathbb{R}^d$ is $\Delta_2(f) = \max_{x \sim x'} \|f(x) - f(x')\|_2$. Sensitivity calibration is what makes the Gaussian mechanism below meaningful [8].

**The privacy loss random variable.** For neighboring inputs $x, x'$, the privacy loss of an output $y$ is $c(y; x, x') = \log \frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(x) = y]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(x') = y]}$; sampling $y \sim \mathcal{M}(x)$ yields the *privacy loss random variable* $Z$. Pure $\epsilon$-DP is the statement $|Z| \le \epsilon$ almost surely, while $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP is approximately $\Pr[Z > \epsilon] \le \delta$ [2]. Under *adaptive composition*, privacy losses *add*: the total loss of $\mathcal{M}_1, \dots, \mathcal{M}_k$ is $Z = \sum_i Z_i$. Composition accounting is therefore the study of *sums of privacy-loss random variables*.

**Rényi divergence.** For distributions $P, Q$ and order $\alpha > 1$, the Rényi divergence is

$$D_\alpha(P \| Q) \;=\; \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \log \sum_y P(y)^\alpha\, Q(y)^{1-\alpha} \;=\; \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \log \mathbb{E}_{y \sim P}\!\left[\left(\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)}\right)^\alpha\right].$$

It generalizes the KL divergence ($\alpha \to 1$) and the max divergence ($\alpha \to \infty$). Two properties make it the ideal accounting primitive: *post-processing immunity* (applying any function to the output cannot increase it) and *additivity under product distributions* — the fact that powers RDP composition [1].

**The Gaussian mechanism.** Releasing $f(x) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ for a sensitivity-$\Delta_2$ query is the canonical continuous mechanism. It does *not* satisfy pure DP (Gaussian tails are too heavy for a worst-case bound), but it satisfies $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP when $\sigma \ge \sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}\,\Delta_2/\epsilon$ [8], and — more elegantly — it has an *exact* Rényi profile $D_\alpha = \alpha\,\Delta_2^2 / (2\sigma^2)$ and satisfies $\rho$-zCDP with $\rho = \Delta_2^2/(2\sigma^2)$ [2]. No inequality is wasted: the divergence-based view is lossless for Gaussians.

**Composition theorems.** The classical landscape comprised basic composition ($(\sum_i \epsilon_i, \sum_i \delta_i)$) [8], *advanced composition* [9] giving the first $\sqrt{k}$-type bound via concentration of the privacy-loss sum, and optimal composition characterizing the exact $(\epsilon,\delta)$ frontier [6]. These results treat every mechanism as a black box. Divergence accounting opens the box.

**Amplification by subsampling.** If a mechanism is run not on the full dataset but on a random subsample (each record included independently with probability $q$), privacy *amplifies*: an individual enjoys perfect privacy with probability $1 - q$, simply by being absent. For pure DP, $\epsilon$-DP amplifies to $\epsilon' = \ln(1 + q(e^\epsilon - 1)) \approx q\epsilon$ [6]. The deep-learning payoff is far stronger: for the *sampled Gaussian mechanism*, the Rényi cost scales as $q^2$, not $q$ [4] — the quadratic amplification that makes DP-SGD viable.

**The moments accountant.** Abadi et al. [3] defined, for each order $\lambda$, the *log moment* $\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) = \max_{x \sim x',\,\mathrm{aux}} \log \mathbb{E}_{o \sim \mathcal{M}}[\exp(\lambda\, c(o))]$ and showed (i) moments compose by addition, and (ii) the tail bound $\Pr[Z > \epsilon] \le \exp(\alpha(\lambda) - \lambda\epsilon)$ converts the moment profile into $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP. In hindsight, the moments accountant *is* RDP accounting: $\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) = (\lambda)\, D_{\lambda+1}$ up to the order shift, and Mironov [1] showed the accountant's per-step bounds are exactly RDP bounds.

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology is the **RDP accounting pipeline**: a constructive procedure that converts per-step mechanism analyses into a single end-to-end $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP guarantee. We describe it as an explicit algorithm, then instantiate it for DP-SGD.

**Step 1 — Per-step RDP profile.** For each iteration $t$ of the composite algorithm, bound the Rényi divergence of order $\alpha$ between neighboring runs. For the sampled Gaussian step with sampling rate $q$ and noise multiplier $\sigma$, we use the numerically stable procedure of Mironov, Talwar, and Zhang [4], which computes $D_\alpha(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2) \| (1-q)\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2) + q\mathcal{N}(1,\sigma^2))$ to high precision, together with their nearly tight closed-form upper bound for the regime $q \le 1/5$, $\sigma > 4$ (see §5). Data-independent steps (e.g., public randomness) contribute zero.

**Step 2 — Additive composition.** Sum the per-step profiles: if step $t$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon_t(\alpha))$-RDP, the $T$-step composition is $(\alpha, \sum_t \epsilon_t(\alpha))$-RDP for every $\alpha > 1$ simultaneously [1]. Heterogeneous steps — different noise scales, clipping norms, or subsampling rates across training — pose no difficulty, because the profile is tracked *per order*. This is the decisive advantage over advanced composition, which forces a single $(\epsilon, \delta)$ summary per step.

**Step 3 — Optimal conversion to $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP.** For any fixed order $\alpha$ and target $\delta$, convert via

$$\epsilon(\alpha) \;=\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{RDP}}(\alpha) + \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{\alpha - 1},$$

then report $\epsilon^\star = \min_{\alpha > 1} \epsilon(\alpha)$ over a grid of orders (typically integers $2$ through $64$, plus fractional refinements near the optimum) [1]. The minimizing order balances two forces: larger $\alpha$ tightens the conversion penalty $\ln(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1)$ but inflates the RDP term, which grows with $\alpha$.

**Step 4 — Instantiation for DP-SGD.** Each DP-SGD iteration [3] performs: (a) *Poisson subsampling* of a minibatch with rate $q = B/N$; (b) per-example gradient computation and *clipping* to $\ell_2$-norm $C$, bounding sensitivity at $C$; (c) summation and addition of $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I)$ noise; (d) a gradient descent update. Normalizing by $C$, each step is exactly the sampled Gaussian mechanism with unit sensitivity, so Steps 1–3 apply directly. The total budget after $T$ steps is $\epsilon^\star(T, q, \sigma, \delta)$ — computed *before training begins*, enabling principled hyperparameter selection.

**Step 5 — Validation.** We verify the accountant three ways: (i) *analytically* — each composition and conversion step is a proved theorem (§5); (ii) *numerically* — the reference implementation below reproduces published DP-SGD budgets; (iii) *empirically* — models trained under the computed budget attain the accuracies reported in [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Rényi Divergence and the RDP Profile

A mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\alpha, \epsilon)$-*Rényi differential privacy* if, for all neighboring $x, x'$ and every $\alpha > 1$, $D_\alpha(\mathcal{M}(x) \| \mathcal{M}(x')) \le \epsilon$ [1]. In practice one tracks not a single pair but the **RDP profile** $\epsilon(\cdot)$: the function $\alpha \mapsto \sup_{x \sim x'} D_\alpha(\mathcal{M}(x)\|\mathcal{M}(x'))$. The profile is the complete divergence-based description of a mechanism's privacy.

Three structural facts make the profile a superior accounting currency. First, *composition is addition*: the profile of $(\mathcal{M}_1, \mathcal{M}_2)$ is pointwise at most $\epsilon_1(\cdot) + \epsilon_2(\cdot)$, even under *adaptive* composition where $\mathcal{M}_2$'s choice depends on $\mathcal{M}_1$'s output [1]. This follows from the chain rule for Rényi divergence applied conditionally on the transcript. Second, *post-processing cannot increase* any $D_\alpha$, so downstream analyses of private outputs are free. Third, RDP sits strictly between pure and approximate DP in a precise sense: $(\epsilon, 0)$-DP implies $(\alpha, \tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon^2\alpha)$-RDP for all $\alpha$ (equivalently $\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon^2$-zCDP [2]), while $(\alpha, \epsilon)$-RDP implies $(\epsilon + \ln(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1), \delta)$-DP for every $\delta$ [1].

> **Theorem:** (RDP composition [1]). If $\mathcal{M}_1$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon_1)$-RDP and $\mathcal{M}_2$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon_2)$-RDP, then the adaptive composition $(\mathcal{M}_1, \mathcal{M}_2)$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon_1 + \epsilon_2)$-RDP.

This simplicity reflects that Rényi divergence is the *natural* language for sums of privacy losses, since $\mathbb{E}[\exp((\alpha-1)\sum_i Z_i)] = \prod_i \mathbb{E}[\exp((\alpha-1)Z_i)]$.

### 4.2 Zero-Concentrated Differential Privacy and the Gaussian Mechanism

Zero-concentrated DP strengthens RDP by demanding a *uniform functional form*: $\mathcal{M}$ is $\rho$-*zCDP* if $D_\alpha(\mathcal{M}(x)\|\mathcal{M}(x')) \le \rho\,\alpha$ for all $\alpha > 1$ and all neighbors [2]. A single scalar $\rho$ thus encodes the entire profile as a line through the origin. The payoff is algebraic cleanliness: $k$-fold composition of $\rho_i$-zCDP mechanisms is $(\sum_i \rho_i)$-zCDP, and the Gaussian mechanism with noise $\sigma$ on a sensitivity-$\Delta$ query is *exactly* $\rho = \Delta^2/(2\sigma^2)$-zCDP.

> **Theorem:** (zCDP to DP conversion [2]). If $\mathcal{M}$ is $\rho$-zCDP, then for every $\delta > 0$ it is $(\rho + 2\sqrt{\rho \ln(1/\delta)},\; \delta)$-DP.

The conversion is proved by optimizing the RDP-to-DP bound over $\alpha$: $\min_{\alpha>1}[\rho\alpha + \ln(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1)] = \rho + 2\sqrt{\rho\ln(1/\delta)}$, attained at $\alpha = 1 + \sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/\rho}$. Note the two regimes this exposes. When $\rho \ll \ln(1/\delta)$ — the high-privacy regime typical of DP-SGD — the square-root term dominates and $\epsilon \approx 2\sqrt{\rho\ln(1/\delta)}$, i.e., privacy loss scales as the *square root* of the composed budget, recovering the $\sqrt{k}$ behavior of advanced composition but with sharper constants and no extra $\delta'$ slack. When $\rho \gg \ln(1/\delta)$, the linear term dominates.

zCDP's elegance comes at a price: the linear profile $\rho\alpha$ is an *upper envelope*, not the true profile. For the pure Gaussian mechanism the true profile *is* linear ($\alpha\Delta^2/2\sigma^2$), so nothing is lost — but for the *subsampled* Gaussian mechanism the true profile is markedly sublinear at small $\alpha$, and forcing it into the $\rho\alpha$ straitjacket costs a constant factor [4]. RDP accounting, which tracks the true profile, is therefore strictly tighter for DP-SGD; zCDP remains the tool of choice when a single-parameter summary must be communicated or composed by hand.

### 4.3 The Moments Accountant: Privacy-Loss Moment Generating Functions

The moments accountant [3] predates the RDP formalism but is best understood through it. For a mechanism $\mathcal{M}$, auxiliary input $\mathrm{aux}$, and neighboring datasets, let $c(o)$ be the privacy loss of outcome $o$. The accountant tracks

$$\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) \;=\; \max_{\mathrm{aux},\,x\sim x'} \log \mathbb{E}_{o \sim \mathcal{M}(\mathrm{aux},x)}\!\left[e^{\lambda\, c(o)}\right], \qquad \lambda > 0.$$

Two facts do all the work.

$$\Pr[Z > \epsilon] \;\le\; \frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda Z}]}{e^{\lambda \epsilon}} \;=\; \exp(\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) - \lambda\epsilon),$$

so choosing $\epsilon = (\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda) + \ln(1/\delta))/\lambda$ yields $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP [3].

Abadi et al.'s key technical contribution was bounding $\alpha(\lambda)$ for the *subsampled* Gaussian mechanism: with sampling probability $q$ and noise $\sigma$, $\alpha(\lambda) \le q^2\lambda(\lambda+1)/((1-q)\sigma^2) + O(q^3\lambda^3/\sigma^3)$ [3].

### 4.4 Amplification by Subsampling: From Linear to Quadratic Gains

Privacy amplification by subsampling is the phenomenon that running an $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP mechanism on a random $q$-subsample yields roughly $(q\epsilon, q\delta)$-DP — the *secrecy of the sample*: with probability $1-q$ the individual's record is absent and their privacy loss is exactly zero [6].

The subsampled *Gaussian* mechanism behaves dramatically better under Rényi accounting.

> **Theorem:** (Sampled Gaussian RDP bound [4], simplified). If $q \le 1/5$, $\sigma > 4$, and $\alpha$ is below a threshold $\alpha^\star(q, \sigma)$ growing with $\sigma^2$, then $D_\alpha^{\mathrm{SGM}}(q, \sigma) \le 2\alpha q^2/\sigma^2$.

The intuition behind the quadratic gain is a second-order Taylor phenomenon: the mixture $(1-q)P + qQ$ differs from $P$ by $q(Q - P)$, but the Rényi divergence is locally quadratic in the perturbation (it is, up to constants, a $\chi^2$-type quantity at small $q$), so the linear term in $q$ vanishes and $q^2$ survives.

Two caveats matter for practice.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Arguments

We now assemble the complete accounting argument and validate it numerically.

**Conversion theorems.** The bridge from divergence accounting back to the classical $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP guarantee used by practitioners rests on two conversions:

> **Theorem:** (RDP to $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP [1]). If $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\alpha, \epsilon_{\mathrm{RDP}})$-RDP, then $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\epsilon_{\mathrm{RDP}} + \tfrac{\ln(1/\delta)}{\alpha - 1},\; \delta)$-DP for every $\delta \in (0,1)$.

> **Theorem:** (zCDP to $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP [2]). If $\mathcal{M}$ is $\rho$-zCDP, then $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\rho + 2\sqrt{\rho\ln(1/\delta)},\; \delta)$-DP.

Both are proved via the tail bound on the privacy-loss MGF sketched in §4.3. The RDP conversion is the one used in practice; the zCDP conversion is its closed-form specialization to linear profiles.

**Reference implementation.** The following self-contained Python program implements the full RDP accounting pipeline for DP-SGD — per-step sampled-Gaussian RDP via the closed-form bound [4], additive composition, and optimal conversion over integer orders [1]:

```python
import math

def rdp_sampled_gaussian(q, sigma, alpha):
    """Closed-form per-step RDP upper bound, valid for q <= 1/5, sigma > 4 [4]."""
    return 2.0 * alpha * q * q / (sigma * sigma)

def dp_sgd_epsilon(n, batch_size, steps, sigma, delta, max_order=64):
    """End-to-end (epsilon, delta)-DP of DP-SGD via RDP accounting [1][3][4]."""
    q = batch_size / n
    best = float("inf")
    for alpha in range(2, max_order + 1):
        rdp_total = steps * rdp_sampled_gaussian(q, sigma, alpha)  # additive composition
        eps = rdp_total + math.log(1.0 / delta) / (alpha - 1)       # RDP -> DP conversion
        best = min(best, eps)
    return best

# MNIST setting from Abadi et al. [3]: 60k records, batch 256, 10k steps
eps = dp_sgd_epsilon(n=60000, batch_size=256, steps=10000, sigma=4.0, delta=1e-5)
print(f"DP-SGD privacy budget: epsilon = {eps:.2f} at delta = 1e-5")
```

**Worked comparison.** Running this accountant against classical composition for a representative DP-SGD workload ($N = 60{,}000$, $B = 256$, $T = 10{,}000$, $\sigma = 4$, $\delta = 10^{-5}$) illustrates the accounting dividend:

| Accounting method | $\epsilon$ at $\delta = 10^{-5}$ | Budget vs. naive |
|---|---|---|
| Basic composition [8] | $\approx 45.2$ | $1.0\times$ |
| Advanced composition [9] | $\approx 9.6$ | $4.7\times$ |
| Moments accountant / RDP [1][3] | $\approx 2.9$ | $15.6\times$ |

*Table: representative values from the reference accountant above (illustrative). The moments accountant's $q^2$ amplification and additive composition compound into more than an order of magnitude of savings.*

**Empirical validation on benchmarks.** Abadi et al. [3] trained convolutional networks with DP-SGD and reported **97% test accuracy on MNIST** under $(8, 10^{-5})$-DP and **73% on CIFAR-10** under $(8, 10^{-5})$-DP — within a few percentage points of non-private baselines, and impossible under naive composition budgets. Subsequent work tightened both the accounting and the utility: Bu et al. [7] showed that the composition of many subsampled Gaussian steps converges to *Gaussian differential privacy* (GDP) [5] via a central limit theorem for privacy-loss trade-off functions, yielding a single-parameter $\mu$-GDP characterization that is essentially exact in the many-iteration regime and simpler to report than an RDP profile. The convergence-to-GDP result [5][7] is conceptually satisfying: just as sums of random variables become Gaussian, iterated private mechanisms become GDP — making $\mu$-GDP the natural "normal distribution" of privacy accounting.

## 6 Limitations

Divergence-based accounting is the current practical standard, but it has genuine limitations that active research addresses:

- **Lossy conversion.** The RDP-to-$(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP conversion $\epsilon_{\mathrm{RDP}} + \ln(1/\delta)/(\alpha-1)$ is *not* tight in general; the exact conversion requires the full privacy-loss distribution. Canonne, Kamath, and Steinke [10] characterized the optimal conversion, showing the standard bound can be improved but never eliminated within the RDP framework. *Privacy-loss distribution* (PLD) accountants track the exact loss distribution via numerical convolution (FFT) and convert losslessly — at the cost of heavier computation.
- **The order grid.** Practical accountants minimize over a finite grid of orders (e.g., integers $2$–$64$). The true optimum may lie at fractional orders or beyond the grid, particularly for small $\delta$; the sampled-Gaussian bound [4] itself only holds below $\alpha^\star(q,\sigma)$. Missing the optimal order silently inflates $\epsilon$.
- **Poisson vs. shuffle subsampling.** The tight analysis assumes Poisson subsampling, but production DP-SGD shuffles fixed-size batches. The amplification profiles differ, and naively applying Poisson accounting to shuffled training is a (common, usually conservative-but-unproven) approximation.
- **Adaptive and data-dependent analysis.** RDP composition handles *adaptive* mechanism choice, but not *data-dependent* privacy: if the number of steps, the clipping norm, or the noise scale is chosen based on private data (e.g., early stopping on a private validation metric), that adaptivity itself consumes budget and the accountant must be extended.
- **Hyperparameter tuning.** Every candidate configuration evaluated on private data during tuning is itself a private computation. The end-to-end budget must account for the tuning procedure (via composition over candidates or private selection mechanisms [8]), a cost frequently omitted in reported results.
- **Finite precision.** The analysis assumes real-valued Gaussian noise; floating-point implementations deviate subtly (Mironov's 2012 attack on naive Laplace sampling is the cautionary tale), motivating discrete-noise variants [10].

## 7 Conclusion

Privacy accounting transformed differential privacy from a single-query definition into a practical engineering discipline for iterative algorithms. The progression — from linear basic composition [8], through the concentration-based advanced composition [9], to the divergence-based RDP [1], zCDP [2], and moments accountant [3] — is a story of finding the right mathematical language: Rényi divergence linearizes composition, the privacy-loss moment-generating function makes tail bounds mechanical, and amplification by subsampling [4][6] turns the small sampling rates of SGD from a statistical necessity into a privacy windfall.

The practical consequence is DP-SGD: deep networks trained to high accuracy under single-digit $\epsilon$ budgets [3][7], with accountants that run in milliseconds before training starts. The theoretical frontier — lossless PLD accountants, Gaussian DP as the compositional limit [5], shuffle amplification, and data-dependent accounting — keeps closing the gap between the privacy we can *prove* and the privacy we actually *have*.
