# Verifiable Delay Functions and Proofs of Sequential Work: The Wesolowski and Pietrzak Protocols, RSA-Group Cryptography, and Distributed Randomness Beacons

## Abstract

A *verifiable delay function* (VDF) forces any evaluator to perform a prescribed number of *sequential* computational steps, while the result can be verified quickly and publicly by anyone. This thesis develops the theory and practice of the two constructions that define the field: **Wesolowski's** constant-size proof of exponentiation and **Pietrzak's** logarithmic-size recursive halving protocol, both of which make the classical Rivest–Shamir–Wagner repeated-squaring time-lock puzzle publicly verifiable inside groups of unknown order. We formalize the syntax introduced by Boneh, Bonneau, Bünz, and Fisch together with its three canonical properties — correctness, soundness, and sequentiality — analyze the adaptive-root and low-order assumptions on which soundness rests, and compare the two protocols quantitatively on proof size, verification cost, and prover overhead. We then trace how VDFs are deployed at scale: Chia's proof-of-time timelords built on ideal class groups, and distributed randomness beacons assembled from threshold cryptography and delay-based unbiasing. We close with the fundamental limits of the paradigm — trusted-setup trade-offs, hardware-driven evaluation asymmetry, and the open problem of transparent post-quantum VDFs.

## 1 Introduction

Decentralized systems repeatedly demand a near-paradoxical primitive: a computation *slow by construction* yet *fast to check*. Randomness beacons must emit values no participant could predict or bias [1]; blockchains must prove elapsed time to late joiners; proofs of replication must show storage providers devoting real sequential work [1]. Hash chains give delay without fast verification; time-lock puzzles give delay but need a secret trapdoor to *verify*, defeating public auditability [6]. Verifiable delay functions close this gap.

Formally, a VDF is a function $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ parameterized by a delay $T$ such that:

1. **Evaluation** requires $T$ *sequential* steps — parallelism offers essentially no speedup.
2. **Output uniqueness** holds: $f(x)$ is a well-defined, deterministic value.
3. **Verification** is exponentially faster than evaluation, typically in time $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda, \log T)$ for security parameter $\lambda$.

Boneh, Bonneau, Bünz, and Fisch gave the first formal treatment [1] — syntax, security notions, and candidates with the first *exponential* evaluation–verification gap. Two breakthroughs then made the repeated-squaring time-lock puzzle publicly verifiable: **Wesolowski** [4][5], with a *single-group-element* proof verifiable in ~two exponentiations, and **Pietrzak** [2][3], with a *logarithmic-size* proof from a recursive halving protocol. Both were motivated by the same production system: Chia's proof-of-space-and-time consensus, which needed publicly verifiable proof that wall-clock time had passed.

Section 2 develops the background, Section 3 the two protocols' mechanics, Section 4 a deep dive into assumptions and deployment trade-offs, Section 5 proof sketches with empirical figures, and Sections 6–7 limitations and conclusions.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From Time-Lock Puzzles to Public Verifiability

Every deployed VDF descends from the Rivest–Shamir–Wagner time-lock puzzle [6]: given RSA modulus $N = pq$ and random $x$, define $y = x^{2^T} \bmod N$. Whoever knows $\varphi(N)$ reduces the exponent ($e \equiv 2^T \pmod{\varphi(N)}$) and finishes in $O(\log N)$ multiplications; everyone else must perform $T$ *sequential* squarings, since each depends on the last — parallelism cannot compress the critical path. This *repeated-squaring assumption* is the field's bedrock hardness hypothesis.

The defect is verification: checking $y = x^{2^T} \bmod N$ *also* seems to need $T$ squarings without the trapdoor $\varphi(N)$ — verification depends on a secret, violating the requirement that it be public and efficient [1][8]. Lenstra and Wesolowski's **Sloth** construction achieved only a polynomial evaluation–verification gap — a *weak* VDF, not a true one [6].

> **Definition (VDF syntax, after [1]).** A verifiable delay function is a triple of algorithms $(\mathsf{Setup}, \mathsf{Eval}, \mathsf{Verify})$:
>
> - $\mathsf{Setup}(\lambda, T) \to (ek, vk)$: on security parameter $\lambda$ and delay $T$, outputs an evaluation key and a verification key in time $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda, \log T)$.
> - $\mathsf{Eval}(ek, x) \to (y, \pi)$: on input $x \in \mathcal{X}$, outputs $y \in \mathcal{Y}$ and a (possibly empty) proof $\pi$, running in sequential time $T$.
> - $\mathsf{Verify}(vk, x, y, \pi) \to \{\mathsf{accept}, \mathsf{reject}\}$: deterministic, running in time $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda, \log T)$.

Three properties complete the definition [1][8]:

- **Correctness.** Honest evaluation always verifies: $\mathsf{Verify}(vk, x, \mathsf{Eval}(ek, x)) = \mathsf{accept}$.
- **Soundness.** No efficient adversary can produce $(x, y', \pi')$ with $y' \ne f(x)$ that verifies, except with negligible probability. (The output must be *unique*: a stronger variant, *uniqueness*, demands that even two accepting proofs for the same $x$ agree on $y$.)
- **Sequentiality.** No adversary with $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda, T)$ parallel processors can compute $f(x)$ in parallel time substantially less than $T$. Boneh et al. formalize this as $(\sigma, \Delta)$-sequentiality: parallel time must be at least $\sigma(T)$ up to a small advantage $\Delta$ [1].

### 2.2 Groups of Unknown Order

Both practical constructions live in a group $\mathbb{G}$ whose order is *unknown* to all parties. The unknown order is what prevents the exponent-reduction trick: without $|\mathbb{G}|$, the exponent $2^T$ cannot be reduced modulo the group order, and repeated squaring becomes the only known route.

Two instantiations dominate:

- **RSA groups** $(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^*$ with $N = pq$ a product of two large *safe primes*. Nobody — ideally not even the generator — may know the factorization; deployments use MPC ceremonies or a *nothing-up-my-sleeve* modulus such as the RSA-2048 factoring-challenge number [7].
- **Class groups of imaginary quadratic fields** $\mathrm{Cl}(\Delta_K)$: unknown, believed-hard-to-compute order, and — crucially — *transparent setup*, since the discriminant derives deterministically from a hash [10]. This is why Chia's VDF uses class groups, not RSA groups [10].

RSA groups contain small subgroups (elements of order 2), so protocols restrict to quadratic residues $QR_N$ or $QR_N^+$ to keep the *low-order assumption* plausible [3][8].

### 2.3 The Fiat–Shamir Transformation

Both Wesolowski's and Pietrzak's protocols are naturally *interactive* public-coin arguments: the verifier issues a random challenge, and the prover responds. To obtain a non-interactive VDF usable in blockchains and beacons, the challenge is derived deterministically by hashing the public transcript — the **Fiat–Shamir heuristic** [2][4]. For Wesolowski, the challenge is a random *prime* $\ell$ of roughly $\lambda$ bits, obtained by hashing $(N, x, y, T)$ and rejection-sampling primes; for Pietrzak, each recursion level derives $r_i = H(x_i, y_i, \mu_i)$ from that level's transcript [7]. Security then rests on the hash function behaving as a random oracle, an assumption made explicit in both security analyses [2][4].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Wesolowski's Proof of Exponentiation

Wesolowski's construction [4][5] is disarmingly simple. To prove that $y = x^{2^T}$ in a group $\mathbb{G}$ of unknown order, the prover computes

$$\pi = x^{\lfloor 2^T / \ell \rfloor},$$

where $\ell$ is a random prime of approximately $\lambda$ bits chosen by the verifier (or derived by Fiat–Shamir as $\ell = H(\mathbb{G}, x, y, T)$ mapped into the primes). The verifier then computes $r = 2^T \bmod \ell$ — cheap, since it is arithmetic modulo the *small* prime $\ell$, costing $O(\log T)$ multiplications — and accepts if and only if

$$\pi^{\ell} \cdot x^{r} \stackrel{?}{=} y.$$

Correctness is a one-line algebraic identity: writing $2^T = q\ell + r$ with $q = \lfloor 2^T/\ell \rfloor$, we have $\pi^{\ell} x^r = x^{q\ell + r} = x^{2^T} = y$. The prover's extra work beyond evaluation is a single long division on the binary exponent $2^T$ by $\ell$ — negligible next to $T$ squarings [4]. The proof is *one group element* (~256 bytes for RSA-2048), and verification costs essentially two exponentiations: one $\lambda$-bit exponentiation ($\pi^{\ell}$) and one tiny one ($x^r$) [4][5].

```python
def eval_vdf(N: int, x: int, T: int) -> int:
    """Sequential evaluation: T repeated squarings. Inherently serial."""
    y = x % N
    for _ in range(T):
        y = (y * y) % N
    return y  # == x^{2^T} mod N

def prove_wesolowski(N: int, x: int, T: int, ell: int) -> int:
    """Prover: pi = x^{floor(2^T / ell)} via long division on the exponent."""
    q = (1 << T) // ell          # conceptual; real impl. divides bit-by-bit
    return pow(x, q, N)

def verify_wesolowski(N: int, x: int, y: int, T: int, pi: int, ell: int) -> bool:
    r = pow(2, T, ell)           # 2^T mod ell -- O(log T) work mod small prime
    return (pow(pi, ell, N) * pow(x, r, N)) % N == y
```

The subtlety is *soundness*: forging a $\pi'$ for false $y'$ requires an $\ell$-th root of $y' x^{-r}$ — breaking the **adaptive-root assumption** (given random $w \in \mathbb{G}$ and random $\lambda$-bit prime $\ell$, finding $u$ with $u^{\ell} = w$ is infeasible) [4][5], plus a **low-order assumption** ($\mu^d = 1 \Rightarrow \mu = 1$ for small $d$) ruling out torsion forgeries [5]. Section 5 makes the reduction precise.

### 3.2 Pietrzak's Recursive Halving Protocol

Pietrzak [2][3] takes a different route to the same language $\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{EXP}} = \{(\mathbb{G}, g, h, T) : h = g^{2^T}\}$. Assume $T$ is a power of two (the protocol generalizes to arbitrary $T$ [2]). The prover and verifier engage in $\log T$ rounds:

1. **Prover** computes the midpoint $v = g^{2^{T/2}}$ and sends it.
2. **Verifier** sends a random challenge $r \in \{1, \dots, 2^{\lambda}\}$.
3. Both compute the *folded* statement $g_1 = g^{r} v$, $h_1 = v^{r} h$ and recurse on $(\mathbb{G}, g_1, h_1, T/2)$.

The recursion bottoms out at $T = 1$ with a direct check $h = g$. Made non-interactive, each challenge is $r_i = H(\mathbb{G}, g_i, h_i, T_i, v_i)$ and the proof is the midpoint list $\{v_i\}_{i=1}^{\log T}$.

The *random linear combination* preserves truth: if $h = g^{2^T}$ then $h_1 = g_1^{2^{T/2}}$, and if the claim is false the folded claim is false except with probability $\le 2^{-\lambda}$ over $r$. Each round halves $T$ — hence $\log T$ group elements and $2\log T$ small exponentiations to verify [2][3].

| | Wesolowski [4][5] | Pietrzak [2][3] |
|---|---|---|
| Proof size | **1 group element** (~256 B) | $\log T$ elements (~10 KB at $T = 2^{40}$) |
| Verification | ~2 exponentiations | ~$2\log T$ small exponentiations (≈ 3 RSA exps) |
| Prover overhead | ~1 exponent division (negligible) | sublinear extra work; ~8000× cheaper than eval at $T \le 2^{40}$ |
| Challenge | random prime $\ell$ (~$\lambda$ bits) | random $r \in [1, 2^{\lambda}]$ per level |
| Core assumption | adaptive root (+ low order) | low order |

### 3.3 Engineering the Challenge: Hashing to Primes

Wesolowski needs a *prime* challenge, so Fiat–Shamir uses hash-to-prime: hash the transcript with a counter, Miller–Rabin-test candidates until a prime of the right size appears [7]. Pietrzak needs only a uniform $\lambda$-bit integer — a plain hash truncation.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Assumption Landscape: Adaptive Roots and Low-Order Elements

Soundness rests on assumptions about groups of *unknown order* — less studied than prime-order groups, where intuition must be checked [6]:

- **Repeated squaring.** Computing $x^{2^T}$ from $x$ requires $T$ sequential group operations, even for adversaries with $\mathrm{poly}(\lambda, T)$ parallel processors. This is a *conjecture*, not a theorem: no unconditional lower bound is known, and its plausibility rests on the absence of any shortcut after decades of scrutiny of the RSW puzzle [1][6].
- **Adaptive-root assumption** (Wesolowski [4][5]). For random $w \in \mathbb{G}$ and a random prime $\ell$ of $\lambda$ bits, finding $u$ with $u^{\ell} = w$ is infeasible. Note the *adaptive* qualifier: the adversary sees $\ell$ before producing $u$, but $w$ is fixed first — this is strictly weaker than assuming RSA itself is hard, yet still non-standard.
- **Low-order assumption** (both, primarily Pietrzak [2][3]). No efficient adversary finds $\mu \ne 1$ and $d < 2^{\lambda}$ with $\mu^d = 1$. In $(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^*$ with safe-prime factors this reduces to avoiding the tiny subgroup $\{\pm 1\}$; in class groups the assumption is that the group has no small torsion, which holds for the discriminants used in practice [2][8].

A comparative survey of the two schemes further shows that Pietrzak's soundness can be based on the low-order assumption alone, whereas Wesolowski needs the stronger adaptive-root assumption — a genuine security-versus-efficiency trade-off, since Wesolowski buys a constant-size proof at the price of a stronger hypothesis [6].

### 4.2 Sequentiality and the Parallelism Barrier

Sequentiality is the least-proved property. The formal demand [1]: no adversary with $\mathrm{poly}(T)$ processors outputs $f(x)$ in parallel time $\sigma(T) < T$ except negligibly. Repeated squaring is *conjectured* to satisfy this since each squaring strictly depends on the previous one — but the conjecture is relative to a *reference hardware model*: faster class-group arithmetic or squaring ASICs compress the per-step constant, and deployed systems (notably Chia) treat VDF speed as an economic parameter, not a mathematical constant [10].

### 4.3 Group Selection: RSA Moduli versus Class Groups

The choice of $\mathbb{G}$ is the single most consequential deployment decision:

1. **RSA groups** offer fast arithmetic and compact elements, but demand *trusted setup*: someone generates $N = pq$ and must verifiably destroy $p, q$ (or run an MPC ceremony) [6][7].
2. **Class groups** $\mathrm{Cl}(\Delta_K)$ offer *transparent setup* — the discriminant derives from a public hash, so no trapdoor ever exists [10] — at the cost of slower form arithmetic and a thinner cryptanalytic literature [8].

Chia chose class groups [10], judging transparent setup worth the arithmetic cost; early Ethereum VDF efforts preferred RSA groups with nothing-up-my-sleeve moduli to dodge the ceremony [7]. The trade-off is *setup trust* versus *arithmetic efficiency*.

### 4.4 Parameter Selection and the Proof-Size Frontier

Concrete parameters from the literature anchor the theory. For $T \le 2^{40}$ and a 2048-bit modulus [2]:

- Pietrzak proofs are ~**10 KB** (40 group elements), verification costs roughly **three RSA exponentiations**, and proof generation is about **8000× cheaper** than evaluation itself [2].
- Wesolowski proofs are a **single group element**, verification about **two exponentiations** [4][5].

$T$ is set so $T$ squarings on reference hardware equal the target wall-clock delay; $\lambda = 128$ fixes $\ell$ at ~128–256 bits. Larger $T$ favors Wesolowski's constant proof size; at modest $T$, $\log T$ elements are cheap anyway.

### 4.5 VDF-Based Randomness Beacons: From Committees to Delay

A randomness beacon must output values that are *unpredictable* before release and *unbiasable* by any participant [1]. The classic committee approach — used by **drand** — runs a distributed key generation (DKG) ceremony, then has a threshold of nodes co-sign each round's message with threshold BLS; the reconstructed signature, hashed, is the beacon output, verifiable against the collective public key [10]. This is fast and practical, but it trusts a threshold of the committee.

VDFs enable a *trust-minimized* beacon [1]: set $r_i = H(\mathsf{Eval}(r_{i-1}))$. The output is unique and deterministic, so all honest evaluators agree; the $T$-step delay means nobody learns $r_i$ early enough to bias processes keyed on it, neutralizing the classic *last-revealer* grinding attack [1]. **Chia** instantiates this at blockchain scale: independent *timelords* run class-group VDFs continuously, broadcasting *proofs of time* that chain blocks and supply deterministic randomness [10] — no committee, only the repeated-squaring conjecture plus one honest, reasonably fast timelord [10].

## 5 Empirical Analysis and Proofs

We now state the core arguments in proof form and collect measured figures.

> **Theorem (Wesolowski correctness [4][5]).** For all $x \in \mathbb{G}$, delay $T$, and prime challenge $\ell$, with $\pi = x^{\lfloor 2^T/\ell \rfloor}$ and $r = 2^T \bmod \ell$, the verifier's check $\pi^{\ell} x^{r} = y$ holds iff $y = x^{2^T}$ *for the honest* $\pi$; in particular honest proofs always verify.
>
> *Proof.* Write $2^T = q\ell + r$ by Euclidean division, so $q = \lfloor 2^T/\ell \rfloor$ and $0 \le r < \ell$. Then $\pi^{\ell} x^{r} = (x^q)^{\ell} x^r = x^{q\ell + r} = x^{2^T} = y$. ∎

> **Theorem (Wesolowski soundness sketch [4][5]).** Under the adaptive-root and low-order assumptions, no PPT adversary outputs $(x, y', \pi')$ with $y' \ne x^{2^T}$ that passes verification, except with negligible probability.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Suppose the adversary forges on challenge $\ell$ with $y' \ne y = x^{2^T}$ and $\pi'^{\ell} x^r = y'$, where $r = 2^T \bmod \ell$. Dividing by the honest relation gives $(\pi'/\pi)^{\ell} = y'/y \ne 1$. Setting $w = y'/y$ and $u = \pi'/\pi$, we obtain $u^{\ell} = w$ — an $\ell$-th root of a group element determined *before* $\ell$ was sampled, contradicting the adaptive-root assumption. The low-order assumption excludes the degenerate case where the adversary exploits torsion to make $u^{\ell} = w$ hold trivially for adversarially chosen $w$ [5]. ∎

> **Theorem (Pietrzak halving soundness [2][3]).** If $(g, h, T) \notin \mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{EXP}}$ (i.e., $h \ne g^{2^T}$), then for random $r \in [1, 2^{\lambda}]$ the folded statement $(g_1, h_1, T/2)$ is also false, except with probability at most $2^{-\lambda}$.
>
> *Proof sketch.* A direct computation shows $h_1 = g_1^{2^{T/2}}$ for $h = g^{2^T}\cdot\delta$, $\delta\ne 1$, iff $\delta$ satisfies a non-trivial polynomial equation in $r$; over random $r \in [1,2^{\lambda}]$ this holds with probability $\le 2^{-\lambda}$ unless $\delta$ has small order — ruled out by the low-order assumption [2]. Union-bound over $\log T$ rounds: total error $O(\log T \cdot 2^{-\lambda})$. ∎

**Empirical figures.** Independent implementation studies confirm the asymptotic picture and quantify the constants [7]:

- **Proof sizes:** Wesolowski ~0.25 KB vs. Pietrzak ~10 KB at $(T, N) = (2^{40}, 2048)$ [2][4].
- **Verification:** Wesolowski ~2 modular exponentiations; Pietrzak ~$2\log T$ small exponentiations ≈ 3 full RSA exponentiations [2]; distributed Wesolowski verification is studied in [7].
- **Prover overhead:** Wesolowski's extra cost is one exponent long-division; Pietrzak's proof generation runs ~8000× faster than evaluation at $T = 2^{40}$ [2][3].
- **Evaluation throughput:** RSA-2048 squaring runs ~$10^5$–$10^6$/s per CPU core [7], so a one-hour delay needs $T \approx 2^{32}$–$2^{33}$; class-group squaring is several-fold slower per step, which is why Chia calibrates iteration counts empirically [10].

## 6 Limitations

**Trusted setup for RSA instantiations.** The RSA VDF is only as trustworthy as the claim that nobody knows $N$'s factors; any leak retroactively voids every proof ever produced — an unusually catastrophic failure mode [6][7].

**The repeated-squaring conjecture is unproven.** Sequentiality rests on the absence of a shortcut, not a lower bound; an algorithmic breakthrough would compress delays silently, so systems must over-provision $T$ [1][6].

**Hardware asymmetry.** VDF security is relative to the fastest *sequential* hardware. Squaring ASICs far outperform CPUs, so a well-funded evaluator learns beacon outputs early — consensus-critical for Chia, where a faster timelord can orphan farmers or mount long-range attacks below half the netspace [10]. Open VDF-hardware designs are a security requirement [10].

**Quantum vulnerability.** Shor's algorithm factors $N$ and breaks class-group structure, destroying both instantiations; no post-quantum VDF with transparent setup and practical verification is known [8][9] — the field's central open problem.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Verifiable delay functions turned Rivest–Shamir–Wagner's private time-lock puzzle into a public primitive, and the Wesolowski and Pietrzak protocols turned the definition into deployable engineering: one with a constant-size proof under a stronger assumption, the other with a logarithmic-size proof under a weaker one [2][4]. Their security story is unusually honest: sequentiality is a conjecture about absent shortcuts, the group assumptions are young, and the hardware model is economic. Yet the payoff is real — blockchains proving elapsed time without committees [10], unbiasable beacons [1], and replication proofs binding storage to sequential work [1]. The next decade's challenge is to keep the proofs small and the assumptions transparent while surviving the quantum transition — a problem that will likely require entirely new mathematics, not merely tighter reductions.
