# Tensor Network Methods for the Classical Simulation of Quantum Circuits: Matrix Product States, Projected Entangled Pairs, and Contraction Complexity at the Supremacy Frontier

## 1. Introduction

The classical simulation of quantum circuits is simultaneously a verification tool, a design aid, and a yardstick against which claims of quantum computational advantage must be measured. The obstruction is elementary and brutal: an $n$-qubit pure state is specified by $2^n$ complex amplitudes, so naive Schrödinger-style simulation — storing and updating the full state vector — hits a memory wall near $n \approx 50$ even on the largest supercomputers. Yet not all quantum states are equally hard to describe. Ground states of gapped local Hamiltonians, shallow circuits on restricted geometries, and weakly entangled dynamics all admit *compressed* representations whose cost scales with the entanglement actually present rather than with the volume of Hilbert space. Tensor networks (TNs) are the mathematical language of this compression: a factorization of a high-order tensor into a network of low-order tensors whose contraction recovers the original object [5].

This thesis develops the theory and practice of tensor-network simulation of quantum circuits. We treat three complementary strategies. First, **state-evolution methods** — matrix product states (MPS) with time-evolving block decimation (TEBD) and the density matrix renormalization group (DMRG) extended to tree tensor networks — which propagate an ansatz forward through the circuit, truncating entanglement at each step [3][4]. Second, **amplitude-contraction methods**, which convert the entire circuit into a tensor network whose contraction yields a single measurement amplitude; here the dominant cost is set not by the state but by the *contraction ordering*, whose optimization is NP-complete and equivalent to computing the treewidth of the network's line graph [6][7]. Third, **sampling-oriented 2D methods** such as boundary-MPS contraction of projected entangled pair states (PEPS), which have recently enabled controlled, GPU-accelerated simulation of physically motivated circuits at scales rivaling hardware [1]. We ground the discussion in the supremacy-circuit benchmarks — random-circuit sampling on the Sycamore architecture — where the interplay of treewidth, slicing, and sampling has repeatedly redrawn the classical frontier [9]. The thesis closes with precise statements of what tensor networks provably cannot do, and the open problems that remain.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Tensor networks and graphical calculus

A tensor network is a set of tensors contracted according to a graph: vertices are tensors, edges are summed-over indices, and dangling edges are open indices. Contraction cost scales as the product of all index dimensions involved. The representation is not unique: inserting any invertible $X$ and $X^{-1}$ on a bond leaves the result invariant, a **gauge freedom** exploited to impose canonical forms [5].

### 2.2 Matrix product states

An $n$-site state with local dimension $d$ admits the **matrix product state** decomposition

$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_{s_1,\dots,s_n} A^{[1]s_1} A^{[2]s_2}\cdots A^{[n]s_n}\,|s_1\cdots s_n\rangle,$$

where each $A^{[k]s_k}$ is a $\chi_{k-1}\times\chi_k$ matrix and the **bond dimensions** $\chi_k$ control the expressivity. Storage costs $O(nd\chi^2)$, exponentially less than $d^n$ whenever $\chi$ stays modest. The MPS form is an iterated Schmidt decomposition: cutting bond $k$ yields

$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{\chi_k} \lambda^{(k)}_\alpha\, |L^{(k)}_\alpha\rangle \otimes |R^{(k)}_\alpha\rangle,$$

with Schmidt coefficients $\lambda_\alpha$. Keeping the $\chi$ largest incurs **discarded weight** $\varepsilon = \sum_{\alpha>\chi}\lambda_\alpha^2$ — the optimal rank-$\chi$ 2-norm approximation by Eckart–Young. Since a bond of dimension $\chi$ carries at most $\log\chi$ ebits across the cut, MPS faithfully represents states whose bipartite entanglement grows at most logarithmically.

> **Theorem (Area law, Hastings 2007).** *The ground state of a gapped local Hamiltonian on a one-dimensional lattice has entanglement entropy $S(\rho_A) = O(1)$ across any bipartition, independent of system size.*

The area law is the reason MPS "just works" for 1D physics: a constant entropy implies a constant bond dimension suffices in principle, and the **DMRG** algorithm of White (1992) variationally optimizes the MPS tensors sweep by sweep to find ground states at cost $O(n\chi^3)$ per sweep [5][8]. Gapless or critical 1D systems violate the strict area law only logarithmically, $S \sim \tfrac{c}{3}\log L$, requiring $\chi \sim \mathrm{poly}(L)$ — still tractable.

### 2.3 TEBD, PEPS, and tree tensor networks

**TEBD** (Vidal, 2003–2004) applies circuit gates directly to the MPS: a two-site gate is contracted with its two tensors, the result is split by singular value decomposition, and the bond is re-truncated to $\chi$ — each update costing $O(\chi^3)$ [3][5]. For Hamiltonian evolution the propagator is first Trotterized into local gates.

**PEPS** (Verstraete & Cirac, 2004) generalizes MPS to two dimensions: tensors sit on a lattice with one physical leg and up to four virtual legs of dimension $D$. The price of loops is steep — exact contraction of a PEPS is **#P-complete** — so all PEPS algorithms are approximate: the *simple update* applies gates at cost $O(D^{d+1})$ ($d$ the coordination number) with a mean-field environment, while the *full update* and boundary-MPS methods contract a boundary at higher polynomial cost for better accuracy [5][1].

**Tree tensor networks (TTNs)** interpolate between the two: a loop-free hierarchical geometry that supports DMRG-style variational compression with cost $O(\chi^4)$, capturing clustered or hierarchical entanglement that a linear MPS chain represents inefficiently. A TTN-DMRG scheme has recently been shown to require less memory than MPS for random circuits with tree-like gate layouts and to improve fidelities for QAOA circuits whose connectivity is exploited explicitly [4].

### 2.4 The supremacy benchmark

The 2019 Sycamore experiment (Arute *et al.*, *Nature* **574**, 505) sampled bitstrings from a 53-qubit, depth-20 random circuit whose output distribution is Porter–Thomas, with fidelity certified by linear cross-entropy benchmarking (XEB). Such circuits are *designed* to maximize entanglement growth and defeat structured simulators: as the lecture notes of Ref. [8] emphasize, useful structured circuits are far easier to simulate than random ones, and supremacy circuits are the adversarial extreme. Classical responses have been formidable: Pan and Zhang's sliced tensor-network contraction computed exact amplitudes for millions of Sycamore bitstrings on 512 GPUs [9]; Villalonga *et al.* pushed a 281 Pflop/s simulation of the depth-20 circuit; and subsequent work has steadily eroded the claimed gap, making contraction-path engineering — not raw flops — the decisive weapon.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Three simulation paradigms

| Paradigm | Object represented | Cost driver | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Schrödinger (MPS/TEBD)** | Full state $|\psi\rangle$ as MPS | Bond dim. $\chi\sim e^{\alpha D}$ | All amplitudes, samples |
| **Feynman (path sum)** | Amplitude as sum over paths | Number of paths $\sim 2^{nd}$ | One amplitude |
| **Schrödinger–Feynman (TN contraction)** | Circuit as TN, output fixed | Treewidth of TN line graph | One amplitude / batch |

In the TN-contraction picture, fixing the output bitstring turns every gate into a tensor and the amplitude $\langle x|U|0\rangle$ into a scalar — the full contraction of the network. Markov and Shi (2008) proved that the optimal contraction sequence corresponds to a minimum-width **tree decomposition** of the network's line graph, so the contraction cost is exponential in the **treewidth** $w$: roughly $O(\exp(w))$ time and memory [7]. For a depth-$D$ circuit on a 2D $n$-qubit grid, $w = O(D\sqrt{n})$, which explains both why shallow 2D circuits are simulable and why depth-20 Sycamore circuits sit at the edge.

### 3.2 Finding contraction orderings

> **Theorem (Markov–Shi 2008; hardness).** *Computing the optimal contraction ordering of a tensor network is NP-complete, by reduction from minimum-width tree decomposition (treewidth) of the line graph* [6][7].

Practice relies on heuristics: greedy pair-contraction (`opt_einsum`), simulated annealing, hypergraph partitioners, and the **Cotengra** optimizer, the workhorse of supremacy-circuit simulation [9][10]. A second lever is **index slicing**: fixing a large index and summing over its values across parallel workers trades exponential memory for parallel work, multiplying flops by the sliced dimension. The optimal slice set and the optimal order are coupled problems, handled by two-phase heuristics that interleave slicing with local order reconfiguration [9].

### 3.3 Gate application and truncation

In MPS-TEBD, a two-site unitary $U$ acting on sites $k,k+1$ is applied as follows:

1. Contract: $\Theta^{s_k s_{k+1}}_{\alpha_{k-1}\alpha_{k+1}} = \sum_{s'_k s'_{k+1}} U^{s_k s_{k+1}}_{s'_k s'_{k+1}} A^{[k]s'_k}_{\alpha_{k-1}\beta} A^{[k+1]s'_{k+1}}_{\beta\alpha_{k+1}}$.
2. Reshape $\Theta$ to a matrix and compute its SVD.
3. Truncate to the $\chi$ largest singular values; renormalize.
4. Reshape back into updated $A^{[k]}, A^{[k+1]}$, restoring canonical form.

The truncation is locally optimal in the 2-norm, but errors compound across gates: after $G$ gates with per-gate discarded weight $\varepsilon$, the global infidelity scales roughly as $G\varepsilon$ in the worst case, so $\chi$ must grow as entanglement accumulates. For TEBD applied to chaotic dynamics, entanglement grows *linearly* in time (Calabrese–Cardy), forcing $\chi$ to grow exponentially — the fundamental wall of state-based simulation.

### 3.4 Sampling from tensor networks

Amplitudes alone do not sample. Two routes dominate: (i) **chain-rule sampling**, computing conditional probabilities $p(x_k|x_{<k})$ from the TN and sampling bit by bit — exact but requiring $n$ contractions; and (ii) **frugal / big-batch rejection sampling**, contracting the network for a large batch of correlated bitstrings and accepting with probability proportional to the amplitude, which amortizes one contraction over many samples when the batch shares most of the network [9]. Recent 2D-TN work replaces the exact boundary with a **boundary MPS** of bond dimension $\chi_b$, generating samples whose distribution converges to the true one as $\chi_b$ grows, with GPUs providing order-of-magnitude speedups over CPUs [1].

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Bond dimension, entanglement, and the area law

The single number that governs MPS simulability is the entanglement entropy across the worst cut. Since $S \le \log\chi$, the area law $S = O(1)$ for gapped 1D ground states implies efficient MPS representation — the theoretical foundation of DMRG's empirical dominance. For *dynamics*, the picture inverts: a global quench generates $S(t) \sim v t$, so $\chi(t) \sim e^{vt}$, and TEBD dies at a time logarithmic in the affordable bond dimension. Random circuits are the extreme case: Ref. [8] notes that when $\chi \sim e^{\alpha D}$ with depth $D$, even computing a single scalar product costs $O(N\chi^3) = O(N e^{3\alpha D})$, vastly better than exact evolution yet still exponential — TN methods soften but do not remove the exponential.

*Practical consequence:* before simulating, estimate the entanglement structure. Area-law states → DMRG/MPS. Volume-law or Porter–Thomas states → abandon state evolution; contract amplitudes with slicing instead.

### 4.2 TEBD in detail: Trotter, sweeps, and error budgets

For Hamiltonian $H = \sum_j h_j$, a second-order Trotter step $e^{-iH\delta t} \approx \prod_j e^{-ih_j\delta t/2}\prod_j e^{-ih_j\delta t/2}$ converts evolution into a brickwork of two-site gates, each applied by the SVD update of §3.3. Two error sources compete: **Trotter error** $O(\delta t^3)$ per step, reduced by small $\delta t$, and **truncation error**, which grows with the number of steps. The optimal $\delta t$ balances them. The *time-dependent variational principle* (TDVP) offers an alternative that projects the Schrödinger equation onto the MPS manifold, avoiding Trotter error and handling long-range interactions more gracefully, at the cost of solving local effective equations [5]. For noisy circuits, recent work replaces deterministic evolution with **locally entanglement-optimal unravelings**: each noise channel is decomposed into an ensemble of pure-state trajectories chosen to minimize the average entanglement (the entanglement of formation), so that each trajectory needs a smaller $\chi$ at fixed accuracy — with closed-form optimal unravelings for general single-qubit noise and rigorous poly$(n,\chi)$ runtime guarantees [2].

### 4.3 PEPS: loops, #P-completeness, and approximate contraction

PEPS captures 2D area-law states that defeat MPS, but exact contraction is #P-complete, so every expectation value is an approximation problem. The **simple update** treats the environment of a bond in mean-field fashion ($O(D^{d+1})$ per gate), and for random-circuit-like states with rapidly scrambling correlations it can be surprisingly competitive; the **full update** and **boundary-MPS** contraction ($O(\chi_b^3 D^4)$ and variants) incorporate the true environment at steep polynomial cost [5][1]. A key recent finding is geometric: on IBM's heavy-hex lattice, loop correlations build up extremely slowly and barely affect local observables even at large depth, whereas on Google's Willow-like square geometry complex loop correlations emerge rapidly and dominate — the processor's *graph* dictates classical hardness as much as its gate count [1].

### 4.4 Contraction ordering: from treewidth to Cotengra

The Markov–Shi correspondence turns ordering into graph theory, but computing the optimum is NP-complete [6][7], and even *parameterized* algorithms carry exponential dependence on the treewidth itself — recent fine-grained analysis develops a treewidth-parameterized dichotomy contingent on the Hadwiger number [10]. In practice the pipeline is:

1. **Coarsen** the circuit TN (fuse single-qubit gates into neighbors).
2. **Order** with a hypergraph partitioner + local search (Cotengra), minimizing a flop/memory cost model.
3. **Slice** the largest intermediate indices to fit device memory, re-optimizing the order around the slices [9].
4. **Contract** pairwise with batched GEMM on GPUs, accumulating big batches of amplitudes.

For Sycamore depth-20, this pipeline reduced the contraction cost by orders of magnitude relative to naive orderings — the difference between infeasible and a 15-hour run on 512 GPUs [9]. Slicing is the enabling trick: it converts one $O(e^w)$-memory contraction into $d_s$ independent subtasks of manageable memory, perfectly parallel.

### 4.5 The supremacy frontier, quantitatively

Supremacy-class simulation cost is best reported as a triple: **(flops, memory, fidelity)**. Exact contraction gives fidelity 1 at maximal cost; sliced contraction targeting XEB-fidelity $f$ trades fidelity for speed — Pan and Zhang's $f = 0.0037$ run is the canonical example [9]. Meanwhile 2D-TN methods build an approximate *state* whose samples converge to the true distribution, reaching numerical precision on structured circuits (Jastrow ansätze, quenches) where brute force cannot [1]. The frontier is a surface in (qubits, depth, fidelity, structure), not a line.

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Complexity scaling of the main algorithms

| Method | Per-step cost | Memory | Exact? | Best regime |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MPS + TEBD | $O(n\chi^3)$ | $O(n\chi^2)$ | No (truncation) | 1D, low entanglement |
| DMRG (MPS) | $O(n\chi^3)$/sweep | $O(n\chi^2)$ | Variational | 1D gapped ground states |
| TTN + DMRG | $O(n\chi^4)$ | $O(n\chi^3)$ | Variational | Hierarchical/clustered entanglement [4] |
| PEPS simple update | $O(nD^{d+1})$ | $O(nD^d)$ | No (env. approx.) | 2D area-law dynamics |
| PEPS boundary MPS | $O(\chi_b^3D^4)$ | $O(\chi_b^2D^2)$ | No | 2D expectation values, sampling [1] |
| TN amplitude contraction | $O(e^{w})$ | $O(e^{w})$ | Yes (unsliced) | Fixed amplitude, any geometry [7][9] |
| Sliced contraction | $d_s \times O(e^{w'})$ | $O(e^{w'})$ | Yes | Supremacy circuits, GPU clusters [9] |

Here $w$ is the treewidth of the circuit's line graph and $w' < w$ the reduced width after slicing $d_s$ index values.

### 5.2 A minimal TEBD implementation

The core of every MPS circuit simulator is the two-site update. The following reference implementation applies a two-qubit gate to an MPS and re-truncates the bond:

```python
import numpy as np

def apply_two_site_gate(A, B, U, chi_max, cutoff=1e-12):
    # Apply 2-qubit gate U to adjacent MPS tensors A, B; truncate bond to chi_max.
    # A: (chi_l, d, chi_m), B: (chi_m, d, chi_r), U: (d, d, d, d).
    chi_l, d, chi_m = A.shape
    chi_r = B.shape[2]
    # 1. Contract gate with the two site tensors -> theta
    AB = np.tensordot(A, B, axes=([2], [0]))            # (chi_l, d, d, chi_r)
    theta = np.tensordot(U, AB, axes=([2, 3], [1, 2]))  # (d, d, chi_l, chi_r)
    theta = theta.transpose(2, 0, 1, 3).reshape(chi_l * d, d * chi_r)
    # 2. SVD split across the bond
    X, lam, Yh = np.linalg.svd(theta, full_matrices=False)
    # 3. Truncate: keep largest chi_max singular values above cutoff
    keep = int(np.sum(lam > cutoff))
    chi_new = min(chi_max, keep)
    discarded = float(np.sum(lam[chi_new:] ** 2))  # discarded weight epsilon
    X, lam, Yh = X[:, :chi_new], lam[:chi_new], Yh[:chi_new, :]
    # 4. Restore canonical form and reshape
    A_new = (X * lam).reshape(chi_l, d, chi_new)
    B_new = Yh.reshape(chi_new, d, chi_r)
    return A_new, B_new, discarded
```

The returned `discarded` weight $\varepsilon = \sum_{\alpha>\chi}\lambda_\alpha^2$ is the per-gate truncation error; monitoring $\sum \varepsilon$ over a run gives a running (pessimistic) infidelity estimate. Production codes (ITensor, TeNPy, quimb) add quantum-number conservation, subspace expansion, and TDVP, but the update above is the conceptual kernel [3][5].

### 5.3 Provable statements

> **Theorem (Contraction lower bound).** *Contracting a tensor network whose line graph has treewidth $w$ requires $\Omega(\exp(w))$ time under the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis, for bounded bond dimension.*

> **Theorem (MPS approximation of area-law states).** *A 1D state with entanglement $S$ across every cut is $\varepsilon$-approximated by an MPS of bond dimension $\chi = \exp(O(S/\varepsilon))$; for gapped ground states $S = O(1)$, so $\chi$ is system-size independent.*

> **Theorem (Entanglement-optimal unraveling, Faehrmann et al. 2026).** *For arbitrary single-qubit noise, the locally entanglement-optimal pure-state unraveling admits a closed-form solution, yielding a TN simulation with rigorous accuracy guarantees in $\mathrm{poly}(n,\chi)$ time* [2].

### 5.4 Worked scaling example

Consider a 1D random brickwork circuit of $n = 100$ qubits at depth $D = 10$. Entanglement across the central cut grows roughly as $S \approx \min(vD, n/2)$ with $v \sim 1$ ebit per layer, so $\chi \sim e^{vD} \approx e^{10} \approx 2.2\times 10^4$ — already beyond comfortable TEBD ($\chi^3 \approx 10^{13}$ flops per gate). By contrast, the same circuit as a TN has treewidth $w \approx D = 10$ (a 1D circuit's line graph is essentially a ladder), so amplitude contraction costs $\sim 2^{10}$ — trivial. This is the canonical lesson: *state methods die with volume-law entanglement; amplitude methods survive whenever the treewidth stays small*, which is why 1D circuits are classically easy in the TN picture at any depth. The hard case is 2D at large depth, where $w = O(D\sqrt{n})$ makes both approaches exponential — the supremacy regime.

## 6. Limitations

1. **The entanglement barrier is fundamental.** Linear entanglement growth under chaotic dynamics forces exponential bond dimension in any state-based method; no truncation scheme evades the Calabrese–Cardy argument. TN methods compress entanglement, they do not eliminate its cost.
2. **Optimal contraction is intractable.** NP-completeness of ordering [6][7] means all large-scale results rest on heuristics; pathological networks exist where greedy or Cotengra orderings are exponentially worse than optimal, and verifying near-optimality is itself hard.
3. **PEPS contraction is uncontrolled.** Exact contraction is #P-complete, so 2D expectation values carry approximation errors from the boundary or environment with no general a posteriori bound; convergence in $\chi_b$ or $D$ must be demonstrated empirically per instance [1][5].
4. **Long-range and all-to-all gates** inflate treewidth: a single layer of arbitrary two-qubit gates on $n$ qubits can push $w$ to $\Theta(n)$, defeating both MPS (volume law) and TN contraction. Structured decompositions (SWAP networks) recover locality only at depth cost.
5. **Noise helps the classical simulator.** Sufficiently strong depolarizing noise collapses the effective entanglement (and the XEB fidelity), making noisy supremacy-class circuits *easier* to simulate — the quantum advantage window requires low error rates *and* high depth simultaneously [2].
6. **Engineering walls.** Sliced contraction is memory-bandwidth bound on GPUs; index slicing multiplies flops; and the preprocessing (ordering plus slicing search) can itself take hours for the hardest instances [9].

## 7. Conclusion

Tensor networks have turned classical simulation into a structural science: simulation cost is governed by the entanglement a circuit generates and the treewidth of the network it induces, attacked by purpose-built algorithms — TEBD/DMRG for low entanglement, Cotengra-optimized sliced contraction for high treewidth, boundary-MPS PEPS for structured 2D circuits [1][3][4][9]. Each claim of classical intractability has been met by a sharper contraction path, and the frontier is now a negotiated surface in (qubits, depth, fidelity) rather than a fixed wall.

Open challenges remain sharp. *Provable* approximation guarantees for PEPS contraction are largely absent. The interplay of noise, entanglement, and simulability — where optimal unravelings [2] now give rigorous footholds — needs extension to correlated, non-Markovian noise. Contraction-path heuristics, while empirically superb, lack worst-case guarantees, and the fine-grained complexity of TN contraction is still being mapped [10]. Finally, as hardware pushes toward hundreds of qubits at low error rates, the question is no longer whether tensor networks can simulate *some* circuits at that scale, but whether any *useful* circuit can escape them — the race between ansatz design and processor design continues.
