# Succinct Non-Interactive Arguments of Knowledge: SNARKs, Polynomial Commitments, and Recursive Proof Composition

## Abstract

Succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge compress proofs of arbitrary computation into a handful of group elements verifiable in milliseconds. This survey traces the modern SNARK stack from first principles: knowledge-sound proof systems with extractable witnesses, arithmetization of programs into rank-1 constraint systems and quadratic arithmetic programs, and the polynomial commitment schemes — above all Kate-Zaverucha-Goldberg — that turn polynomial identities into checkable claims. We compare the major construction families: Groth16's pairing-optimal circuit-specific proofs, universal updatable systems such as PLONK and Marlin, and transparent IOP-based designs including Spartan and STARKs that dispense with trusted setup. We then examine recursive proof composition and incrementally verifiable computation, where Nova's folding schemes and HyperNova's generalization to customizable constraint systems achieve constant recursion overhead without SNARKs inside the loop. Throughout we weigh setup assumptions, prover costs, proof sizes, and post-quantum prospects, and close on the fundamental limits — non-falsifiable knowledge assumptions and prover scalability — that define the field's open frontier.

## 1 Definitions and the SNARK Formalism

Fix a finite field F and an NP relation R with language L_R = { x | exists w : (x, w) in R }. A *succinct non-interactive argument of knowledge* (SNARK) for R is a triple of algorithms (Setup, Prove, Verify) with the following properties. Setup(1^lambda) outputs a proving key pk and a verification key vk, possibly together with a trapdoor tau that must be discarded. Prove(pk, x, w) outputs a proof pi, and Verify(vk, x, pi) outputs accept or reject. *Completeness* demands that honest proofs of true statements verify. *Knowledge soundness* is the distinguishing feature of the "K": for every probabilistic polynomial-time prover P* that convinces the verifier on statement x with non-negligible probability, there exists an extractor E, running in time polynomial in lambda and the inverse success probability, that outputs a witness w with (x, w) in R. *Succinctness* requires |pi| and the verifier's running time to be bounded by poly(lambda, log |C|), where |C| measures the size of the computation being proved — in particular, both must be *sublinear* in the witness size [8][9].

> **Theorem:** Bitansky et al. showed that SNARKs for NP exist under falsifiable assumptions only in models with non-black-box extraction, and the first practical constructions rely on *knowledge-of-exponent* type assumptions in bilinear groups together with a structured common reference string.

Several refinements matter in practice. An *adaptive* SNARK remains sound when the adversary chooses the statement after seeing the reference string; a *preprocessing* (or *indexed*) SNARK splits Setup into an offline phase depending only on a size bound and an online phase deriving circuit-specific keys. *Zero-knowledge* SNARKs (zk-SNARKs) additionally require a simulator that produces proofs indistinguishable from real ones without the witness. Finally, the *setup model* is a first-order design axis: *circuit-specific* setups (Groth16) bind the reference string to one circuit, *universal* setups work for all circuits up to a size bound, *updatable* setups allow sequential contributors to refresh the trapdoor, and *transparent* setups need no secret randomness at all [1][3][6].

The intellectual lineage runs from Micali's CS proofs — Kilian's four-message protocol compiling PCPs with Merkle trees — through the pairing-based linear-PCP compilers of the 2010s. Gennaro, Gentry, Parno, and Raykova reduced circuit satisfiability to divisibility of polynomials (quadratic arithmetic programs, QAPs), yielding the Pinocchio system; Groth then compressed the proof to three group elements and minimized verifier work, the construction now known as Groth16 [1][9]. The modern era is defined less by any single system than by the *modular* view: an information-theoretic core (an interactive oracle proof or linear PCP) compiled to non-interactivity by a polynomial commitment scheme and the Fiat-Shamir heuristic [5].

## 2 Arithmetization: From Circuits to Polynomial Relations

Every SNARK begins by *arithmetizing* the computation: translating "the program executed correctly" into algebraic constraints over F. The dominant intermediate representation is *rank-1 constraint system* (R1CS). An R1CS instance is a triple of m-by-n matrices A, B, C over F; it is satisfied by a witness vector z (with z_1 = 1 conventionally) when the Hadamard relation (A z) circ (B z) = C z holds. Each row encodes one multiplicative constraint of the form <a_i, z> times <b_i, z> = <c_i, z>, which generalizes both multiplication gates and linear consistency checks [4][8].

The QAP reduction converts the m constraints into a *single* polynomial divisibility check. For each wire index j, interpolate degree-(m-1) polynomials A_j, B_j, C_j with A_j(omega^i) = a_{ij} (and analogously for B, C) over a set of evaluation points omega^i. Given the witness, form the combined polynomials A(X) = sum_j z_j A_j(X), B(X), C(X), and the *constraint polynomial* P(X) = A(X) B(X) minus C(X). Then P vanishes on all m evaluation points exactly when every R1CS constraint holds, i.e. P(X) is divisible by the *target polynomial* T(X) = product_i (X minus omega^i). The prover's core task becomes exhibiting the quotient H(X) = P(X) / T(X); the verifier checks one divisibility instead of m constraints [1][9].

> **Definition (QAP satisfiability):** An R1CS instance is satisfiable iff there exist polynomials A, B, C of degree below m and H of degree below m such that A(X) B(X) minus C(X) = H(X) T(X) as a formal polynomial identity, with A, B, C constrained to the linear span of the interpolated column polynomials.

Alternative arithmetizations trade constraint count against prover algebra. *PLONKish* arithmetization expresses each gate as q_L a plus q_R b plus q_O c plus q_M a b plus q_C = 0 with copy constraints enforced by a permutation argument, permitting custom gates that compress complex operations (range checks, elliptic-curve arithmetic) into few constraints [3]. *AIR* (algebraic intermediate representation) encodes uniform repeated computation as polynomial constraints on consecutive rows of an execution trace, the native language of STARKs. *CCS* (customizable constraint systems) generalizes R1CS, PLONKish, and AIR simultaneously without overhead, and is the native constraint language of the HyperNova folding scheme [5].

A crucial algebraic fact underlies all these encodings: checking polynomial identities can be reduced to checking *evaluations at random points* via the Schwartz-Zippel lemma, provided the field is large. The SNARK therefore never manipulates the witness vector directly after arithmetization; it commits to polynomials and proves claims about their evaluations. This is precisely the interface supplied by polynomial commitment schemes.

## 3 Polynomial Commitment Schemes

A *polynomial commitment scheme* (PCS) lets a prover commit to a polynomial p of degree at most d with a short digest com, and later prove that p(z) = y for a verifier-chosen point z, with communication independent of d. The commitment must be *binding* (the prover cannot open one commitment to two different evaluations) and ideally *hiding* and *homomorphic*. Kate, Zaverucha, and Goldberg gave the first constant-size construction, now universally called KZG [2].

The KZG scheme works in a bilinear group setting e: G_1 times G_2 -> G_T with generators g_1, g_2. Setup samples a secret tau and publishes the structured reference string { g_1^{tau^i} } and { g_2^{tau^i} } for i up to d, then *must discard* tau — anyone retaining it can forge proofs. To commit to p(X) = sum c_i X^i, the prover computes C = g_1^{p(tau)} = product_i (g_1^{tau^i})^{c_i}, a single group element. To prove p(z) = y, the prover forms the quotient polynomial q(X) = (p(X) minus y) / (X minus z), which is a genuine polynomial exactly when the claimed evaluation is correct, and sends pi = g_1^{q(tau)}. The verifier checks the pairing equation:

e(C / g_1^y, g_2) = e(pi, g_2^{tau} / g_2^{z})

Correctness follows from bilinearity: both sides equal e(g_1, g_2)^{q(tau)(tau - z)}. Binding reduces to the t-strong Diffie-Hellman assumption (strengthened to a knowledge assumption when the scheme must be extractable inside larger protocols), and the scheme is additively homomorphic in the committed polynomials [2][7].

```python
def kzg_verify(C, z, y, pi, g1, g2, g2_tau, pairing, sub, div):
    # Check e(C - [y]_1, [1]_2) == e(pi, [tau]_2 - [z]_2)
    lhs = pairing(sub(C, g1**y), g2)
    rhs = pairing(pi, sub(g2_tau, g2**z))
    return lhs == rhs
```

Several properties of KZG shaped the SNARK landscape. *Batching* techniques let one group element serve as an opening proof for many polynomials at one point, or one polynomial at many points, which PLONK-style systems exploit to keep proofs small [7]. The trusted setup is *universal and updatable*: one powers-of-tau ceremony serves every circuit up to the degree bound, and participants can sequentially contribute randomness so that security holds as long as *one* contributor is honest — the model behind production ceremonies such as Ethereum's KZG ceremony for EIP-4844 data-availability sampling [1].

Alternatives trade the pairing-based trusted setup for transparency or different assumptions:

| Scheme | Assumption | Setup | Commitment size | Opening proof |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| KZG [2] | t-SDH / pairing | Trusted, universal, updatable | 1 G_1 | 1 G_1 |
| Bulletproofs IPA | Discrete log | Transparent | O(log d) | O(log d) |
| Dory | SXDH | Trusted, universal | O(log d) | O(log d) |
| FRI | Collision-resistant hash | Transparent | O(log^2 d) | O(log^2 d) |
| DARK | Groups of unknown order | Transparent | O(log d) | O(log d) |

The pattern is a trilemma among *setup transparency*, *proof succinctness*, and *verifier efficiency*: pairing-based schemes give the smallest proofs but need trusted ceremonies; hash-based schemes like FRI need none but pay in proof size and prover hashing; discrete-log schemes sit in the middle with logarithmic proofs and transparent setup at the cost of linear-time verification [6].

## 4 Universal Constructions

### 4.1 Groth16: the pairing-optimal baseline

Groth's 2016 system remains the efficiency frontier for circuit-specific SNARKs: proofs are exactly three group elements (two in G_1, one in G_2), and verification costs three pairings plus work linear in the *public input* size only. The prover encodes the witness into the QAP polynomials, randomizes with the CRS trapdoor shifts, and outputs the randomized evaluations; the verifier checks two pairing equations enforcing the divisibility relation and the linear-span restriction. The price is a per-circuit trusted setup whose trapdoor, if retained, permits unbounded forgery, and a proving key linear in the circuit size [1][9].

### 4.2 Sonic, Marlin, and PLONK: universal updatable SRS

Sonic (Maller et al.) was the first practical SNARK with fully succinct verification under a universal SRS, but its prover overhead was high. PLONK recast both the arithmetization and the permutation argument around *evaluations on a multiplicative subgroup* rather than monomial coefficients, yielding a five-round protocol with roughly an order of magnitude fewer exponentiations than Sonic and proofs of about nine G_1 elements. Marlin independently achieved a universal SRS with a two-tier polynomial-commitment design separating the circuit-independent and circuit-dependent phases. The common architectural lesson: a *single* updatable powers-of-tau ceremony can serve all circuits up to a size bound, and per-circuit preprocessing derives only public parameters, eliminating the per-deployment ceremony that plagued Groth16 [3][9].

### 4.3 Transparent IOP-based SNARKs

A parallel lineage removes trusted setup entirely by compiling *interactive oracle proofs* with hash-based commitments. STARKs combine the FRI low-degree test with Merkle-committed execution traces, giving transparent, plausibly post-quantum proofs at the cost of larger (tens of kilobytes) proofs and heavy prover hashing. Spartan builds a transparent SNARK for R1CS from the sum-check protocol and a linear-time polynomial commitment distilled from Bootle-Chiesa-Groth techniques: the prover performs O(N) field operations for an N-sized instance — concretely the fastest prover asymptotics among transparent systems — with proof size and verifier time sublinear after preprocessing, compressible to polylogarithmic by one layer of composition with an outer SNARK [6][10].

### 4.4 Lookup arguments and custom gates

Plookup-style *lookup arguments* prove that each entry of a query vector appears in a public table, letting circuits replace expensive non-arithmetic operations (bitwise ops, S-boxes, range checks) with table membership. Combined with PLONKish custom gates, lookups cut constraint counts by orders of magnitude for hash-heavy circuits and virtual-machine instruction sets; UltraPLONK and successors made lookups a standard component of production proving stacks [3].

### 4.5 The comparative landscape

| System | Setup | Proof size | Prover | Verifier | Trust assumption |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Groth16 [1] | Circuit-specific trusted | 3 group elements | O(n log n) | O(\|x\|) + 3 pairings | q-PKE / generic group |
| PLONK [3] | Universal updatable | ~9 G_1 | O(n log n) | O(1) pairings-dominated | KZG / AGM |
| Marlin | Universal updatable | ~10 G_1 | O(n log n) | O(\|x\| log n) | KZG / AGM |
| Spartan [6] | Transparent | O(sqrt n) / polylog | O(n) field ops | Sublinear / polylog | Discrete log / CRHF |
| STARK | Transparent | O(log^2 n) | O(n log n) hashes | O(log^2 n) | CRHF (PQ-plausible) |
| Nova IVC [4] | Transparent | O(\|F\|), compressible | 2 MSMs per step | Constant circuit | Discrete log |

The table exposes the central engineering trade: *concrete proof size and verifier cost* (Groth16, PLONK) against *setup ceremony risk* and *prover scalability* (Spartan, STARKs), with recursion — the subject of the next section — increasingly used to compress the proofs of the latter class after the fact [4][6].

## 5 Recursive Proof Composition

*Incrementally verifiable computation* (IVC) captures the problem of proving a long sequential computation y = F^{(l)}(x_0) step by step: given a proof pi_i attesting the first i steps, the prover produces pi_{i+1} for i+1 steps with work *independent of i*, and the verifier checks the final proof in time independent of l. The naive construction — a SNARK circuit that verifies the previous SNARK proof — suffers a fundamental bottleneck: expressing pairing-based verification inside the circuit is enormously expensive, and historically required *cycles of elliptic curves* (e.g. MNT cycles) where each curve's scalar field matches the other's base field so that "the verifier verifies proofs about itself" can be arithmetized [4][10].

Halo and its successors attacked the problem by accumulating verification work rather than verifying it inside the circuit at each step, deferring the expensive check to the end. Nova took a more radical step: it *avoids SNARKs entirely* inside the recursion. The key primitive is a *folding scheme*, which reduces the task of checking *two* instances of a relation to checking *one* — a weaker, cheaper demand than proving. Nova constructs a folding scheme for *relaxed R1CS* (R1CS augmented with a slack vector and scalar that absorb the cross terms arising when two instances are randomly combined), and shows it implies IVC: at each step the prover folds the fresh step-instance into a single running instance with work dominated by two multiexponentiations of size O(|F|), and recursion overhead of merely a constant number of constraints — about two scalar multiplications expressed as a circuit, the smallest in the literature. The IVC proof itself is O(|F|) group elements, but a final compression with a standard zkSNARK yields O(log |F|)-sized zero-knowledge proofs. Nova needs no trusted setup and no FFTs, instantiating over any cycle of discrete-log-hard curves [4].

HyperNova generalizes folding from R1CS to *customizable constraint systems*, cutting the prover's cryptographic cost to a *single* multiscalar multiplication per fold — optimal for MSM-based commitments — supporting multi-folding of many instances at once (hence proof-carrying data, not just linear IVC), achieving zero knowledge "for free" by folding-based randomization instead of an outer zkSNARK, and introducing CycleFold, a general technique for instantiating folding-based recursion on curve cycles with minimal overhead [5].

> **Theorem:** Nova's folding-based IVC achieves prover work of two O(|F|)-sized multiexponentiations per step and constant recursion overhead, with knowledge soundness reducing to the discrete-logarithm assumption in the random-oracle model; HyperNova extends this to CCS with a single MSM per fold.

The practical consequence is a clean separation of concerns that now structures the field: a fast *folding-based* layer handles unbounded iteration with minimal per-step cryptography, while a succinct *compressing* SNARK (often KZG-based PLONK) is applied once at the end. Rollups, succinct blockchains, and verifiable state machines increasingly compile to this two-layer shape [4][5][10].

## 6 Limitations

The theory carries structural caveats that no engineering refinement has removed. First, *knowledge soundness* in all efficient SNARKs rests on *non-falsifiable* knowledge-of-exponent assumptions (or the algebraic group model, which formalizes the same intuition): there is no known reduction from breaking Groth16 or KZG-based PLONK to a standard falsifiable assumption, and Gentry-Wichs showed that SNARKs with linear extraction cannot be proved secure under falsifiable assumptions via black-box reductions. Second, pairing- and discrete-log-based systems fall to quantum attacks — Shor's algorithm breaks the pairing groups and the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm alike — leaving hash-based STARKs as the only plausibly post-quantum family, at significant concrete cost [6][9].

Third, the Fiat-Shamir transform moves security into the *random-oracle model*, and its concrete instantiation with a specific hash function admits pathological counterexamples in theory, even though no practical attack on deployed parameterizations is known. Fourth, prover costs remain formidable: FFTs and multiexponentiations over hundreds of millions of constraints demand specialized hardware and dominate the economics of proving networks. Fifth, trusted-setup ceremonies, while updatable and multi-party, concentrate systemic risk — a compromised ceremony for a widely reused SRS breaks *every* deployment sharing it — and transparent systems pay for their trustlessness with larger proofs. Finally, arithmetization itself is lossy labor: expressing arbitrary programs as rank-1 constraints or trace polynomials can inflate computation by large constant factors, and mismatches between the constraint model and the application (notably for recursion-unfriendly proof systems) continue to motivate new constraint languages such as CCS [5][10].

## 7 Conclusion

The SNARK literature has converged on a modular anatomy: *arithmetization* reduces computation to polynomial relations, *polynomial commitments* bind the prover to those polynomials with succinct openings, *interactive-oracle or linear-PCP cores* supply the information-theoretic soundness, and the *Fiat-Shamir heuristic* removes interaction. Along each axis the field has mapped a design space — circuit-specific versus universal versus transparent setups, pairing-based versus hash-based commitments, QAP versus PLONKish versus AIR encodings — with Groth16, PLONK, Spartan, and STARKs occupying distinct Pareto points [1][3][6]. Recursive composition, revolutionized by Nova's folding schemes and HyperNova's generalization to customizable constraint systems, has turned incrementally verifiable computation from a theoretical curiosity requiring exotic curve cycles into a practical systems primitive with constant recursion overhead [4][5]. The open frontiers are now well-defined: falsifiable or at least better-understood knowledge assumptions, post-quantum succinctness matching pairing-based concrete efficiency, prover hardware and algorithms that make billion-constraint proofs routine, and constraint languages that narrow the gap between programs as written and relations as proved. Progress on any one of these would reshape which point of the design space production systems occupy.
