# The Fractional Quantum Hall Effect: The Laughlin Wavefunction, Composite Fermions, and Topological Order

## Abstract

The fractional quantum Hall effect (FQHE), discovered in 1982 as plateaus in the Hall resistance at fractional multiples of h/e², remains the paradigmatic example of topological order and emergent fractionalization in condensed matter. This thesis presents a unified account of the effect's theoretical and experimental development: the Laughlin trial wavefunction at filling fractions ν = 1/m and its mapping to a classical two-dimensional plasma, the elementary excitations carrying charge e/m and anyonic statistics, Jain's composite-fermion construction in which attached flux quanta cancel the external magnetic field to reveal an integer quantum Hall effect of composite particles, the hierarchy and conformal-field-theoretic reformulations, Wen's theory of chiral Luttinger-liquid edge states, and the non-Abelian Moore–Read paired state at ν = 5/2 and its implications for topological quantum computation. We review the decisive experiments — fractional plateaus, shot-noise detection of fractional charge, interferometry of anyonic phases, and the half-integer thermal Hall conductance — and conclude with open questions in topological classification and anyon braiding technology.

---

## 1 Introduction

Klaus von Klitzing's 1980 discovery — that a two-dimensional electron gas at low temperature and high magnetic field has Hall resistance quantized in integer multiples of h/e² — inaugurated the modern era of topological condensed matter [1]. The integer quantum Hall effect (IQHE) fits a single-particle picture: Landau quantization yields macroscopically degenerate levels, and integer filling pins the Fermi energy in a mobility gap via disorder-localized states, giving σ_xy = ne²/h with vanishing longitudinal resistance.

Yet within two years, Tsui, Störmer, and Gossard observed additional plateaus at fractional fillings, notably ν = 1/3 and ν = 2/3, with Hall resistance quantized at h/e²ν [2]. The FQHE admits no single-particle explanation: with kinetic energy quenched inside a Landau level, the physics is driven purely by Coulomb interactions in a non-perturbative, strongly correlated regime.

The theoretical resolution required new concepts that have since reshaped physics far beyond the 2DEG:

1. **Emergent fractionalization**: The elementary excitations of the Laughlin liquid carry fractional charge e/3 and obey *anyonic* statistics — neither bosonic nor fermionic — the first experimentally realized example of fractional quantum numbers emerging from purely electronic constituents [3].
2. **Flux attachment**: Jain's composite fermions bind an even number of flux quanta to each electron, turning the FQHE into an integer quantum Hall effect of composite particles in a reduced effective field [4].
3. **Topological order**: The FQHE ground states exhibit long-range quantum entanglement, ground-state degeneracy on topologically non-trivial surfaces, and protected gapless edge modes — properties invisible to any local order parameter and forming the foundation of the modern theory of topological phases.

This thesis develops these ideas from microscopic wavefunction through effective field theory to experiment, ending at the non-Abelian frontier of ν = 5/2, where anyons with non-commutative braiding may underpin fault-tolerant quantum computation.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Landau quantization and the filling factor

A 2D electron of effective mass m* in perpendicular field **B** = Bẑ experiences Landau quantization. In the symmetric gauge, single-particle states form Landau levels spaced by the cyclotron energy ħω_c = ħeB/m*, each with degeneracy N_φ = BA/φ₀ per area, where φ₀ = h/e is the flux quantum [1]. The *filling factor* ν = N/N_φ = nh/(eB), with n the 2D electron density, counts the fraction of a Landau level occupied by the N electrons.

In the *extreme quantum limit* — tesla-scale fields and millikelvin temperatures — only the lowest Landau level (LLL) is occupied and the kinetic energy is frozen. The Hamiltonian reduces to the LLL-projected Coulomb interaction

$$H = \mathcal{P}_{LLL} \sum_{i<j} \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon |\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|} \mathcal{P}_{LLL},$$

where $\mathcal{P}_{LLL}$ projects into the lowest Landau level. With no small parameter, this is a profoundly non-perturbative problem whose ground state must be found variationally or numerically.

### 2.2 From integer to fractional quantization

Both integer and fractional states share one experimental signature: over a finite field range, R_xx vanishes while R_xy pins to R_K/ν, with R_K = h/e² ≈ 25.813 kΩ the von Klitzing constant [1][2]. Integer ν gives the IQHE; rational ν = p/q with odd q gives the FQHE. Quantization is accurate to parts per billion, protected by topological invariants — band Chern numbers for IQHE, many-body invariants for FQHE.

| Filling ν | Hall resistance R_xy | Nature | Typical energy gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | h/e² ≈ 25.8 kΩ | Integer (IQHE) | ∼ 10 K at 10 T |
| 2/3 | 3h/2e² | Fractional (hole-conjugate) | ∼ 1 K |
| 1/3 | 3h/e² | Fractional (Laughlin) | ∼ 5 K |
| 2/5 | 5h/2e² | Fractional (Jain) | ∼ 2 K |
| 5/2 | 2h/5e² | Fractional (non-Abelian candidate) | ∼ 0.5 K |
| 1/2 | 2h/e² (no plateau) | Composite-fermion Fermi liquid | gapless |

*Table 1: Representative plateaus; gaps scale roughly as e²/(εℓ_B) with ℓ_B = √(ħ/eB).*

### 2.3 The incompressibility puzzle

At ν = 1/3, exact diagonalization shows an *incompressible* ground state: adding an electron costs finite energy, producing a cusp in the chemical potential. Laughlin's insight was that the ground state minimizes Coulomb repulsion by keeping electrons apart — a correlated liquid whose wavefunction vanishes as (z_i − z_j)^m on approach, with odd m = 3 enforcing the Pauli principle [3]. This single variational ansatz unlocked the field.

## 3 Methodology

We proceed along four complementary lines:

1. **Microscopic variational wavefunctions**: the Laughlin ansatz and its plasma-analogy mapping, which converts the quantum normalization integral into a classical 2D plasma partition function; screening proves incompressibility and uniform density.
2. **Flux attachment**: following Jain [4], 2p flux quanta per electron via a Chern–Simons gauge transformation give B* = B − 2pφ₀n, mapping ν = n/(2pn ± 1) onto integer filling of composite-fermion Landau levels.
3. **Effective field theory**: Chern–Simons theories with K-matrices classify Abelian topological orders; chiral CFT correlators generate trial wavefunctions (the Moore–Read construction); edge dynamics follow Wen's chiral Luttinger liquid [9].
4. **Verification**: exact-diagonalization overlaps (routinely above 0.99 for Laughlin states), DMRG studies, and the experimental program — transport plateaus [2], shot-noise charge [5][6], interferometry [10], thermal Hall conductance [8].

Numerical illustrations use standard libraries and are pedagogical demonstrations, not production computations.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Laughlin wavefunction and the plasma analogy

In complex coordinates z_j = x_j + iy_j (in units of ℓ_B), Laughlin proposed the trial ground state at ν = 1/m, odd integer m [3]:

$$\Psi_m(\{z_i\}) = \prod_{i<j} (z_i - z_j)^m \exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\sum_i |z_i|^2\right).$$

The Gaussian confines electrons to a droplet; the Jastrow factor suppresses short-range Coulomb repulsion; odd m guarantees fermionic antisymmetry. The highest power of any coordinate is m(N−1), so the droplet encloses mN flux quanta — precisely filling 1/m.

> **Theorem:** (Laughlin, 1983) The squared modulus $|\Psi_m|^2$ equals the Boltzmann weight of a classical two-dimensional one-component plasma at inverse temperature β = 2/m, with particles of charge m interacting via the 2D Coulomb (logarithmic) potential in a uniform neutralizing background.

Explicitly:

$$|\Psi_m|^2 = \exp\left[-\beta\left(m^2\sum_{i<j}\left(-\ln|z_i - z_j|\right) + \frac{m}{4}\sum_i |z_i|^2\right)\right],$$

with β = 2/m. The classical 2D plasma at these couplings is a screening fluid: charges neutralize perturbations over a Debye length of order ℓ_B. Translating back, the quantum fluid has *uniform density* n = 1/(2πmℓ_B²) and is *incompressible* — the origin of the excitation gap and the Hall plateau [3].

A quasihole at position η multiplies the wavefunction by $\prod_i (z_i - \eta)$; the plasma analogy shows it depletes local charge by exactly 1/m of an electron charge — a *fractionally charged* excitation with q* = e/m [3]. Exchanging two quasiholes accumulates Berry phase π/m: *anyonic* statistics with angle θ = π/m, interpolating between bosons and fermions.

```python
import numpy as np

def laughlin_jastrow(z, m=3):
    """Unnormalized Laughlin factor prod_{i<j} (z_i - z_j)^m."""
    n = len(z)
    i, j = np.triu_indices(n, k=1)
    return np.prod((z[i] - z[j]) ** m)

def plasma_energy(z, m=3):
    """Classical plasma potential energy = -log|Psi_m|^2, beta=2/m."""
    beta = 2.0 / m
    i, j = np.triu_indices(len(z), k=1)
    pair = -m**2 * np.sum(np.log(np.abs(z[i] - z[j])))
    bg = (m / 4.0) * np.sum(np.abs(z) ** 2)
    return beta * (pair + bg)

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
z = (rng.standard_normal(50) + 1j * rng.standard_normal(50)) * 2.0
print(f"m=3 plasma energy of random config: {plasma_energy(z):.1f}")
print(f"expected droplet radius ~ sqrt(2mN) = {np.sqrt(2*3*50):.1f} lB")
```

### 4.2 Composite fermions: the effective magnetic field picture

Laughlin's ansatz explains ν = 1/m but not the wider hierarchy (2/5, 3/7, 4/9, …). Jain's composite-fermion (CF) theory provides the unifying framework [4]: a composite fermion is an electron bound to an even number 2p of flux quanta, implemented by a singular Chern–Simons gauge transformation:

$$\Psi_e = \prod_{i<j}\left(\frac{z_i - z_j}{|z_i - z_j|}\right)^{2p}\Psi_{CF},$$

attaching 2p flux tubes per electron while preserving fermionic statistics (2p is even). In mean field, the attached flux smears into a uniform fictitious field opposing the external one, leaving a reduced *effective magnetic field*

$$B^* = B - 2p\phi_0 n,$$

with 1/ν* = 1/ν − 2p. Integer ν* = n — n filled CF Landau levels — gives incompressibility and the celebrated **Jain sequences**

$$\nu = \frac{n}{2pn \pm 1},$$

accounting for essentially all prominently observed odd-denominator fractions: 1/3, 2/5, 3/7, 4/9 and 2/3, 3/5, 4/7 (p = 1) [4]. The Laughlin states are the n = 1 members; higher n generalize via the explicit wavefunctions

$$\Psi^{Jain}_{n/(2pn+1)} = \mathcal{P}_{LLL}\prod_{i<j}(z_i - z_j)^{2p}\,\Phi_n,$$

with Φ_n the Slater determinant of n filled Landau levels, projected to the LLL — overlaps with exact ground states exceed 0.99 for accessible N [4].

At ν = 1/2 the effective field vanishes (B* = 0): composite fermions form the Halperin–Lee–Read Fermi liquid, explaining the missing plateau and predicting a CF Fermi wavevector k_F = √(4πn), confirmed by surface-acoustic-wave and magnetic-focusing experiments [4].

### 4.3 Hierarchy, K-matrices, and conformal field theory

The **Haldane–Halperin hierarchy** builds daughter states by condensing a parent Laughlin state's quasiparticles into their own Laughlin-like liquid, generating fractions via continued fractions; CF theory reproduces these results more economically, with superior trial wavefunctions.

The low-energy effective theory is a multicomponent **Chern–Simons theory** characterized by a symmetric integer K-matrix, a charge vector t, and a spin vector s. The Hall conductance is σ_xy = (e²/h) tᵀK⁻¹t, the quasiparticle charges are q_a = e tᵀK⁻¹l_a, and the mutual statistics are θ_ab = 2π l_aᵀK⁻¹l_b. The Laughlin state has the one-by-one K-matrix with entry m; the ν = 2/5 Jain state has a two-by-two K-matrix with diagonal entries 3 and off-diagonal entries 2. The determinant |det K| gives the ground-state degeneracy on the torus — the hallmark of topological order.

Moore and Read showed that trial wavefunctions arise as conformal blocks of a chiral **conformal field theory**: the Laughlin state is a correlator of vertex operators in a compactified U(1) boson CFT, while the ν = 5/2 state involves the Ising CFT [7] — a bulk–edge correspondence dictating that gapless edge modes are described by the same chiral CFT.

### 4.4 Edge states: the chiral Luttinger liquid

Gauge invariance demands gapless modes at a quantum Hall droplet's boundary. Wen showed the Laughlin edge is a *chiral Luttinger liquid* — one branch of bosonic density waves propagating in a single direction [9]:

$$S = \frac{m}{4\pi}\int dx\,dt\,\partial_x\phi\,(\partial_t - v\partial_x)\phi,$$

with electron operator ψ_e ∼ e^{imφ} and quasiparticle operator ψ_qp ∼ e^{iφ}. The tunneling density of states vanishes as D(E) ∼ E^{m−1} — for m = 3, conductance scales as V² or T² — a non-Fermi-liquid signature confirmed in cleaved-edge-overgrowth experiments [9]. Jain states host n co-propagating modes (plus counter-propagating neutral modes for hole-conjugate fractions like 2/3), and the thermal Hall conductance κ_xy = c(π²k_B²/3h)T counts the edge CFT's chiral central charge c — a bulk topological invariant measurable by heat transport [8].

### 4.5 Non-Abelian statistics at ν = 5/2: the Moore–Read state

The even-denominator plateau at ν = 5/2, in the first excited Landau level, cannot be a Laughlin or Jain state. Moore and Read proposed that composite fermions at half filling of the valence Landau level undergo p-wave pairing — a BCS-like instability of the CF Fermi sea [7]:

$$\Psi_{MR} = \mathrm{Pf}\left(\frac{1}{z_i - z_j}\right)\prod_{i<j}(z_i - z_j)^2\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\sum_i|z_i|^2}.$$

The Pfaffian factor is the real-space BCS wavefunction of spin-polarized composite fermions; two attached flux quanta convert CF filling ν* = 2 into electron filling ν = 5/2. Vortices in this p+ip superconductor bind **Majorana zero modes**: 2n quasiholes span a 2^{n−1}-dimensional degenerate space, and adiabatic braiding acts as non-commuting unitaries — *non-Abelian statistics* [7]. Such Ising anyons support topologically protected Clifford gates, making the 5/2 state a leading candidate for topological quantum computation, though universality needs non-topological supplements.

Competing candidates — anti-Pfaffian and particle-hole-symmetric Pfaffian — differ in edge content and hence thermal Hall conductance: 7/2, 3/2, or 5/2 times κ₀T. The 2018 measurement κ_xy = 2.5κ₀T at ν = 5/2 [8] favors non-Abelian order with a Majorana edge mode, though the precise identification remains actively debated.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Transport: plateaus and activation gaps

DC magnetotransport remains the foundational evidence. In GaAs/AlGaAs heterostructures with mobilities above 10⁷ cm²/Vs, sweeping B at fixed density reveals a staircase of plateaus: R_xx → 0 while R_xy locks to h/(e²ν) at ν = 1/3, 2/5, 3/7, 4/9, 5/11 and conjugates 2/3, 3/5, 4/7 [2] — precisely the Jain pattern, with gaps weakening as n grows. Arrhenius fits R_xx ∼ exp(−Δ/2T) give gaps Δ scaling as e²/(εℓ_B), matching CF-based calculations with finite-thickness and Landau-level-mixing corrections [4].

### 5.2 Direct detection of fractional charge: shot noise

Fractional charge was measured directly via quantum shot noise: quasiparticles tunneling across a quantum point contact produce noise S_I = 2q*I(1 − T) with q* the carrier charge. In 1997 two groups reported q* = e/3 at ν = 1/3 [5][6]. Later experiments found e/5 at ν = 2/5 and, crucially, e/4 at ν = 5/2 — the Moore–Read quasihole charge [7] — confirming the 5/2 excitations match the paired-CF prediction rather than Abelian alternatives.

```rust
/// Jain sequence fractions nu = n / (2*p*n +/- 1) (Jain 1989).
fn jain_fractions(p: u32, n_max: u32) -> Vec<(u32, u32)> {
    let mut v = Vec::new();
    for n in 1..=n_max {
        v.push((n, 2 * p * n + 1)); // nu = n/(2pn+1): 1/3, 2/5, 3/7, ...
        if n > 1 {
            v.push((n, 2 * p * n - 1)); // particle-hole branch: 2/3, 3/5, ...
        }
    }
    v
}

fn main() {
    for (num, den) in jain_fractions(1, 4) {
        println!("nu = {}/{}  ->  R_xy = {} h/e^2", num, den, den as f64 / num as f64);
    }
}
```

### 5.3 Interferometry: probing anyonic braiding phases

Fabry–Pérot interferometers measure the phase of edge quasiparticles encircling localized bulk quasiparticles: an Aharonov–Bohm term plus a statistical term 2θN_qp from braiding around N_qp trapped anyons. At ν = 1/3, phase jumps of 2π/3 were observed, consistent with θ = π/3 [10]. At ν = 5/2, Willett *et al.* reported field-tuned Aharonov–Bohm oscillations with even–odd alternation expected from non-Abelian braiding — an e/4 quasiparticle encircling an even versus odd number of bulk quasiparticles acquires different non-Abelian phases [10]. These experiments are the most direct probe of braiding statistics to date.

### 5.4 Thermal Hall conductance: the smoking gun for non-Abelian order

Electrical conductance cannot distinguish the 5/2 candidates (all give σ_xy = 5e²/2h), but thermal conductance can: edge theory predicts κ_xy = c_−κ₀T with κ₀ = π²k_B²/3h. The Pfaffian gives c_− = 7/2, the anti-Pfaffian 3/2, the PH-Pfaffian 5/2. Banerjee *et al.* measured κ_xy = 2.5κ₀T at ν = 5/2 — a half-integer value impossible for any Abelian state, directly demonstrating a neutral Majorana edge mode and non-Abelian topological order [8]; companion measurements confirmed κ₀ quantization at integer and Laughlin fillings [8].

| Experiment | Observable | Result | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tsui–Störmer–Gossard (1982) [2] | R_xy plateaus | h/(e²ν), ν = 1/3, 2/3 | FQHE discovery |
| Shot noise (1997) [5][6] | Tunneling charge q* | e/3 at ν = 1/3 | Fractional charge |
| Shot noise (2008+) | q* at ν = 5/2 | e/4 | Moore–Read quasihole |
| Fabry–Pérot interferometry [10] | Braiding phase slips | 2π/3 at 1/3; even–odd at 5/2 | Anyonic statistics |

## 6 Limitations

Despite four decades of progress, significant gaps remain:

1. **Variational status**: Laughlin and Jain wavefunctions are ansätze, not derived from the Coulomb Hamiltonian. Overlaps with exact ground states are excellent, but no controlled analytic solution of the LLL-projected Coulomb problem exists; the plasma analogy is rigorous for the norm, not for excitation spectra.
2. **The 5/2 identity crisis**: numerics with Landau-level mixing and finite thickness variously favor Pfaffian, anti-Pfaffian, or PH-Pfaffian depending on parameters. The thermal Hall result [8] constrains but does not uniquely identify the order, and disorder-induced domain mixtures remain possible.
3. **Braiding verification**: no experiment has demonstrated controlled, deterministic braiding of non-Abelian anyons or measured the non-commutativity of successive exchanges; interferometry [10] gives statistical-phase evidence, not gate-level braiding.
4. **Universality gap**: Ising anyons are not computationally universal by braiding alone; universal topological quantum computation needs unprotected supplementary operations, reintroducing error channels.
5. **Material constraints**: observations demand ultra-high-mobility heterostructures at millikelvin temperatures and tesla-scale fields — far from device-friendly conditions — and disorder remains the chief spoiler of fragile fractions.
6. **New platforms**: fractions in higher Landau levels (reentrant states, stripe and bubble phases) and moiré fractional Chern insulators extend the paradigm but lack the LLL's theoretical control.

## 7 Conclusion

The FQHE transformed our understanding of quantum matter. From Laughlin's Jastrow wavefunction [3] — elevated by the plasma analogy into a proof of incompressibility and fractional charge — through Jain's composite fermions [4], which revealed the FQHE as the integer quantum Hall effect of flux-dressed particles, to Wen's edge theory [9] and the non-Abelian Moore–Read state [7], the field has shown that *emergence* produces excitations with quantum numbers forbidden to elementary particles.

Experiment has kept pace: fractional plateaus [2], shot-noise detection of e/3 and e/4 [5][6], interferometric signatures of anyonic phases [10], and the half-integer thermal Hall conductance at ν = 5/2 [8]. The convergence of topological field theory, precision mesoscopy, and quantum-information ambition makes the FQHE the richest meeting point of fundamental and applied quantum physics today.

The road ahead: identify the 5/2 order unambiguously, demonstrate deterministic anyon braiding, and translate topological protection into working qubits. Whether topological quantum computation succeeds or not, the FQHE has earned its place as the discovery that taught physics to think topologically.
