# P4 and Programmable Data Planes: Match-Action Pipelines, PISA Architectures, and the In-Network Computing Paradigm

## Abstract

The fixed-function forwarding chip is obsolete as an intellectual artifact. This article presents a unified treatment of programmable data planes: the P4 packet-processing language [1][3], the reconfigurable match-table (RMT) and protocol-independent switch architecture (PISA) hardware substrate [2], and the compilation and stateful-programming techniques that bind language to silicon [4][5][6]. We formalize the match-action pipeline as a constrained execution model—feed-forward stages, bounded per-stage memory, single-cycle stateful atoms—and show how this model sustains line-rate forwarding at terabit scale. We then trace the intellectual leap from *forwarding* to *computing*: the in-network computing paradigm [7], in which programmable switches execute application logic such as key-value caching [8] and distributed machine-learning aggregation [9] at line rate. We quantify measured speedups (2+ billion queries per second; up to 5.5× training throughput), derive the bandwidth and memory bounds governing these systems, and identify the fundamental limits—no floating point, no loops, stage-local state—that bound what the network can compute.

##1 Introduction

For three decades, network forwarding hardware obeyed a simple bargain: the switch vendor fixed the set of recognized protocols at tape-out, and operators configured *tables* but never *behavior*. OpenFlow widened the configuration surface but preserved the bargain's essence—the pipeline remained fixed-function, and every new header or forwarding semantic required a new ASIC generation. The P4 project [1] shattered this arrangement by making the *parser*, the *match-action pipeline*, and the *deparser* all programmable in a high-level, target-independent language, while the RMT work [2] proved that such programmability could be realized in hardware at line rate with area and power comparable to fixed-function chips. Together, language and architecture closed the loop: a protocol-independent data plane.

The consequences extend well beyond forwarding. Once the data plane is programmable, the switch is no longer merely a packet mover; it is a massively parallel, deeply pipelined computer that happens to sit on every network path. The in-network computing paradigm [7] asks which computations—aggregation, caching, consensus primitives, telemetry—are profitably offloaded onto that computer, subject to its severe architectural constraints. This article argues that the match-action pipeline is best understood not as a networking artifact but as a *constrained execution model*, analogous to the way the streaming or MapReduce models constrain computation to enable scale. The constraints are severe: no loops, no floating point, tens of megabytes of SRAM, per-packet budgets measured in single clock cycles. Yet, as NetCache [8] and SwitchML [9] demonstrate, the right computations—those with small state, integer arithmetic, and partition-aggregate structure—run at billions of operations per second where servers cannot follow.

We proceed as follows. Section 2 reviews the hardware lineage from RMT to PISA and the language lineage from P4-14 to P4-16. Section 3 describes our analytic method: cost models of the pipeline, the compiler mapping problem, and the atom abstraction for stateful programs. Section 4 develops the deep technical content in four movements. Section 5 presents empirical results and two formal results with proof sketches. Section 6 confronts the paradigm's limits, and Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### The RMT breakthrough

Bosshart et al. [2] proposed the *reconfigurable match-table* (RMT) model: a packet-processing pipeline of roughly 32 stages clocked at ~1 GHz, where each stage contains match memory (TCAM for ternary matches, SRAM for exact matches) and action units, fronted by a programmable parser and trailed by a programmable deparser. The key quantitative claim was that this generality costs little: a 64-port 10 Gb/s (640 Gb/s) RMT chip occupies roughly the same die area (~200 mm²) as a fixed-function switch of equal throughput. Programmability is thus a *reallocation* of the same transistor budget, not a luxury tax on performance.

The architecture generalizes the fixed pipeline into the *protocol-independent switch architecture* (PISA), commercialized most prominently in the Barefoot Tofino family: a parser extracts headers into a packet header vector (PHV); a sequence of match-action stages transforms the PHV; the deparser re-serializes selected headers. The PHV—typically a few kilobits of wide containers—is the architectural narrow waist through which all state flows between stages.

### The P4 language

P4 [1] is a domain-specific language for programming protocol-independent packet processors. The original P4-14 was target-coupled; P4-16 restructured the language around an *architecture model* that cleanly separates the portable program (parser, match-action controls, deparser) from target-specific externs (meters, counters, checksum units) [3]. The Portable Switch Architecture (PSA) standardizes this contract, enabling one P4 program to compile to software (BMv2), FPGA, SmartNIC, and PISA ASIC targets. The control plane programs tables at runtime through APIs such as P4Runtime, while the data plane executes the compiled pipeline at line rate.

### Stateful processing and compilation

Two research threads complete the picture. First, compiling P4 to RMT-class hardware is a nontrivial placement problem: tables with dependencies must be assigned to stages, header fields packed into PHV containers, and memory partitioned across stages—formulated and solved in [4] via table-dependency graphs and constraint solving. Second, stateful algorithms (congestion control, load balancing, measurement) demand atomic read-modify-write on per-packet state. The Domino system [5] introduced *packet transactions*—sequential, atomic code blocks—and *atoms*: hardware primitives (e.g., the Pairs atom, ~6000 µm² in 32 nm) guaranteed to execute within one clock cycle, with a compiler mapping transactions to atom pipelines and rejecting programs that cannot run at line rate. The dRMT architecture [6] later disaggregated RMT's stage-local memory into a shared pool behind a crossbar and replaced rigid stages with run-to-completion processors, eliminating the performance cliffs that arise when a program's resource demands misalign with fixed stage boundaries.

##3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds on three complementary tracks.

**Analytic cost modeling.** We treat the match-action pipeline as an abstract machine with explicit resource budgets: stage count *S*, per-stage match width *W* and memory *M*, PHV capacity *P*, and a one-cycle atomicity bound on stateful actions. From these we derive feasibility conditions for programs and bandwidth bounds for applications. This is the method of [2] and [5]: hardware truth first, language design second.

**Compiler-centric reasoning.** Following [4], we view P4 compilation as a *mapping problem*: a program's table-dependency graph must embed into the pipeline's stage graph subject to memory, width, and dependency constraints. Programs that violate the embedding are rejected—not slowed down—which makes the compiler a theorem prover for line-rate feasibility.

**Empirical grounding.** We anchor every claim in measured systems: NetCache's 2+ billion queries per second [8], SwitchML's up to 5.5× training speedups [9], Domino's atom area overheads [5], and dRMT's elimination of RMT performance cliffs [6].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Match-Action Pipeline as an Execution Model

Strip away the networking vocabulary and the RMT/PISA pipeline is a *feed-forward dataflow machine*. A packet enters the programmable parser, which executes a finite-state parse graph and deposits fields into the PHV. Each of the *S* stages then performs: (i) key construction from PHV containers, (ii) a match lookup in TCAM/SRAM, and (iii) an action executed by a very-long-instruction-word (VLIW) ALU bundle over the PHV. The deparser emits the surviving headers. Every packet traverses every stage in exactly *S* cycles, so throughput is the clock rate and latency is *S* cycles—*deterministically*.

This determinism is the model's defining virtue and its defining constraint. Contrast it with a CPU: the pipeline trades Turing-completeness for *timing closure*. The following table summarizes the resource envelope of the PISA-class machine.

| Resource | Typical bound | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Pipeline stages | 12–32 | Table-dependency depth must fit; deep programs rejected |
| Per-stage SRAM | ~1–2 MB | Total state tens of MB; no large tables |
| TCAM width/depth | narrow, power-hungry | Ternary matches budgeted sparingly |
| PHV width | a few Kb | Header state carried forward is scarce |
| Stateful action | 1 clock cycle | Read-modify-write must be an *atom* [5] |
| Arithmetic | integer ALU only | No floating point, no division [9] |
| Control flow | feed-forward only | No loops; bounded iteration via unrolling |

The parser is itself a programmable state machine, and its TCAM-based parse table is scarce: deep or variable-length headers (tunnels, options) can exhaust the parser before the pipeline is even reached—a failure mode invisible to software-trained programmers.

### 4.2 The P4 Language and Its Compilation

P4-16 programs are structured around the architecture model [3]:

```p4
/* P4-16 sketch: NetCache-style in-network key-value lookup */
struct headers { ethernet_t eth; ipv4_t ip; udp_t udp; ncache_t nc; }

parser IngressParser(packet_in pkt, out headers h) {
    state start { pkt.extract(h.eth); transition select(h.eth.etherType) {
        0x0800: parse_ipv4; default: accept; } }
    /* ... further parse states ... */
}

control Ingress(inout headers h, inout metadata m,
                inout standard_metadata_t sm) {
    register<bit<128>>(65536) value_store;
    action cache_hit(bit<32> idx) {
        bit<128> v; value_store.read(v, idx);
        h.nc.value = v; h.nc.hit = 1;
    }
    action cache_miss() { m.lookup_key = h.nc.key; }
    table kv_lookup {
        key = { h.nc.key : exact; }
        actions = { cache_hit; cache_miss; }
        size = 65536;
    }
    apply { kv_lookup.apply(); }
}
```

Compilation [4] proceeds in three phases. *Frontend* elaboration resolves the architecture model and produces an intermediate representation of the parse graph and control flow. *Table mapping* builds the table-dependency graph (TDG): an edge *A → B* exists if B's match key or action depends on A's action. Tables with no path between them may share a stage (parallelism); dependent tables must occupy successive stages (serialization). *Placement* then solves a constrained assignment: tables to stages, keys to PHV containers, entries to SRAM/TCAM blocks—an integer-programming problem where infeasibility is reported as a compile error rather than a runtime slowdown. This *reject-don't-degrade* discipline is the compiler's great strength: a P4 program that compiles is *guaranteed* line rate, because the hardware has no dynamic scheduling to fall back on.

The discipline also explains dRMT's motivation [6]. In RMT, memory is *stage-local*: SRAM unused by stage 7 cannot be reclaimed by stage 9, and a program needing 13 stages of memory but only 4 of compute still consumes 13 stages of both. dRMT disaggregates memory into a centralized pool accessed via crossbar and replaces lockstep stages with run-to-completion match-action processors, so resource allocation follows the program's actual demands. The cost is a modest area increase (the crossbar); the benefit is the elimination of pathological *performance cliffs* where a near-fitting program suddenly fails to map.

### 4.3 Stateful Processing: Atoms and Packet Transactions

Stateless match-action suffices for forwarding. It does not suffice for the algorithms that make networks *adaptive*: AQM, load balancing, telemetry, and—critically—in-network computing all maintain state across packets. The hazard is the pipeline's throughput contract: at 1 GHz, a new packet may arrive every cycle, so a stateful read-modify-write must complete *atomically within one cycle* or packets observe torn state.

Domino [5] resolves this with a clean abstraction stack. The programmer writes *packet transactions*: sequential, atomic, isolated code blocks resembling ordinary imperative code. The compiler lowers each transaction to a pipeline of *atoms*—hardware primitives such as `ReadAddWrite`, `PredicatedReadAddWrite`, and the most expressive `Pairs` atom—each synthesized to complete in one clock cycle. If a transaction cannot be scheduled onto the available atoms within the cycle bound, the compiler *rejects* it—the stateful analogue of §4.2's discipline.

The area economics are favorable: even the most expressive Pairs atom occupies ~6000 µm² in 32 nm, so a 200 mm² switch chip can host hundreds of atoms at under 2% area overhead [5]. Stateful line-rate programmability is thus a *compiler* feat: finding the largest useful instruction set whose every member provably closes timing.

### 4.4 In-Network Computing: From Forwarding to Computing

With a programmable, stateful, line-rate substrate in hand, the question inverts: rather than asking what the network can forward, ask what the network can *compute*. Sapio et al. [7] argue that in-network computation is a "dumb idea whose time has come": the computations worth offloading are precisely the *dumb* ones—simple, associative aggregations over data already flowing through the switch. Their DAIET prototype for partition-aggregate workloads (e.g., MapReduce shuffles) demonstrated 86.9–89.3% data reduction, attacking the dominant cost of data-center applications: network communication, not CPU.

Two systems define the paradigm's empirical frontier.

**NetCache [8]** places a key-value cache *inside* the switch data plane. Hot-key queries are detected, indexed, cached, and served entirely in the pipeline; the design insight is that the switch's role is *load balancing* rather than hit-ratio maximization, so even modest hit rates (<50%) suffice. A single Tofino-class switch serves 2+ billion queries per second for 64K items (16-byte keys, 128-byte values) while consuming a small fraction of hardware resources, improving throughput 3–10× and halving latency for up to 40% of queries under skewed workloads. Coherence is handled by a lightweight invalidation protocol co-designed with the storage servers.

**SwitchML [9]** attacks distributed ML training, where gradient synchronization dominates iteration time. Workers stream quantized gradient chunks to the switch, which aggregates them in on-chip registers and multicasts the sums back—a collective communication primitive executed *in the data plane*. The co-design challenges are instructive: switch ALUs operate on integers, so gradients are quantized; on-chip memory is small, so updates are chunked and streamed; and aggregation is an all-to-all primitive with no per-packet loss visibility, so a scoreboarding protocol with end-host retransmission provides reliability. Result: up to 5.5× training throughput on real DNN benchmarks over unmodified Ethernet.

The pattern generalizes: given a *partition-aggregate* workload whose aggregation operator is associative and integer-expressible, bound its state to on-chip memory and co-design the end-host protocol around the switch's limits. Telemetry and consensus primitives fit the template; floating-point-heavy workloads do not.

##5 Empirical Results/Proofs

Measured results anchoring this article (as reported in the cited works):

| System | Contribution | Key measured result |
|---|---|---|
| RMT [2] | Programmable match-action hardware | 640 Gb/s at ~1 GHz, ~200 mm²—parity with fixed-function |
| P4 compiler [4] | TDG-based mapping to RMT | Real programs (switch.p4) compile to ≤32 stages |
| Domino [5] | Packet transactions → atoms | 300 Pairs atoms < 2% die area; line-rate stateful code |
| dRMT [6] | Disaggregated memory/compute | Line rate with fewer processors; no performance cliffs |
| DAIET [7] | In-network partition-aggregate | 86.9–89.3% traffic reduction |
| NetCache [8] | In-switch key-value cache | 2+ B queries/s; 3–10× throughput; −50% latency (40% of queries) |
| SwitchML [9] | In-network gradient aggregation | Up to 5.5× training throughput on real DNNs |

We now formalize the paradigm's central quantitative claim.

> **Theorem (In-network aggregation bandwidth).** *Consider n workers, each producing a gradient vector of m elements per training iteration, connected through a single programmable switch with on-chip aggregation registers. In-network aggregation reduces the traffic incident on the aggregation point during the reduction phase from n·m to m elements per iteration—a factor-n reduction—and holds each worker's link traffic at exactly 2m elements per iteration, independent of n.*

> *Proof sketch.* Each worker partitions its gradient vector into chunks that fit the switch's per-packet payload and register budget, quantizing floats to integers [9]. The switch maintains one accumulator register per chunk slot; on receiving worker *i*'s chunk *c*, it executes the single-cycle atom `acc[c] ← acc[c] + chunk_i[c]`. After all *n* workers' chunks for slot *c* arrive (tracked by a scoreboard counter), the switch multicasts `acc[c]` to all workers and resets the accumulator. The aggregation point—the switch pipeline itself—thus receives exactly *m* elements' worth of additions (one chunk stream whose running sum is stored, not forwarded) rather than *n·m* distinct elements as a parameter server would. Each worker sends *m* elements and receives *m* elements regardless of *n*; the factor-*n* incast at a server-attached link vanishes. Reliability follows from the end-host retransmission protocol: lost chunks are detected via scoreboard timeouts and re-sent, preserving the exactly-once addition invariant. ∎

> **Theorem (Stage-budget feasibility).** *A P4 program whose table-dependency graph has longest path length L (in tables) and whose per-stage aggregate match width and memory demands are (W, M) compiles to a PISA pipeline with S stages, per-stage width W_max and memory M_max at line rate iff L ≤ S, W ≤ W_max, and M ≤ M_max (up to the compiler's packing efficiency).*

> *Proof sketch.* Necessity: dependent tables must occupy distinct successive stages (feed-forward pipeline, §4.1), so L > S is unmappable; per-stage resource overuse violates the hardware budget by construction. Sufficiency: given L ≤ S, topologically order the TDG and assign tables greedily to stages respecting dependencies; pack keys into PHV containers and entries into SRAM/TCAM blocks via the placement ILP [4]. Because the pipeline admits no dynamic scheduling, the static assignment *is* the execution schedule, and line rate follows from the one-packet-per-cycle pipeline invariant. The "up to packing efficiency" caveat is where dRMT [6] improves on RMT: disaggregated memory raises achievable packing toward the information-theoretic bound. ∎

##6 Limitations

The paradigm's constraints are not engineering inconveniences; they are *architectural invariants* of the timing-closed pipeline, and they delimit what in-network computing can ever become.

- **No floating point, no division.** Switch ALUs are integer-only [9]. Every application must quantize, scale, or reformulate—a lossy step that ML training tolerates but scientific computing may not.
- **No loops, no backward edges.** P4's feed-forward control flow forbids iteration over packets or state; algorithms must be *unrolled* or recast as per-packet recurrences. This rules out, e.g., iterative graph traversals in a single pass.
- **Stage-local, scarce state.** Tens of megabytes of SRAM shared with forwarding tables; registers cannot be shared across stages [5]. Working sets must be small or streamed.
- **One-cycle atomicity.** Stateful updates must fit an atom's single-cycle bound. Compare-and-swap loops, multi-word transactions, and anything resembling a lock are inexpressible.
- **Parser and PHV scarcity.** Deep headers exhaust parse resources; wide per-packet metadata exhausts PHV containers. The *plumbing* can fail before the *logic* does.
- **Weak consistency and debuggability.** Packets from different pipelines observe register state with no defined ordering; there is no printf, no debugger, and no replay—only counters and sampled digests.
- **Security surface.** A programmable data plane is an *attacker-programmable* data plane: malicious or buggy P4 can exfiltrate, amplify, or corrupt at line rate, and verification of P4 programs remains an open research frontier.

These limits suggest the paradigm's steady state: switches as *accelerators for associative, integer, small-state reductions*—a valuable but bounded role, complementary to rather than competitive with end-host computation.

##7 Conclusion

The arc from RMT [2] to P4 [1][3] to in-network computing [7][8][9] is the arc of an execution model maturing: first the hardware proved programmability need not cost performance; then the language made that programmability portable and the compiler made it *safe by construction*; then systems builders discovered that the same pipeline could compute, not merely forward. Its constraints—feed-forward stages, bounded memory, single-cycle atoms—are precisely what make its guarantees possible: deterministic throughput and latency, and compile-time proof of line rate. In-network computing thrives exactly where those constraints align with the workload: associative aggregation over small integer state, co-designed with end-host protocols that absorb the switch's limitations. The network will not become a general-purpose computer. But for the computations shaped like its pipeline, it is already the fastest computer in the data center.
