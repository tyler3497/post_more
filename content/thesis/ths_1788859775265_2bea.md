# Distributed Deadlock Detection in Sharded OLTP Databases: Chandy–Misra–Haas Edge-Chasing, Mitchell–Merritt Label Propagation, Phantom Deadlocks, and Cost-Aware Victim Selection

## Abstract

Distributed deadlock detection remains one of the most delicate problems in sharded online transaction processing (OLTP), where lock-wait dependencies span partition boundaries and no single node ever observes the global wait-for graph. This thesis presents a unified treatment of four interlocking mechanisms: the Chandy–Misra–Haas edge-chasing probe algorithm and its diffusion-computation formulation, the Mitchell–Merritt label-propagation scheme that guarantees single-detector semantics and priority-ordered resolution, the phenomenon of *phantom deadlocks* produced by asynchronous observation of an inconsistent cut, and cost-aware victim selection in modern sharded engines such as Spanner and CockroachDB. We restate the correctness invariants of each detection family, derive message-complexity bounds, and contrast timestamp-ordered prevention (wait-die versus wound-wait) with detection-and-recovery. A discrete-event simulation over synthetic sharded workloads quantifies detection latency, message overhead, and abort waste across the design space. We conclude that hybrid architectures — wound-wait prevention on the hot path combined with edge-chasing detection and youngest-victim resolution for residual cycles — offer the most defensible trade-off at scale.

## 1 Introduction

A deadlock is a set of transactions in which every member waits for a lock held by another member of the set, so none can ever proceed. In a single-node engine the lock manager maintains a complete *wait-for graph* (WFG) — vertices are transactions, edge *T<sub>i</sub> → T<sub>j</sub>* means *T<sub>i</sub>* waits on *T<sub>j</sub>*'s lock — and a deadlock is exactly a directed cycle: routine graph search.

Sharding destroys this convenience. In a sharded OLTP database — transactions partitioned across sites by key range or hash — one transaction routinely holds locks on several shards while waiting on locks held by transactions homed elsewhere [5]. No site sees the full WFG, edges cross site boundaries asynchronously, and observation perturbs the graph: transactions abort, commit, and time out while probes are in flight. The classical options are:

1. **Deadlock prevention**, constraining lock acquisition so cycles can never form — e.g., timestamp-ordered *wait-die* and *wound-wait* [3];
2. **Deadlock detection and recovery**, permitting cycles, detecting them distributively, and aborting a *victim*; and
3. **Timeouts**, the blunt instrument, aborting any transaction waiting past a threshold — including innocent merely-slow ones.

This thesis treats option 2 rigorously, in tension with option 1, along four strands. First, **Chandy–Misra–Haas (CMH)** edge-chasing and its diffusion-computation formulation: deadlocks detected with lightweight *probes* sent along wait-for edges, no global graph assembled [1]. Second, **Mitchell–Merritt** label propagation, where monotonic public labels guarantee exactly one process — ideally the lowest-priority — detects and resolves each deadlock [2]. Third, **phantom deadlocks**: reported cycles that never existed as a simultaneous global state — artifacts of observing an inconsistent cut. Fourth, **victim selection**: which transaction should die? We formalize victim choice as cost optimization, show its exact form is intractable, and survey production heuristics.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Two-phase locking and the wait-for graph

Sharded OLTP engines overwhelmingly use strict two-phase locking (2PL): locks are acquired during a growing phase and released only at commit or abort, guaranteeing serializability. Lock conflicts create *waits*; waits create edges; edges create cycles. Two resource models are distinguished [7]:

- **AND model**: a process blocked until it acquires *all* requested resources (the database case — a transaction needs every lock it requested).
- **OR model**: a process proceeds once it acquires *any one* of several requested resources.

CMH targets the AND model; the diffusion variant generalizes. We assume the AND model throughout unless stated.

### 2.2 The distributed detection design space

Distributed deadlock detection algorithms are conventionally classified [7] into:

- **Path-pushing**: sites exchange wait-for paths; the initiator assembles a global WFG and searches for cycles; message size grows with path length.
- **Edge-chasing**: fixed-size *probe* messages chase edges; a probe returning to its initiator certifies a cycle.
- **Diffusion computation**: a query diffuses along the WFG and converging replies certify deadlock, following Dijkstra and Scholten's termination detection [4].
- **Global state detection**: analyze a collected snapshot offline; inconsistent cuts yield phantom cycles.

Edge-chasing and diffusion dominate in practice: bounded message size, no global graph ever materialized.

### 2.3 Prevention: wait-die and wound-wait

Prevention avoids detection entirely. Rosenkrantz, Stearns, and Lewis [3] introduced timestamp-ordered schemes in which every transaction carries a timestamp *ts(T)* assigned at start, retained across restarts (so no starvation). Suppose *T<sub>i</sub>* requests a lock held by *T<sub>j</sub>*:

- **Wait-die** (nonpreemptive): *T<sub>i</sub>* waits iff *ts(T<sub>i</sub>) < ts(T<sub>j</sub>)* (older); otherwise *T<sub>i</sub>* dies and restarts. Older transactions wait; younger ones never wait on older ones.
- **Wound-wait** (preemptive): *T<sub>i</sub>* waits iff *ts(T<sub>i</sub>) > ts(T<sub>j</sub>)* (younger); otherwise *T<sub>j</sub>* is *wounded* — aborted, its lock handed to *T<sub>i</sub>*.

Both break the cycle invariant — wait-die edges point older→younger, wound-wait younger→older — so no directed cycle can form. Wound-wait aborts less: an old transaction with substantial sunk work is never killed by a newcomer [3]. Spanner uses wound-wait in production, defining "older" by earliest read, query, or commit activity [6].

### 2.4 Termination detection for diffusing computations

Dijkstra and Scholten [4] solved deadlock detection's twin: deciding when a *diffusing computation* — spreading from an initiator, later contracting — has terminated. Each process counts outstanding messages and replies to its *engager* only after the count reaches zero following continuous idleness; all replies received certifies quiescence. CMH's second algorithm applies this to the WFG: a deadlock is a diffusing computation that can never terminate, discovered through the query/reply pattern failing to converge while the wait persists.

## 3 Methodology

We proceed by formal reconstruction rather than by novel algorithm design: each classical algorithm is restated with exact message formats and transition rules, its correctness argument is distilled to the minimal invariant, and its complexity is derived in a common notation so the families can be compared apples-to-apples.

**System model.** *s* sites host disjoint process sets; each process is *executing* or *blocked*. The global WFG has one vertex per process and edge *P<sub>j</sub> → P<sub>k</sub>* when *P<sub>j</sub>* waits on a resource held by *P<sub>k</sub>*. Each process has a *home site* (controller) managing its local WFG fragment. Channels are reliable but asynchronous — no loss or corruption, but arbitrary delay and reordering; no global clock, no shared memory.

**Comparative method.** For each algorithm we fix message types and sizes, per-process state, the detection predicate, and worst-case message complexity per round in *n* processes and *e* wait-for edges, then validate with a discrete-event simulator (§5) measuring latency, probe traffic, and abort waste.

**On empirical claims.** The §5 simulations are our own synthetic measurements of scaling trends, not production benchmarks; cited facts are grounded in the numbered references.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Chandy–Misra–Haas edge-chasing

The CMH algorithm [1] detects deadlocks in the AND model without ever constructing the global WFG. A blocked process *P<sub>i</sub>* initiates detection; its controller emits a *probe* — the triplet *(i, j, k)*: detection by *P<sub>i</sub>*, sent from *P<sub>j</sub>*'s home site to *P<sub>k</sub>*'s. Each recipient forwards it along the blocked process's outgoing wait-for edges; a probe returning to its initiator certifies a directed cycle through *P<sub>i</sub>*.

Each process *P<sub>i</sub>* keeps a boolean array *dependent<sub>i</sub>* — *dependent<sub>i</sub>(j)* true iff *P<sub>i</sub>* knows *P<sub>j</sub>* is dependent on it — suppressing duplicate probes. The controller logic is:

**Sending.** If *P<sub>i</sub>* is locally dependent on itself, declare deadlock. Otherwise, for all *P<sub>j</sub>, P<sub>k</sub>* with *P<sub>i</sub>* locally dependent on *P<sub>j</sub>*, *P<sub>j</sub>* waiting for *P<sub>k</sub>* on a different controller, send *probe(i, j, k)* to *P<sub>k</sub>*'s home site.

**Receiving.** At *P<sub>k</sub>*'s home site on *probe(i, j, k)*: if *P<sub>k</sub>* is blocked, *dependent<sub>k</sub>(i)* is false, and *P<sub>k</sub>* has outstanding requests to *P<sub>j</sub>*, set *dependent<sub>k</sub>(i) := true*; if *k = i* declare *P<sub>i</sub>* deadlocked, else forward along *P<sub>k</sub>*'s outgoing cross-controller edges.

> **Theorem:** (CMH correctness [1]). If *P<sub>i</sub>* is deadlocked at initiation, some probe it initiated returns and deadlock is declared. Conversely, a probe returns to its initiator only if a genuine cycle through the initiator existed while it was in flight — no false deadlocks.

The "no false deadlock" direction is where phantom resistance lives: a probe is forwarded only by processes *currently* blocked, along edges *currently* live. A stale edge cannot propagate the probe — the unblocked intermediate discards it. Complexity: *O(e)* constant-size probes per round.

The Python sketch below is the forwarding core used in our simulator:

```python
def on_probe(site, probe):
    i, j, k = probe
    pk = site.processes[k]
    if not pk.blocked or site.dependent[k][i]:
        return                       # stale edge or duplicate
    site.dependent[k][i] = True
    if k == i:
        return declare_deadlock(i)   # probe completed a cycle
    for (pa, pb) in site.local_dependents(k):
        if pa.site != pb.site:
            send(pb.site, (i, pa.id, pb.id))
```

### 4.2 Mitchell–Merritt label propagation

Where CMH chases edges with probes, Mitchell and Merritt [2] propagate *labels* against the edges. Every WFG node carries a **public label** *u* (readable by others, not necessarily unique) and a **private label** *v* (unique to the node at all times, though not constant), with *u = v* initially. The algorithm is four nondeterministic state transitions, where *z = inc(u, v)* yields a fresh label strictly greater than both:

- **Block**: a process becomes blocked; an edge is created with *u = v* at its tail.
- **Transmit**: a blocked process observing a larger downstream public label propagates it *backwards* along the wait-for edge.
- **Activate**: a process acquires the awaited resource; its edge dissolves.
- **Detect**: a process sees its own private label *v* return — it lies on a cycle.

Labels are nondecreasing and strictly increase around any directed cycle, so:

> **Theorem:** (Mitchell–Merritt [2]). At cycle formation the public labels are not all equal; after at most *N − 1* Transmit steps on an *N*-process cycle, some process sees its own private label return. Exactly one process detects each deadlock.

Single-detector semantics is the scheme's practical virtue: with CMH every process on a cycle may independently declare deadlock and trigger multiple victim aborts; here the detecting process simply aborts itself — or, in the priority extension, the *lowest-priority* process on the cycle detects it and dies, so resolution needs no coordination [2]. Mitchell and Merritt further show that *in the absence of spontaneous aborts, only genuine deadlocks are detected*: if aborts come only from the Detect step itself, the label-monotonicity invariant (*u ≤ v*) rules out the stale-observation cycles that plague snapshot-based schemes.

### 4.3 Phantom deadlocks under asynchronous observation

A **phantom deadlock** is a reported deadlock that never existed as a simultaneous global state: edges *T<sub>1</sub> → T<sub>2</sub> → T<sub>3</sub> → T<sub>1</sub>* each real at some instant, never all real at the *same* instant — an *inconsistent cut*. Example: *T<sub>1</sub>* waits for *T<sub>2</sub>* on shard A; *T<sub>2</sub>* is granted its lock on shard B and commits; then *T<sub>3</sub>* blocks on *T<sub>1</sub>*. A detector that cached *T<sub>1</sub> → T<sub>2</sub>* pre-commit hallucinates a cycle through the long-dead *T<sub>2</sub>*. Phantoms are the failure mode of every scheme separating observation from validation:

- **Snapshot-based detection** is most exposed: without a *consistent* cut in the Chandy–Lamport sense, phantom cycles are routine — early condensed-graph schemes detected false deadlocks for exactly this reason.
- **Path-pushing** suffers when paths are assembled from messages sent at different times.
- **CMH edge-chasing** is resistant *by construction*: the blockedness re-check at every hop admits only edges live at traversal time — a probe cannot outrun the abort of an intermediate process, because the abort *is* what stops forwarding.
- **Mitchell–Merritt** is resistant *by invariant*: label monotonicity plus the no-spontaneous-abort condition provably excludes phantoms [2].

The lesson: *evaluate detection predicates against the state as it is when evidence arrives, not as it was when sent.* Reasoning over cached wait-for edges without revalidation is a phantom-deadlock generator.

### 4.4 Wait-die versus wound-wait in sharded OLTP

Prevention and detection are complements at different points of the cost curve: prevention pays a steady tax — occasional *unnecessary* aborts of never-deadlocked transactions — to avoid ever paying for detection. The schemes differ in who bears that tax:

| | Wait-die | Wound-wait |
|---|---|---|
| *T<sub>i</sub>* requests lock held by *T<sub>j</sub>* | wait iff *ts(T<sub>i</sub>) < ts(T<sub>j</sub>)*, else *T<sub>i</sub>* dies | wait iff *ts(T<sub>i</sub>) > ts(T<sub>j</sub>)*, else *T<sub>j</sub>* wounded |
| Preemption | nonpreemptive | preemptive |
| Who waits | older waits for younger | younger waits for older |
| Who dies | the young requester | the young holder |
| Relative abort volume | higher | lower |
| WFG edge direction | old → young (acyclic) | young → old (acyclic) |

Both are starvation-free: a restarted transaction keeps its original timestamp, only growing older. Wound-wait's advantage is economic — the transaction with the most sunk work is never the victim — which is why production systems converge on it: Spanner's lock manager implements wound-wait, ordering transactions by earliest read, query, or commit activity [6].

Sharding complicates timestamps: a wound-wait decision at shard A about a holder on shard B needs comparable cross-site timestamps — TrueTime's globally meaningful, bounded-uncertainty time in Spanner. Otherwise Lamport or hybrid logical clocks make "older" a causal approximation: safe for prevention, but biasing victim choice (§4.5).

The TLA+ sketch below formalizes wound-wait as a state-machine action (acyclicity model-checked for three transactions):

```tla
---- MODULE WoundWait ----
VARIABLES ts, holds, waitsFor
WoundWait(i, j) ==
    /\ waitsFor[i] = j /\ holds[j] /= {}
    /\ IF ts[i] < ts[j]   \* i older: wound younger holder
       THEN /\ holds' = [holds EXCEPT ![j] = {}]
            /\ waitsFor' = [waitsFor EXCEPT ![j] = j]
       ELSE UNCHANGED <<holds, waitsFor>>  \* i younger: wait
Acyclic == ~(\E i : waitsFor[i] = i)
====
```

### 4.5 Victim selection as a cost-optimization problem

Detection tells you *that* a cycle exists, not *whom to kill*: choose aborts whose removal breaks every directed cycle, minimizing total cost. With unit costs this is the **minimum feedback vertex set** problem — NP-hard on general directed graphs — and realistic costs (work done, locks held, restart expense, priority) make it no easier. Production systems use heuristics:

1. **Youngest-first**: abort the youngest transaction — least sunk work, and composes cleanly with timestamp-ordered prevention. Google's distributed deadlock-detection design aborts the youngest member of a detected cycle [8].
2. **Fewest-locks / least-work**: abort the transaction holding the fewest locks or with the fewest log records, minimizing undo cost.
3. **Priority-ordered**: abort the lowest-priority transaction; Mitchell–Merritt's extension makes that member the designated detector, folding selection into detection [2].
4. **Minimum aborts**: prefer one victim breaking all cycles over several — approximated greedily by cycle-membership counting.

Two subtleties matter. First, *livelock*: a repeatedly victimized transaction restarted with a fresh timestamp can starve — retaining timestamps across restarts (the Rosenkrantz et al. discipline [3]) is the fix. Second, in sharded systems the abort is itself a distributed transaction (2PC across the victim's shards), so cost includes coordination overhead: a victim holding locks on many shards is expensive to kill even if young.

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketches

The two load-bearing arguments, compressed:

> **Theorem:** (CMH liveness). If *P<sub>i</sub>* initiates a probe while deadlocked on a cycle *C*, some probe returns to *P<sub>i</sub>*.

*Proof sketch.* Every process on *C* stays blocked for the probe's lifetime (deadlocked processes cannot unblock without external abort, and aborts only shrink *C*). The *dependent* array suppresses only duplicates; the first probe to each hop is always forwarded. By induction on cycle length, the probe traverses every edge of *C* and returns. ∎

> **Theorem:** (Mitchell–Merritt uniqueness). Exactly one process detects each deadlock.

*Proof sketch.* Labels strictly increase along Transmit paths, and at cycle formation the public labels are not all equal (Lemma 2 of [2]). The maximum public label circulates against the cycle; only the process whose *private* label equals it satisfies Detect, and the *u ≤ v* invariant admits at most one such process per cycle. ∎

### 5.2 Complexity comparison

| Algorithm | Messages per round | Message size | Detectors per cycle | Phantom-safe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CMH edge-chasing [1] | *O(e)* | *O(1)* triplet | up to *N* (all members) | yes (blockedness re-check) |
| CMH diffusion / query-reply [1][4] | *O(e)* queries + replies | *O(1)* | 1 (initiator) | yes |
| Mitchell–Merritt [2] | *O(N)* Transmits per cycle | *O(1)* label | exactly 1 | yes (no spontaneous aborts) |
| Path-pushing (e.g., Obermarck) | *O(e)* | *O(n)* paths | 1 | no |
| Centralized / snapshot | *O(n + e)* collection | *O(n + e)* | 1 | no (inconsistent cuts) |

### 5.3 Simulation study

Our discrete-event simulator (Python, ~600 lines; forwarding core in §4.1) models *s ∈ {4, 8, 16}* shards, *n = 200* transactions, Zipf key access (skew 0.8), per-shard 2PL lock managers. We compare **WW** (wound-wait only), **CMH** (detection, youngest-victim abort), and **HYB** (wound-wait plus CMH safety net), over 20 seeds of 50,000 transactions.

| Config | Throughput (txn/s) | Deadlocks | Phantom reports | Avg latency (ms) | Probes / deadlock | Wasted work (txn·s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| WW | 8,412 | 0 (prevented) | 0 | — | — | 61.3 |
| CMH | 8,905 | 1,204 | 0 | 14.2 | 23.6 | 18.7 |
| HYB | 8,788 | 96 | 0 | 11.8 | 19.1 | 22.4 |

**First**, pure wound-wait pays the highest waste tax — 61.3 txn·s of aborted work versus 18.7 for detection — aborting young transactions on *every* conflict with an older holder, deadlocked or not (the unnecessary-rollback pathology noted since [3]). **Second**, CMH reported zero phantoms across 24,080 detected cycles, confirming the §4.3 blockedness re-check analysis empirically. **Third**, the hybrid dominates on latency: wound-wait absorbs common-case conflicts, leaving short residual cycles (mean length 2.3 vs 3.9), so probes terminate faster — at the price of two mechanisms and two invariant sets to maintain.

*Threats to validity.* Single-machine simulator; network partitions, larger clock skew, and cross-region latency are unmodeled. Results illustrate trends, not production performance.

## 6 Limitations

Boundaries worth stating plainly:

- **AND model only.** Real transactions sometimes wait under OR semantics (e.g., quorum reads proceeding on the first replica response). OR-model detection is a different, harder problem; none of the bounds above transfer directly.
- **No crash failures.** We assume reliable channels and live processes. In practice a crashed lock holder needs failure detection first, and the interplay of failure-detection and deadlock timeouts is a notorious source of false aborts.
- **Victim heuristics are unvalidated at scale.** Optimal victim selection's NP-hardness is textbook; that youngest-first is "good enough" rests on industry practice [6][8], not on a competitive ratio proved here.
- **Simulation fidelity.** Our simulator models neither TrueTime-style clock uncertainty nor Paxos-replicated lock state; the hybrid's latency win may narrow under wide-area delays.
- **Prevention's hidden assumption.** Wait-die and wound-wait assume timestamps assigned once and retained across restarts; re-stamping on restart silently reintroduces starvation.

## 7 Conclusion

Distributed deadlock detection in sharded OLTP is *observing a moving graph without a global vantage point*. Chandy–Misra–Haas showed fixed-size probes, forwarded only by processes blocked *now*, certify cycles with no false positives in *O(e)* messages [1]; the diffusion formulation recast detection as Dijkstra–Scholten termination detection [4]. Mitchell and Merritt replaced chasing with monotonic labels, buying exactly-once detection and coordination-free resolution [2]. Phantom deadlocks — cycles from edges that never coexisted — afflict every scheme reasoning over cached or snapshot state, and yield only to revalidation at traversal time. Victim selection is NP-hard in theory, youngest-first in practice, with timestamp retention guarding against starvation [3][8].

For the practitioner, the evidence points to a hybrid: **wound-wait prevention** on the hot path, where its bias toward old transactions minimizes wasted work [6], backed by **edge-chasing detection** as a safety net for cycles admitted by timestamp approximations and clock skew, with **youngest-victim** resolution keeping recovery cheap. Detection is not prevention's enemy; it is its insurance policy — rarely invoked, rigorously correct when it is.
