# Private Information Retrieval: Information-Theoretic and Computational Constructions

## Abstract

Private information retrieval (PIR) allows a client to fetch the *i*-th record of a public database without revealing *i* to the server that holds it. Introduced by Chor, Goldreich, Kushilevitz, and Sudan [1], the problem splits into two classical regimes: information-theoretic PIR, secure against computationally unbounded adversaries but requiring replicated, non-colluding servers, and computational PIR, in which a single server suffices under cryptographic hardness assumptions. This article surveys both regimes and the modern systems frontier. We reconstruct the Chor et al. impossibility result and the multi-server protocols that circumvent it, the Kushilevitz–Ostrovsky quadratic-residuosity construction that broke the single-server barrier [2], and the lattice-based lineage — from XPIR through SealPIR's compressed queries [4] to OnionPIR's response-efficient design [5] — that made single-server PIR concretely practical. We then examine preprocessing PIR, where client-side hints yield sublinear amortized server time [6], and the communication–computation trade-off landscape mapped by recent systems work [7]. Throughout we track the asymptotic and concrete costs that determine deployability, and we close with the open problems that remain.

## 1 Introduction

Consider a client who wishes to read a single record from a large public database — a blocklist entry, a certificate-transparency log record, a row of a contact-discovery directory — without disclosing *which* record she reads. The database operator is assumed *honest but curious*: it answers correctly but records everything it sees. Downloading the entire database trivially hides the query, but at a communication cost linear in the database size *n*, which is prohibitive for databases of billions of records. *Private information retrieval* (PIR) is the study of protocols that do strictly better.

Formally, the server holds a database DB in {0,1}^n (or, equivalently, *N* records of *l* bits each), the client holds an index *i* in {1, …, n}, and after the interaction the client learns DB[i] while the server learns nothing about *i*. The qualifier "nothing" admits two readings, and the entire field organizes around them:

- **Information-theoretic PIR (IT-PIR).** Privacy holds even against an adversary with unbounded computational power. Chor et al. proved that with a single server this forces the client to download all *n* bits [1] — so IT-PIR replicates the database across *k ≥ 2* servers assumed not to collude, and asks how little they must communicate.
- **Computational PIR (CPIR).** Privacy holds only against polynomial-time adversaries. Here a single server suffices: the query is protected by encryption, and the server computes over ciphertexts homomorphically. The price is computational — every query touches a large fraction of the database — and the central question is how small the communication can be.

> **Theorem (Chor et al. [1]):** Any single-server PIR protocol with information-theoretic privacy has communication complexity at least *n* bits. In particular, downloading the whole database is optimal.

The last decade has made PIR deployable: lattice-based constructions (SealPIR [4], OnionPIR [5]) brought queries to tens of kilobytes and responses within small constant factors of baseline, while *preprocessing* PIR sidesteps the linear server-time bound in an amortized sense via client-side hints [6]. This article reconstructs how these threads fit together — assumptions, costs, and open problems.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The information-theoretic setting

In the *k*-server model, each of *k* servers holds an identical copy of DB. The client sends a query *q_j* to server *j*, receives an answer *a_j*, and reconstructs DB[i]. Privacy requires that for each server *j*, the distribution of *q_j* be independent of *i*. Correctness requires reconstruction to succeed with overwhelming probability. The *communication complexity* is the total number of bits exchanged; the *server computation* is the work each server performs per query.

The founding positive result is the 2-server scheme of Chor et al. [1]. The client picks a uniformly random vector *r* in {0,1}^n, sends *r* to server 1 and *r ⊕ e_i* to server 2, where *e_i* is the *i*-th unit vector. Each server replies with the inner product of its query with DB (over GF(2)); XOR-ing the two answers yields DB[i], while each server's view is uniform and independent of *i*. The cost is *O(n)* bits — no better than the trivial protocol — but arranging DB as a *d*-dimensional cube and applying the idea recursively along each axis yields *O(n^{1/d})* communication with 2^d servers, and in particular a 2-server scheme with *O(n^{1/3})* communication [1].

Later work drove multi-server communication to *subpolynomial* levels via *locally decodable codes*: Katz and Trevisan showed any smooth LDC yields a PIR protocol matching the code's locality [3]; Yekhanin's matching-vector codes gave 3-server *n^{o(1)}* communication, Efremenko removed the underlying conjecture, and Dvir and Gopi reached the 2-server setting [8].

### 2.2 The computational setting

Chor and Gilboa first relaxed perfect privacy to computational privacy, and Kushilevitz and Ostrovsky gave the first single-server scheme with sublinear communication, *O(n^ε)* for any constant *ε > 0*, under the quadratic-residuosity assumption [2]. What followed: Cachin–Micali–Stadler reached *polylogarithmic* communication under *φ*-hiding; Lipmaa reached *O(l log n + k log² n)* from additive homomorphism; Gentry–Ramzan reached log-squared communication; Kiayias et al. brought the *rate* to 1 in 2015 [2]. All required linear server public-key work per query — the cost profile behind the lattice and preprocessing revolutions below.

### 2.3 Lattice-based homomorphic encryption in one paragraph

Modern practical CPIR is built on BFV, whose security rests on Ring-LWE. BFV packs integer vectors into *slots* (SIMD batching) and supports addition plus limited-depth multiplication, with *noise* growing per operation. Ciphertexts dwarf plaintexts by thousands-fold — the *expansion factor* — so the art of SealPIR, OnionPIR, and successors is managing expansion: compressing queries, controlling noise, shrinking responses [4], [5], [7].

## 3 Methodology

This survey proceeds by *reconstruction*: for each construction we restate its security target, the measure it optimizes, and the mechanism achieving it. Running metrics:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *n* | database size in bits (or *N* records of *l* bits) |
| *C(n)* | total communication (query + response) in bits |
| *T(n)* | server computation per query |
| *rate* | \|record\| / \|response\|, the communication rate |
| *k* | number of servers (IT-PIR) |

The threat model is the *semi-honest* server. Privacy is indistinguishability of query distributions: for indices *i, i′* and any polynomial-time *A*,

> **Definition (Computational query privacy):** | Pr[A(q(i)) = 1] − Pr[A(q(i′)) = 1] | ≤ negl(λ),

where *q(i)* is the query for index *i* and *λ* the security parameter. Information-theoretic privacy replaces the negligible bound by zero. To make the classical 2-server scheme concrete, the following toy implementation exhibits the exact information-theoretic mechanism — each share individually uniform, the pair jointly revealing:

```python
import secrets

def two_server_query(n: int, i: int):
    """Return (q1, q2): each uniform in {0,1}^n, with q1 ^ q2 == e_i."""
    r = secrets.randbits(n)
    e_i = 1 << (n - 1 - i)
    return r, r ^ e_i          # server 1 sees r, server 2 sees r ^ e_i

def two_server_answer(db_bits: int, q: int, n: int) -> int:
    """Inner product of query with database over GF(2)."""
    return bin(db_bits & q).count("1") % 2

# correctness: a1 ^ a2 == bit i of db, since <r,db> ^ <r^e_i,db> == <e_i,db>
```

Everything below battles two lower bounds: Chor et al.'s *n*-bit single-server IT bound [1], and the Beimel–Ishai–Malkin observation that a server touching only *o(n)* bits leaks the query — server work is linear in *n* unless the client holds preprocessing state [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Information-theoretic multi-server PIR and the LDC connection

The cube recursion shows IT-PIR's central tension: communication falls as server count grows, but each server must not collude. The Katz–Trevisan reduction turns the problem into coding theory — a smooth LDC of locality *r* yields an *r*-server protocol with matching communication [3] — and refinements pushed 2-server communication to *2^{Õ(√log n)}* [8]. *t*-private PIR tolerates collusions of up to *t* servers; Woodruff–Yekhanin gave *O(td)*-server schemes with *O(n^{1/d})* communication [8].

*Practical reading.* Multi-server IT-PIR is the most communication-efficient PIR known asymptotically, but its non-collusion assumption is fragile in practice: two cloud providers that merge, or two virtual machines on one hypervisor, silently collapse the security model. This fragility is the economic argument for the single-server computational line.

### 4.2 The Kushilevitz–Ostrovsky single-server construction

The 1997 breakthrough of Kushilevitz and Ostrovsky [2] showed that cryptography dissolves the Chor et al. barrier: a *single* server can answer privately with sublinear communication if the query is encrypted under a *linearly homomorphic* scheme whose ciphertexts hide a trapdoor predicate. Their instantiation uses quadratic residuosity modulo a composite *m = p₁p₂* of unknown factorization.

Arrange DB as a *√n × √n* matrix; the client wants bit *x_{i,j}*. She generates *m* and *r_1, …, r_{√n}* in **Z**_m^×, all quadratic residues except *r_i*, a non-residue with Jacobi symbol +1, and sends *m* plus the vector (*O(√n)* elements). The server computes per column *A_c = ∏_t r_t^{x_{t,c}}* and returns the *√n* values. Residues multiply to residues, so *A_c* is a residue iff *x_{i,c} = 0*; the client tests residuosity with *p₁, p₂* and reads the bit.

> **Theorem (Kushilevitz–Ostrovsky [2]):** Under the quadratic-residuosity assumption, there is a single-server PIR protocol with communication *O(n^ε)* for every constant *ε > 0*.

*Proof sketch.* Correctness is the residuosity calculation above. Privacy reduces to QR: each query component has Jacobi symbol +1, and any server distinguishing the query for *i* from that for *i′* distinguishes a residue from a non-residue. Recursion — nesting the matrix construction *d* times — drives communication to *O(n^{1/d})*; choosing *d = 1/ε* gives the claim. ∎

The Haskell sketch below captures the type-level essence — the client's trapdoor is a *capability* the server's view lacks:

```haskell
-- Client holds the factorization (trapdoor); server sees only the modulus.
data Client = Client { p1 :: Integer, p2 :: Integer, modulus :: Integer }
data ServerView = ServerView { modulus :: Integer, queryVec :: [Integer] }

-- Jacobi symbol +1 throughout: server cannot separate residues from non-residues.
isResidue :: Client -> Integer -> Bool
isResidue (Client p1 p2 _) a = legendre a p1 == 1 && legendre a p2 == 1
  where legendre x p = powmod x ((p - 1) `div` 2) p  -- Euler criterion

-- Answer bit for column c: QR  <=>  x_{i,c} == 0, since only r_i is a non-residue.
decodeColumn :: Client -> Integer -> Integer
decodeColumn c a = if isResidue c a then 0 else 1
```

### 4.3 Lattice-based PIR: from XPIR to SealPIR and OnionPIR

The QR-based scheme is asymptotically sublinear but concretely heavy: each query element is a full modular exponentiation. The lattice era began with XPIR (Melchor et al.), which replaced number-theoretic homomorphism with Ring-LWE ciphertexts supporting the core PIR operation — a homomorphic dot product between an encrypted selection vector and the plaintext database. But XPIR's query was enormous: one ciphertext per database row.

**SealPIR** [4] attacked the query side: the client encrypts a short seed; the server *expands* it homomorphically into the full selection vector via BFV automorphisms, then folds a *d*-dimensional hypercube one axis per round. *Query compression* cut upload by orders of magnitude and *amortized processing* batched queries into SIMD slots — roughly 32 KB per query for a million entries [5] — and the design shipped as an open-source library on Microsoft SEAL.

**OnionPIR** [5] attacked the *response* side, where SealPIR still suffered ~100× expansion. Composing two somewhat-homomorphic schemes and controlling noise across recursion layers — the "onion" — collapses the response to 4.2× overhead. A *stateful* variant evaluates copy networks homomorphically, cutting computation 1.8–22× [5]. The Rust sketch below shows the modern lattice-PIR query — parameters, not just mathematics, are now the design surface:

```rust
/// Parameters for a lattice-based (SealPIR-style) PIR query.
pub struct PirQueryParams {
    pub poly_modulus_degree: usize, // e.g. 4096: controls slots & security
    pub coeff_modulus_bits: Vec<u64>,
    pub plain_modulus: u64,          // plaintext space; small => less noise
    pub dimensions: Vec<usize>,     // recursion dims, product == num records
}

pub struct PirQuery {
    pub params: PirQueryParams,
    /// One compressed ciphertext; server expands to the selection vector.
    pub compressed: Vec<u8>,
}
```

**MulPIR** [7] completes the picture: *multiplicative* homomorphism in the recursion trades server computation for communication — 80% less bandwidth than SealPIR at similar computation, up to 35% lower monetary server cost for large entries. The same work cut Gentry–Ramzan's server computation 85% for larger queries [7]. The lesson: no single best PIR exists, only a Pareto frontier over record size, query rate, and who pays for computation.

### 4.4 Preprocessing PIR: sublinear amortized server time

Every scheme so far obeys the Beimel et al. lower bound: the server touches *Ω(n)* bits per query, or privacy collapses [6]. Preprocessing PIR changes the game by changing the model. In an *offline phase*, the client interacts with the server once — at linear cost — and stores a compact *hint* about the database. In the *online phase*, the client combines the hint with a short query the server answers in *sublinear* time, then *refreshes* the hint so the next query stays private.

Corrigan-Gibbs and Kogan introduced the two-server version (EUROCRYPT 2020) with punctured-pseudorandom-set hints. The single-server version [6] is deeper: after fetching a small hint, the client makes a *bounded* number of adaptive queries at sublinear server time each — sublinear *amortized* cost under standard assumptions (DDH, QR, LWE) — and matching lower bounds show the time-vs-storage trade-off is optimal. Follow-ups reached *Õ(√n)* server time with *Õ(1)* amortized bandwidth two-server (Shi et al.), then single-server (Lazzaretti–Papamanthou) — near-optimal in every dimension. The TLA+ sketch below formalizes the shared phase structure:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE PreprocessingPIR ------------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals
VARIABLES db, hint, queryCount, phase  \* phase \in {"offline", "online"}

Init == phase = "offline" /\ queryCount = 0

\* Offline: linear server work, client stores a compact hint.
Offline == phase = "offline"
        /\ hint' \in HintSpace(db)       \* succinct, query-independent
        /\ phase' = "online"
        /\ UNCHANGED <<db, queryCount>>

\* Online: sublinear server work per query; hint is refreshed for privacy.
OnlineQuery(i) == phase = "online"
        /\ hint' \in Refresh(hint, i)    \* re-randomize so queries unlink
        /\ queryCount' = queryCount + 1
        /\ UNCHANGED <<db, phase>>

Next == Offline \/ \E i \in 1..N : OnlineQuery(i)
=============================================================================
```

*Practical reading.* Preprocessing PIR answers the objection "PIR can never be fast because the server must scan everything": the scan is amortized away, at the cost of client storage, a bounded query budget per hint, and a refresh protocol whose privacy must itself be proved.

### 4.5 Rate, communication complexity, and the asymptotic frontier

*Rate* — record size over response size — measures closeness to the insecure baseline. Gentry–Ramzan (2005) reached log-squared communication for consecutive bits; Kiayias et al. pushed the rate to 1 in 2015 [2]. The table below organizes the single-server computational lineage:

| Scheme | Year | Communication | Assumption | Key idea |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kushilevitz–Ostrovsky | 1997 | *O(n^ε)* | QR | residue/non-residue matrix queries |
| Cachin–Micali–Stadler | 1999 | polylog(*n*) | *φ*-hiding | recursive *φ*-hiding encryption |
| Lipmaa | 2004 | *O(l log n + k log² n)* | DCR (Damgård–Jurik) | length-flexible additive homomorphism |
| Gentry–Ramzan | 2005 | *O(log² n)* | *φ*-hiding variant | consecutive-bit retrieval, optimal rate regime |
| Kiayias et al. | 2015 | rate → 1 | DCR-like | constant-rate PIR |
| SealPIR | 2018 | ~64 KB total (1M entries) | RLWE | compressed queries, BFV expansion |
| OnionPIR | 2021 | 4.2× response overhead | RLWE | layered SHE composition, noise control |
| MulPIR | 2021 | −80% vs SealPIR | RLWE | multiplicative-homomorphism recursion |

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

### Proof sketches for the classical core

The privacy proof of Kushilevitz–Ostrovsky is a textbook reduction. Suppose a polynomial-time server distinguishes queries for indices *i* and *i′* with non-negligible advantage. Both queries are vectors of *√n* elements of Jacobi symbol +1, differing only in that position *i* holds a non-residue in the first and position *i′* in the second. A standard hybrid argument isolates a single position where the two distributions differ — exactly the QR-vs-non-residue distinguishing game — contradicting the quadratic-residuosity assumption [2]. Correctness is the residuosity computation of §4.2, which the client evaluates with Euler's criterion using the trapdoor factorization.

For lattice schemes the obligations shift to *noise analysis*: after expansion, *d* recursion layers, and modulus switching, response noise must stay below the decryption threshold — a parameter-selection argument that is why these papers devote pages to concrete parameter sets [4], [5].

### Reported concrete performance

Asymptotics hide the constants that decide deployability. The literature reports the following concrete figures, which we collect here for comparison:

| Scheme / setting | Reported figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| SealPIR, 1M entries | query ≈ 32 KB | [5] |
| OnionPIR response | 4.2× overhead vs insecure baseline (prior art ≈ 100×) | [5] |
| Stateful OnionPIR | 1.8–22× less computation than stateless | [5] |
| MulPIR vs SealPIR | −80% communication, ≈ same computation | [7] |
| MulPIR, large entries | up to −35% total monetary server cost | [7] |
| Optimized Gentry–Ramzan | −85% server computation for larger queries | [7] |
| Preprocessing PIR (single-server) | *Õ(√n)* server time, *Õ(√n)* bandwidth, optimal trade-off | [6] |
| Near-optimal preprocessing PIR | *Õ(√n)* server time, *Õ(1)* amortized bandwidth | Lazzaretti–Papamanthou, via [6]'s model |

Two patterns emerge: *responses* were neglected for twenty years while queries shrank, until OnionPIR attacked them directly [5]; and the frontier is now *economic* — MulPIR is measured in server dollars, not just bits [7].

## 6 Limitations

**The linear-time wall, and what gets around it.** Without client-side state, the server must touch *Ω(n)* bits per query — this is unconditional [6]. Preprocessing PIR evades it only in an amortized sense, and only for a *bounded* number of queries per hint; an adversary that observes hint-refresh traffic across epochs may mount attacks outside the single-epoch security proof. Stateful schemes like stateful OnionPIR additionally assume the client maintains synchronization with the server across queries [5].

**Assumption fragility.** QR and *φ*-hiding fall to Shor's algorithm. Lattice schemes (RLWE) are the plausible post-quantum survivors, but PIR's extreme evaluation depth and aggressive modulus switching push parameters to regimes where each new optimization re-opens the security analysis [4], [7].

**Collusion and malice gaps.** Multi-server IT-PIR assumes non-collusion — fragile when "two servers" means two accounts with one provider. Single-server CPIR assumes semi-honest behavior; *verifiable* PIR against malicious servers remains far more expensive, and keyword PIR adds hashing machinery with its own failure probabilities [7].

**Updates and dynamism.** Nearly all schemes assume a *static* database; re-running linear-time preprocessing after every update destroys amortized gains, and update-friendly preprocessing PIR is nascent. Losing client state likewise forces re-running the expensive offline phase.

**Small records, big ciphertexts.** Lattice ciphertexts span thousands of bytes regardless of payload, so for small records the rate collapses into overhead [5]. Rate-1 schemes [2] fix this asymptotically, but their concrete constants lag the lattice systems' engineering maturity.

## 7 Conclusion

PIR has traveled from impossibility theorem to deployment candidate: Chor et al. showed what information theory forbids [1]; Kushilevitz–Ostrovsky showed what cryptography permits [2]; the lattice lineage [4], [5], [7] showed what engineering affords; preprocessing PIR [6] showed even the linear-time bound is negotiable with client state. Each step traded scarcities: servers for assumptions, communication for computation, online time for hints and storage.

Open problems: can single-server preprocessing PIR reach *Õ(1)* online time and bandwidth with *unbounded* queries per hint? Can rate-1 lattice PIR match OnionPIR's concrete response sizes for small records? Can verifiable PIR close its gap with the semi-honest baseline?
