# Single-Particle Cryo-EM: Bayesian 3D Reconstruction, Manifold Embedding, and the Resolution Revolution

## Abstract

Single-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has, within a single decade, matured from a low-resolution imaging curiosity into a mainstream structural biology technique capable of resolving individual atoms in protein molecules. This thesis develops the mathematical and computational foundations of the modern reconstruction pipeline: the weak-phase forward model and the projection-slice theorem that recast structure determination as an inverse problem in Fourier space; the empirical Bayesian formulation of orientation assignment and density estimation implemented in RELION [1] and cryoSPARC [2]; gold-standard Fourier shell correlation resolution estimation anchored by the 0.143 criterion [3]; and the manifold-embedding framework that recovers continuous conformational spectra from ensembles of noisy snapshots [5]. We trace how direct electron detectors catalysed the resolution revolution [7] recognised by the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry [4], culminating in atomic-resolution reconstructions at 1.22 Å [8][9], and analyse the deep-learning variational methods, notably cryoDRGN [6], that now extend Bayesian reconstruction to fully heterogeneous data.

## 1 Intro

*The three-dimensional structure of a biomolecule is the physical script of its function.* For most of the twentieth century, reading it at atomic resolution meant growing well-ordered crystals or restricting oneself to small proteins amenable to NMR. Single-particle cryo-electron microscopy broke both constraints at once: molecules are imaged directly, flash-frozen in vitreous ice under near-native solution conditions, and a three-dimensional Coulomb potential map is recovered computationally from tens or hundreds of thousands of noisy two-dimensional projections [4].

The obstacles are formidable. To outrun radiation damage, the total dose is limited to a few tens of electrons per square ångström, so each particle image carries a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) often below one tenth — the molecule barely distinguishable from shot noise. Worse, the viewing direction of every particle is unknown *a priori*, turning reconstruction into a joint estimation problem over orientations and density in which neither can be solved without the other. For decades these difficulties confined cryo-EM to nanometer resolution and invited scepticism about whether atomic detail was attainable at all.

Between 2012 and 2014 two developments dissolved the impasse: direct electron detectors with dramatically improved detective quantum efficiency that record dose-fractionated movies, allowing beam-induced motion to be corrected computationally, and a rigorous empirical Bayesian framework that learns its regularisation from the data rather than from user-tuned *ad hoc* filters [1][7]. Resolution leapt to the atomic scale within a few short years — the *resolution revolution* [7] — recognised by the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry [4] and culminating in 1.22 Å reconstructions resolving individual atoms and even hydrogens [8][9].

This thesis develops the mathematical spine of that achievement: the weak-phase forward model and projection-slice theorem, the empirical Bayesian maximum *a posteriori* estimator and its expectation–maximisation solver, gold-standard Fourier shell correlation with the 0.143 criterion, and the manifold-embedding framework for continuous conformational spectra — followed by the empirical record, the remaining limitations, and the variational deep-learning methods extending Bayesian reconstruction to fully heterogeneous data.

## 2 Background

**Three inventions made the field.** In the early 1980s Jacques Dubochet solved the specimen problem: flash-freezing water into vitreous rather than crystalline ice preserves biomolecules' native shape even in the microscope vacuum [4]. Between 1975 and 1986 Joachim Frank solved the computation problem in principle, developing the alignment, classification and 3D reconstruction methods that merge fuzzy two-dimensional images into a sharp 3D structure [4]. And in 1990 Richard Henderson proved the physics problem soluble, imaging bacteriorhodopsin in 3D at atomic resolution and showing electron microscopy could rival X-ray crystallography [4].

**Image formation is linear in the weak-phase limit.** At 300 keV the electron wavelength is roughly two hundredths of an ångström, and biological specimens behave as weak phase objects: the recorded image is, to an excellent approximation, a projection of the specimen's Coulomb potential, modulated by the microscope's contrast transfer function (CTF) and buried in noise. The CTF in Fourier space takes the oscillatory form

CTF(s) = −(√(1 − A²) · sin χ(s) + A · cos χ(s)), χ(s) = πλs²(Δz − λ²s²C_s/2),

with *s* the spatial frequency, Δz the defocus, C_s the spherical aberration coefficient, and *A* the amplitude contrast fraction (typically under one tenth). The CTF crosses zero repeatedly and inverts contrast in alternating bands, so phases must be flipped and amplitudes weighted using per-micrograph defocus estimates.

**The reconstruction problem is an inverse problem with missing geometry.** Each particle image *X_i* is modelled as

X_i = C_i · P(R_i) V + ε_i,

where *V* is the unknown 3D density, *P(R_i)* projects it along the unknown rotation *R_i* in SO(3), *C_i* applies the CTF and translation, and *ε_i* is noise. Neither the orientations nor the volume are known. Classical approaches attacked the geometry first: the common-lines theorem of angular reconstitution exploits the shared one-dimensional line between any two projections in Fourier space, enabling *ab initio* orientation determination. Projection matching refined angles against reprojections of a reference but introduced reference bias, in which noise aligned to a wrong reference reproduces that reference's features. Maximum-likelihood methods replaced hard assignments with probability-weighted integrals, suppressing bias at formidable computational cost — setting the stage for a fully probabilistic treatment.

## 3 Methodology

The modern single-particle pipeline is a chain of statistical estimators, each correcting one corruption of the ideal experiment:

1. **Movie alignment.** The total dose is fractionated into tens of frames; beam-induced motion is estimated and corrected by aligning frames, restoring high-resolution contrast that drift would otherwise destroy.
2. **CTF estimation.** Defocus, astigmatism and higher-order aberrations (beam tilt, trefoil, anisotropic magnification) are fitted per micrograph or per particle [8].
3. **Particle picking.** Tens of thousands to millions of particle windows are extracted, increasingly by learned pickers.
4. **2D classification.** Images are aligned and clustered into class averages, boosting SNR by averaging and discarding contaminants.
5. **3D classification.** Heterogeneous mixtures are separated into discrete compositional or conformational states.
6. **Gold-standard 3D auto-refinement.** The dataset is split into two independent halves refined separately; their Fourier shell correlation yields an unbiased resolution estimate [1][3].
7. **CTF refinement and Bayesian polishing.** Per-particle defocus, aberrations and motion trajectories are re-estimated, squeezing out the final resolution gains.
8. **Post-processing.** Soft masking, B-factor sharpening and local-resolution estimation produce the final interpretable map.

The intellectual core of steps 5–7 is the **empirical Bayesian formulation** [1]. Rather than maximising the likelihood alone, one maximises the posterior

V̂ = argmax_V  Σ_i log Σ_φ P(X_i | φ, V) P(φ) + log P(V),

where *φ* ranges over orientations and translations and the prior *P(V)* regularises the reconstruction. The decisive innovation of RELION was to make the prior *empirical*: the variance of the Gaussian prior on each Fourier shell is estimated from the data during refinement, so the Wiener-like filter that weights each Fourier component is learned, not tuned [1]. Orientation uncertainty is never collapsed to a point estimate; the expectation–maximisation algorithm integrates over the full posterior distribution of alignments in the E-step and reconstructs from probability-weighted images in the M-step. cryoSPARC implements the same regularised likelihood target but replaces deterministic EM with stochastic gradient descent and branch-and-bound search, achieving *ab initio* reconstruction and classification in hours on a single workstation [2].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Forward Model and the Projection-Slice Theorem

All of single-particle reconstruction rests on one geometric identity relating real-space projections to Fourier space.

> Theorem: (Fourier projection-slice.) Let *V* be an integrable density on three-dimensional space and let *P_ωV* denote its line-integral projection along the unit direction *ω*. Then the two-dimensional Fourier transform of *P_ωV* equals the restriction of the three-dimensional Fourier transform of *V* to the plane through the origin perpendicular to *ω*.

> Proof: Write the projection as an integral along *ω* and insert it into the 2D Fourier integral over the perpendicular plane. Fubini's theorem merges the line and plane integrals into a single volume integral whose phase factor depends only on the component of the frequency vector orthogonal to *ω*. The result is precisely the 3D Fourier transform evaluated on that central slice. ∎

The theorem converts the unknown-orientation problem into *slice insertion*: each image determines one central slice of the 3D transform, and the reconstruction is complete once slices cover Fourier space densely. Three consequences follow. First, resolution in any direction is limited by angular sampling — the Crowther criterion — so preferred orientations degrade maps anisotropically. Second, CTF correction becomes per-slice division with Wiener regularisation near the zeros, and a spread of defocus values fills in mutual zero crossings. Third, at atomic resolution the flat-slice approximation breaks down: Ewald-sphere curvature places high-frequency information on a spherical cap, which 1.2 Å reconstructions must correct [8].

### 4.2 Empirical Bayes and Regularised Likelihood Optimisation

The RELION estimator [1] places an independent zero-mean Gaussian prior on every Fourier component, with variance τ²(s) constant within each resolution shell *s* but unknown. The log-posterior becomes

log P(V | {X_i}) = Σ_i log Σ_φ P(X_i | φ, V) P(φ) − Σ_s (1/2τ²(s)) Σ_{j in shell s} |V_j|² + const,

and maximising it by expectation–maximisation yields, in the M-step, a reconstruction in which each Fourier component is a Wiener-filtered average:

V̂_j = Σ_i w_{ij} C_{ij}* X_{ij} / (Σ_i w_{ij} |C_{ij}|² + σ²/τ²(s)).

Here *w_{ij}* is the posterior probability that image *i* arose from orientation *φ* contributing to voxel *j*, and σ²/τ²(s) is the learned regulariser — the noise-to-signal ratio of shell *s*, estimated as τ²(s) = Σ|V_j|² over the shell. Where the data determine a shell well, the filter passes signal; where they do not, the prior suppresses noise. No user-chosen filter curves are needed: the algorithm discovers the spectral SNR itself, which is why the Bayesian formulation proved so robust and drove the field's rapid expansion [1].

A minimal illustration of the M-step filter in Python:

```python
import numpy as np

def wiener_m_step(num, den, tau2, sigma2):
    """One Fourier shell of the RELION M-step.
    num : posterior-weighted sum of CTF-corrected images
    den : posterior-weighted sum of squared CTFs
    tau2: empirical prior variance of this shell (learned)
    sigma2: noise variance
    """
    regulariser = sigma2 / tau2          # learned, not tuned
    volume_shell = num / (den + regulariser)
    # update the empirical prior from the reconstruction itself
    tau2_new = np.mean(np.abs(volume_shell) ** 2)
    return volume_shell, tau2_new

def estimate_tau2_per_shell(volume_ft, shells):
    """Empirical Bayes: the prior variance is the shell power."""
    return [np.mean(np.abs(volume_ft[s]) ** 2) for s in shells]
```

The E-step dominates cost, evaluating every image's likelihood against model projections over a discretised orientation grid — historically hundreds of thousands of CPU hours, now accelerated by GPUs and cryoSPARC's stochastic optimisation [2].

### 4.3 Gold-Standard Fourier Shell Correlation

Resolution in cryo-EM is a statistical claim about the map that must be protected against overfitting. The gold-standard procedure [1] splits the particle set into two independent halves, refines each without information exchange, and correlates the half-maps shell by shell in Fourier space:

FSC(s) = Σ_{j in s} F1_j F2_j* / √(Σ_{j in s} |F1_j|² · Σ_{j in s} |F2_j|²).

Because noise is independent between halves, any correlation must arise from shared signal — provided masks have soft edges and no high-resolution reference features leak between halves, since tight masks inflate the FSC artificially.

> Theorem: (The 0.143 criterion.) Under the noise model of Rosenthal and Henderson, the resolution at which the half-map FSC equals 0.143 is the resolution at which the correlation between the combined full map and the true underlying signal equals one half [3].

> Proof: For two half-maps with independent noise, the correlation of the averaged map with the true signal, C_ref, relates to the half-map FSC by C_ref = √(2·FSC/(1+FSC)). Substituting FSC = 0.143 gives C_ref = √(0.286/1.286) = √(0.25) = 0.5, the conventional crystallographic threshold for interpretable signal. ∎

The criterion is conservative by construction: it measures the *reproducible* signal between two fully independent refinements. A compact Rust implementation of the shell correlation at the heart of every resolution estimate:

```rust
/// Fourier shell correlation for one resolution shell.
fn fsc_shell(f1: &[num::Complex<f64>], f2: &[num::Complex<f64>]) -> f64 {
    let cross: num::Complex<f64> = f1.iter().zip(f2.iter())
        .map(|(a, b)| a * b.conj()).sum();
    let n1: f64 = f1.iter().map(|a| a.norm_sqr()).sum();
    let n2: f64 = f2.iter().map(|b| b.norm_sqr()).sum();
    cross.norm() / (n1 * n2).sqrt()
}

/// Gold-standard resolution: first shell where FSC drops below 0.143.
fn gold_standard_resolution(shells: &[Vec<num::Complex<f64>>],
                            halves: &[Vec<num::Complex<f64>>],
                            apix: f64, box_size: usize) -> f64 {
    for (k, (s1, s2)) in shells.iter().zip(halves.iter()).enumerate() {
        if fsc_shell(s1, s2) < 0.143 {
            let freq = (k as f64 + 1.0) / (apix * box_size as f64);
            return 1.0 / freq; // resolution in angstroms
        }
    }
    f64::INFINITY
}
```

Local resolution extends the idea voxel by voxel (e.g. ResMap), revealing flexible domains at lower resolution within an otherwise atomic map — a bridge to the heterogeneity problem of the next section.

### 4.4 Manifold Embedding of Conformational Continua

Discrete 3D classification assumes the sample occupies a handful of states. Many machines of the cell — the ribosome, the spliceosome — instead traverse *continuous* conformational spectra, and forcing them into discrete classes blurs the very motions of interest. Manifold embedding treats each particle image as a point sampled from a low-dimensional manifold embedded in the high-dimensional pixel space, and recovers that manifold's intrinsic geometry.

Introduced to cryo-EM by Dashti and colleagues [5], the construction proceeds as follows. Within a narrow orientational aperture — so image differences reflect conformation, not viewing angle — pairwise similarities define a Gaussian kernel W_ij = exp(−d²(x_i, x_j)/ε) with a defocus-aware distance. Normalising *W* yields the Markov transition matrix of a random walk on the data graph; its eigenvectors approximate the eigenfunctions ψ_k of the Laplace–Beltrami operator on the conformational manifold, giving coordinates in which Euclidean distance approximates diffusion distance.

Applied to roughly fifteen hundred ribosome snapshots, the embedding revealed a manifold of intrinsic dimensionality five and — via Boltzmann inversion of the occupancy, ΔG = −k_B T ln ρ — produced a free-energy landscape over which the ribosome diffuses as a Brownian nanomachine, with minimum-energy paths tracing functional trajectories [5]. The kernel at the heart of the method, in Haskell:

```haskell
-- Diffusion-map kernel for conformational embedding (Dashti et al.)
gaussianKernel :: Double -> (Image -> Image -> Double) -> [Image] -> [[Double]]
gaussianKernel eps dist imgs =
  [ [ exp (-(dist x y) ^ 2 / eps) | y <- imgs ] | x <- imgs ]

-- Row-normalise to obtain the Markov transition matrix P = D^-1 W
markovMatrix :: [[Double]] -> [[Double]]
markovMatrix w = [ map (/ s) r | r <- w, let s = sum r ]
```

The modern successor of this geometric programme is variational: cryoDRGN [6] replaces the fixed kernel with a deep generative model, encoding each image into a latent variable *z* and decoding *z* into a full 3D density via a neural field, thereby learning a continuous distribution over structures directly from the data. The manifold intuition survives intact — the latent space *is* the learned conformational manifold — while the Bayesian machinery of §4.2 is amortised by neural networks.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The resolution revolution [7] is the empirical vindication of the theory above: direct detectors supplied the signal, the Bayesian pipeline [1][2] the estimator, and map quality compounded super-linearly because better references align particles better, improving references in turn:

| Year | Structure | Resolution | Significance |
|------|-----------|-----------|--------------|
| 2013 | TRPV1 ion channel | 3.4 Å | First de novo cryo-EM structure of a membrane protein without crystals [7] |
| 2014 | Yeast mitochondrial large ribosomal subunit | 3.2 Å | Atomic model from single particles; revolution announced [7] |
| 2015 | Human γ-secretase | 3.4 Å | Drug target; conformational classification of an asymmetric complex |
| 2016 | Glutamate dehydrogenase | 1.8 Å | Then-record resolution; ordered water molecules visible |
| 2020 | β3 GABA_A receptor | 1.7 Å | New electron source, energy filter and camera generation [8] |
| 2020 | Mouse apoferritin | 1.22 Å | Individual atoms; hydrogens in difference maps [8] |
| 2020 | Mouse apoferritin (monochromated) | 1.25 Å | Independent route to atomic resolution via optics [9] |

Two regularities underpin these gains. First, the Rosenthal–Henderson B-factor law: resolution improves with the logarithm of particle number, so each ångström costs an order of magnitude more particles — the 1.34 Å apoferritin map needed roughly nine hundred thousand particles against seventy thousand for its 1.75 Å predecessor. Second, heterogeneity analysis has moved from discrete classes to continua: cryoDRGN uncovered residual heterogeneity in high-resolution ribosome datasets, a new 50S assembly intermediate, and large-scale spliceosome motions, all from the same images behind the consensus maps [6].

The correctness of the gold-standard refinement loop can be stated as a temporal invariant — suitable for machine-checked specification:

```tla+
---- MODULE GoldStandard ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Particles, Shells
VARIABLES halfA, halfB, fsc, converged

Init == halfA \in SUBSET Particles
        /\ halfB = Particles \ halfA
        /\ converged = FALSE

RefineHalf(h) == h' = Refine(h)   \* EM refinement, no cross-talk

Next == \/ /\ ~converged
           /\ halfA' = RefineHalf(halfA)
           /\ halfB' = RefineHalf(halfB)
           /\ fsc' = [s \in Shells |-> FSC(halfA', halfB', s)]
           /\ converged' = ResolutionStable(fsc')
        \/ UNCHANGED <<halfA, halfB, fsc, converged>>

\* Safety: halves never exchange information before FSC evaluation
NoCrossTalk == [] (halfA \cap halfB = {})
====
```

## 6 Limitations

The remaining limitations are physical as much as computational:

- **Preferred orientation and the air–water interface.** Many proteins adsorb to the air–water interface during grid preparation, adopting a narrow range of views; by the projection-slice theorem, missing Fourier slices are unrecoverable computationally — only better biochemistry (tilted collection, novel supports) helps.
- **Radiation damage.** The dose limit is fundamental: every elastic event carrying structural information is accompanied by inelastic events destroying it. Atomic resolution remains restricted to exceptionally stable specimens such as apoferritin [8][9]; flexible complexes stall regardless of particle count.
- **The small-molecule frontier.** SNR scales with molecular mass, and routine sub-3 Å structure determination still demands specimens of order one hundred kilodaltons or more; a 3.7 Å reconstruction of a 40 kDa RNA marks the current edge of feasibility.
- **Higher-order optics.** At 1.2 Å, aberrations once negligible — beam tilt, trefoil, anisotropic magnification, Ewald-sphere curvature — become first-order effects that must be estimated per particle or per micrograph [8].
- **Validation.** FSC guards against overfitting between halves, but model bias, mask-induced correlation inflation, and map sharpening artefacts still require independent validation (half-map model FSC, Q-scores, EMRinger).
- **Compute.** Heterogeneous reconstruction with neural fields [6] trades the CPU clusters of the RELION era for GPU weeks, and dataset sizes now reach tens of terabytes per structure.

## 7 Conclusion

Single-particle cryo-EM's ascent from blurry blobs to atomic coordinates rests on a coherent edifice: a linear forward model governed by the projection-slice theorem, an empirical Bayesian estimator that learns its own regularisation, a gold-standard validation protocol with a principled 0.143 threshold, and a geometric framework unfolding conformational continua from snapshot ensembles. The 2017 Nobel Prize [4] recognised the pioneers, the resolution revolution [7] the convergence of detectors and algorithms, and the 1.22 Å reconstructions [8][9] proved electrons can see atoms. The frontier has moved from *resolution* to *heterogeneity*: variational methods descended from manifold embedding [5][6] aim to recover not one structure but the full Boltzmann ensemble — the movie, not the snapshot, of the molecular machine.
