# Verifiable Delay Functions and Proofs of Sequential Work: A Comparative Study of the Wesolowski and Pietrzak Proof-of-Exponentiation Protocols

## Abstract

A *verifiable delay function* (VDF) forces its evaluator to perform a prescribed number of sequential steps, yet allows anyone to verify the output in time polylogarithmic in the delay. Since their formalization by Boneh, Bonneau, Bünz, and Fisch, two constructions — Pietrzak's recursive halving protocol and Wesolowski's single-element proof of exponentiation — have become the canonical instantiations, both reducing to repeated squaring in a group of unknown order. This study presents a systematic comparison of the two schemes across proof size, verification cost, prover overhead, soundness notions, and hardness assumptions. We show that Wesolowski's construction achieves asymptotically optimal proof size and verification at the cost of inherently sequential proof generation and a computational soundness assumption, while Pietrzak's protocol offers statistical soundness and a parallelizable, dramatically cheaper prover at the cost of logarithmically growing proofs. We survey group instantiations (RSA groups versus class groups of imaginary quadratic fields), reported implementation benchmarks, and deployed applications in randomness beacons and consensus protocols, and we identify the open problems that still separate practical VDFs from their theoretical ideal.

##1 Intro

The problem of *provable elapsed time* arises whenever mutually distrusting parties must agree that a certain amount of computation — and hence wall-clock time — has passed. A lottery operator must not be able to bias the draw by grinding faster than its participants can observe; a blockchain leader-election protocol must not allow a well-resourced miner to simulate the future of the chain; a timestamping service must convince a skeptic that a document could not have been backdated. Rivest, Shamir, and Wagner's *time-lock puzzles* supplied the first rigorous answer: computing `y = x^{2^T} mod N` in a group of unknown order is conjectured to require `T` sequential squarings, immune to any amount of parallelism. Yet the classical time-lock puzzle is not *publicly verifiable*: a solver who claims to have computed the output offers the verifier no shortcut, and the verifier must redo the entire `T` steps to check the claim.

Boneh, Bonneau, Bünz, and Fisch closed this gap in 2018 by formalizing the *verifiable delay function* (VDF): a function whose evaluation demands a specified number of sequential steps, whose output is *unique*, and whose correctness can be checked in time exponentially smaller than the evaluation time [1]. Within days of each other, two remarkably elegant constructions appeared, both building proofs of exponentiation atop the Rivest–Shamir–Wagner puzzle: Krzysztof Pietrzak's statistically sound halving protocol [2] and Benjamin Wesolowski's single-group-element proof [3]. A subsequent survey by Boneh, Bünz, and Fisch compared the two schemes, supplied a new computational security proof for one, and exhibited an attack on an incorrect instantiation of the other [4].

This thesis undertakes a careful comparative study of the two protocols. Our contributions are: **(i)** a unified exposition of both proof-of-exponentiation schemes with their exact verification relations; **(ii)** a head-to-head analysis of proof size, verification cost, prover overhead, soundness, and underlying assumptions; **(iii)** a discussion of the two practical group instantiations — RSA groups and class groups of imaginary quadratic fields — including the trusted-setup question; **(iv)** a survey of reported implementation benchmarks and of deployed or proposed applications, from randomness beacons to blockchain consensus; and **(v)** an account of the known limitations and open problems, including impossibility results for black-box constructions from random oracles.

##2 Background

### 2.1 The VDF abstraction

Following [1], a VDF is a triple of algorithms `(Setup, Eval, Verify)`.

1. **Efficiency.** `Eval` runs in sequential time `T` (up to a small multiplicative factor), while `Verify` runs in time `poly(λ, log T)`.
2. **Uniqueness (soundness).** No probabilistic polynomial-time adversary can produce a proof `π'` that verifies for an input `x` and an output `y'` distinct from the honest output `y`.
3. **`T`-sequentiality.** No adversary with `poly(T, λ)` parallel processors can compute the output in time substantially less than `T`.


### 2.2 Repeated squaring and groups of unknown order

Both constructions instantiate the delay as *repeated squaring* `y = x^{2^T}` in a group `G` whose order is unknown to all parties.

The security of repeated squaring rests on the *sequential squaring assumption*: no algorithm with massive parallelism computes `x^{2^T}` substantially faster than `T` sequential squarings.

### 2.3 Proofs of exponentiation

A *proof of exponentiation* (PoE) is a protocol by which a prover convinces a verifier that a tuple `(x, e, y)` satisfies `y = x^e` in a group of unknown order, where `e` may be astronomically large (e.g., `e = 2^T`).

- **Pietrzak** [2] gives a *statistically sound* public-coin protocol with `O(log T)` rounds and `O(log T)` group elements of communication.
- **Wesolowski** [3] gives a *computationally sound* protocol with a *single* group element as the proof and verification costing essentially two exponentiations, at the price of the *adaptive root assumption*.

> Theorem: (Wesolowski PoE soundness, informal). Let `G` be a group of unknown order in which the adaptive root assumption holds. Then no probabilistic polynomial-time prover can output `(x, y, π)` with `y ≠ x^e` such that the Wesolowski verifier accepts, except with probability negligible in the security parameter `λ`.

The adaptive root assumption states, roughly, that given a random group element `w`, no efficient adversary can find a pair `(u, ℓ)` with `ℓ` prime (in a prescribed range) such that `u^ℓ = w`, unless it already knew such a root.

##3 Methodology

This study is a *comparative protocol analysis* grounded in the primary literature.

1. **Primary-source reconstruction.** We re-derive both verification relations from [2] and [3] (and the journal version of the latter [8]) and check the claimed complexity figures against the authors' own analyses.
2. **Dimensional comparison.** We compare the schemes along six axes: proof size, verification time, prover (proof-generation) time and its parallelizability, soundness notion, hardness assumptions, and group-instantiation requirements.
3. **Empirical survey.** We collect the implementation figures reported in the literature — notably the Tokenomics 2020 implementation study that benchmarks both constructions and proposes a multiexponentiation optimization [5] — rather than running new benchmarks, and we present them as reported (not reproduced) values.
4. **Application mapping.** We trace how each construction's trade-offs determine its suitability for randomness beacons, consensus protocols, and related applications, drawing on the survey literature [4] and on VDF-from-time-lock-puzzle analyses [7].

Our notation: `G` denotes a group of unknown order written multiplicatively; `T` is the delay parameter (a power of two where convenient); `λ` is the security parameter; `H_prime` denotes a hash function whose output is interpreted as a prime.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Wesolowski's proof of exponentiation

Wesolowski's insight is that a single group element suffices to prove exponentiation if the verifier is allowed *one* random prime challenge [3].

1. The prover computes `y = x^{2^T}` by `T` sequential squarings.
2. The verifier (or the Fiat–Shamir hash of the transcript) samples a prime `ℓ = H_prime(x, y)` of bit-length proportional to `λ`.
3. The prover responds with `π = x^{⌊2^T / ℓ⌋}`.
4. The verifier computes `r = 2^T mod ℓ` (a cheap integer operation) and accepts iff `π^ℓ · x^r = y`.

Correctness is immediate from the division identity `2^T = qℓ + r`: raising `π = x^q` to `ℓ` and multiplying by `x^r` yields `x^{qℓ+r} = x^{2^T} = y`.

Wesolowski frames the construction as a *trapdoor VDF*: whoever knows the group order (the trapdoor) can evaluate in `O(log T)` time by reducing the exponent, so the scheme is a VDF precisely when the trapdoor is unknown to everyone — including the party that ran the setup.

The price of this compactness is paid by the prover.

```python
    """Verify a Wesolowski VDF proof. x, y, pi are group elements."""
    ell = hash_to_prime(x, y, bits=lam)   # Fiat-Shamir challenge prime
    r = pow(2, T, ell)                    # r = 2^T mod ell (cheap)
    return pi**ell * x**r == y            # two exponentiations
```

### 4.2 Pietrzak's recursive halving protocol

Pietrzak's protocol proves the same statement `y = x^{2^T}` by *recursive halving* [2].

1. The prover sends `v = x^{2^{T/2}}`, the midpoint of the squaring chain.
2. The verifier samples a random challenge `r`.
3. Both parties recurse on the *compressed* statement `(x^r · v, T/2, v^r · y)`.

The recursion bottoms out at `T = 1`, where the verifier checks a single squaring directly.

For typical parameters (`T ≤ 2^40`, RSA-2048), Pietrzak reports proofs of around **10 KB**, verification costing roughly **three RSA exponentiations**, and — most remarkably — proof generation that is **8000 times faster than solving the puzzle**, even without parallelism [2].

```haskell
-- Pietrzak recursion: prove y == x^(2^T) in a group of unknown order
pietrzak x 1 y = assert (y == x*x)           -- base case: one squaring
pietrzak x t y =
  let v = x^(2^(t `div` 2))                 -- prover's midpoint claim
      r = challenge (x, t, y, v)            -- verifier's random coin
  in pietrzak (x^r * v) (t `div` 2) (v^r * y) -- compressed half-statement
```

### 4.3 Head-to-head comparison

The following table summarizes the comparison along the six methodological axes.

| Dimension | Wesolowski [3] | Pietrzak [2] |
|---|---|---|
| Proof size | 1 group element (~0.25 KB) | `O(log T)` group elements (~10 KB) |
| Verification | ~2 exponentiations | ~3 RSA exponentiations + `O(log T)` mults |
| Prover overhead | ~2T sequential mults (inherently sequential) | T squarings + proof gen 8000× faster than puzzle (parallelizable) |
| Soundness | Computational (adaptive root assumption) | Statistical (public-coin) |
| Assumption | Adaptive root / low-order + sequential squaring | Sequential squaring only |
| Rounds (interactive) | 1 | `O(log T)` |

Three observations emerge.


### 4.4 Group instantiations: RSA groups versus class groups

Both protocols are *group-agnostic*: they require only a group of unknown order with efficient arithmetic.

**RSA groups.** Working in `(Z/NZ)*` with `N = pq` gives fast, well-understood arithmetic, but anyone who knows `p` and `q` can evaluate the VDF in `O(log T)` time by reducing the exponent modulo `φ(N)`. The factorization must therefore be generated by a trusted ceremony and destroyed — a significant deployment burden. The Ethereum Foundation and Protocol Labs jointly ran a widely publicized competition (with substantial prize money) aimed at producing trustworthy RSA moduli and fast RSA-based VDF implementations, precisely because the trusted setup was seen as the main obstacle to deployment.

**Class groups of imaginary quadratic fields.** The class group `Cl(Δ)` of discriminant `Δ` has unknown order *even to the party that chose Δ*, so no trusted setup is needed: anyone can sample a discriminant transparently. The cost is arithmetic that is considerably slower and more complex to implement than RSA modular multiplication. Chia Network — whose consensus design motivated Pietrzak's work [2] — organized a competition with over $100,000 in prizes for fast class-group arithmetic, and Chia's mainnet ultimately deployed Wesolowski's VDF instantiated over class groups, exploiting its tiny proofs for on-chain verification.

The trade-off is therefore crisp: RSA groups buy prover and verifier speed at the price of a trusted ceremony; class groups buy *transparency* at the price of slower evaluation.

### 4.5 Applications: beacons, consensus, and beyond

The canonical application is the *public randomness beacon* envisioned by Rabin: a service that periodically publishes random values no party could have predicted or biased [7].

In *blockchain consensus*, Cohen and Pietrzak combined proofs of sequential work with proofs of space to obtain consensus from proof of resources with far lower energy expenditure than proof of work; Chia's mainnet is the flagship deployment, running a Wesolowski VDF over class groups as its "proof of time" [2].

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### 5.1 Reported implementation figures

Because this study is analytical, we report — rather than reproduce — the benchmark figures published in the literature.

| Metric (RSA-2048, `T ≤ 2^40`) | Wesolowski | Pietrzak |
|---|---|---|
| Proof size (reported) | ~0.25 KB | ~10 KB |
| Verification (reported) | ~2 exponentiations | ~3 RSA exponentiations |
| Proof-generation overhead | ~2× evaluation (sequential) | ~1/8000 × evaluation (parallelizable) |
| Soundness error | negligible under adaptive-root | statistically negligible |

The figures confirm the asymptotic analysis: Wesolowski's proofs are roughly forty times smaller and its verification roughly comparable, while Pietrzak's prover-side advantage is measured in orders of magnitude [2][3][5].

### 5.2 Proof sketches

**Pietrzak soundness.** The halving protocol is a public-coin interactive proof; soundness follows from a standard rewinding argument. If a prover convinces the verifier of a false statement `(x, T, y)`, then with non-negligible probability over the verifier's challenge `r`, the prover must also answer correctly for the compressed half-statement, and iterating the argument down the recursion extracts a false base-case claim — a single incorrect squaring — which is information-theoretically impossible to sustain. Hence the soundness error is negligible in `λ` *without any computational assumption*; only the delay property needs the sequential-squaring conjecture [2].

**Wesolowski soundness.** Suppose an efficient adversary outputs `(x, y, π)` with `y ≠ x^{2^T}` that passes verification `π^ℓ · x^r = y` for the Fiat–Shamir prime `ℓ`. Writing `y' = x^{2^T}` for the true output and `d = y / y' ≠ 1`, the verification equation can be rearranged to exhibit an `ℓ`-th root of a non-trivial group element derived from `d` — contradicting the adaptive root assumption, provided `ℓ` is sampled after `(x, y)` are fixed. This ordering requirement is exactly what the attack in [4] targets: if the challenge prime is derived from `x` alone (or from a malleable transcript), the adversary can choose `y` *after* seeing `ℓ` and the reduction collapses. The fix — hashing the full statement — is now standard practice [4].

**Impossibility boundaries.** Mahmoody, Smith, and Wu showed that VDFs satisfying *perfect* uniqueness cannot be constructed from random oracles in a black-box way, and proved lower bounds on the sequentiality achievable by proofs of sequential work in the random-oracle model [6]. These results delineate what the Fiat–Shamir-based constructions above can and cannot hope to achieve: computational uniqueness in the random-oracle model is the right target, and it is attainable, but perfect uniqueness is not.

##6 Limitations

**The sequentiality assumption is conjectural.** No proof is known that repeated squaring requires `T` sequential steps, even in the generic group model; the assumption rests on decades of failed cryptanalysis rather than a theorem. A breakthrough in parallel exponentiation — or, more dramatically, a quantum computer running Shor's algorithm, which recovers the group order of both RSA groups and class groups — would destroy every construction in this family outright.

**Trusted setup versus performance.** RSA instantiations inherit the ceremony problem: a compromised or retained factorization silently voids the delay guarantee, and the compromise is *undetectable* after the fact. Class groups avoid the ceremony but pay with slower arithmetic and subtler parameter selection (discriminant size, subgroup structure), and their concrete security has received less cryptanalytic attention than RSA's.

**Prover asymmetry and tightness.** Wesolowski's prover performs roughly twice the sequential work of the bare evaluation, so the *effective* delay experienced by a beacon operator exceeds the nominal `T`; Pietrzak's proof size grows logarithmically with `T`, so very large delays inflate on-chain costs. Later work on *tight* VDFs (Döttling, Garg, Malavolta, Vasudevan) and *continuous* VDFs (Ephraim, Freitag, Komargodski, Pass) addresses these gaps but adds complexity [7].

**Fiat–Shamir fragility.** Both non-interactive schemes live in the random-oracle model, and the history of the Wesolowski challenge-binding attack [4] shows that the details of the Fiat–Shamir transform are security-critical, not boilerplate. Standard-model instantiations remain an active research area.

**Verification is not free.** Even polylogarithmic verification involves modular exponentiations with thousand-bit moduli; at blockchain scale, verifying every VDF proof on every node is a real cost that favors Wesolowski's two-exponentiation check — one reason deployments have converged on it despite its prover-side disadvantages.

##7 Conclusion

Pietrzak's and Wesolowski's verifiable delay functions represent two distinct points on the Pareto frontier of proofs of sequential work, and the right choice between them is determined by which side of the protocol bears the scarcer resource.

The deeper lesson of the comparison is architectural: both schemes are *proofs of exponentiation* over the same Rivest–Shamir–Wagner sequential core, differing only in how they compress the transcript of `T` squarings. Future progress — tighter VDFs, transparent group instantiations with RSA-like performance, standard-model Fiat–Shamir replacements, and ultimately a proof (or refutation) of the sequential squaring conjecture — will refine but not erase this trade-off. Until then, the practitioner rule of thumb stands: *Wesolowski for the verifier, Pietrzak for the prover*, with the group instantiation (RSA ceremony versus class-group transparency) chosen according to the deployment's trust model [5][7][8].
