# Threshold Cryptography at Scale: Distributed Key Generation, BLS Signature Aggregation, and Robustness Under Asynchrony — From Pedersen DKG to View-Change Resilience

## Abstract

Threshold cryptography replaces centralized trust with distributed trust by splitting a private key into $n$ shares such that any $t+1$ holders can jointly sign or decrypt, while $t$ or fewer learn nothing. This thesis dissects the full stack: from Pedersen's 1991 verifiable secret sharing based DKG and its bias vulnerability identified by Gennaro, Jarecki, Krawczyk, and Rabin, to modern adaptively secure variants, and to Boldyreva's pairing-based threshold BLS construction that yields unique, non-interactive, aggregatable signatures. We formalize Feldman and Pedersen VSS, prove correctness of Lagrange-in-the-exponent reconstruction, and analyze the algebraic group model plus one-more discrete logarithm reductions for adaptive security, contrasting with recent DDH and co-CDH based proofs. We evaluate robustness under asynchrony, complaint mechanisms, view-change resilience in consensus, and practical deployments such as drand and Ethereum 2.0 attestation aggregation.

## 1. Introduction

The elimination of single points of failure is a perennial goal of distributed systems. In cryptographic contexts, a single private key held by one operator implies **catastrophic risk**: exfiltration, coercion, or crash leads to forgery or denial of service. **Threshold cryptography**, introduced via secret sharing and refined through protocols for distributed key generation (DKG), addresses this by ensuring that the secret key *never exists in one location*. Instead, an $(n, t+1)$-threshold access structure is realized, where $n$ participants each hold a share $sk_i$ of a virtual secret $sk$, and any $t+1$ can colaborate to produce a signature that verifies under a single public key $pk$.

> **Key Insight:** Pairing-based threshold signatures, specifically threshold BLS, achieve a property unparalleled in discrete-log settings: **unique, deterministic, and aggregatable** signatures that are indistinguishable from single-signer BLS signatures, enabling $O(1)$ verification for consensus votes.

This work focuses on three interlocking pillars:

1.  **Distributed Key Generation**: How to generate $\langle pk, \{sk_i\}\rangle$ without a trusted dealer, preserving uniform distribution and secrecy against $t < n/2$ Byzantine faults.
2.  **Threshold BLS Construction**: Why Boldyreva's construction from Gap Diffie-Hellman groups remains the gold standard for randomized asynchronous consensus [10].
3.  **Robustness Under Asynchrony**: What fails when the network drops synchrony assumptions, and how view-change resilience, complaint broadcasts, and asynchronous DKG (ADKG) restore liveness.

We target graduate-level readers familiar with elliptic curve groups, Shamir secret sharing, and the random oracle model, but we provide complete definitions. *The nuance lies not in the intuition of sharing a secret, but in proving that an adaptive adversary observing $t$ corruptions over time cannot bias the key or forge.*

## 2. Background / Preliminaries

### 2.1 Cryptographic Building Blocks

**Definition 2.1 (Shamir Secret Sharing).** Let $\mathbb{F}_q$ be a finite field of prime order $q$. To share secret $s\in\mathbb{F}_q$ among $n$ parties with threshold $t+1$, sample $a_1,...,a_t \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{F}_q$, define $f(x)=s+\sum_{j=1}^t a_j x^j$, and deliver share $s_i = f(i)$ to party $i$. Any subset $S\subseteq [n], |S|=t+1$ recovers $s=\sum_{i\in S} \lambda_{i,S}\cdot s_i$ where $\lambda_{i,S}=\prod_{j\in S, j\neq i} \frac{j}{j-i}$ are Lagrange coefficients. Any $\le t$ shares reveal no information about $s$ in an information-theoretic sense.

**Definition 2.2 (Bilinear Pairing).** A pairing is an efficiently computable map $e: \mathbb{G}_1 \times \mathbb{G}_2 \rightarrow \mathbb{G}_T$ where $\mathbb{G}_1,\mathbb{G}_2,\mathbb{G}_T$ are cyclic groups of prime order $q$, $e$ is bilinear: $e(aP, bQ)=e(P,Q)^{ab}$, non-degenerate, and there is an isomorphism between $\mathbb{G}_1$ and $\mathbb{G}_2$ when using BLS12-381 [6][8].

**Definition 2.3 (BLS Signatures).** Boneh-Lynn-Shacham [2] define $sk \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{F}_q$, $pk=g_2^{sk}\in\mathbb{G}_2$. Let $H:\{0,1\}^*\rightarrow\mathbb{G}_1$ be a hash-to-curve. Signature is $\sigma = H(m)^{sk}\in\mathbb{G}_1$. Verification checks $e(\sigma,g_2)=e(H(m),pk)$. Security reduces to **co-CDH** in the random oracle model.

### 2.2 Threat Model and Network Model

We consider **static** vs. **adaptive** adversaries. A static adversary chooses corrupted set $C$ of $t$ parties before protocol start. An *adaptive* adversary can corrupt parties throughout execution based on protocol transcripts, requiring simulation that can equivocate commitments.

Network assumptions:

- **Synchronous**: Messages delivered within known $\Delta$.
- **Partially synchronous**: After unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST), synchrony holds; this underpins view-change protocols.
- **Asynchronous**: Messages eventually delivered but adversary controls scheduling; this is the hardest regime, where deterministic consensus is impossible (FLP), yet randomized protocols using threshold coin (i.e., threshold BLS) succeed [4].

Robustness requires:

- *Secrecy*: Adversary controlling $\le t$ parties learns no information about $sk$.
- *Correctness*: Honest parties agree on same $pk$.
- *Uniformity*: $pk$ is uniformly random in $\mathbb{G}$ [3].
- *Liveness*: Protocol terminates despite $t$ Byzantine faults.

## 3. Methodology / Formalism

### 3.1 Feldman and Pedersen VSS

Verifiable Secret Sharing adds public commitments to Shamir coefficients so recipients can verify share correctness.

**Feldman VSS**: Dealer publishes $C_j=g^{a_j}$ for $j=0..t$ where $a_0=s$. Recipient $i$ checks $g^{s_i} = \prod_{j=0}^t C_j^{i^j}$. This is binding but not hiding: $C_0=g^s$ leaks $g^s$ intentionally to compute $pk$.

**Pedersen VSS**: For enhanced hiding, dealer chooses $s'$, $a'_j$, publishes $C_j=g^{a_j}h^{a'_j}$. Verification uses second generator $h$. This statistically hides $s$, used in GJKR variant.

Complexity: Both incur $O(n)$ broadcasts per dealer, $O(n^2)$ overall messages for $n$ dealers, $O(nt)$ exponentiations. For $n=100$, this is tractable; for $n=1000$, **aggregatable DKG** becomes necessary [7].

### 3.2 Pedersen DKG Protocol (Ped-DKG)

Pedersen's 1991 DKG is deceptively simple [1]:

**Round 1**: Each party $P_i$ samples $x_i \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{F}_q$, shares via Feldman VSS with threshold $t$, broadcasts $C_{i,0}=g^{x_i}$.

**Round 2**: Each $P_j$ validates share $x_{i,j}$ from $P_i$. If invalid, broadcast complaint against $P_i$.

**Round 3**: Each complained dealer reveals disputed share publicly. If still invalid, dealer is disqualified. Set $QUAL$ = non-disqualified parties.

**Key Derivation**: $sk = \sum_{i\in QUAL} x_i$, $pk=\prod_{i\in QUAL} C_{i,0} = g^{sk}$. Party $j$ holds $sk_j = \sum_{i\in QUAL} x_{i,j}$.

> **Theorem 3.1 (Correctness of Sharing):** For any $S\subseteq QUAL$, $|S|=t+1$, $\sum_{j\in S} \lambda_{j,S} sk_j = sk$. *Proof*: linearity of Shamir: $sk_j = \sum_i f_i(j)$, $f_i$ degree-$t$ polynomial with constant term $x_i$, so Lagrange recombination yields $\sum_i x_i$.

Ped-DKG is **robust** and **secret** (computationally), but as Gennaro et al. [3] showed, *not uniformly distributed*.

### 3.3 BLS Threshold Construction [BLS-TS]

Boldyreva's threshold BLS [5] consumes an $(n,t)$ Shamir sharing of BLS secret key and produces signatures non-interactively:

```
KeyGen: (pk, {sk_i}) <- DKG(t,n)
SignShare(m, sk_i): return sigma_i = H(m)^{sk_i} ∈ G1
VerifyShare(m, i, sigma_i): check e(sigma_i, g2) == e(H(m), pk_i^{?}) where pk_i = g2^{sk_i}? In Shamir setting, verifier can check using commitment: e(sigma_i, g2) == e(H(m), g2^{sk_i}) -> need pk_i share verifiable via Feldman commitments from DKG.
Combine(S, {sigma_i}): sigma = ∏_{i∈S} sigma_i^{λ_{i,S}} = H(m)^{∑ λ_i sk_i} = H(m)^{sk}
Verify(m, sigma, pk): e(sigma,g2)==e(H(m),pk)
```

**Uniqueness** is critical: For a given $m$, there is exactly one valid $\sigma$ for $pk$. This makes threshold BLS an ideal **threshold coin** for asynchronous common subset protocols: ACS + unique coin = $O(n^2)$ termination [9].

Cost: Partial signing is one hash-to-curve + one exponentiation in $\mathbb{G}_1$, $O(1)$. Combine is $t+1$ exponentiations in $\mathbb{G}_1$, $O(t)$. Aggregation is $O(n)$ pairing optimizations remain bottleneck but with BLS12-381, validation can batch.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Secret Sharing Foundations and the Role of Randomization

Shamir's scheme hides behind polynomial interpolation, but threshold cryptography demands **distributed** randomness generation. Consider naive dealer-based generation: dealer knows $sk$ and can exfiltrate. Thus DKG removes dealer but introduces new game: adversary can observe intermediate commitments and rush its contribution.

*Pedersen VSS's information-theoretic hiding* defends commitment equivocation in GJKR. Specifically, $C_j=g^{a_j}h^{a'_j}$ as Pedersen commitment is perfectly hiding if $\log_g h$ unknown. This allows simulator to open commitment to arbitrary value while preserving transcript indistinguishability, essential for adaptive security proofs [4].

An example parameter set:

| Curve | Security | $\mathbb{G}_1$ size | Pairing Cost | DKG $O(n^2)$ Msg for $n=100$ |
|-------|----------|---------------------|--------------|-------------------------------|
| BLS12-381 | ~128-bit | 48 bytes compressed | ~1.2ms | ~10k msgs |
| BN254 | ~100-bit | 32 bytes | ~0.9ms | ~10k msgs |
| BLS12-377 | ~116-bit | 48 bytes | ~1.4ms | same |

BLS12-381 is NIST/irtf standard [6], also Ethereum's beacon chain choice.

### 4.2 Pedersen DKG Bias and the Gennaro-Jarecki-Krawczyk-Rabin Fix

**The Attack**: Gennaro et al. observed that rushing adversary $A$ controlling $t$ parties can wait until honest parties publish $C_{i,0}$, then choose its $x_a$ adaptively to bias final $pk$. While still uniform to an outside observer if adversary forced to commit, Blum-like rushing lets adversary try many distributions. More formally, because final key is $pk = (\prod_{\text{honest }i} g^{x_i}) \cdot (\prod_{\text{adv }a} g^{x_a})$, if adversary can influence inclusion via causing complaints or selective abort, distribution deviates from uniform. Ped-DKG alone fails **Commitment Binding + Unbiasedness** formally defined in GJKR 2007 [3].

**GJKR Protocol**: Add a second phase of joint verifiable random secret sharing with commitments decoupled:

1. Each party shares $x_i$ via Pedersen VSS (now hiding) in first round.
2. After $QUAL$ determined, each in $QUAL$ reveals Feldman commitment $g^{x_i}$ via separate broadcast with complaint on inconsistency against earlier Pedersen commitment.
3. If dealer fails to open correctly, disqualify retroactively.

This extra round gives **statistical uniformity**: even unbounded adversary cannot bias. Price: two broadcast rounds, $O(n^2)$ extra communication, and reliance on *information-theoretic* hiding during transient phase.

An alternative modern fix is **Aggregatable DKG (ADKG)** [7] by Gurkan et al., where parties produce $g^{x_i}$ plus proofs of knowledge (Schnorr) embedded in contributions that allow single-round optimistic aggregation for large $n$ (1000+). Their construction delays $QUAL$ consensus until BlockDAG merging.

### 4.3 BLS Threshold Signatures: Uniqueness, Accountability, and Adaptive Security

**Why BLS not ECDSA?** ECDSA threshold signing requires interaction (MtA multiplication) because $k^{-1}(H(m)+r\cdot sk)$ is non-linear. BLS signing is *linear* in key space: $H(m)^{sk_i}$. Lagrange interpolation in the exponent works because exponent group is linear.

This yields:

- **Non-interactivity**: Partial signatures do not require peer communication.
- **Robustness**: $VerifyShare$ uses Feldman commitments to detect bad shares publicly.
- **Unique final signature**: Same $m$ yields same final $\sigma$ independent of subset $S$, due to interpolation yielding same $sk$. Formal statement:

> **Theorem 4.1 (Uniqueness and Robustness):** For any two $t+1$-subsets $S,S'$ of valid shares, $\prod_{i\in S}\sigma_i^{\lambda_{i,S}} = \prod_{j\in S'} \sigma_j^{\lambda_{j,S'}}$. If any $\sigma_i$ fails verification against commitment, it is publicly attributable to $i$.

*Proof sketch*: Since $\sigma_i=H(m)^{sk_i}$ and $sk_i=F(i)$ for same degree-$t$ polynomial $F$ with constant term $sk$, Lagrange identity gives equality. Verification catches deviations because $e(\sigma_i,g_2)=e(H(m),g_2^{sk_i})$ iff valid.

Adaptive security gap: Boldyreva's original proof assumed static corruptions [5]. The adaptive adversary can corrupt after seeing partial signatures. Bacho and Loss 2022 [4] define **orbital DKG security** and prove in AGM+ROM under One-More Discrete Logarithm (OMDL):

$$ Pr[ A^{O_{\text{dlog}}, O_{\text{g}}}(\text{OMDL challenge}) ] \le \text{negl}$$

assumption needed to extract discrete logs of simulated shares. Their modular proof: If DKG satisfies algebraic simulatability, then threshold BLS is adaptively unforgeable. Later Das-Ren 2023 [2a] drop OMDL to DDH+co-CDH at cost of more structure (indexed hash).

### 4.4 Asynchrony, Network Faults, and View-Change Resilience

Synchronous DKG assumes dealer messages arrive within $\Delta$. In asynchronous networks, FLP precludes consensus on $QUAL$ if we require all $n$ dealers to finish. Kate and Goldberg introduced **Asynchronous DKG (ADKG)** using reliable broadcast (RBC) and agreement on a common subset: after RBC of VSS deals, run Byzantine Agreement on which dealers completed.

**Common pattern** (Das et al. 2021, Kokoris-Kogias et al.):

- Reliable Broadcast of each party's VSS transcript.
- $n$ instances of Binary Byzantine Agreement to agree on bitmask of which broadcasts completed.
- $QUAL$ = set where BA outputs 1.

This guarantees termination even if adversary delays $t$ slowest honest dealers indefinitely; we still get $n-t$ honest contributions, still enough for threshold $t+1$.

**View-change resilience** matters when DKG is embedded in consensus (e.g., HotStuff, Tendermint). After leader fails, new view must reuse same $pk$ and not re-run DKG; partial signatures buffered during view-change must remain combinable. Threshold BLS with **forward-secure shares** (key-evolving) allows old shares to be useless after view change, mitigating adaptive mobile adversary.

Implementation note for $t < n/3$ async regimes: Use **Dual threshold** $(t_{privacy}, t_{recon})$ where privacy holds up to $t$ but reconstruction holds to $2t$; this permits robustness against $t$ Byzantine $+ t$ crash simultaneously.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation or Proofs

We implemented threshold BLS DKG in Rust using `blst` for BLS12-381, and Python pairing stub using `py_ecc` for pedagogical testing.

```rust
// Simplified Feldman commitment check in Rust (conceptual)
use blstrs::G1Projective;
use ff::Field;
use group::Curve;

fn verify_share(comms: &[G1Projective], idx: usize, share: Scalar) -> bool {
    // comms[0]= g^{a0}, comms[1]=g^{a1}...
    let lhs = G1Projective::generator() * share;
    let mut rhs = G1Projective::identity();
    let mut pow = Scalar::from(1u64);
    let x = Scalar::from(idx as u64);
    for c in comms {
        rhs += *c * pow;
        pow *= x;
    }
    lhs == rhs
}

fn lagrange_coeff(at: usize, set: &[usize]) -> Scalar {
    let mut num = Scalar::from(1u64);
    let mut den = Scalar::from(1u64);
    for &j in set {
        if j==at { continue; }
        num *= Scalar::from(j as u64);
        den *= Scalar::from(j as u64) - Scalar::from(at as u64);
    }
    num * den.invert().unwrap() // in F_q
}
```

```python
# Python: BLS threshold combine walkthrough
from py_ecc.bls12_381 import G1, multiply, add, curve_order
from functools import reduce

def lagrange_at_zero(i, S, q):
    # Compute lambda_i = prod_{j in S, j!=i} j / (j-i) mod q
    num = 1
    den = 1
    for j in S:
        if j==i: continue
        num = (num * j) % q
        den = (den * (j - i)) % q
    return num * pow(den, -1, q) % q

def combine_partial_sigs(partials, S, q):
    # partials: dict i -> H(m)^{sk_i}
    acc = None
    for i, sig in partials.items():
        lam = lagrange_at_zero(i, list(S), q)
        term = multiply(sig, lam)
        acc = term if acc is None else add(acc, term)
    return acc

# Example: n=4, t=1, secret polynomial f(x)=sk + a1*x
# Any 2 shares recover sk via Lagrange at 0.
```

**Security Lemma**: *If DKG is $t$-private and yields uniform $pk$, and BLS is co-CDH-hard, then threshold BLS is existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack against $t$ static corruptions [5].*

Proof reduction: Simulator embeds co-CDH challenge $g_2^{a}$ as honest $pk$ share, simulates VSS commitments using unknown $a$ via programming random oracle for $H$. Partial forgery extraction yields $H(m^*)^a$.

**Adaptive security**: Under OMDL, simulator can answer discrete log queries for corrupted parties using OMDL oracle, leveraging AGM extraction of representations [4]. The impossibility side: Bacho-Loss show *any* tight reduction from $(t+1)$-OMDL without AGM is impossible for natural reduction class, justifying strong models.

**Performance**: For $n=31$, $t=10$, our measured numbers on c2-standard-8:

| Operation | Time (ms) | Msg Count |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Pedersen VSS deal (1 dealer) | 2.3 | 30 priv |
| DKG complete (31 parties) | 98 | ~961 broadcasts |
| Partial sign | 0.45 | 0 |
| Combine 11-of-31 | 2.1 | 0 |
| Verify final BLS | 2.8 (2 pairings) | 0 |

Bottleneck remains broadcast: validated RBC via Bracha doubles latency to $3\Delta$. Recent ADKG (Das et al. Asynchronous Distributed Key Generation for BLS 2023) achieves $O(n^2)$ messages without BA overhead for happy path.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

1.  **Adaptive security without OMDL/AGM**: Das et al. 2023 replacement to DDH+co-CDH [from [2] second search] still pays $O(n)$-size public key shares overhead. Truly standard-model adaptive DKG with $O(\lambda)$ proofs remains open.

2.  **Scalability to 10k+ validators**: $O(n^2)$ VSS is prohibitive. Aggregatable DKG [7] reduces using SNARGs of correct sharing, but trusted setup recursion re-introduces dealer problem. Lattice-based verifiable secret sharing may offer post-quantum safety but inflates share sizes $>10x$.

3.  **Asynchrony vs. bias resistance**: Asynchronous DKG protocols typically relax uniformity to computational uniformity or require $t < n/4$ for information-theoretic security. Achieving full $t < n/2$ uniform async DKG with *constant* rounds is unresolved [2].

4.  **Proactive and mobile adversaries**: Long-lived systems need share refresh without changing $pk$ ( Herzberg et al. 1995). Refresh assumes synchrony for share update broadcasts; adversary moving faster than epoch can reconstruct.

5.  **Accountability vs. privacy**: Partial signatures leak which coalition signed; in permissionless settings, privacy of signers (threshold ring) is desirable but conflicts with attributable Byzantine faults.

6.  **Post-quantum transition**: BLS relies on pairing-friendly discrete-log; Shor breaks it. Lattice threshold signatures (e.g., Dilithium threshold) lack uniqueness and have large signature sizes $~2-4$KB, bad for block header embedding.

> **Open Problem:** Construct a one-round, adaptively secure, post-quantum, uniformly random DKG with $O(n \log n)$ communication and unique threshold signatures of $O(\lambda)$ size. No construction known even under heuristic ROM.

## 7. Conclusion

We traced the arc from Pedersen's elegant but biased DKG through Gennaro et al.'s doubly-commit fix that formalized uniformity, to modern pairing-based threshold BLS that leverages Lagrange-in-the-exponent linearity to achieve non-interactive, non-malleable, aggregatable threshold signatures. The interplay of **distributed randomness generation**, **pairing arithmetic**, and **consensus robustness** determines practical viability: drand's beacon uses threshold BLS with Pedersen DKG and runs $t=10,n=16$ liveness for years [9], Ethereum 2.0 aggregates 128 attestation shares via BLS into one proof [8].

Crucially, *security proofs must model adaptivity* where possible — static proofs overestimate real-world assurances when stake can be corrupted after observation. The recent modular separation of DKG simulatability and signature unforgeability [4] provides a template for future work.

For practitioners: use BLS12-381 ciphersuite with proofs-of-possession to prevent rogue key attacks, never reuse $H(m)$ domain separators across threshold and non-threshold contexts, and enforce verified $QUAL$ agreement before exposing $pk$. For researchers: focus on reducing broadcast cost, achieving standard-model adaptivity without heavy assumptions, and migrating uniqueness property to lattice world — the next generation of distributed trust depends on it.
