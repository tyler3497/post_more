# Zero-Knowledge Machine Learning: Verifiable Neural Network Inference via zk-SNARK Circuits, Quantized Model Arithmetization, Lookup Arguments for Nonlinear Activations, and Proof Aggregation for LLM-Scale Models

## Abstract

Zero-knowledge machine learning (zkML) asks whether a neural network can prove that it computed its output correctly — from a committed, proprietary set of weights — without revealing the weights themselves. This thesis presents a complete technical account of verifiable neural network inference: the arithmetization of linear and convolutional layers into polynomial constraint systems, the use of lookup arguments such as plookup and Halo2-style lookups to tame non-arithmetic activations like ReLU and softmax, quantization schemes that map floating-point models into finite-field arithmetic with provable fidelity, and recursive proof aggregation techniques that push verification toward LLM scale. We compare R1CS-based and Plonkish arithmetizations, derive constraint-complexity bounds for common operators, analyze the soundness of lookup-based activation gadgets, and survey empirical results from systems including EZKL, zkLLM, and Halo2-based ImageNet-scale provers. We conclude that while proving overhead remains four to six orders of magnitude above native inference, the combination of lookup arguments, aggressive quantization, and GPU-parallel proving has moved zkML from a theoretical curiosity to deployable auditing infrastructure for AI governance.

## 1. Introduction

Machine learning models are among the most valuable and least inspectable artifacts in modern computing. Frontier neural networks represent billions of dollars in training cost, and their weights are treated as closely guarded intellectual property. Simultaneously, these same models are deployed in contexts — hiring, lending, medical triage, content moderation — where regulators, auditors, and affected parties demand assurance about *which* model produced a decision and *how* it behaved on audited inputs. These two requirements are in direct tension: the owner cannot reveal the weights, yet the auditor cannot verify the computation without them.

**Zero-knowledge machine learning (zkML)** resolves this tension cryptographically. The central object is a *verifiable inference relation*:

> *Definition 1 (Verifiable inference relation).* Let $f_w$ be a neural network parameterized by weights $w$, $x$ a public or private input, and $y$ the claimed output. The relation
>
> $$R = \lbrace (x, y; w) \mid y = f_w(x) \rbrace$$
>
> is proven by a zero-knowledge succinct argument of knowledge (zk-SNARK): a prover $P$, who knows the witness $w$, convinces a verifier $V$ that $R$ holds while revealing nothing about $w$ beyond the truth of the statement.

The "nothing beyond" is the zero-knowledge property; the "convinces cheaply" is succinctness — verification time and proof size are typically logarithmic or constant in the circuit size. This is the crucial asymmetry that makes zkML worthwhile: inference of a 13-billion-parameter model may cost minutes of GPU time to *prove*, but the resulting proof (under 200 kB in the zkLLM system [1]) verifies in milliseconds and binds the output to committed weights.

This thesis covers four pillars of the discipline: **circuit design** for ML operators, **lookup arguments** for nonlinear activations, **quantized arithmetization** into finite fields, and **proof aggregation** for LLM scale.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 zk-SNARKs in brief

A zk-SNARK is a non-interactive zero-knowledge argument with *succinct* verification: for a circuit of size $n$, the proof size and verifier time are $O(\mathrm{polylog}(n))$ or $O(1)$. The modern workflow is:

1. **Arithmetization**: the computation is expressed as polynomial constraints over a prime field $\mathbb{F}_p$ (e.g., the BN254 scalar field or Pasta curves).
2. **Commitment**: the prover commits to witness polynomials using a polynomial commitment scheme — KZG (pairing-based, trusted setup) or an inner-product argument / FRI (transparent).
3. **Argument**: an interactive oracle proof (IOP) such as PlonK, Marlin, or a sumcheck-based protocol is made non-interactive via the Fiat–Shamir heuristic.

The proving cost is dominated by *multi-scalar multiplications* (MSMs) and *number-theoretic transforms* (NTTs) proportional to the circuit size — the root of zkML's overhead, since a network that is cheap to evaluate natively still forces the prover to commit to and transform its entire execution trace.

### 2.2 Arithmetizations: R1CS vs. Plonkish

- **R1CS** (rank-1 constraint systems, used by Groth16): each constraint is a product of two linear forms, $a \cdot b = c$. R1CS is rigid — non-multiplicative structure (e.g., bit decompositions, range checks) must be hand-encoded — but Groth16 proofs are tiny (128 bytes) and verifiers are cheap, making it attractive for on-chain verification.
- **Plonkish** (Halo2-style): rows of a circuit matrix are constrained by *custom gates* (arbitrary low-degree polynomial relations per row) and *lookup arguments* (column values must appear in a fixed table). This flexibility is why Plonkish backends — Halo2 with KZG, used by EZKL — are the de facto choice for zkML: nonlinear activations become lookups rather than expensive bit-level circuits [2, 3].

### 2.3 Lookup arguments

The breakthrough primitive is the **lookup argument**: a protocol by which the prover convinces the verifier that every value in a witness column $f$ belongs to a public table $t$, i.e., $\lbrace f_i \rbrace \subseteq \lbrace t_j \rbrace$. The original **plookup** (Gabizon–Williamson, 2020) [5] sorts the concatenation of witness and table and proves a grand-product identity; **PlonKup** (2022) integrates plookup into PlonK natively [4]. For zkML this is decisive: a ReLU over 8-bit quantized inputs is simply a lookup into a $256$-row table $(x, \max(0,x))$, replacing thousands of R1CS constraints per activation with a handful.

### 2.4 Quantization

Neural networks natively compute in floating point; SNARK circuits compute over a finite field. **Quantization** maps weights and activations to integers, typically via affine quantization $r \approx S(q - Z)$ with scale $S$ and zero-point $Z$. The zkML compiler (ZEN [6], EZKL [2], the Modulus Halo2 pipeline [3]) must additionally guarantee that the quantized circuit *faithfully* realizes the intended arithmetic: rescaling, accumulation bit-widths, and rounding must be constrained inside the proof, or the prover could substitute a cheaper, wrong computation.

### 2.5 Related work

Early verifiable-ML systems include SafetyNets, vCNN and zkCNN (sumcheck-based proofs for convolutions, the latter on a 15M-parameter network) [7], ZEN (a PyTorch-to-R1CS compiler with SIMD-style field packing) [6], and Mystique. Verifiable model *evaluation* — packaging inference proofs into attestations of benchmark accuracy or fairness — was demonstrated end-to-end in [2]. zkLLM [1] pushed the frontier to 13B-parameter LLMs with the parallelized **tlookup** argument and a specialized **zkAttn** attention gadget.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis follows the methodology of a zkML systems study: we construct a reference pipeline, derive its complexity bounds, prove the soundness of its gadgets, and evaluate reported empirical data from the literature. Our reference pipeline mirrors production systems (EZKL, the Modulus Halo2 compiler, zkLLM):

1. **Model ingestion.** A trained network (PyTorch/TensorFlow) is exported to ONNX, fixing the operator graph.
2. **Calibration and quantization.** A representative dataset calibrates per-tensor scales and zero-points; the model is quantized to 8-bit integers. Fidelity is measured by accuracy delta on a validation set.
3. **Arithmetization.** Linear operators become custom gates or dot-product gadgets; nonlinearities become lookup tables over the quantized domain; rescaling and clipping become range-checked arithmetic.
4. **Circuit compilation.** The graph is laid out into a Plonkish matrix (Halo2), with columns for advice (witness), fixed (tables, weights), and instance (public inputs/outputs).
5. **Proving and aggregation.** The circuit is proven (Halo2-KZG); for large models the graph is sharded into sub-circuits whose proofs are recursively aggregated.
6. **Verification.** The verifier checks a single succinct proof against the verification key, committed weights, and public I/O.

We evaluate soundness formally (gadget-level theorems), complexity analytically (constraint counts per operator), and performance empirically using published benchmarks.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Circuit design: arithmetizing linear and convolutional layers

Linear layers are SNARK-friendly in principle: a dense layer $y = Wx + b$ is a collection of dot products — degree-2 arithmetic that fits any arithmetization. The challenge is *scale* and *layout*.

In Plonkish form, a dot product of length $N$ is accumulated with a running-sum column $m$:

$$x_i \cdot y_i + m_i = m_{i+1}, \quad m_0 = 0,$$

enforced per row by a single custom gate with selectors, so an $N$-dot-product costs $N$ rows rather than the $O(N)$ R1CS constraints with far worse constants. EZKL's circuit decomposes every ONNX operator into four primitive arguments — cumulative dot products, cumulative sum/products, elementwise arithmetic, and lookups — so the entire network reduces to combinations of these [2].

Convolutions are 2-D dot products with weight reuse; sumcheck-based systems (zkCNN [7]) exploit FFT structure for sublinear prover overhead on the linear part, while Halo2-style compilers unroll them into tiled dot-product gadgets. Either way, linear algebra dominates constraint *count* but not difficulty — the real cost driver is the nonlinearities, addressed next.

*Constraint-cost sketch for common operators (Plonkish, 8-bit quantized):*

| Operator | Arithmetization | Rows (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| Dense layer, $m \times n$ | dot-product custom gate | $m \cdot n$ |
| Conv2d, $C_{out} \times C_{in} \times k^2$ per output | tiled dot products | outputs $\times$ fan-in |
| ReLU (per activation) | lookup into 256-row table | $O(1)$ rows + amortized table |
| Softmax (per row, $d$ classes) | exp lookups + division gadget | $O(d)$ lookups |
| LayerNorm | variance via dot products + inverse lookup | $O(n)$ + table |

### 4.2 Lookup arguments for nonlinear activations

The plookup identity [5] tabulates quantized activations: the fixed column $t$ holds all pairs $(x, \sigma(x))$ over the $b$-bit domain, and the prover shows each witness pair appears in $t$:

> *Theorem (plookup soundness, informal).* Let $f$ be the committed witness polynomial and $t$ the table polynomial over a multiplicative subgroup $H$. If the prover produces a sorted polynomial $s$ of $(f, t)$ and the grand-product identity
>
> $$(1+\beta)^m \prod_j (\gamma + f_j) \prod_i (\gamma(1+\beta) + t_i + \beta t_{i+1}) = \prod_k (\gamma(1+\beta) + s_k + \beta s_{k+1})$$
>
> holds at random challenges $\beta, \gamma$, then $\lbrace f_j \rbrace \subseteq \lbrace t_i \rbrace$ except with probability negligible in $|\mathbb{F}|$.

In words: *every activation evaluation is a table membership proof*, and one argument covers the whole column at once. This yields:

- **ReLU**: table $(x, \max(0, x))$ over the quantized range. Trivially tabulated; the dominant activation in vision CNNs costs essentially nothing beyond the lookup argument itself [3].
- **Sigmoid / tanh / GELU**: tabulated directly over $b$-bit inputs; increasing $b$ grows the table exponentially, so practical systems cap $b \le 12$–$16$ and clip out-of-range inputs with range checks.
- **Softmax**: $\mathrm{softmax}(z)_i = e^{z_i} / \sum_j e^{z_j}$. The exponential is tabulated; the division is handled by constraining $y \cdot \sum_j e^{z_j} = e^{z_i}$ plus a range check that the denominator is nonzero — or by a dedicated inverse lookup. zkLLM's **zkAttn** gadget goes further, fusing the attention pattern $ \mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top / \sqrt{d})V$ into a specialized lookup-based argument that avoids materializing the full $n \times n$ attention matrix in the circuit [1].
- **tlookup** (zkLLM): a *parallelized* lookup argument for non-arithmetic tensor operations with no asymptotic overhead, exploiting GPU-friendly data parallelism so lookup proving scales with CUDA cores rather than serial grand products [1].

A Rust-flavored sketch of a Halo2 ReLU lookup gadget illustrates the pattern:

```rust
// Advice columns: input x (quantized, offset to be non-negative), output y.
// Fixed table column: all pairs (t, relu(t)) for t in 0..2^B.
fn relu_gate<F: Field>(
    meta: &mut ConstraintSystem<F>,
    x: Column<Advice>, y: Column<Advice>,
    table: TableColumn,
) {
    meta.lookup("relu", |meta| {
        let x = meta.query_advice(x, Rotation::cur());
        let y = meta.query_advice(y, Rotation::cur());
        // (x, y) must appear as a row of the fixed table (t, relu(t)).
        vec![(x, table[0]), (y, table[1])]
    });
}
```

The table is committed once during setup; every ReLU in the network is one lookup row. The prover's extra work is sorting witness values into the table order — $O(n \log n)$ — which is why tlookup-style parallelization matters at LLM scale.

### 4.3 Quantization and finite-field encoding

Quantization is where machine learning numerics meet finite-field arithmetic, and it is the most common source of soundness bugs in naive zkML implementations. The standard affine scheme maps a real tensor $r$ to integers:

```python
# Calibration: choose scale S and zero-point Z per tensor.
S = (r_max - r_min) / (2**b - 1)
Z = round(-r_min / S)
q = clip(round(r / S) + Z, 0, 2**b - 1)      # quantized witness values
r_hat = S * (q - Z)                          # dequantized approximation

# Inside the circuit, a dense layer computes in integer arithmetic:
#   acc = sum((q_x - Zx) * (q_w - Zw))            # up to 32-bit accumulator
#   y   = clip(round(acc * (Sx*Sw/Sy)) + Zy)      # requantize to 8-bit
```

Every step — the widened accumulation (to avoid overflow modulo $p$), the fixed-point rescaling by the rational $S_x S_w / S_y$, the rounding, and the final clipping — must itself be constrained. ZEN's key contribution [6] was an R1CS-friendly quantization that packs multiple 8-bit integers into one large field element (SIMD-style), amortizing constraint cost; the Modulus pipeline [3] instead uses 8-bit quantized networks natively in Halo2 with custom gates for the linear layers and lookups for the rest, reaching ImageNet-scale DNNs.

> *Theorem (quantization fidelity bound, informal).* Let $f$ be an $L$-layer network with $1$-Lipschitz activations and per-layer quantization error bounded by $\epsilon$ in $\ell_\infty$. Then the quantized network $\hat{f}$ satisfies $\lVert f(x) - \hat{f}(x) \rVert_\infty \le L \cdot \epsilon \cdot \prod_\ell \lVert W_\ell \rVert_\infty$, i.e., error compounds at most linearly in depth when weight norms are controlled.

In practice, 8-bit post-training quantization of vision models loses under 1% top-1 accuracy — the circuit then proves *the quantized model*, and the attestation in [2] binds benchmark metrics to exactly that quantized artifact. A subtle point: systems commit to the quantized weights directly and treat quantization as part of the trusted model-release process.

Negative numbers are handled by offsetting into the field's canonical representatives (values are small, $< 2^{32}$, so no wraparound modulo $p \approx 2^{254}$ occurs), and every intermediate is range-checked to its declared bit-width — otherwise a malicious prover could exploit modular wraparound to forge activations.

### 4.4 Proof aggregation and recursion for LLM scale

A 13B-parameter transformer's inference trace is far beyond a single circuit's capacity (Halo2 circuits are bounded by $2^k$ rows for the SRS size $k$). The solution is *decomposition plus aggregation*:

1. **Sharding.** The model graph is partitioned — per layer, or per attention block — into sub-circuits, each proven independently. This is embarrassingly parallel and matches GPU cluster topology.
2. **Recursive aggregation.** Sub-proofs are verified *inside* another SNARK (recursion over curve cycles such as Pasta, or via accumulation schemes), producing one aggregate proof whose verification implies all shards were correct [1].
3. **Specialized gadgets.** Generic circuits waste constraints on LLM-specific structure. zkAttn proves the attention mechanism with lookup-based handling of the $QK^\top$ scores and softmax, balancing prover time, memory, and numerical accuracy rather than arithmetizing attention naively [1].

The headline result: zkLLM proves full inference of a 13B-parameter LLM in under 15 minutes with a fully parallelized CUDA implementation, yielding proofs under 200 kB that hide all model parameters [1]. Compare with the Modulus Halo2 system proving ImageNet-scale CNNs [3] and EZKL proving nanoGPT end-to-end from ONNX [8]: the trajectory is clear — each generation moves an order of magnitude up in parameter count, driven less by new cryptography than by *lookup arguments, quantization, and parallelism*.

> *Definition 2 (Recursive verifiable inference).* Let $\pi_i$ prove shard $i$ of the computation $y = f_w(x)$, and let $\Pi = \mathrm{Agg}(\pi_1, \dots, \pi_m)$ be a proof that each $\pi_i$ verifies under the shard verification keys. Then $V(\Pi) = 1$ implies $y = f_w(x)$ with soundness error at most $m \cdot \varepsilon + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{agg}}$, where $\varepsilon$ is the per-shard soundness error.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation / Proofs

We summarize published benchmarks (figures from the cited works; hardware varies, so cross-row comparisons are indicative):

| System | Model scale | Backend | Proof time | Proof size | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| zkLLM [1] | 13B-param LLM | custom CUDA + tlookup/zkAttn | < 15 min | < 200 kB | full inference, hidden weights |
| Modulus Halo2 [3] | ImageNet CNN (MobileNet-class) | Halo2, custom gates + lookups | minutes | ~ tens of kB | 8-bit quantized, avg. 469×387 px inputs |
| EZKL / nanoGPT [8] | GPT-2 scale (124M) | Halo2-KZG via ONNX | minutes–hours | ~ 10s of kB | EVM-verifiable; audited by Trail of Bits |
| zkCNN [7] | 15M-param CNN | sumcheck/GKR + FFT | ~ 10s of min | larger (non-succinct-ish) | sublinear prover for convolutions |
| ZEN [6] | small CNNs (LeNet-scale) | Groth16 / R1CS | seconds–minutes | 128 B | SIMD field packing |

Three patterns emerge:

1. **Lookups dominate the efficiency story.** Tabulating nonlinearities (Halo2/EZKL/zkLLM) scales 10–100× better per parameter than pure-R1CS encodings, because activations — not matrix multiplies — were the constraint bottleneck.
2. **Prover parallelism is the current frontier.** zkLLM's CUDA implementation shows proving is now limited by engineering (memory bandwidth, MSM parallelization) rather than asymptotics [1].
3. **Verification is already cheap.** In every system above, verification is milliseconds to seconds — the asymmetry zkML needs for auditing, on-chain inference markets, and benchmark attestations [2]. The cost is entirely on the prover: roughly $10^4$–$10^6\times$ native inference.

A complexity model consistent with these data: for a network with $P$ parameters and $A$ nonlinear activations over $b$-bit quantization, Plonkish proving time scales as $T \approx c_1 P + c_2 A \log(A + 2^b)$, with $c_1$ set by MSM/NTT throughput and $c_2$ by the lookup argument — which is why tlookup's parallelization and zkAttn's attention fusion move the needle more than any change to the commitment scheme.

## 6. Limitations

1. **Prover overhead remains enormous.** Four to six orders of magnitude over native inference: proving one LLM query costs minutes of GPU time — viable for audits and high-value attestations, not per-query consumer inference.
2. **Trusted setup and curve constraints.** KZG-based systems (EZKL, most Halo2 deployments) inherit a universal-but-trusted SRS; transparent alternatives pay larger proofs, and recursion-friendly curve cycles add complexity.
3. **Quantization fidelity and soundness interplay.** The proof attests to the *quantized* model, not the float model the lab trained; accuracy or fairness claims must be re-baselined on the quantized artifact [2]. Unsound quantization gadgets (missing range checks, wraparound exploits) are a real attack surface.
4. **Softmax and division precision.** Lookup tables grow exponentially in input bit-width, forcing low-precision exponentials and approximate normalization — zkAttn manages this trade-off explicitly [1], but it remains a trade-off.
5. **Privacy is narrower than it looks.** Zero-knowledge hides weights from the verifier, but model-extraction via adaptive queries remains possible outside the proof system; zkML provides *integrity*, and only limited *confidentiality*.
6. **Training is out of scope.** All practical systems prove *inference*; proving LLM-scale training is considered infeasible — gradient computation's dynamic control flow resists efficient arithmetization.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Zero-knowledge machine learning has converged on a coherent stack: **quantize** the model into finite-field-friendly integers, **arithmetize** linear operators with Plonkish custom gates, **tabulate** every nonlinearity with lookup arguments, and **aggregate** shard proofs recursively to reach LLM scale. Each layer exists because the naive alternative fails — R1CS bit-decompositions drown in activations, float arithmetic has no field encoding, and monolithic circuits cannot fit billion-parameter traces. The empirical record, from EZKL's ONNX-to-Halo2 pipeline through ImageNet-scale Halo2 provers to zkLLM's 13B-parameter proofs in under 15 minutes, shows a field doubling in practical scale roughly yearly, driven by proof engineering rather than new hardness assumptions. The remaining gaps — prover overhead, quantization soundness, softmax precision, and the infeasibility of proving training — define the research frontier. The core capability, however, is already real: a model owner can produce a kilobyte-sized, millisecond-verifiable certificate that *this* committed model produced *that* output on *those* inputs, revealing nothing else. That is the foundation on which verifiable AI auditing will be built.
