# Learned Index Structures: Recursive Model Indexes, the PGM-Index, and ALEX — From Error-Bounded CDF Approximation to Dynamic Adaptation with Worst-Case Bounds and Concurrency Control

## Abstract

The learned index replaces the classical branch-and-descend mechanics of the B-tree with a learned model of the data distribution: because the rank of a key in a sorted array equals the scaled cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the keys, any model that approximates the CDF predicts record positions directly, correcting its bounded prediction error with a local "last-mile" search. This thesis develops a unified technical account of the three canonical architectures that defined the field between 2018 and 2020. We formalize the Recursive Model Index (RMI) of Kraska et al. [1] as a hierarchical, layer-greedy CDF approximator and characterize the tuning brittleness documented in the critical re-analysis of Stoian et al. [5]. We reconstruct the PGM-index of Ferragina and Vinciguerra [2] as an optimal piecewise-linear ε-approximation with a recursive segment-endpoint hierarchy, give its space and query bounds, and incorporate the recent correction by Gæde et al. [8] showing the streaming construction yields a 3-approximation rather than a strictly optimal cover. We then derive ALEX [3], the first practical updatable learned index, from its gapped-array data nodes, model-based insertion, exponential last-mile search, and cost-model-driven structural adaptation, and we extend the analysis to the concurrency-control discipline of ALEX+ (optimistic per-node locking with lock coupling). Comparative tables, proof sketches, complexity analyses, and reported empirical figures are provided throughout, concluding with the fundamental limitations — distribution shift, adversarial degeneration, and the tension between error-bounded optimality and dynamic adaptability — that define the current research frontier.

## 1 Introduction

For over four decades, the B-tree and its cache-conscious variants dominated main-memory and external-memory indexing. Its contract is simple and worst-case honest: locate any key in a sorted array of *n* records in *O*(log<sub>B</sub> *n*) node visits, each visit a binary or linear descent through key separators. Yet the B-tree is distribution-blind: it allocates the same structural budget to a near-uniform key set as to a pathological one, and it performs the same number of pointer chases regardless of how "easy" the data is.

Kraska, Beutel, Chi, Dean, and Polyzotis [1] observed that an index is, at its mathematical core, a *model*. A B-tree index maps a key to the position of a record in a sorted array; equivalently, it approximates the empirical CDF of the keys, since for a key *x* at sorted position *i*,

> **Observation 1 (Index = CDF).** *Let the sorted keys be x<sub>1</sub> ≤ … ≤ x<sub>n</sub> and let F be the empirical CDF. Then i = n · F(x<sub>i</sub>). A model f̂ with ‖f̂ − nF‖<sub>∞</sub> ≤ ε predicts every position up to a bounded error, and a local search over ±ε positions recovers exactness.*

This single observation ignited a research program. The 2018 Recursive Model Index [1] showed neural and linear model hierarchies outperforming cache-optimized B-trees by up to 70% while shrinking the index by an order of magnitude — but only for static, read-only data. The PGM-index [2] (2020) answered with *provable* worst-case bounds via optimal piecewise-linear ε-approximation, compressing the index by up to three orders of magnitude. ALEX [3] (2020) answered the dynamism objection with model-based inserts into gapped arrays and cost-driven structural adaptation, beating B-trees by up to 4.1× on read-write workloads with up to 2000× smaller footprint. Subsequent work generalized (CARMI [4]) and critiqued (Stoian et al. [5]) these designs.

This thesis unifies these three architectures under one analytical framework — the *error-bounded CDF approximation problem* — and traces how each generation traded off the axes of space, query time, build cost, dynamism, and concurrency. Our contributions are: (i) a formal treatment of RMI training, search, and its hyperparameter fragility; (ii) a reconstruction of the PGM-index's recursive ε-bounded construction with corrected optimality claims per [8]; (iii) a derivation of ALEX's gapped-array mechanics, exponential search, and cost-model adaptation; (iv) an analysis of worst-case bounds and the ALEX+ concurrency protocol; and (v) a critical assessment of limitations and open problems.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The classical baseline

A B<sup>+</sup>-tree with fanout *B* stores *n* keys in *O*(*n*/*B*) nodes, answers point queries in *O*(log<sub>B</sub> *n*) random accesses, and supports inserts, deletes, and splits in amortized *O*(log<sub>B</sub> *n*). Cache-optimized variants (e.g., FAST, ART) reduce pointer chasing but not the asymptotic profile. Crucially, the tree makes *no use* of the data distribution: the descent is identical whether the CDF is a straight line (uniform keys) or a fractal.

### 2.2 The learned-index primitive

Fix a sorted array *A*[0…*n*−1] of keys. Define the empirical CDF *F*(*x*) = |{*k* ∈ *A* : *k* ≤ *x*}| / *n*. A learned index is a function *f̂*: Key → ℤ satisfying

```
|f̂(x) − rank(x)| ≤ ε   for all x,          (1)
```

where *rank*(*x*) = *n*·*F*(*x*) and *ε* ≥ 0 is the *maximum absolute error*. A point lookup proceeds in two phases:

1. **Prediction:** compute *p* = *f̂*(*q*), an *O*(1) model evaluation.
2. **Last-mile search:** binary (or exponential) search in *A*[*p* − ε … *p* + ε], costing *O*(log ε).

Total query time is therefore *O*(eval(*f̂*) + log ε). The design problem is to choose a model class minimizing a joint objective over *ε* (search cost), *|f̂|* (space), and build time — the *space–accuracy tradeoff* at the heart of every learned index [1, 2, 7].

### 2.3 Piecewise linear approximation

The workhorse model is the *piecewise linear* (PL) function: a set of *m* segments {(s<sub>1</sub>, t<sub>1</sub>), …, (s<sub>m</sub>, t<sub>m</sub>)} whose union approximates the CDF points (*x<sub>i</sub>*, *i*) within *L*<sub>∞</sub> error *ε*. The *optimal PLA problem* asks for the minimum *m* achieving error *ε*. A classic streaming algorithm (O'Rourke's) greedily extends each segment as far as possible; Gæde et al. [8] recently clarified that this greedy procedure yields a *3-approximation* to the minimum-complexity cover, correcting the earlier optimality claim in [2] — a nuance we return to in §4.2.

### 2.4 Evaluation methodology

The community evaluates learned indexes on real-world SOSD datasets (*books*, *fb*, *osm*, *wiki* — 200M 64-bit keys each) plus synthetic uniform/lognormal workloads, measuring lookup throughput, build time, index size, and increasingly insert throughput and tail latency [5, 7]. We organize reported figures along these axes in §5.

## 3 Methodology

We analyze the three architectures through a uniform framework with five design axes:

| Axis | RMI [1] | PGM-index [2] | ALEX [3] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Model class | Layered regressors (linear/NN) | Recursive ε-PLA segments | Linear model per node |
| Error guarantee | Per-leaf empirical (min/max) | Provable ε per level | Empirical; cost-model adaptivity |
| Space | *O*(Σ layer sizes), small | *O*(*m*) segments, *m* ≪ *n* | Model + gapped arrays (~*n*/0.7 slots) |
| Point query | *O*(Σ eval + log ε) | *O*(*h* log ε), *h* small | *O*(eval + log ε), exponential search |
| Updates | Retrain only | Log-structured dynamic variant | Model-based insert, splits/merges |
| Concurrency | None | None (static core) | ALEX+: optimistic per-node locks |

Analytically, we use approximation theory (ε-covers, segment lower bounds) and amortized analysis (logarithmic method for dynamization). Empirically, we report figures from the original papers and independent re-analyses [3, 4, 5, 7]. All three architectures are *order-preserving* in the sense that their models are monotone in the key, which is what makes range scans correct: once the start position is found, the scan proceeds sequentially through the sorted array.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Recursive Model Indexes: Hierarchical CDF Approximation

**Architecture.** An RMI is a directed acyclic graph of models organized in *k* layers *l*<sub>0</sub>, …, *l*<sub>k−1</sub>. Layer *l*<sub>0</sub> holds a single root model *f*<sub>0</sub><sup>0</sup>; each subsequent layer partitions the key space more finely. Given key *x*, the lookup evaluates

```
j1 = f_0^0(x)          # choose child in layer 1
j2 = f_1^{j1}(x)       # choose child in layer 2
...
p  = f_{k-1}^{jk-1}(x) # predicted position
```

and then performs a last-mile search in [*p* − ε<sub>max</sub>, *p* + ε<sub>max</sub>], where the error bounds are recorded per leaf model during training [1]. The order-preserving requirement — *x*<sub>1</sub> ≤ *x*<sub>2</sub> ⇒ *f*(*x*<sub>1</sub>) ≤ *f*(*x*<sub>2</sub>) at each node — guarantees range-query correctness, since the predicted interval for a key interval stays contiguous.

**Training.** RMI training is *layer-greedy*: train the root on all (*x<sub>i</sub>*, *i*) pairs, use its predictions to partition the data among layer-1 models, train each on its partition, and recurse. The reference implementation [1] is a *compiler* producing C++ code; its optimizer performs grid search over the enormous configuration space (model types per layer, layer widths, leaf error thresholds) against the space/performance Pareto frontier.

> **Theorem (RMI search cost).** *Lookup in a k-layer RMI costs Σ<sub>i</sub> eval(f<sub>i</sub>) + O(log ε), where ε is the leaf model's recorded maximum absolute error. Space is Σ<sub>i</sub> |l<sub>i</sub>|·sizeof(model).*

**The brittleness problem.** Stoian et al. [5] conducted the definitive critical analysis and found RMIs *hard to use*: performance collapses on non-smooth real-world distributions because the greedy layer-wise training cannot revise upper-layer partitioning mistakes; the hyperparameter space is exponentially large with no principled defaults; and reported gains often relied on synthetic-friendly configurations. CDFShop [4] responds with Pareto-optimal search restricted to two-layer RMIs. This fragility motivated the error-bounded, parameter-light designs of §4.2–4.3 — most notably RadixSpline [7], which needs only (ε, radix-bits), and the PGM-index, which needs only ε.

### 4.2 The PGM-Index: Optimal Piecewise-Linear Approximation with Provable Bounds

**Construction.** The PGM-index [2] builds a hierarchy of piecewise-linear ε-approximations *bottom-up*. Let level 0 be the PLA of the CDF points with error ε, yielding *m*<sub>0</sub> segments. Level 1 is the PLA (with error ε) of the *first keys* of level-0's segments; recurse until one segment remains. Querying descends: at each level, evaluate the segment covering *q* (found via the previous level's prediction ±ε), predict the position of *q*'s segment in the next level, and correct within ±ε. The query descends cleanly because every level is ε-bounded, so the total error telescopes.

The number of segments contracts geometrically: if level *i* has *m<sub>i</sub>* segments, then *m<sub>i+1</sub>* ≤ *m<sub>i</sub>* / (2ε + 1) roughly, since each level-(*i*+1) segment covers at least ~2ε segment endpoints. Hence the height *h* = *O*(log<sub>ε</sub> *m*<sub>0</sub>), a small constant in practice (≤ 5 for 200M keys).

> **Theorem (PGM query bound [2]).** *Predecessor and range queries cost O(h · log ε) = O(log m<sub>0</sub> · log ε), and the index occupies O(m<sub>0</sub>) space — succinct in that m<sub>0</sub> ≪ n on compressible distributions.*

**Optimality, corrected.** The PGM paper claimed the streaming greedy PLA construction is a *minimum-complexity* ε-cover computable in *O*(*n*) time [2, Lemma 1]. Gæde et al. [8] showed this claim is incorrect as stated: the greedy algorithm outputs a cover whose complexity is at most **3×** the optimum (an *ε-cover* 3-approximation), and they give a corrected dynamic framework achieving worst-case update bounds.

**Variants.** Ferragina and Vinciguerra [2] derive three: (i) a *distribution-aware* PGM weighting segments by query frequency (the first learned index to adapt to workload, not just data); (ii) a *compressed* PGM exploiting repetitiveness among learned models to shrink the already-succinct footprint; and (iii) a *multicriteria* PGM auto-tuning (ε, height) in seconds over hundreds of millions of keys to satisfy evolving space–time constraints.

**Dynamization.** The fully-dynamic PGM applies the *logarithmic method*: maintain *O*(log *n*) static PGM levels of geometrically growing capacity plus an insertion buffer; inserts go to the buffer, and full levels merge downward. This yields amortized *O*(log² *n*) updates with worst-case query bounds preserved [2], at the cost of update constants that ALEX's in-place approach (§4.3) substantially beats in practice.

### 4.3 ALEX: Model-Based Inserts and the Gapped Array

**The update problem.** RMI and the static PGM are read-only: a single insert can invalidate every model's error bound. ALEX [3] is the first learned index engineered for mixed read-write workloads, fusing learned models with proven storage techniques.

**Data nodes and the gapped array.** ALEX's leaves are *data nodes*: arrays holding up to a capacity of key–payload pairs with *gaps* (empty slots) interspersed, targeting a density *d* ∈ [*d*<sub>l</sub>, *d*<sub>h</sub>] = [0.6, 0.8]. Each data node stores a linear model (slope, intercept) mapping keys to array positions.

**Model-based insert.** To insert key *k*:
1. Traverse the RMI skeleton to the responsible data node.
2. Predict *pos* = model(*k*).
3. If slot *pos* is free, place *k*; else shift neighboring elements into nearby gaps (bounded shifting, like a packed-memory array).
4. Update node statistics (number of inserts, average search iterations).

Because the model predicts *where* the key belongs in sorted order, inserts into well-modeled regions are *O*(1) amortized — the model *is* the placement policy. This is the deep idea: the learned CDF doubles as an insertion oracle.

**Exponential last-mile search.** ALEX replaces binary last-mile search with *exponential search* from the predicted position: probe at offsets 1, 2, 4, 8, … until bracketing the key, then binary-search the bracket. When the model is accurate (small error), this is *O*(log ε<sub>actual</sub>) with tiny constants and excellent cache behavior; it degrades gracefully as *O*(log *n*) when the model is wrong [3].

**Cost-model adaptation.** Each data node tracks empirical statistics — expected lookup cost (from model error) and insert cost (from shifting distance). When density exceeds *d*<sub>h</sub> or the cost model predicts cheaper alternatives, ALEX chooses among: *expand* (reallocate with more gaps), *retrain* (refit the model, possibly splitting the node), or *split sideways/downward* (creating new data nodes or internal nodes). Underfull nodes merge. This closed-loop controller is what makes ALEX *adaptive*: the structure continuously re-optimizes itself against the observed workload, with reported results of up to 2.2× faster than the original learned index on reads (15× smaller), and up to 4.1× faster than B<sup>+</sup>-trees on read-write mixes while never performing worse, at up to 2000× smaller index size [3].

```python
# ALEX-style model-based insert (simplified)
def alex_insert(node, key, payload):
    pos = int(node.slope * key + node.intercept)   # model prediction
    pos = clamp(pos, 0, node.capacity - 1)
    if node.slots[pos] is None:
        node.slots[pos] = (key, payload)           # O(1) fast path
    else:
        pos = shift_into_gap(node, pos)            # bounded shifting
        node.slots[pos] = (key, payload)
    node.stats.inserts += 1
    if node.density() > D_HIGH or node.expected_cost() > RETRAIN_THRESHOLD:
        adapt_structure(node)                      # expand / retrain / split
```

### 4.4 Worst-Case Bounds, Distribution-Aware Variants, and Concurrency Control

**The bounds spectrum.** The three designs sit at different points on the rigor–practicality spectrum:

| Structure | Query worst case | Space worst case | Update worst case | Proven? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RMI [1] | *O*(Σ eval + log ε<sub>emp</sub>) | Config-dependent | N/A (static) | Empirical only |
| PGM [2] | *O*(*h* log ε) | *O*(*m*), *m* 3-approx optimal [8] | Amortized *O*(log² *n*) (dynamic) | Yes |
| ALEX [3] | *O*(depth + log *n*) degrades gracefully | *O*(*n* / *d*<sub>l</sub>) slots | Amortized *O*(log *n*), splits *O*(*n*) worst case | Expected-case (cost model) |

The honest summary: PGM owns the *worst-case* story, ALEX owns the *practical dynamic* story, and RMI owns the *historical* story — a division the critical analysis [5] and benchmarking studies [7] corroborate.

**ALEX+ concurrency.** The follow-up ALEX+ design adds concurrency via *optimistic per-node locking with lock coupling*: readers traverse lock-free, validating per-node version counters; writers acquire the leaf's lock, and structural modifications (splits, expansions) use lock coupling upward, holding at most a constant number of node locks along the path. Deadlock is avoided by a global lock ordering (leaf-to-root, then sibling order). Because model-based inserts usually touch only one data node, write–write contention localizes to hot key regions — the same skew that hurts any ordered index — while read scalability is near-linear on multi-core [3].

**Haskell sketch of the ε-bounded query contract.** The following executable specification captures the invariant every level of a PGM/ALEX hierarchy must satisfy:

```haskell
-- An epsilon-bounded predictor: prediction within ±eps of the true rank
type Rank = Int
predict :: (Key -> Double) -> Double -> Key -> Rank
predict model eps key = clamp 0 (n-1) (round (model key))
  -- contract: |predict model eps key - rank key| <= eps

lastMile :: Array Key -> Rank -> Int -> Key -> Maybe Rank
lastMile arr p eps q = binarySearch arr (max 0 (p-eps)) (min (n-1) (p+eps)) q
-- Total work: O(eval) + O(log eps); correctness follows from the contract.
```

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch: segment lower bound and the 3-approximation

> **Lemma (Segment lower bound).** *Let S = {(x<sub>i</sub>, i)}<sub>i=1..n</sub> be CDF points. Any ε-approximate piecewise-linear cover of S needs m ≥ n / (2ε + 1) segments in the worst case (e.g., adversarial sawtooth data), and the greedy streaming algorithm produces m ≤ 3·m* segments where m* is optimal [8].*

*Proof sketch.* Each segment can cover points whose *y*-values span at most 2ε + slope effects; the greedy algorithm extends each segment maximally, so between any two consecutive greedy segment endpoints there is a point forcing any optimal cover to start a new segment within a bounded window — the classic charging argument giving the factor 3. ∎

> **Corollary (PGM height).** *With m<sub>i+1</sub> ≤ m<sub>i</sub>/(2ε+1) ≤ m<sub>i</sub>/3 for ε ≥ 1, the recursive construction has height h ≤ log<sub>3</sub> m<sub>0</sub> + 1. For n = 2·10⁸ and ε = 64, h ≤ 5 in practice [2].*

fn lookup<Cdf: Fn(u64) -> usize>(arr: &[u64], cdf: &Cdf, eps: usize, q: u64) -> Option<usize> {

## 6 Limitations

1. **Distribution shift and adversarial data.** All three designs assume the training distribution resembles the query distribution. Under adversarial inserts (e.g., keys engineered to maximize model error), RMI error bounds explode, ALEX splits pathologically toward a degenerate linked structure, and even the PGM's *m* approaches *n*/(2ε+1) — learned indexes offer *no* better worst case than classical structures on hostile data [5, 8].
2. **Update amortization vs. latency.** ALEX's amortized *O*(log *n*) inserts hide *O*(*n*)-element shifts and node retrains; the dynamic PGM's logarithmic merges create write stalls. Bursty writes remain the weak point versus B-trees' bounded splits.
3. **Tuning and automation.** RMI's configuration space is effectively unnavigable by hand [5]; PGM reduces this to one parameter (ε) and ALEX to density thresholds plus cost-model weights — progress, but workload-aware auto-tuning (the multicriteria PGM [2], CARMI's cost model [4]) is still nascent.
4. **Concurrency and NUMA.** ALEX+ optimistic locking scales reads well, but hot-key write contention serializes on single data nodes, and model retraining under concurrent mutation requires careful epoch discipline not yet standardized.
5. **Beyond one dimension.** Extending the CDF trick to multi-dimensional and spatial data requires space-filling curves or learned partitionings that distort locality; the survey in [6] shows this remains an open, active frontier.

---

## 7 Conclusion

Learned indexes reframe indexing as *distribution learning*: the RMI [1] proved the concept with hierarchical CDF regression; the PGM-index [2] made it rigorous with ε-bounded piecewise-linear hierarchies and provable worst-case bounds (with the optimality constant corrected to a 3-approximation by [8]); and ALEX [3] made it practical for dynamic workloads through gapped arrays, model-based inserts, exponential last-mile search, and cost-driven adaptation, extended to concurrent settings by ALEX+'s optimistic locking. The field's arc is thus from *empirical promise* through *theoretical discipline* to *systems practicality*.
