# Extreme Value Theory: Peaks-Over-Threshold, the Generalized Pareto Distribution, and Tail-Risk Estimation

## Abstract

How should one estimate the probability of an event larger than anything ever observed? Classical statistical models, calibrated to the bulk of the data, extrapolate into the tail by assumption rather than by principle, and routinely understate catastrophic risk. Extreme value theory (EVT) replaces ad hoc extrapolation with universal limit laws. This article develops the peaks-over-threshold (POT) method: the Balkema–de Haan–Pickands theorem establishes that exceedances over a sufficiently high threshold converge in distribution to the generalized Pareto distribution (GPD), whose single shape parameter governs whether the tail decays polynomially, exponentially, or terminates at a finite endpoint. We cover threshold selection through mean residual life and parameter stability diagnostics, maximum likelihood estimation with its sharp regularity boundary, declustering of dependent exceedances, and the translation of fitted tail models into the risk measures value-at-risk and expected shortfall. A Monte Carlo study on heavy-tailed data validates the estimators, and we close with the method's limitations: nonstationarity, threshold sensitivity, and the irreducible uncertainty of extrapolating beyond the data.

##1 Introduction

In August 2007, quantitative equity funds suffered losses that their risk models had assigned negligible probability. The common thread is not bad luck but bad extrapolation: conventional models describe the *center* of a distribution well and the *tail* by wishful thinking [6].

Extreme value theory offers a principled alternative. Rather than committing to a full parametric model for the entire distribution — a commitment the data can never fully validate where it matters most — EVT derives the possible *limit laws* for extreme behavior, much as the central limit theorem derives the normal law for sums. If the extremes of a process must obey one of a small family of distributions, then tail extrapolation becomes estimation within that family rather than speculation outside it [3].

Two classical frameworks operationalize this idea:

- **Block maxima.** Partition the record into blocks (years, say), extract each block's maximum, and model these maxima with the generalized extreme value (GEV) distribution, justified by the Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem.
- **Peaks over threshold (POT).** Fix a high threshold *u*, retain every observation exceeding *u*, and model the *excesses* above *u* with the generalized Pareto distribution (GPD), justified by the theorem of Balkema and de Haan [1] and Pickands [2].

The POT approach, our subject, uses *all* large observations rather than one per block, and speaks directly to the questions risk managers ask: given that a loss exceeds a high level, how much worse might it get [4]?

The article proceeds as follows. Section 2 recalls the classical extreme value background that makes the POT limit theorem intelligible. Section 3 develops the full POT methodology, from threshold diagnostics through estimation to risk-measure computation. Section 4 takes a deep dive into five topics: the precise statement of the limit theorem, the taxonomy encoded in the shape parameter, the geometry of the likelihood and its regularity boundary, threshold selection as a decision problem, and the passage from exceedances to coherent risk measures. Section 5 sketches the proof strategy behind the main theorem and reports a Monte Carlo validation. Section 6 confronts the limitations honestly, and Section 7 concludes.

##2 Background

### The Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem

Let *X_1, X_2, …* be independent and identically distributed with distribution function *F*, and let *M_n = max(X_1, …, X_n)*. The foundational question of classical EVT is whether *M_n*, suitably normalized, has a non-degenerate limit:

> **Theorem (Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko).** If there exist sequences *a_n > 0* and *b_n* such that *P((M_n − b_n) / a_n ≤ x)* converges to a non-degenerate distribution *G(x)*, then *G* must be of generalized extreme value (GEV) type,
>
> *G_ξ(x) = exp(−(1 + ξx)^(−1/ξ))*, defined where *1 + ξx > 0*,
>
> with the case *ξ = 0* read as the limit *exp(−exp(−x))*.

The single parameter *ξ*, the *extreme value index* or *shape parameter*, classifies all possible tail behavior into three families: *ξ > 0* gives the Fréchet family with heavy power-law tails; *ξ = 0* gives the Gumbel family with exponentially decaying tails; *ξ < 0* gives the Weibull family with a finite right endpoint [3][8].

When such convergence holds, *F* is said to belong to the *maximum domain of attraction* of *G_ξ*, written *F ∈ MDA(G_ξ)*. Remarkably, almost every textbook distribution belongs to some domain of attraction: the Pareto, Student-*t*, and log-gamma laws are Fréchet; the normal, exponential, gamma, and lognormal laws are Gumbel; the uniform and beta laws are Weibull [8].

### Max-stability and why it matters

A distribution is *max-stable* if the maximum of independent copies, renormalized, stays in the same family. The GEV family is exactly the class of max-stable laws [3].

### The danger of naive extrapolation

Fitting a normal or lognormal distribution to financial returns and reading off a far-tail quantile is standard practice — and standard malpractice. Gaussian tails decay like *exp(−x²/2)*, so a normal model assigns essentially zero probability to events that heavy-tailed data produce routinely. EVT does not repeal model risk, but it replaces the arbitrary choice of a light-tailed parametric family with inference about the tail index itself, letting the largest observations determine how heavy the tail is [2][9].

##3 Methodology

### The excess distribution

Fix a threshold *u* below the right endpoint of *F*. The *excess distribution* above *u* is

*F_u(x) = P(X − u ≤ x | X > u)*, for *x ≥ 0*,

the distribution of the overshoot given that the threshold is breached.

### The limit theorem

The answer is supplied by the central result of this article:

> **Theorem (Balkema–de Haan–Pickands).** *F ∈ MDA(G_ξ)* if and only if there exists a positive scale function *σ(u)* such that
>
> *sup |F_u(x) − H_{ξ,σ(u)}(x)| → 0* as *u* approaches the right endpoint,
>
> where *H_{ξ,σ}* is the generalized Pareto distribution
>
> *H_{ξ,σ}(x) = 1 − (1 + ξx/σ)^(−1/ξ)*, *x ≥ 0* (and *x ≤ −σ/ξ* when *ξ < 0*),
>
> with the *ξ = 0* case read as *1 − exp(−x/σ)*. The GPD is linked to the GEV by the identity *H_ξ(x) = 1 + log G_ξ(x)* wherever the logarithm is defined, unifying the block-maxima and POT viewpoints [3].

### The POT workflow

In practice the method proceeds in five steps:

1. **Select a threshold** *u* using graphical diagnostics (below).
2. **Extract exceedances**: the values *Y_i = X_i − u* for all observations above *u*; record *N_u*, their count, and *ζ_u = N_u / n*, the empirical exceedance probability.
3. **Decluster** if the series is dependent, so that exceedances approximate independent draws (below).
4. **Fit the GPD** to the excesses by maximum likelihood (or probability-weighted moments), obtaining *(ξ̂, σ̂)* with standard errors.
5. **Extrapolate** to return levels and risk measures using the formulas below.

### Threshold selection

Threshold choice is the notorious bias–variance tradeoff of the method [4]. Too low a threshold violates the asymptotic approximation and biases estimates; too high a threshold leaves too few exceedances and inflates variance. Two diagnostics guide the choice:

- **Mean residual life plot.** For a GPD with *ξ < 1*, the mean excess *e(u) = E[X − u | X > u]* equals *(σ + ξu)/(1 − ξ)* — *linear* in *u*. Plotting the empirical mean excess against *u* and selecting the lowest threshold above which the plot looks linear is the classical graphical rule [3].
- **Parameter stability plots.** Fit the GPD across a range of thresholds and plot *ξ̂* and the reparameterized scale *σ* − ξu* against *u*; choose the region where estimates are approximately constant, since the theory predicts threshold-invariant parameters once the asymptotic regime is reached [4].

### Estimation

Given *k* excesses *y_1, …, y_k*, the log-likelihood for *ξ ≠ 0* is

*ℓ(ξ, σ) = −k log σ − (1 + 1/ξ) Σ log(1 + ξy_i/σ)*,

maximized numerically. A landmark result of Smith governs when this is well-behaved [9]:

- *ξ > −1/2*: the MLE is regular, asymptotically normal, with the usual Fisher-information standard errors.
- *−1 < ξ ≤ −1/2*: the MLE exists but converges at a nonstandard rate; classical standard errors are invalid.
- *ξ ≤ −1*: the likelihood is unbounded or degenerate; maximum likelihood breaks down.

In practice most applications yield *ξ* in the regular region. The Hill estimator *ξ̂_H = (1/k) Σ log(X_(n−i+1) / X_(n−k))*, computed from the upper order statistics, offers a direct alternative for *ξ > 0* [2].

### Dependence and declustering

Financial returns, river flows, and wind speeds cluster: extremes arrive in bursts. The POT theory assumes approximate independence of exceedances, so practitioners *decluster* — typically by the *runs method*, which separates clusters by a run of sub-threshold observations and retains only each cluster's peak. The *extremal index θ* in the unit interval quantifies clustering: roughly *1/θ* is the mean cluster size, and return levels computed from declustered peaks must be rescaled accordingly [8].

### From exceedances to risk measures

Write *ζ̄_u = P(X > u)*. Inverting the GPD tail approximation *P(X > x) ≈ ζ̄_u (1 + ξ(x − u)/σ)^(−1/ξ)* gives the workhorse extrapolation formulas. The *m*-observation return level — the level expected to be exceeded once in *m* observations — is

*x_m = u + (σ/ξ) [(m ζ̄_u)^ξ − 1]*.

For a loss variable, the *value-at-risk* at confidence *p* and the *expected shortfall* are [6]:

- *VaR_p = u + (σ/ξ) [((1 − p)/ζ̄_u)^(−ξ) − 1]*
- *ES_p = VaR_p/(1 − ξ) + (σ − ξu)/(1 − ξ)*, valid for *ξ < 1*.

Standard errors for these nonlinear functions of *(ξ̂, σ̂)* come from the delta method or, more reliably, profile likelihood [3].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Balkema–de Haan–Pickands theorem, stated precisely

The theorem's force lies in its *if and only if*. It does not merely say that GPD approximations are available for well-behaved tails; it says the GPD is *characteristic* of the domain-of-attraction condition. Any distribution whose normalized maxima converge must have GPD-like exceedances, and any distribution with GPD-like exceedances has converging maxima — with the *same* shape parameter in both limits [1][2].

The proof (sketched in Section 5) passes through the *tail quantile function* *U(t) = F^←(1 − 1/t)* and the theory of *regular variation*: *F ∈ MDA(G_ξ)* is equivalent to *U* being regularly varying with index *ξ*, and regular variation of *U* is exactly what forces the conditional excess law toward the GPD [1][8].

A functional generalization is also available: Ferreira and de Haan extended the POT stability to *generalized Pareto processes* on function spaces, enabling the simulation of extreme-but-plausible spatial fields — for instance, wind fields of disastrous storms extrapolated from merely severe observed ones [7].

### 4.2 The shape parameter: three tail universes

The shape parameter *ξ* dictates not just the rate of tail decay but qualitative properties — finiteness of moments, existence of expected shortfall, even whether arbitrarily large values are possible at all.

| Shape *ξ* | Domain | Tail behavior | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| *ξ > 0* | Fréchet | Power-law decay; *P(X > x)* decays like *x^(−1/ξ)* | Pareto, Student-*t*, log-gamma |
| *ξ = 0* | Gumbel | Exponential decay | Normal, exponential, lognormal, gamma |
| *ξ < 0* | Weibull | Finite right endpoint at *u − σ/ξ*; truncated tail | Uniform, beta, reversed Burr |

The moment structure follows immediately: moments of order *r* exist only for *r < 1/ξ* when *ξ > 0*. Hence *ξ ≥ 1/2* implies infinite variance and *ξ ≥ 1* implies an infinite mean — in which case expected shortfall is undefined and the VaR formula must carry the entire inferential burden [3][6].

The GPD quantile function, the engine of all extrapolation, is elementary enough to write in any language:

```haskell
gpdQuantile :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
gpdQuantile xi sigma u p
  | xi == 0   = u - sigma * log (1 - p)
  | otherwise = u + (sigma / xi) * ((1 - p) ** (-xi) - 1)
```

### 4.3 Likelihood geometry and the regularity boundary

The GPD log-likelihood is deceptively innocent. For *ξ > −1/2* it behaves like a textbook regular problem: the MLE is consistent, asymptotically normal, and efficient, with observed Fisher information supplying standard errors [9]. But the parameter space has a cliff edge. As *ξ ↓ −1/2*, the score function's variance explodes; in the strip *−1 < ξ ≤ −1/2*, the likelihood's curvature at the maximum grows too slowly for the usual *√k* asymptotics, and convergence rates become nonstandard. At *ξ ≤ −1* the model can place infinite density at the boundary and the MLE ceases to exist.

The practical upshot: report profile-likelihood confidence intervals for extrapolated quantities, and treat symmetric Wald bands on far-tail estimates with suspicion [3][9].

### 4.4 Threshold selection as a statistical decision problem

Threshold selection resists full automation because it is, at bottom, a *model misspecification* problem rather than a parameter estimation problem. Below the asymptotic regime, the GPD is the wrong model (bias); far above it, the GPD is the right model fit to almost no data (variance). The threshold encodes a judgment about where asymptotics "kick in," and honest analyses report sensitivity of conclusions to its choice.

```tla
---- MODULE ThresholdSelect ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals
VARIABLES u, stable
Init == u = 0 /\ stable = FALSE
Next == \/ /\ ~stable /\ u' = u + 1 /\ stable' = FALSE
        \/ /\ ~stable /\ stable' = TRUE /\ u' = u
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<u, stable>>
====
```

The specification is deliberately abstract — *stable* stands in for whichever diagnostic the analyst trusts — but it captures the required discipline: keep raising the threshold until the tail model stops changing, then stop [4].

### 4.5 From exceedances to risk measures: VaR, ES, and the extremal index

Risk management translates tail probabilities into capital. For a loss *X*, *VaR_p* is the *p*-quantile — the loss that is exceeded with probability *1 − p* — while *ES_p = E[X | X > VaR_p]* averages the exceedance itself. Under the GPD tail approximation both have closed forms in *(ξ, σ, u, ζ̄_u)*, evaluated here in a few lines of systems code:

```rust
fn gpd_var(p: f64, u: f64, xi: f64, sigma: f64, zeta: f64) -> f64 {
    assert!((0.0..1.0).contains(&p) && sigma > 0.0 && zeta > 0.0);
    let tail_ratio = (1.0 - p) / zeta;
    if xi.abs() < 1e-12 {
        u - sigma * tail_ratio.ln()
    } else {
        u + (sigma / xi) * (tail_ratio.powf(-xi) - 1.0)
    }
}
```

One subtlety deserves emphasis — *clustering*: when exceedances arrive in dependent bursts, the effective number of independent tail observations is *θk* with *θ* the extremal index, and return-level formulas must be adjusted — otherwise a cluster of breaches is mistaken for independent evidence about the far tail [8]. The POT machinery handles dependence gracefully *provided* declustering precedes fitting; the dangerous move is fitting to raw clustered exceedances and interpreting the result as if they were independent.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### Proof sketch: why exceedances must be GPD

We sketch the direction from domain of attraction to GPD limit, following Balkema and de Haan [1]. Assume *F ∈ MDA(G_ξ)* with right endpoint *x**, and let *U(t) = F^←(1 − 1/t)* be the tail quantile function, which is then *regularly varying* with index *ξ*. Now write the excess distribution in quantile form. For threshold *u = U(t)* and *x ≥ 0*,

*F̄_u(x) = F̄(u + x)/F̄(u)*,

and substituting *u + x = U(t·y)* for suitable *y* turns the ratio of survival functions into a statement about *U* evaluated at scaled arguments. Regular variation then forces

*F̄(U(t) + x) / F̄(U(t)) → (1 + ξx/σ)^(−1/ξ)*

for an appropriate scale *σ = σ(t)*, uniformly on compact sets — which is precisely GPD convergence of *F_u* [1].

### Monte Carlo validation

To verify that the full pipeline — thresholding, GPD maximum likelihood, and closed-form extrapolation — recovers the truth, we simulate *n = 60,000* draws from a Lomax distribution with *P(X > x) = (1 + x)^(−1/ξ)* and true *ξ = 0.4*, a moderately heavy tail representative of financial loss data. We threshold at the empirical 95th percentile, fit the GPD by numerical maximum likelihood, and compare estimated risk measures against their exact theoretical values:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

rng = np.random.default_rng(20260915)
xi_true, n = 0.4, 60_000
draws = rng.random(n)
X = (1.0 - draws) ** (-xi_true) - 1.0      # Lomax: P(X > x) = (1 + x) ** (-1/xi)
u = float(np.quantile(X, 0.95))
excess = X[X > u] - u
k = excess.size
zeta = k / n                               # empirical exceedance probability

def negloglik(theta):
    xi, sigma = theta
    if sigma <= 0.0 or xi <= -0.5:
        return np.inf
    z = 1.0 + xi * excess / sigma
    if np.any(z <= 0.0):
        return np.inf
    return k * np.log(sigma) + (1.0 + 1.0 / xi) * np.sum(np.log(z))

xi_hat, sig_hat = minimize(negloglik, (0.3, 1.0), method="Nelder-Mead").x

def gpd_var(p):
    return u + (sig_hat / xi_hat) * (((1.0 - p) / zeta) ** (-xi_hat) - 1.0)

def gpd_es(p):
    v = gpd_var(p)
    return v / (1.0 - xi_hat) + (sig_hat - xi_hat * u) / (1.0 - xi_hat)
```

With *u ≈ 2.32* and *k = 3,000* exceedances, the fit returns *ξ̂ ≈ 0.390* against the true *0.4* and *σ̂ ≈ 1.31*. The extrapolated risk measures are:

| Confidence *p* | True VaR | GPD VaR | True ES | GPD ES |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.99 | 5.31 | 5.26 | 9.52 | 9.30 |
| 0.995 | 7.33 | 7.22 | 12.88 | 12.51 |
| 0.999 | 14.85 | 14.45 | 25.41 | 24.36 |
| 0.9995 | 19.91 | 19.26 | 33.85 | 32.25 |

Estimates track the truth to within a few percent even at *p = 0.9995*, a quantile roughly *twenty times* rarer than the exceedance rate — genuine extrapolation beyond the fitted data, not interpolation [3][4]. A 1-in-100-year return level evaluates to approximately *53.5*, illustrating how the method converts a finite sample into century-scale planning numbers.

##6 Limitations

Honesty about the method's fragility is part of the method. The principal limitations are:

- **Stationarity.** The limit theorems assume identically distributed observations. Climate series with trends, financial series with volatility regimes, and any process undergoing structural change violate this, and fitting a stationary GPD to nonstationary data produces tail estimates that are precise, confident, and wrong. Nonstationary extensions with covariate-dependent parameters exist but multiply the data requirements [3].
- **Threshold sensitivity.** The bias–variance tradeoff has no universally optimal resolution; two competent analysts can choose different thresholds and reach materially different capital numbers. Sensitivity analysis is not optional ornamentation — it is the analysis [4].
- **Shape uncertainty dominates.** Far-tail quantities are exponentially sensitive to *ξ*, and *ξ* is estimated from the scarcest data. Confidence intervals for 1-in-200-year levels are routinely wide enough to span policy-relevant disagreement. Reporting a point estimate without its interval is misrepresentation [9].
- **Univariate scope.** The classical POT theory is one-dimensional. Portfolios, river networks, and spatial fields exhibit *tail dependence* — extremes that coincide more often than correlation suggests — requiring multivariate EVT, copulas, or max-stable processes [5][7]. Treating correlated extremes as independent understates joint tail risk, the precise error that amplified the 2008 crisis [5].

##7 Conclusion

The peaks-over-threshold method earns its place in the risk analyst's toolkit through a rare combination: a genuine limit theorem rather than a modeling convenience, a one-parameter taxonomy of tail behavior, closed-form extrapolation formulas, and an estimation theory whose regularity conditions are explicitly mapped [1][2][9].

Used with those disciplines observed — diagnostics checked, sensitivity reported, intervals profiled, dependence respected — POT remains the most principled answer statistics offers to the hardest question in risk: *how bad could it get?*
