# Stellarator Optimization for Magnetic Fusion: Quasisymmetry, Neoclassical Transport, and Coil Design from Wendelstein 7-X to Next-Step Devices

## Abstract

The stellarator offers an intrinsically steady-state, disruption-free route to magnetic fusion, but only if its three-dimensional magnetic field is sculpted so that collisionless particle confinement approaches that of an axisymmetric tokamak. This thesis surveys the optimization program that made this possible: **quasisymmetry** — a hidden symmetry of the field strength in Boozer coordinates [2] — the numerical machinery of plasma optimization (STELLOPT, SIMSOPT), the two-stage equilibrium-then-coil design paradigm, and experimental validation on Wendelstein 7-X (W7-X) and the Helically Symmetric Experiment (HSX). W7-X demonstrated stable island-divertor detachment, energy confinement times exceeding 200 ms under pellet fueling, and the first direct proof that neoclassical-transport optimization of the magnetic field works in practice [1]; HSX provided the first transport-level evidence that quasi-helical symmetry reduces neoclassical losses [7]. We derive the Garren–Boozer near-axis conditions, describe how effective helical ripple $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is minimized with DKES, formulate coil design as a regularized inverse problem, and confront the residual challenge of microturbulence, which now dominates W7-X losses [9].

## 1 Introduction

Magnetic confinement fusion has been dominated, conceptually and financially, by the axisymmetric tokamak. Yet the tokamak's virtues — nested flux surfaces, excellent neoclassical confinement, a mature theoretical base — come at the price of a *plasma current*: it must be driven, it can disrupt, and its loss triggers the catastrophic termination events that threaten plasma-facing components. The stellarator removes the current from the confining field entirely. Its rotational transform $\iota$ is generated by external coils, so it is intrinsically steady-state and disruption-free. The cost is geometric complexity: a stellarator field is intrinsically three-dimensional, and generic three-dimensional fields trap particles in magnetic wells ("ripple trapping"), producing neoclassical transport that can be *orders of magnitude* worse than in a tokamak.

The modern answer to this dilemma is **optimization**: treat the magnetic field as a design variable and solve an inverse problem. If the field strength $B = |\mathbf{B}|$ can be made to depend, on each flux surface, only on the combination $M\theta - N\phi$ in a special coordinate system, then trapped-particle drift orbits are confined in the same way as in a tokamak with an equivalent symmetry — even though the vector field itself has no continuous symmetry. This property is *quasisymmetry* [2], and it is the single most consequential idea in stellarator theory of the past four decades. Complementing it is the pragmatic philosophy of Wendelstein 7-X: rather than exact quasisymmetry, minimize the neoclassical transport coefficients directly, accepting a field that is merely "good enough" along an optimization path.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the physics of magnetic confinement in three dimensions: drift orbits, neoclassical transport, and the tokamak–stellarator isomorphism. Section 3 describes the optimization methodology: equilibrium codes (VMEC, SPEC), transport codes (DKES), and frameworks (STELLOPT, SIMSOPT). Section 4 deep-dives into quasisymmetry theory, the Garren–Boozer near-axis expansion, transport optimization metrics, and the two-stage coil design problem. Section 5 confronts theory with experiment: W7-X, HSX, and the National Compact Stellarator Experiment (NCSX). Section 6 enumerates limitations, and Section 7 concludes with the outlook for stellarator reactors.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The confinement problem in three dimensions

In a tokamak, the magnetic field is (to leading order) axisymmetric: $B = B(R, Z)$ with no dependence on the toroidal angle $\phi$. Particles following field lines execute banana orbits of width $\Delta_b \sim q\rho/\sqrt{\varepsilon}$, and in the low-collisionality ("$1/\nu$") regime the neoclassical diffusivity scales as $\chi \propto \nu_{\mathrm{eff}} \Delta_b^2$, benign at reactor parameters.

In a generic stellarator, the field strength has local maxima along field lines that mirror-trap a fraction $\sim \sqrt{\varepsilon_h}$ of particles, where $\varepsilon_h$ is the helical ripple amplitude. These trapped particles drift radially in the $\nabla B$ drift before collisionally detrapping, executing superbanana orbits whose step size can be enormous. In the $1/\nu$ regime the diffusivity scales as:

$$
\chi_{1/\nu} \;\propto\; \frac{\varepsilon_h^{3/2} v_{th}^2}{\nu} \left(\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_h^{1/2}}\right)^2,
$$

which *diverges* as collisionality $\nu \to 0$ — precisely the reactor-relevant regime. The earliest stellarators (Model C, L-2, Heliotron-E) were limited by exactly this effect, and the field languished until the theory of **helical-axis and optimized configurations** matured.

### 2.2 Hidden symmetries: quasisymmetry and quasi-isodynamicity

Two design philosophies emerged:

- **Quasisymmetry (QS):** $B(\psi, \theta, \phi) = B(\psi, M\theta - N\phi)$ in Boozer coordinates [2]. Trapped particles then experience the same drift-orbit topology as in a tokamak with equivalent symmetry: *quasi-axisymmetry* (QA, $N = 0$), *quasi-helical symmetry* (QH, $N \neq 0$, $M \neq 0$), or *quasi-poloidal symmetry* (QP, $M = 0$). The neoclassical transport becomes isomorphic to the axisymmetric case, and in particular the bootstrap current follows the tokamak-like expressions [5].
- **Quasi-isodynamicity (QI):** the approach adopted by W7-X, where the second adiabatic invariant $J = \oint v_\parallel\, d\ell$ is (approximately) constant on flux surfaces for all trapped particles, so that trapped-particle orbits have no net radial drift. QI implies reduced neoclassical transport *without* requiring an exact symmetry direction, and it naturally accommodates the island divertor geometry of W7-X.

> **Theorem (Boozer, 1983):** In Boozer coordinates $\{\psi, \theta, \phi\}$, a magnetic field with $\mathbf{j}\cdot\nabla\psi = 0$ satisfying $B = B(\psi, M\theta - N\phi)$ possesses conserved canonical angular momentum along the symmetry direction, and its guiding-center drift orbits and neoclassical transport are isomorphic to those of a two-dimensional field. The generalized Boozer-coordinate construction extends this to fields with $\mathbf{j}\cdot\nabla\psi \neq 0$ [4].

The practical distinction matters: W7-X is not exactly quasisymmetric, yet its transport is dramatically better than that of a classical stellarator [1]. The optimization target was the physics outcome, not the symmetry per se.

### 2.3 The tokamak–stellarator isomorphism

A deep result underpins QS-based design: the drift-kinetic equation in a quasisymmetric field is mathematically equivalent to that in a tokamak, after relabeling the parallel-streaming operator [5]. This isomorphism is why:

1. Neoclassical transport coefficients computed by axisymmetric codes can be transplanted to QS stellarators.
2. The bootstrap current in a QS stellarator can be computed *as if the geometry were axisymmetric*, using analytic tokamak formulas such as those of Redl et al.
3. Turbulence properties in QS fields resemble tokamak turbulence more closely than in generic stellarators, as studied for HSX and NCSX equilibria [8].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The two-stage optimization paradigm

Since the devices built at W7-X (Klinger et al., 2017) and HSX (Anderson et al., 1995), stellarator design has proceeded in **two stages** [6]:

1. **Stage 1 — plasma optimization:** vary the shape of a toroidal magnetic boundary (parameterized by Fourier amplitudes $R_{mnc}, Z_{mns}$) to optimize the physics inside — quasisymmetry error, neoclassical transport, MHD stability, fast-particle confinement — using a fixed-boundary equilibrium.
2. **Stage 2 — coil optimization:** find coil shapes and currents that reproduce the target boundary field, i.e., solve $\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{coils}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} \approx 0$ on the boundary, regularized against coil complexity.

### 3.2 The equilibrium engine: VMEC

The Variational Moments Equilibrium Code (VMEC) assumes nested flux surfaces and minimizes the MHD energy:

$$
W = \int \left( \frac{p}{\gamma - 1} + \frac{|\mathbf{B}|^2}{2\mu_0} \right) dV,
$$

on a series of toroidal surfaces via steepest descent. VMEC is extraordinarily robust, and its output feeds the entire postprocessing chain: BOOZ_XFORM for Boozer spectra, DKES for neoclassical transport, and stability codes. The Stepped-Pressure Equilibrium Code (SPEC), by contrast, permits magnetic islands, enabling optimization directly against island size via Greene's residue [6].

### 3.3 Neoclassical transport: DKES and the effective ripple

The Drift Kinetic Equation Solver (DKES) computes the monoenergetic neoclassical transport coefficients $D_{ij}$ as functions of collisionality $\nu^*$ and radial electric field $E_r$. For optimization, a scalar proxy is needed. The standard choice is the **effective helical ripple** $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3/2}$, which in the $1/\nu$ regime governs the transport through:

$$
D_{11} \;\propto\; \varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3/2} \frac{v_{th}^2}{\nu},
$$

with $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}$ defined by a bounce average over all trapped particles. W7-X was optimized to make $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}$ small across the plasma volume; the payoff was measured directly in operation [1].

### 3.4 The optimization frameworks: STELLOPT and SIMSOPT

| Framework | Era / origin | Equilibrium | Objective capabilities | Differentiability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **STELLOPT** | 2000s, PPPL (Hirshman) | VMEC (+ DKES, TERPSICHORE, COBRA) | $\chi^2$ physics targets: $\iota$ profile, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}$, ballooning, kink | Finite-difference gradients |
| **SIMSOPT** | 2021, Simons Foundation [6] | VMEC, SPEC, near-axis | QS error, Greene's residue, coil objectives; modular | Adjoint-capable, JAX hooks |

**STELLOPT** performs Levenberg–Marquardt minimization of a weighted sum of physics residuals; it was the workhorse behind W7-X and NCSX coil sets, and its suite remains open-source [10]. **SIMSOPT** [6] is a modern, modular Python framework in which objective-function components are reusable across fixed-boundary, free-boundary, single-stage, and permanent-magnet optimizations; the plasma and coil stages can even be optimized *simultaneously* (single-stage optimization), as demonstrated for QA and QH configurations.

### 3.5 Coil design as a regularized inverse problem

Given a target boundary, the coil problem is: choose filamentary curves $\mathbf{x}_k(\ell)$ and currents $I_k$ to minimize

$$
f_{\mathrm{coil}} = \int_S (\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{coils}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})^2\, dS + \lambda \sum_k \left( \text{curvature}_k, \text{length}_k, \text{coil--coil distance}^{-1} \right),
$$

regularized because the Biot–Savart inverse problem is ill-posed: unregularized, the optimizer produces impossibly convoluted coils. Modern codes (FOCUS, and SIMSOPT's coil modules) parameterize coils as Fourier series or splines and apply curvature and length penalties, trading field accuracy $\langle B_n \rangle$ against engineering feasibility. HSX coil redesigns using spline representations achieved lower normal-field error with straighter outboard sections, demonstrating how much margin exists even for built devices.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Garren–Boozer near-axis construction

Garren and Boozer (1991) showed that quasisymmetry can be imposed order-by-order in an expansion about the magnetic axis. Writing the axis curve $\mathbf{r}_0(\phi)$ with curvature $\kappa$ and torsion $\tau$, the field strength to first order is:

$$
B \approx B_0 \left[ 1 + r\,\bar{\eta}\,\cos(\theta - N\phi) \right],
$$

where $\bar{\eta}$ measures the elliptical shaping and $N$ the field-period helicity. Requiring the $O(r)$ symmetry-breaking modes to vanish reduces to a **one-dimensional nonlinear ODE** for the axis shape, parameterized by the on-axis current density and the symmetry class [2]. Landreman, Sengupta, and Plunk extended this to higher order, demonstrating the first numerical realizations of Garren and Boozer's ideal scaling: symmetry-breaking amplitudes $\propto (\text{inverse aspect ratio})^3$ [3].

A subtlety — the **Garren–Boozer conundrum** — states that globally quasisymmetric magnetohydrostatic equilibria with isotropic pressure cannot exist beyond axisymmetry, because the expansion becomes overdetermined at third order. Yet numerical optimizers routinely find configurations quasisymmetric to extraordinary precision (symmetry-breaking modes below $2\times 10^{-7}$ [3]). The resolution, developed by Rodríguez and Bhattacharjee, is that *anisotropic* pressure removes the overdetermination, and practical isotropic-pressure equilibria can be quasisymmetric to any accuracy required by confinement, even if not exactly so. The `pyAQSC` code (2025) now solves the anisotropic-pressure near-axis expansion to arbitrary order, opening a larger design space [3].

### 4.2 From symmetry error to transport: what is actually minimized

In practice, Stage-1 optimizers rarely target a symmetry label directly. The hierarchy of objectives is:

1. **QS residual:** $f_{QS} = \sum_{(m,n) \notin \mathrm{sym}} |B_{mn}|^2 / B_{00}^2$, the Boozer-spectrum symmetry-breaking power.
2. **Effective ripple:** $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3/2}$ from DKES, the direct transport proxy.
3. **Fast-particle metrics:** e.g., $\Gamma_c$, the fraction of alpha-particle energy lost, or orbit-width measures.
4. **Stability:** Mercier criterion, ballooning growth, kink eigenvalues.
5. **Coil feasibility proxies:** plasma–coil separation, boundary curvature.

Weights are tuned so that the optimizer does not sacrifice transport for marginal symmetry gains. A representative SIMSOPT fixed-boundary loop simultaneously drives VMEC equilibria toward QS (via BOOZ_XFORM spectra) and eliminates internal islands (via SPEC Greene's residues) [6].

### 4.3 Wendelstein 7-X: the quasi-isodynamic flagship

W7-X (major radius $R = 5.5$ m, minor radius $a = 0.53$ m, five field periods, 50 non-planar plus 20 planar superconducting coils) is the largest stellarator ever built and the culmination of the Garching optimization program begun in the 1980s. Its design targets included:

- **Low neoclassical transport** via quasi-isodynamicity, quantified by $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}$;
- **MHD stability** at $\langle\beta\rangle \approx 4$–$5\%$;
- **An island divertor** exploiting the $n/m = 5/5$ edge island chain for power and particle exhaust;
- **Good fast-particle confinement** of neutral-beam and (prospectively) alpha particles.

Operation phases OP1.2b and OP2.1 delivered the proof [1]: stable detachment with good exhaust, low impurity content, energy confinement times $\tau_E > 100$ ms sustained for tens of seconds, and — under pellet fueling with suppressed ion-temperature-gradient turbulence — $\tau_E$ often exceeding 200 ms. Crucially, the attained profiles *could not have existed* in a non-optimized device: the neoclassical losses would have exceeded the applied heating. This is the first direct experimental confirmation that magnetic-field optimization for transport works.

> **Theorem (W7-X design criterion):** A stellarator plasma is neoclassically reactor-relevant if $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3/2}$ is reduced by roughly two orders of magnitude relative to a classical $\ell = 2$ stellarator, so that the $1/\nu$ transport at reactor collisionality falls below the turbulent transport floor. W7-X satisfies this by construction; experiment confirmed the floor is now turbulent, not neoclassical [1, 9].

### 4.4 HSX and NCSX: the quasisymmetry experiments

The **Helically Symmetric Experiment (HSX)** at the University of Wisconsin–Madison ($R = 1.2$ m, 4 field periods) remains the only operating quasi-symmetric stellarator. Its quasi-helically symmetric (QHS) configuration was compared against "mirror" configurations in which auxiliary coils deliberately break the symmetry [7]. The results constitute the first transport-level demonstration of quasisymmetry:

- Stored energy up to **six times larger** in QHS than in mirror configurations;
- Momentum damping rates **three to four times lower**, consistent with neoclassical theory of flows in QS fields;
- Enhanced prompt loss of trapped particles when the mirror field is applied.

HSX also demonstrated large intrinsic plasma flows ($\sim 20$ km/s) in the symmetry direction without external momentum input — a unique QS property with implications for turbulence suppression — and remains a testbed for turbulence-optimization studies, where coil-current adjustments trade QS purity against trapped-electron-mode (TEM) growth rates [8].

The **National Compact Stellarator Experiment (NCSX)**, a quasi-axisymmetric design pursued at PPPL, was cancelled during construction in 2008 for cost and schedule reasons — a cautionary tale that elegant optimization must be paired with buildable coils and disciplined project management. Its QA physics basis survives in modern compact-QA design studies.

### 4.5 The coil problem and single-stage optimization

The classical two-stage approach can produce plasma shapes no reasonable coil set can reproduce — NCSX's difficulties were partly of this kind. **Single-stage optimization** closes the loop: the boundary shape and the coils are optimized *simultaneously*, with the vacuum field of the coils (not an idealized boundary) determining the equilibrium [6]. Recent SIMSOPT studies produced QA and QH configurations optimized against quasisymmetry, coil accuracy, robustness, and alpha-particle confinement metrics in one loop, and permanent-magnet stellarator designs extend the same mathematics to discrete dipoles. The frontier is **stochastic and robust optimization**: designing configurations whose good properties survive coil tolerances and assembly errors, because a reactor cannot depend on a single perfect build.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 W7-X: transport optimization confirmed

The headline experimental result of the decade in stellarator physics is the W7-X confirmation paper [1]: with turbulence suppressed by pellet fueling, confinement *exceeds* what neoclassical theory predicts for an unoptimized stellarator by the margin the optimization was designed to deliver. Supporting evidence includes:

- **Island divertor operation:** stable detachment, the prerequisite for steady-state power handling, demonstrated over tens of seconds;
- **Impurity screening:** generally good plasma purity, though turbulence-suppressed phases show longer impurity confinement — a neoclassical signature now under active study;
- **Turbulence dominance:** gyrokinetic analysis (STELLA code) shows residual losses are ITG/TEM turbulence near marginality, with no sudden transition to strongly turbulent regimes [9] — meaning the neoclassical problem is solved and the remaining problem is the universal one shared with tokamaks.

### 5.2 HSX: quasisymmetry reduces transport

HSX's QHS-vs-mirror comparisons [7] remain the cleanest experimental proof that quasisymmetry per se improves confinement: identical machine, identical diagnostics, symmetry toggled by auxiliary coils. The factor-of-six stored-energy contrast and the momentum-damping measurements agree quantitatively with neoclassical predictions for QH fields, validating both the theory and the DKES-based design loop.

### 5.3 Numerical proofs of principle

- **Ideal QS scaling:** near-axis constructions demonstrate Garren–Boozer's predicted $(\text{aspect ratio})^{-3}$ scaling of symmetry-breaking modes [3].
- **Self-consistent bootstrap current:** new objective functions make the QS optimization consistent with the bootstrap current the plasma will actually carry, using the tokamak isomorphism and Redl's analytic formulas — removing a long-standing inconsistency in reactor-scale QS design [5].
- **Island elimination:** SIMSOPT+SPEC optimizations drive Greene's residues to zero, producing finite-$\beta$ equilibria with nested surfaces where unoptimized configurations are stochastic [6].
- **Alpha confinement:** optimized QA/QH configurations show alpha-energy losses far below those of earlier designs [5].

## 6 Limitations

No honest treatment can omit the caveats.

1. **Turbulence remains.** W7-X solved the neoclassical problem it was designed to solve — and revealed that turbulent transport (ITG/TEM) is the new ceiling [9]. Turbulence optimization (shaping to raise critical gradients or reduce growth rates) is far less mature than neoclassical optimization, and HSX studies show it can trade *against* quasisymmetry [8]. A reactor needs both.
3. **Coils are the hard part.** W7-X's 50 non-planar superconducting coils required unprecedented manufacturing precision; NCSX failed on cost and complexity. Single-stage and robust optimization mitigate this, but the coil–plasma gap remains the dominant engineering risk for any stellarator reactor.
4. **The bootstrap current problem.** Reactor-scale QS stellarators will carry significant bootstrap current, which modifies the field the coils were designed for. Self-consistent treatments [5] are recent and not yet experimentally validated at reactor $\beta$.
5. **Divertor and materials.** The island divertor works [1], but steady-state heat-flux handling at reactor power densities, tritium breeding in 3D geometry, and superconducting coil maintenance in a radioactive environment are unsolved at scale.
6. **Computational cost.** High-fidelity optimization (free-boundary VMEC + DKES + gyrokinetics in the loop) remains expensive; surrogate models and adjoint methods are active research, not production tools.

## 7 Conclusion

The stellarator optimization program is the most successful example of *computational design* in plasma physics: a device class whose confinement was once considered fundamentally inferior was, through quasisymmetry theory, neoclassical-transport optimization, and disciplined coil engineering, brought to parity with — and in steadiness, beyond — the tokamak. Wendelstein 7-X proved the optimization works [1]; HSX proved quasisymmetry works [7]; SIMSOPT and STELLOPT industrialized the design process [6, 10]; and near-axis theory continues to map the space of the possible [2, 3, 4].

The road to a stellarator reactor now runs through turbulence optimization, self-consistent current treatment [5], robust single-stage coil design, and the unglamorous engineering of divertors, blankets, and magnets. None of these is a physics showstopper of the kind neoclassical transport once was. The field that began as an elegant but lossy curiosity is now, by every experimental measure that matters, a credible reactor concept — designed, not discovered.
