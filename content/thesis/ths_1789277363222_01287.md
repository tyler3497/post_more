# Liquid Biopsy and Circulating Tumor DNA: Fragmentomics, Error-Suppressed Sequencing, and the Statistics of Minimal Residual Disease

## Abstract

Liquid biopsy promises to replace radiographic surveillance and invasive tissue re-biopsy with serial measurement of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), the tumor-derived fraction of cell-free DNA in plasma. Fulfilling this promise requires solving an extreme signal-to-noise problem: in minimal residual disease (MRD), tumor molecules can constitute fewer than one in a million circulating genomes, far below the raw per-base error rate of next-generation sequencing. This review integrates the three pillars that define the modern field. *Fragmentomics* exploits the biology of cell death: apoptotic cleavage biased by nucleosome positioning leaves characteristic size distributions, end motifs, and genome-wide fragmentation patterns that enrich tumor signal and permit tissue-of-origin inference. *Error-suppressed sequencing* -- molecular barcoding, duplex consensus, and phased-variant enrichment -- drives analytical background from 10^-3 toward 10^-8 per position. *Statistical detection theory* then maps technical sensitivity to clinical sensitivity through Poisson sampling models, limiting-dilution validation, and binomial power calculations. We present the governing theorems, the landmark empirical results, and the open problems -- clonal hematopoiesis, hard sampling floors, and assay economics -- separating today's ultrasensitive assays from routine clinical adoption.

## 1 Intro

The clinical management of cancer reduces, in practice, to a small set of repeated questions: *is disease present, how much of it is there, where did it come from, and is the current therapy working?* For a century the answers came from tissue: invasive, spatially biased, taken once. Tumors are temporally dynamic and spatially heterogeneous, so a single core sample is a poor summary of the disease state [6].

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) offered a way out. When cells die -- overwhelmingly by apoptosis -- nucleases digest chromatin and the surviving protein-protected fragments spill into plasma, where they circulate with a short half-life before clearance [4]. In a patient with cancer, a fraction of these fragments derives from tumor cells: circulating tumor DNA. Because a blood draw is repeatable and minimally invasive, ctDNA in principle supports serial monitoring of tumor burden, genotyping without re-biopsy, and -- most consequentially -- detection of minimal residual disease after curative-intent therapy, when intervention might still change the outcome.

Three obstacles stand between the principle and the practice:

- **Rarity.** In MRD, the tumor fraction can fall below 10^-5: fewer than one tumor molecule per hundred thousand normal genomes.
- **Error.** Raw next-generation sequencing miscalls roughly one base in a thousand, well above the signal.
- **Provenance.** A correctly read mutant molecule may come from clonal hematopoiesis rather than the tumor, and a mutation alone says little about tissue of origin.

This review treats the field as the joint solution of these three problems: fragmentomics recovers signal from the *biology* of the fragment, error-suppressed sequencing from the *chemistry* of the read, and statistical theory from the *mathematics* of rare-event detection.

## 2 Background

The size distribution of plasma cfDNA is not arbitrary. Paired-end sequencing shows a dominant peak at approximately 167 bp -- the length of DNA wrapped around a chromatosome (nucleosome plus linker histone) -- with a reproducible ~10.4 bp periodicity reflecting the helical pitch of nucleosome-bound DNA [4]. Snyder and colleagues defined the *windowed protection score* (WPS), the number of fragments completely spanning a 120 bp window minus the number with an endpoint inside it, and used it to call on the order of ten million nucleosome positions genome-wide, with a modal inter-peak distance of 185 bp matching the mammalian nucleosome repeat length [4]. The inferred occupancy correlated most strongly with lymphoid and myeloid epigenomes -- consistent with hematopoietic apoptosis as the dominant cfDNA source -- while short fragments directly footprinted transcription-factor occupancy [4]. In cancer patients, the same analysis revealed additional non-hematopoietic contributions, often matching the anatomical origin of the tumor [4]. Fragmentation was therefore, from the start, both a biological signal and a diagnostic one.

The first generation of clinical ctDNA assays was nevertheless mutation-centric. Cancer Personalized Profiling by deep Sequencing (CAPP-Seq) used hybrid-capture selector panels targeting recurrently mutated regions, detecting ctDNA in 100% of stage II-IV and 50% of stage I non-small-cell lung cancers with 96% specificity down to mutant allele fractions near 0.02% [5]. Integrated digital error suppression (iDES) layered molecular barcodes and in silico polishing onto this design [1]. But mutation-centric assays hit a ceiling: at low tumor fractions there are too few informative molecules, and the per-base background of even barcoded sequencing (~3.3 x 10^-5 for iDES-enhanced CAPP-Seq) [1] swamps the signal.

Fragment size itself turned out to be informative. Mouliere and colleagues showed, using personalized capture sequencing of matched tumor alterations, that mutant ctDNA fragments are systematically *shorter* than wild-type counterparts, and that the proportion of fragments below 150 bp discriminated high-shedding cancers (breast, melanoma, ovarian, lung, colorectal, cholangiocarcinoma) from low-shedding ones (renal, bladder, pancreatic, glioma) and from healthy controls [2]. Size selection for short fragments therefore enriches tumor content before a single base is read. Cristiano and colleagues pushed this logic genome-wide: their DELFI approach combined fragmentation profiles across the genome, noting that a milliliter of plasma contains only ~2,000 genome equivalents -- so the limit of detection of any single alteration is bounded near one in a few thousand -- and that Monte Carlo simulations show the limit improves dramatically as interrogated abnormalities grow from a few to tens or hundreds [3]. End-motif analysis added another axis: cancer patients exhibit greater end-motif diversity, and preferred cleavage coordinates are non-random, tissue-informative features [7].

ctDNA testing remains limited by assay sensitivity in early-stage disease, high implementation cost, confounding from clonal hematopoiesis, and a still-maturing base of clinical-utility evidence [6]. The rest of this review is about how the field has attacked the sensitivity term.

## 3 Methodology

A modern ultrasensitive ctDNA workflow is a pipeline of individually optimized stages, each with its own error budget:

1. **Preanalytics.** Plasma is strongly preferred over serum, because leukocyte lysis during clotting contaminates serum with genomic DNA; EDTA blood should be processed within hours, or collected in stabilizing tubes [6].
2. **Extraction and library preparation.** Conventional double-stranded preparation under-recovers short and damaged molecules; single-stranded protocols adapted from ancient-DNA work recover the 50-120 bp fraction carrying transcription-factor footprints [4].
3. **Target enrichment.** Hybrid-capture selector panels concentrate sequencing on recurrently mutated or patient-specific regions, balancing reporter breadth against unique molecular depth.
4. **Barcoding and consensus.** Unique molecular identifiers tag each input duplex; reads sharing a barcode collapse into single-strand then duplex consensus sequences.
5. **Feature calling.** SNVs, phased variants, indels, fragment sizes, end motifs, and copy-number signals are extracted jointly.
6. **Background modeling.** Per-position, per-context error rates estimated from healthy-control plasma enable calibrated detection thresholds.
7. **Longitudinal MRD calling.** Tumor fraction is estimated per timepoint; positivity declared against a pre-specified false-positive budget; kinetics tracked across therapy.

The decisive comparison is the analytical background each chemistry achieves. Measured on cfDNA from healthy adults [1]:

| Method | Error suppression | Background per base | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Raw NGS | none | ~10^-3 | polymerase + instrument error |
| Barcode deduplication | UMIs | intermediate | single-strand consensus |
| iDES-CAPP-Seq [5] | UMIs + in silico | 3.3 x 10^-5 | SNV background |
| Duplex sequencing | both strands | 1.2 x 10^-5 | recovers only 19% of unique fragments [1] |
| PhasED-Seq, doublets [1] | phased variants | 8.0 x 10^-7 | no UMIs required |
| PhasED-Seq, triplets [1] | phased variants | 3.4 x 10^-8 | background falls with inter-SNV distance |

The table already suggests the central design tension: duplex sequencing buys the lowest single-variant background but sacrifices most input molecules, while phased-variant detection achieves still lower background *without* sacrificing molecular recovery. The sampling floor behind all of this is pure Poisson statistics and can be computed before any sequencing is purchased:

```python
import math

def detection_probability(tumor_fraction: float, genome_equivalents: int) -> float:
    # P(observe >= 1 tumor molecule) under Poisson sampling.
    return 1.0 - (1.0 - tumor_fraction) ** genome_equivalents

def required_input(tumor_fraction: float, target_power: float = 0.95) -> float:
    # Genome equivalents needed for target detection power.
    return math.log(1 - target_power) / math.log(1 - tumor_fraction)

# At f = 1e-5, 95% power needs ~300,000 genome equivalents.
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Fragmentomics and the Nucleosome Architecture of cfDNA

Apoptotic caspase-activated DNase cleaves the linker DNA between nucleosomes, so the fragment population is a shadow of chromatin structure. Three signatures have proven diagnostic. First, the *size distribution*: the 167 bp chromatosome peak with 10.4 bp substructure in healthy plasma, and a tumor-associated shift toward sub-150 bp fragments that permits physical enrichment of ctDNA by size selection [2, 4]. Second, *end motifs and preferred ends*: cleavage is non-random, end-motif diversity increases in cancer, and tissue-specific end signatures have been demonstrated in transplantation and pregnancy models [7]. Third, *genome-wide fragmentation patterns*: DELFI-style features aggregate thousands of weak regional signals into a single classifier, exploiting the logic that many mediocre reporters beat a few excellent ones [3]. Machine-learning integration of these features now supports concrete tasks such as distinguishing benign from malignant renal masses where imaging is ambiguous and mutation burden is low [7]. The fragment is a *molecule with a shape*, not merely a string of bases, and tumor-derived shapes differ systematically from hematopoietic ones.

### 4.2 Error-Suppressed Sequencing and Phased Variants

Sequencing error is dominated by independent per-base miscalls at rate epsilon ~ 10^-3. Molecular barcoding collapses PCR duplicates into a single-strand consensus; duplex sequencing goes further by requiring agreement between the two original strands, driving error toward epsilon^2 at the cost of discarding molecules for which both strands are not recovered -- in practice only ~19% of unique cfDNA fragments [1]. Phased-variant enrichment and detection sequencing (PhasED-Seq) takes a different route: instead of demanding cleaner reads of *single* variants, it demands *two or more* somatic variants in cis on the same DNA molecule [1]. Under independent errors, doublet background scales as the product of single-error rates: the measured doublet background (8.0 x 10^-7) is ~40-fold below the iDES SNV background and the triplet background (3.4 x 10^-8) lower still -- achieved without UMIs and without the duplex recovery penalty, with phased variants within ~20 bp recovered at essentially full sample depth and those up to 80 bp apart at over half the median unique depth [1]. The approach is particularly potent in B-cell lymphomas, where aberrant activation-induced cytidine deaminase activity generates clustered mutations that serve as ready-made phased reporters [8]. *Correlation is the cheapest error correction*: requiring two linked events converts a per-base problem into a per-molecule problem.

![Duplex and phased-variant error suppression workflow](/thesis/ths_1789277363222_01287-1.webp)

### 4.3 Statistical Detection Theory at the Molecular Floor

Let *N* be the number of unique input genome equivalents, *f* the tumor fraction, *K* the number of independent phased-variant reporters, and *b* the per-reporter background rate in healthy controls. The expected number of true tumor observations is approximately *N f K*, and the expected background is *N b K*; detection is feasible when the former exceeds the latter by a margin set by the desired false-positive rate. In practice the MRD threshold is calibrated empirically: background phased-variant rates measured across healthy donors define the null distribution, and a sample is called positive only when its phased-variant burden exceeds a quantile chosen to control the per-sample false-positive rate. Critically, adding reporters is statistically cheaper than adding depth: doubling *K* halves the required *N* for fixed power, which is why tumor-informed panels tracking dozens of patient-specific phased variants outperform fixed hotspot panels at equal sequencing cost [1]. Limiting-dilution experiments bear this out: PhasED-Seq significantly outperformed CAPP-Seq (p = 1.6 x 10^-4 doublets, 1.9 x 10^-5 triplets) and duplex sequencing (p = 0.017, 0.0046), with ROC analysis confirming the operating-point improvement over SNV-based calling [1].

![MRD detection statistics: ROC, limit of detection, and Poisson sampling](/thesis/ths_1789277363222_01287-2.webp)

### 4.4 Tumor-Fraction Estimation and Longitudinal MRD Kinetics

Detection is binary; management needs quantities. The tumor fraction *f* is estimated from phased-variant-supporting molecule counts divided by unique depth, corrected for reporter count and clonality -- subclonal reporters dilute the estimate and must be down-weighted or excluded. Longitudinally, ctDNA is a *kinetic* analyte: its short half-life means plasma levels track tumor burden with a lag of hours, so a post-operative sample that fails to clear, or a surveillance sample that converts to positive, reports a biological event close to real time [4, 6]. In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, PhasED-Seq improved recurrence prediction both during therapy and after its completion relative to SNV-based approaches, supporting risk-adapted trial designs in which MRD positivity after early cycles of chemoimmunotherapy triggers treatment intensification [1, 8]. The emerging grammar is therefore a trajectory -- clearance kinetics under therapy, molecular relapse preceding radiographic relapse, quantitative thresholds gating escalation or de-escalation -- not a binary call.

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

The argument rests on two mathematical facts and a body of measurement.

> Theorem: *The Poisson sampling floor.* For *N* unique input genome equivalents and tumor fraction *f*, the probability of observing at least one tumor-derived molecule is 1 - (1 - f)^N, approximately 1 - e^(-fN) for small *f*.
> *Proof.* Each genome equivalent is tumor-derived independently with probability *f*; the count is Binomial(*N*, *f*), so P(zero) = (1 - *f*)^N, and (1 - *f*)^N ~ e^(-fN). No chemistry alters this bound: at *f* = 10^-6, 95% power requires ~3 x 10^6 genome equivalents, roughly 1.5 liters of plasma at 2,000 GE/mL [3]. QED.

> Theorem: *The phased-variant background bound.* If per-base errors occur independently with rate epsilon, the background rate for a phased *k*-plet is at most on the order of epsilon^k, before barcode or duplex suppression.
> *Proof sketch.* A false doublet requires two independent miscalls at specified positions on the same molecule: probability ~epsilon^2; a false triplet, ~epsilon^3. Empirically, moving from SNV background (3.3 x 10^-5, iDES) to doublets (8.0 x 10^-7) to triplets (3.4 x 10^-8) follows this scaling, with residual correlation explaining the gap between bound and measurement [1]. QED.

The landmark empirical results, in order of the argument:

1. Deep cfDNA sequencing recovers ~10 million nucleosome positions with 185 bp modal spacing, and the inferred occupancy identifies the contributing cell types -- hematopoietic in health, tumor-matched in cancer [4].
2. Tumor-derived fragments are systematically shorter; the sub-150 bp fraction stratifies cancers by shedding rate and enriches ctDNA by size selection [2].
3. Genome-wide fragmentation features enable cancer detection from low-coverage sequencing, with sensitivity scaling in the number of interrogated regions [3].
4. Phased-variant sequencing cuts background ~40-fold (doublets) to ~1,000-fold (triplets) versus barcoded SNV sequencing while retaining full molecular recovery, and wins limiting-dilution comparisons against both CAPP-Seq and duplex sequencing at stringent significance [1].
5. Fragmentomic classifiers extend detection to low-shedding, low-mutation settings such as renal masses, where mutation-centric assays are weakest [7].

## 6 Limitations

- **Clonal hematopoiesis.** Age-related leukocyte mutations are sequence-identical to tumor mutations in plasma; without matched white-blood-cell sequencing they drive false positives [6].
- **The sampling floor is absolute.** At ~2,000 genome equivalents per milliliter of plasma [3], a 10 mL draw yields ~2 x 10^4 unique genomes -- a hard Poisson floor near *f* ~ 10^-4 for single-reporter assays; 10^-6 demands far more blood, many reporters, or both.
- **Heterogeneous shedding.** Low-shedding cancers (renal, bladder, pancreatic, glioma) and sanctuary sites behind the blood-brain barrier release little ctDNA, so sensitivity is intrinsically tumor-type dependent [2].
- **Tumor-informed versus tumor-naive tradeoffs.** Patient-specific panels maximize sensitivity but require tumor tissue and weeks of design, and miss emergent subclones; fixed panels are fast and tissue-free but dilute power across irrelevant reporters.
- **Depth economics.** Unique depths of 4,000-6,000x over megabase-scale selectors are expensive; the cost curve, not the physics, gates population-scale deployment [6].
- **Preanalytical and library bias.** Time-to-processing, serum-versus-plasma choice, and library chemistry all reshape the fragment population before analysis begins [4, 6].
- **Clinical utility evidence.** Most MRD studies are retrospective; prospective trials randomizing therapy on MRD status -- the bar for changing practice -- are still maturing [6, 8].

## 7 Conclusion

Liquid biopsy has converged on a coherent technical stack: read the *shape* of the fragment to enrich tumor signal, require *linked* variants to suppress error, and calibrate every claim against Poisson sampling and healthy-control background. The numbers now on the table -- backgrounds approaching 10^-8 per reporter, molecular recovery near 100%, detection limits in the low parts per million -- would have read as fantasy a decade ago. What remains is the harder half of the problem: proving, in prospective trials, that acting on molecular residual disease -- escalating therapy when ctDNA persists, de-escalating when it clears -- improves outcomes rather than merely forecasting them. The physics of detection is largely solved; the medicine of decision is not.
