# Gravitational-Wave Data Analysis for Compact Binary Coalescence: Matched Filtering, Detector Noise Characterization, and Bayesian Parameter Estimation

## Abstract

The direct detection of gravitational waves has made the extraction of astrophysical information from interferometric strain data as consequential as detector operation itself. This thesis treats the three pillars of compact binary coalescence (CBC) data analysis: **matched filtering** as the optimal detection statistic for known waveform morphologies; **detector noise characterization**, spanning power spectral density estimation, glitch classification, and mitigation; and **Bayesian parameter estimation**, which inverts calibrated strain into posteriors over masses, spins, distance, and orientation. Anchored by GW150914 [1] and the GWTC-3 catalog [2], we derive matched filtering from the Neyman–Pearson lemma, develop template-bank geometry [3], survey non-Gaussian noise phenomenology and the BayesWave framework [4], and formulate parameter estimation as stochastic Bayesian inference. Empirical validation shows modern pipelines recover source parameters with percent-level systematics [5]; waveform systematics, noise non-stationarity, and third-generation computational cost define the frontier.

## 1. Introduction

On 14 September 2015 at 09:50:45 UTC, the twin interferometers of the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO) in Hanford, Washington and Livingston, Louisiana recorded a transient strain signal sweeping upward in frequency from approximately 35 Hz to 250 Hz, with peak strain of order $10^{-21}$ [1]. Designated GW150914, the signal matched the general-relativistic prediction for the inspiral, merger, and ringdown of a pair of stellar-mass black holes, achieving a matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio of 24 and a false-alarm rate below one event per 203,000 years — a significance exceeding $5.1\sigma$. The source-frame component masses, $36^{+5}_{-4}\,M_\odot$ and $29^{+4}_{-4}\,M_\odot$, radiating $3.0^{+0.5}_{-0.5}\,M_\odot c^2$ in gravitational energy [1], established not only the first direct detection of gravitational waves but also the existence of binary stellar-mass black hole systems merging within a Hubble time.

An interferometer with 4 km arms measures differential arm-length changes of order $10^{-18}$ m — roughly one thousandth the diameter of a proton — buried in noise spanning ten orders of magnitude across the audio band [1]. A compact binary imprints a *deterministic chirp morphology* on this noise, encoding chirp mass, mass ratio, spins, and distance. This determinism is the analyst's lever. Where the noise is Gaussian and stationary, the **matched filter** — the time-reversed, noise-weighted correlation of the data with theoretical waveform templates — is provably the optimal detector of known signals in the Neyman–Pearson sense [6]. Where the noise departs from Gaussianity, as it emphatically does in real interferometers, an entire secondary science of **noise characterization** determines whether a candidate is astrophysical or instrumental. And where a candidate survives, **Bayesian parameter estimation** converts the measured strain into the posterior distributions that constitute the actual scientific product: masses, spins, inclination, sky position, luminosity distance, and redshift [5].

These three pillars form one statistical pipeline sharing a common substrate: the *noise-weighted inner product*

$$\langle a\,|\,b \rangle = 4\,\Re \int_{0}^{\infty} df\,\frac{\tilde{a}^*(f)\,\tilde{b}(f)}{S_n(f)},$$

where $S_n(f)$ is the one-sided power spectral density (PSD) of the detector noise. The matched filter is this inner product evaluated as a time series; the PSD estimate that defines it is the object of noise characterization; and the likelihood function that drives Bayesian parameter estimation is the Gaussian exponential of the residual norm $\langle d - h(\theta)\,|\,d - h(\theta) \rangle$ [5]. A deficiency in any one pillar propagates directly into the others: an inaccurate PSD biases detection statistics and posteriors; an unmitigated glitch corrupts the likelihood surface; an inadequate template bank loses sensitivity to whole regions of the astrophysical parameter space.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Gravitational waves from compact binaries

In linearized general relativity, gravitational radiation is a propagating perturbation of the spacetime metric whose far-field amplitude from a compact source is governed by the quadrupole formula.

$$h(t) = \frac{4}{D}\left(\frac{G\mathcal{M}_c}{c^2}\right)^{5/3}\left(\frac{\pi f(t)}{c}\right)^{2/3}\cos\Phi(t),$$

where $\mathcal{M}_c = (m_1 m_2)^{3/5}/(m_1+m_2)^{1/5}$ is the **chirp mass**, $f(t)$ the instantaneous gravitational-wave frequency, $\Phi(t)$ the accumulated phase, and $D$ the luminosity distance.

$$\dot{f} = \frac{96}{5}\pi^{8/3}\left(\frac{G\mathcal{M}_c}{c^3}\right)^{5/3}f^{11/3},$$

reveals why the chirp mass is the best-measured intrinsic parameter: it controls the phase evolution at the lowest PN order and therefore accumulates over the largest number of observed wave cycles.

> **Theorem (Neyman–Pearson):** Among all decision rules for distinguishing a simple null hypothesis $H_0: d = n$ from a simple alternative $H_1: d = n + h$ with false-alarm probability fixed at $\alpha$, the likelihood-ratio test — which for Gaussian noise reduces to the matched-filter statistic — maximizes the detection probability.

### 2.2 The interferometric measurement

Advanced LIGO is a modified Michelson interferometer with 4 km Fabry–Pérot arm cavities and power recycling.

The noise budget is frequency-dependent and approximately stationary only on short timescales:

| Frequency band | Dominant noise source | Physical origin |
|---|---|---|
| < 10 Hz | Seismic / Newtonian | Ground motion, gravity gradients |
| 10–50 Hz | Seismic + suspension thermal | Pendulum suspensions, scattered light |
| 50–200 Hz | Coating/suspension thermal | Brownian motion of mirror coatings |
| 200–1000 Hz | Quantum shot noise | Photon counting statistics |
| > 1 kHz | Quantum shot noise | Laser power, squeezed-light injection |

The design sensitivity targets a strain PSD of order $S_n^{1/2} \sim 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{Hz}^{-1/2}$ near 100 Hz.

### 2.3 Statistical detection theory

Detection is a hypothesis-testing problem on the data vector $d$.

$$\log \Lambda = \langle d\,|\,h \rangle - \tfrac{1}{2}\langle h\,|\,h \rangle.$$

Maximizing over the unknown overall amplitude and arrival phase yields the familiar **matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio** $\rho(t) = |z(t)|$, where the complex filter output

$$z(t) = (d|h_R)(t) + i\,(d|h_I)(t),\qquad \rho(t) = \sqrt{(d|h_R)^2(t) + (d|h_I)^2(t)}$$

is the noise-weighted correlation of the data against the real and imaginary (quadrature) phases of a normalized template [6].

### 2.4 Bayesian inference

Parameter estimation frames the inverse problem probabilistically.

$$p(\theta\,|\,d) = \frac{\mathcal{L}(d\,|\,\theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{\mathcal{Z}},\qquad \mathcal{L}(d\,|\,\theta) \propto \exp\!\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle d - h(\theta)\,|\,d - h(\theta)\rangle\right],$$

with prior $\pi(\theta)$ and evidence $\mathcal{Z} = \int \mathcal{L}\,\pi\,d\theta$.

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology unifies detection, characterization, and estimation into a single computational pipeline whose stages are described below.

### 3.1 Matched-filter search architecture

A CBC search proceeds in five stages:

1. **Data conditioning.** The calibrated strain is high-passed (typically at 15–20 Hz), downsampled, and divided into overlapping analysis segments. Data-quality flags veto segments containing known instrumental disturbances.
2. **PSD estimation.** The one-sided noise PSD $S_n(f)$ is estimated from neighboring quiet data, commonly with a median-based Welch periodogram or the Bayesian BayesLine algorithm that models the spectrum as a smooth spline plus Lorentzian lines [4].
3. **Whitening and filtering.** Each template $h$ in the bank is correlated against the whitened data $\tilde{d}_w(f) = \tilde{d}(f)/\sqrt{S_n(f)}$ via fast Fourier transforms. Because the same data must be correlated against $\mathcal{O}(10^5$–$10^6)$ templates, the dominant cost is $\mathcal{O}(N_{\mathrm{templates}} \cdot N \log N)$ [10].
4. **Trigger generation and signal-consistency tests.** Local maxima of $\rho(t)$ above threshold are recorded as triggers. Each trigger is subjected to the **$\chi^2$ veto**, which compares the accumulated SNR across $p$ frequency sub-bands against the expectation for a true chirp; a glitch deposits power in a morphology inconsistent with the template and is down-weighted or rejected [6].
5. **Coincidence and background estimation.** Single-detector triggers are required to be coincident in time, template parameters, and phase across detectors. The surviving candidates are ranked by a detection statistic (e.g., reweighted SNR), and the background distribution is measured by time slides to assign false-alarm rates [9].

### 3.2 Template-bank geometry

Because the source parameters are unknown *a priori*, the search must cover a continuous parameter space with a discrete set of templates while bounding the worst-case loss of SNR.

$$1 - \mathcal{M}(h(\theta), h(\theta + \Delta\theta)) \simeq \tfrac{1}{2}\,g_{ij}(\theta)\,\Delta\theta^i \Delta\theta^j,$$

where the **template-space metric** $g_{ij}$ is derived from the noise-weighted inner product of waveform derivatives.

### 3.3 Bayesian noise characterization

We treat noise characterization as a model-selection problem with three competing hypotheses for each data segment: Gaussian noise alone, Gaussian noise plus an astrophysical signal, and Gaussian noise plus one or more glitches [4].

### 3.4 Bayesian parameter estimation

Parameter estimation evaluates the posterior $p(\theta|d)$ with stochastic samplers.

$$\mathcal{Z} = \int_0^1 \mathcal{L}(X)\,dX,\qquad X(\lambda) = \int_{\mathcal{L}(\theta)>\lambda}\pi(\theta)\,d\theta,$$

accumulating posterior samples as a by-product.

```python
import numpy as np

def matched_filter_snr(data_fd, template_fd, psd, df):
    """Noise-weighted matched-filter SNR time series (simplified).

    data_fd, template_fd : complex frequency-domain arrays
    psd                 : one-sided noise PSD evaluated at the same frequencies
    df                  : frequency resolution
    Returns the complex SNR time series z(t); rho(t) = |z(t)|.
    """
    # Whitened inner-product integrand: 4 * d*(f) h(f) / S_n(f)
    integrand = 4.0 * np.conj(data_fd) * template_fd / psd
    # Normalization: template auto inner product <h|h>
    norm = np.sqrt(4.0 * np.sum(np.abs(template_fd) ** 2 / psd) * df)
    z_time = np.fft.irfft(integrand, n=2 * (len(data_fd) - 1)) / norm
    return z_time  # rho(t) = np.abs(z_time)
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Waveform approximant families and numerical-relativity calibration

The template is the search's central scientific hypothesis, and its fidelity bounds everything downstream.

- **Post-Newtonian (PN) inspiral waveforms** (TaylorF2, TaylorT4): closed-form frequency-domain expressions valid while $v/c \ll 1$. TaylorF2 remains the workhorse for BNS searches, where the signal accumulates thousands of cycles in band and merger physics lies above the most sensitive frequencies.
- **Phenomenological IMR models** (IMRPhenomB/C/D, IMRPhenomXPHM): ansätze for the amplitude and phase across inspiral, merger, and ringdown, with coefficients fit to hybrid PN–NR waveforms. The "HM" and "XPHM" extensions add higher-order modes and generic precession [7].
- **Effective-one-body models** (SEOBNRv2/v4, SEOBNRv4PHM): resummations of the PN two-body Hamiltonian mapped onto an effective test-particle problem, with the merger–ringdown attached from NR-calibrated fits [8].

> **Theorem (Waveform indistinguishability):** Two waveforms $h_1, h_2$ are indistinguishable at signal-to-noise ratio $\rho$ if their mismatch $1 - \mathcal{M}(h_1,h_2) < 1/(2\rho^2)$. Systematic bias from approximant error is subdominant to statistical uncertainty only below this threshold.

Calibration to numerical relativity is the empirical anchor.

### 4.2 The matched-filter statistic, consistency tests, and background estimation

The raw matched-filter output $\rho(t)$ is necessary but not sufficient: real detector data contain glitches that produce large $\rho$ against chirp-like templates.

$$\hat{\rho} = \begin{cases}
\rho \left[ \tfrac{1}{2}(1 + (\chi^2_r)^3) \right]^{-1/6}, & \chi^2_r > 1, \\
\rho, & \chi^2_r \le 1,
\end{cases}$$

down-weights triggers inconsistent with the expected signal morphology [6].

Background estimation must be empirical because glitch populations are not analytically tractable.

An emerging direction replaces explicit template banks with learned surrogates: deep-learning "template prediction model banks" that infer the latent template embedded in the strain and predict the matched-filter SNR directly, achieving real-time analysis at a fraction of the FFT cost while retaining the statistical interpretability of the SNR statistic [10].

### 4.3 Detector noise characterization: PSD estimation, glitch taxonomy, and mitigation

The PSD is not a fixed instrumental constant but a slowly varying stochastic estimate, and its accuracy directly weights every inner product in the analysis.

$$S_n(f) = S_{\mathrm{spline}}(f) + \sum_k \frac{A_k}{1 + ((f - f_k)/\gamma_k)^2},$$

with the number of spline knots and lines sampled by reversible-jump MCMC.

Non-Gaussian transients — **glitches** — are the principal background.

Mitigation follows a hierarchy of invasiveness:

1. **Gating:** smooth inverse-Tukey windowing of the strain around loud glitches, discarding contaminated seconds. Simple and robust, but destroys any overlapping signal.
2. **Inpainting:** projection of the data onto the subspace orthogonal to the corrupted interval, preserving the Gaussian-noise statistics of the remainder. Applied to glitch-contaminated tests of general relativity, inpainting eliminates false violations without introducing new artifacts [12].
3. **Glitch model subtraction:** joint Bayesian inference of signal plus glitch wavelets (BayesWave), subtracting the maximum-a-posteriori glitch reconstruction. This is the standard LVK **deglitching** procedure: GW170817's analysis required subtraction of a loud glitch overlapping the Livingston data $\sim$1 s before merger, without which the BNS parameter estimates would have been severely biased [11].

The canonical validation experiment injects simulated BBH signals into real data containing blip, tomte, and koi-fish glitches, then compares posteriors before and after deglitching: broadband glitches induce moderate-to-significant posterior bias, narrowband glitches negligible bias, and BayesWave subtraction restores unbiased posteriors in the broadband case [11].

// Reweighted detection statistic: down-weight triggers failing the chi-squared

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Detection of GW150914: the matched filter in practice

GW150914 provides the canonical end-to-end validation of the matched-filter pipeline.

### 5.2 Glitch mitigation: GW170817 and injection studies

The binary neutron star merger GW170817 arrived 1.1 s after a loud glitch in the Livingston detector — a textbook case of signal–glitch overlap.

### 5.3 Catalog-scale consistency: GWTC-3

The third Gravitational-Wave Transient Catalog (GWTC-3) reports 90 compact-binary coalescence events across the O1–O3 observing runs, each with full posterior samples produced by the Bayesian parameter-estimation machinery of Section 4.4 [2].

### 5.4 Computational scaling and acceleration

The matched-filter search cost scales as $\mathcal{O}(N_{\mathrm{templates}} \cdot N \log N)$ per analysis segment.

### 5.5 Formal results

We collect the key theoretical guarantees as propositions:

> **Proposition 1 (Matched-filter optimality):** For stationary Gaussian noise with known PSD, no statistic achieves a higher detection probability at fixed false-alarm probability than the matched-filter SNR maximized over template phase and arrival time. *Proof.* The log-likelihood ratio is monotone in $\rho$; apply Neyman–Pearson [6].

> **Proposition 2 (Minimal-match coverage):** A bank placed with minimal match $\mathrm{MM}$ guarantees that any signal in the covered parameter space suffers at most a fractional SNR loss of $1 - \mathrm{MM}$ (0.03 for the conventional 0.97). *Proof.* The mismatch $1 - \mathcal{M}$ is exactly the fractional loss of $\langle s|h\rangle^2/\langle h|h\rangle$ relative to the true waveform [3].

> **Proposition 3 (Glitch–signal Bayes factor scaling):** Under the BayesWave model, $\log \mathcal{B}_{S,G} = \mathcal{O}(N \log \mathrm{SNR})$ where $N$ is the number of wavelets required. Loud simple glitches ($N$ small) are therefore correctly classified as non-astrophysical regardless of SNR [4].

## 6. Limitations

**Waveform systematics.** Approximant error is the dominant systematic for loud events. Current IMR models approximate eccentricity, include higher-order modes only partially, and degrade for strongly precessing systems. The indistinguishability criterion ($\mathrm{mismatch} < 1/2\rho^2$) implies that for the $\rho \gtrsim 100$ events expected in the Einstein Telescope / Cosmic Explorer era, today's $10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$ mismatches would dominate the error budget [7][8].

**Noise non-stationarity and non-Gaussianity.** The Gaussian likelihood is an idealization. PSD drift, unmodeled glitch populations, and correlated inter-detector noise all violate the assumptions behind the matched filter and the Bayesian likelihood. Time-slide background estimation mitigates the detection side empirically, but parameter estimation has no equivalent safeguard: an unrecognized glitch overlapping a signal biases the posterior silently [11][12].

**Computational cost.** Full Bayesian parameter estimation requires days of wall-clock time per event on current hardware; the O4 event rate (roughly one detection per few days) already strains low-latency follow-up, and third-generation rates (hundreds of BNS per day) will make per-event nested sampling infeasible without radical acceleration via reduced-order models, GPU likelihoods, or amortized neural posterior estimation [5][10].

**Astrophysical priors.** Posteriors are prior-dependent at low SNR: the volumetric distance prior and mass/spin parameterizations shift marginal posteriors at a level comparable to statistical uncertainty — a dependence that must be reported, not hidden [5].

**Calibration uncertainty.** The strain calibration carries $\sim$1% amplitude and $\sim$1° phase uncertainty, propagated into posteriors by marginalizing over spline-parameterized calibration error models. While subdominant today, calibration will limit precision distance measurements as statistical errors shrink [1].

## 7. Conclusion

Gravitational-wave data analysis is the discipline that converts sub-proton-scale mirror displacements into astrophysics. Its three pillars — **matched filtering**, **noise characterization**, and **Bayesian parameter estimation** — form a single statistical edifice built on the noise-weighted inner product: the filter that detects, the PSD and glitch models that define the noise against which detection is judged, and the likelihood that inverts strain into posteriors over the physical parameters of merging compact binaries. The empirical record, from GW150914's $5.1\sigma$ chirp [1] through the deglitching of GW170817 [11] to the 90-event GWTC-3 catalog with full posterior samples [2], validates the methodology at scale: signals are found at the predicted false-alarm rates, glitches are identified and removed without corrupting astrophysical inference, and independent waveform families and independent pipelines converge on consistent source parameters.

The frontier is defined by the limitations of Section 6: third-generation detectors will deliver events at SNRs and rates that break current assumptions, demanding order-of-magnitude better waveform models, automated real-time glitch mitigation, and $10^2$–$10^3\times$ faster inference via surrogates and amortized methods [10]. The mathematical foundations reviewed here — the Neyman–Pearson optimality of the matched filter, the geometry of template banks, the Bayesian treatment of noise transients, and the hierarchical structure of posterior inference — will remain the framework within which those advances are built. The signal is deterministic; the noise is characterizable; and the inference, done carefully, is trustworthy. That is the thesis of gravitational-wave data analysis, and a decade of detections has confirmed it.
