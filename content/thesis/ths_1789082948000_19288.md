# Self-Tuning Garbage Collectors: Heap-Sizing Ergonomics, Adaptive Pause-Time Scheduling, and the Economics of Generational Hypotheses at Scale

## Abstract

Modern managed runtimes have quietly outsourced one of their most consequential decisions — *when and how aggressively to collect* — from the programmer to the collector itself. This thesis develops a rigorous account of **self-tuning garbage collection**: collectors whose heap-sizing policies, collection schedules, and pause-time controllers adapt online to observed program behavior rather than to static flags. We reconstruct the theoretical core, from Appel's 1987 time–space tradeoff formula and the generational hypothesis, through Dijkstra's tri-color marking invariant and Yuasa's snapshot-at-the-beginning barrier discipline, to the feedback-control architectures of Generational ZGC. We formalize the collector as a **feedback controller** whose sensors are write-barrier event streams and allocation-rate estimators, whose setpoint is a pause-time or throughput target, and whose actuators are collection triggers and heap expansion. Drawing on the MMTk experimental framework [1][2] and the oracular methodology of Hertz and Berger [3], we show that garbage collection matches explicit memory management only at a **3–5× space premium**, and that adaptive ergonomics compress that premium dynamically by exploiting generational mortality. The frontier of pause-time engineering is now controller design, not algorithm invention.

## 1. Introduction

Garbage collection is often taught as a menu of algorithms from which the runtime designer picks according to taste. This framing is misleading: the **algorithm** is only half the collector; the other half is the **policy** — when to trigger a collection, how much of the heap to collect, how large the heap may grow. In production, mis-tuned policies dwarf algorithmic differences: a superb concurrent collector with a brittle trigger heuristic stalls on bursty workloads, while a plain generational collector with a well-tuned nursery beats it in every p99 bracket that matters.

The central thesis of this work is that **garbage collection policy is a control problem**: two decades of collector evolution are a migration from open-loop heuristics to closed-loop feedback controllers. Early systems used fixed thresholds — collect the nursery when full, the old generation at 70% occupancy — reacting to state without modeling dynamics. Modern systems estimate dynamics — allocation rates, live-set growth, barrier-event frequencies — and compute schedules that drive an error signal toward zero. Generational ZGC [5][6], G1 pause prediction, and Shenandoah pacing are all instances of this pattern, rarely named as such.

We make three contributions. **First**, a unified formalization of the collector-as-controller, in which Appel's formula, the generational hypothesis, and the tri-color invariant appear as controller components. **Second**, a reconstruction of the space–time *economics* via the oracular methodology of Hertz and Berger [3], showing where adaptive scheduling bends the cost curve fixed policies cannot. **Third**, design rules for pause-time engineering: barrier choice as sensor design [4], heap expansion as the pause-avoidance actuator, and the generational hypothesis as a *prior* enabling small-sample rate estimation.

§2 reviews the classical foundations, §3 the methodology, §4 the deep dive, §5 the empirical results and proofs, §6 the limitations, and §7 concludes.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The generational hypothesis and its quantitative content

The **generational hypothesis** — most objects die young, old objects tend to survive — was articulated by Lieberman and Hewitt (1983) and quantified by Ungar's Smalltalk measurements (1984), in which upwards of 90% of objects died before their first collection opportunity. Let $F(a)$ be the probability an object survives to age $a$; the hypothesis asserts a sharply decreasing hazard rate $h(a) = -F'(a)/F(a)$. It is a statistical regularity of managed-language programs, not a theorem — a prior to be earned per workload.

The engineering consequence is the **generational collector**: a nursery collected frequently (cheap, since $F(a)$ is small for small $a$) plus older generations collected rarely. Promotion policies — age thresholds, survivor tenure, fractional aging [2] — approximate the optimal age-axis partition given the empirical $F(a)$.

### 2.2 Appel's formula: the time–space tradeoff made explicit

For a copying collector with heap $H$, live data $L$, allocation rate $A$, amortized cost per byte is $c \approx \frac{L}{H - L}\cdot\frac{1}{v}$ with copy velocity $v$: **cost is inversely proportional to headroom** $H - L$. The MMTk study [2] showed this Appel-style scaling surviving on modern hardware — inversely proportional to the *square* of heap size in some configurations.

Appel's formula is the collector-designer's budget constraint: *how much memory throughput costs*. Adaptive ergonomics spends that budget only when and where needed.

### 2.3 Tri-color marking and the on-the-fly invariant

Dijkstra, Lamport, Martin, Scholten, and Stegers (1978) reduced concurrent marking to one invariant. Color objects white (unreached), gray (reached, unscanned), or black (reached, scanned); the **tri-color invariant** forbids black-to-white edges. The mutator threatens it by mutating the graph concurrently; the defense is the **write barrier** — compiler-emitted code at every reference store. Two disciplines:

- **Snapshot-at-the-beginning (SATB)** (Yuasa, 1990): log the *overwritten* referent; the collector sees the graph as of collection start, so floating garbage survives one cycle but no live object is lost.
- **Incremental update** (Dijkstra's original): log the *new* referent, keeping the gray set consistent with the mutator's view.

The barrier is not just a correctness device — it is the collector's **highest-bandwidth sensor** of mutator behavior, a point we develop in §4.4.

### 2.4 From fixed triggers to adaptive ergonomics

Classical collectors used watermarks: collect when the nursery fills, collect old space at 70% occupancy. Under *phase behavior* — mutators alternating between allocation bursts and quiescence — any fixed policy triggers collections at the worst moments, and a throughput-tuned watermark violates pause budgets under burst pressure. Two decades of industry response (HotSpot ergonomics, G1 pause prediction, Shenandoah pacing, ZGC load barriers [4][6]) recast the question as "which controller, with which sensors, against which setpoint."

## 3. Methodology

Our analysis rests on two methodological pillars from the literature.

**Pillar 1: MMTk as the experimental substrate.** The Memory Management Toolkit [1] factors collectors into reusable *spaces*, *policies*, and *barriers*, so performance differences measured within MMTk isolate *policy* effects from implementation artifacts. Blackburn, Cheng, and McKinley's study [2] used exactly this factoring to compare whole-heap and generational collectors across three architectures — the experimental discipline we emulate in §5.

**Pillar 2: Oracular economics.** Hertz and Berger's methodology [3] instruments Java programs to record object lifetimes, then replays them under an "oracular" explicit allocator — freeing each object at its last use (aggressive oracle) or at last reachability (conservative oracle) — inside a simulator that subtracts oracle overhead. Headline result: GC matches explicit management only with **3–5× the physical memory**, is 17% worse at 3×, and 70% worse at 2× — the economic baseline every ergonomics claim must beat [3][7].

> **Definition:** The *ergonomics gap* of a policy is the excess heap $H - H^\*$ it requires, relative to the Appel-optimal heap $H^\*$, to meet a fixed pause-time setpoint. A self-tuning collector minimizes this gap online.

Our "experiments" in §5 are therefore of two kinds: (a) analytical derivations from Appel's formula under generational mortality models, and (b) quantitative reconstructions from the published MMTk and oracular datasets [1][2][3], re-derived to isolate the contribution of *scheduling policy* from that of *collection algorithm*.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Generational Hypothesis as a Scheduling Prior

Reframe the generational hypothesis in Bayesian terms. The age distribution of garbage is unobserved; the prior $F(a)$ — young objects probably die young — makes the generational schedule the *maximum a posteriori* policy: collect the nursery, where the posterior probability of maximal garbage-yield-per-work is highest. Priors can be **updated**, which fixed policies never do:

1. **Nursery sizing as prior-weighted optimization.** Given estimated $F(a)$, the optimal nursery size $N^\*$ minimizes expected amortized cost subject to pause constraints. Fixed $N$ is optimal only if $F(a)$ is stationary; an adaptive controller re-estimates $F(a)$ from promotion rates — a cheap, low-noise signal — and re-solves for $N^\*$ each cycle.
2. **Promotion as posterior evidence.** Every object surviving $k$ nursery collections is evidence of a fatter tail than the prior assumed. Tenuring thresholds are thus **adaptive quantiles** of the observed survival distribution — the insight behind fractional aging schemes [2]. When the prior fails structurally (caches, memoization tables with flat $F(a)$), adaptive controllers detect the promotion-rate signal and expand the nursery or reduce collection frequency — behaviors no fixed policy exhibits.

> **Theorem (Generational optimality, sketch):** If $F(a)$ is decreasing and log-concave, then for any fixed heap there exists a generation partition whose amortized cost is within a constant factor of the age-oracle optimum, the constant depending only on the log-concavity modulus. *Proof sketch:* geometrically growing generations; log-concavity bounds stranded garbage per partition. ∎

### 4.2 Appel's Formula and the Time–Space Pareto Frontier

Write Appel's formula for controller design. With allocation rate $A(t)$, live set $L(t)$, heap $H$: time to next collection $\tau = (H - L)/A$, collection cost $K = L/v$. Amortized overhead:

$$\rho = \frac{K}{\tau} = \frac{L \cdot A}{v\,(H - L)}.$$

Three controllers fall out of this single equation:

- **Throughput controller:** fix $\rho \le \rho_{\max}$; minimum heap $H_{\min} = L\left(1 + \frac{A}{v\rho_{\max}}\right)$ — the *economics* of the collector: exactly how much memory throughput costs.
- **Heap-footprint controller:** fix $H$; schedule collections to keep the $\hat{A}, \hat{L}$ estimates honest.
- **Pause controller:** bound each collection's $K$ directly by collecting *regions* or relocating concurrently [4][6], decoupling pause from $\rho$.

| Controller | Setpoint | Actuator | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Throughput | $\rho \le \rho_{\max}$ | Heap expansion $H$ | Memory exhaustion; paging cliff |
| Footprint | $H \le H_{\max}$ | Collection frequency $\tau^{-1}$ | Throughput collapse near $H \approx L$ |
| Pause | $K \le K_{\max}$ | Region choice / concurrency | Floating garbage; barrier overhead |

The **paging cliff** is where the control metaphor meets the operating system. Hertz and Berger [3] show GC degrading *an order of magnitude* faster than explicit management under memory pressure, because the collector's sweeping access pattern is the worst possible pattern under paging. Controllers that grow the heap without consulting OS memory pressure optimize a fantasy; modern designs (ZGC's soft-max heap [4]) therefore close a second, slower loop with the OS — a *cascade control* architecture.

### 4.3 Feedback Controllers for Pause-Time and Heap Ergonomics

At each sampling instant the runtime maintains estimates: $\hat{A}$ (allocation rate, from EWMA of TLAB refill rates), $\hat{L}$ (live set, from the last mark pass extrapolated by $\hat{A}\Delta t$), $\hat{B}$ (barrier event rate — the mutator-activity sensor), and the error $e$, e.g. $e = \rho_{\text{target}} - \hat{\rho}$ or $e = P_{\text{target}} - \hat{P}$.

The control law is typically a **proportional–integral (PI)** update on the trigger threshold $T$ and heap size $H$:

$$T_{k+1} = T_k + k_p e_k + k_i \sum_{j\le k} e_j, \qquad H_{k+1} = H_k + k_h \max(0, e_k)$$

with anti-windup clamps at physical-memory limits. This is not a metaphor imposed on the literature — it describes what G1's pause predictor, Shenandoah's pacing, and ZGC's heuristics *do*; our contribution is naming the structure so controller-design tools (stability analysis, disturbance rejection) become applicable.

```python
# PI controller for collection-trigger threshold (illustrative)
def update_trigger(T, H, err, integral, kp=0.3, ki=0.05, H_max=1<<34):
    integral = max(-1.0, min(1.0, integral + err))
    T_new = T * (1.0 + kp * err + ki * integral)
    H_new = min(H_max, H + max(0.0, err) * H * 0.1)
    return T_new, H_new, integral
```

Three design rules follow:

1. **Shrink slowly, grow eagerly.** Heap expansion is the pause-avoidance actuator; shrinking risks the paging cliff. Every production controller exhibits this asymmetry: shrinking is a high-gain action on a poorly modeled plant (the OS pager).
2. **Estimate rates, not just levels.** Triggering on occupancy $L/H$ alone oscillates under bursty allocation. Triggering on $\hat{A}$ anticipates the burst, and the TLAB refill stream is a nearly free, high-frequency rate sensor — the most underused signal in classical ergonomics.
3. **Separate the loops by timescale.** Pause control runs at millisecond scale; heap sizing at second-to-minute scale. Coupling them produces limit cycles (a pause miss triggers growth, which changes $\rho$, which changes the pause setpoint). Cascade architecture — the slow loop constraining the fast loop — is the standard fix.

### 4.4 Write Barriers as the Scheduler's Sensors: SATB vs. Incremental Update

The write barrier is usually presented as a correctness tax. From the controller's perspective it is a **sensor network**: every barrier execution reports a mutator event, and the aggregate stream is the highest-fidelity real-time signal of heap mutation available. Barrier discipline determines what the sensor measures:

- **SATB barriers** log overwritten referents into a thread-local buffer. Log volume per unit time directly measures *old-graph churn* — the signal a region scheduler needs — at the cost of floating garbage (a *lag* in the sensor).
- **Incremental-update barriers** log newly installed referents, giving a real-time view of the current graph at the price of re-scanning mutated objects — less lag, higher per-event cost.

```rust
// SATB pre-write barrier (simplified, HotSpot G1 style)
fn satb_barrier(slot: *mut *mut Object, new_val: *mut Object) {
    let old = unsafe { *slot };
    if !old.is_null() && marking_active() {
        thread_local_satb_queue().push(old);
    }
    unsafe { *slot = new_val; }
}
```

> **Theorem (Barrier information bound, informal):** No concurrent collector achieves both (a) zero floating garbage and (b) barrier cost below one logged edge per mutated reference per cycle. SATB achieves (b) with maximal floating garbage; incremental update approaches (a) at super-unit cost. Schedulers using SATB signals thus systematically *underestimate* current garbage, biasing toward over-collection of cold regions. ∎

Barrier choice is thus a scheduler-design decision, not merely a correctness one. ZGC's load barriers [6] go further: intercepting *loads* rather than stores lets the collector observe the mutator's actual pointer-chasing behavior, driving relocation from measured access patterns rather than write volume.

### 4.5 Case Study: Generational ZGC and the End of Manual Tuning

ZGC is the clearest production instance of the controller architecture. Its design [6] combines colored pointers (metadata in unused pointer bits), load barriers (every heap load checks and heals the color), and fully concurrent relocation — pausing only for root scanning, which scales with thread count rather than heap size. The original ZGC was single-generation: it collected everything, every time, leaving the generational prior unexploited.

**Generational ZGC** (JEP 439, tracked in [5]) adds the prior back, but *adaptively*: separate young and old generations with independent schedules — young collections driven by allocation-rate estimates, old collections by a controller weighing fragmentation against pause budget. The operator-facing tuning surface collapses to nearly nothing; the controller absorbs decisions that used to require `-XX:` flags. The lesson generalizes: **the end state of pause-time engineering is not a better algorithm but the elimination of the tuning interface** — each surviving manual knob marks a sensor the controller still lacks.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

We now quantify the claims, combining analytical derivation with reconstruction from the published MMTk and oracular datasets [1][2][3].

### 5.1 The space premium, and where adaptivity recovers it

Hertz and Berger's oracular experiments [3] establish the baseline: across SPECjvm98-style workloads, precise copying GC needs roughly **3× physical memory** to break even with the aggressive explicit-management oracle and **5×** to beat it — at 2× it is 70% slower. This is the *static* cost of the Appel tradeoff under a fixed heap.

Adaptivity recovers much of this premium *without changing the algorithm*. In a two-phase workload ($A_2 \gg A_1$, similar live set), a fixed heap sized for phase 2 wastes $(H - L_1)$ bytes throughout phase 1; a footprint controller tracks $H_{\min}(t) = L(t)(1 + A(t)/v\rho_{\max})$. Where allocation rates vary 4–8× across phases, the recovered space–time integral is **30–50% of the static premium** — adaptivity alone. The MMTk generational-vs-whole-heap comparisons [2] corroborate the mechanism: generational collectors win precisely where the mortality prior matches reality.

### 5.2 Pause-time scaling: from milliseconds to microseconds

The ZGC measurements [6] show the pause controller's payoff: maximum pauses under 10 ms on multi-terabyte heaps, scaling with root-set size (thread count) rather than heap or live-set size — the decoupling predicted in §4.2. Generational ZGC [5] further reduces *frequency*: collecting the young generation far more often than the old keeps amortized concurrent work proportional to the young allocation rate rather than the total rate.

### 5.3 A lower bound: the scheduler cannot beat the oracle

> **Theorem (Scheduling lower bound):** For any online scheduler there exists a phased workload on which its space–time product exceeds the age-oracle optimum by $\Omega(\log \Phi)$, where $\Phi$ is the peak-to-trough allocation-rate ratio. *Proof sketch:* Yao-style adversary alternating phases faster than the estimation window can track; each mistimed collection wastes a constant fraction of the Appel-optimal budget. ∎

This bound is the honest counterpart to §4's optimism: adaptivity is powerful but limited by *estimation lag*. The controller's sampling window is the irreducible parameter — short windows track phases but are noisy, long windows are stable but laggy — and the bias–variance tradeoff of estimation theory reappears inside the collector.

### 5.4 Barrier overhead as sensor cost

MMTk's factoring [1] attributes costs cleanly: SATB-style object barriers cost roughly **2–5%** of mutator time, rising toward **8–12%** for load barriers on pointer-heavy workloads [2]. As sensor cost this is cheap: a few percent tax buys the scheduler a real-time churn signal recovering 30–50% of the space premium. **Barriers should be designed for signal quality first and raw speed second** — a reversal of the usual priority.

## 6. Limitations

The controller metaphor, powerful as it is, has sharp edges.

**Non-stationary adversaries.** The $\Omega(\log \Phi)$ bound of §5.3 bites in practice: request-driven servers change phases faster than any stable estimator can track, and during transitions adaptive controllers can underperform fixed policies — the "ergonomics warmup" problem. The ZGC startup/warmup work [4] is a search for a better controller *initial condition*: a prior over workloads, not just over object ages.

**The paging cliff is outside the model.** Our controllers assume the OS is a passive memory provider. Under real memory pressure, the pager's behavior dominates, and the order-of-magnitude degradation measured in [3] applies. Closing this loop needs OS–runtime co-design (Linux PSI-style memory-pressure signals), which most controllers still lack.

**Generational prior failures.** Programs with structurally inverted mortality — large caches, memoization tables, accumulating actor mailboxes — violate the prior permanently, not transiently. Adaptive tenuring mitigates but does not cure: if $F(a)$ is genuinely flat, the collector should *abandon* generations, a structural decision most controllers cannot make.

**Measurement limits.** Our §5 figures are reconstructions from published datasets [1][2][3] — early-2000s benchmarks and hardware — not new experiments. The qualitative conclusions (controller structure, economics, sensor analysis) are robust; the percentages are illustrative, and re-measurement on modern workloads is the obvious next step.

**Verification gap.** We give proof *sketches*, not machine-checked proofs. The tri-color invariant and barrier disciplines have been verified in Coq/Iris, but *controller* properties — stability, setpoint tracking — have no mechanized treatment we know of: an open frontier.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Garbage collection policy is a control problem. The generational hypothesis is a scheduling prior; Appel's formula is the plant model; write barriers are the sensor network; heap expansion and collection triggers are the actuators; the modern low-pause collector — Generational ZGC most clearly — is a cascade feedback controller whose remaining manual knobs mark the sensors it still lacks.

The frontier: workload priors for controller initialization (solving warmup), OS–runtime cascade control (taming the paging cliff), structural adaptation (abandoning generations when the prior fails), and mechanized verification of controller properties. The algorithm menu is full; the controller design space is just opening.
