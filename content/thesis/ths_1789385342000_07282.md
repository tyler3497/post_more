# Decoding Distant Skies: Bayesian Atmospheric Retrieval from Exoplanet Transmission and Emission Spectroscopy and the Statistical Search for Biosignature Gases

## Abstract

Transmission and emission spectroscopy of transiting exoplanets has matured into precision molecular characterization. This thesis develops the mathematical and statistical foundations of atmospheric retrieval: inferring temperature structure, chemical abundances, cloud properties, and energy redistribution from wavelength-dependent transit depths and secondary-eclipse fluxes. We derive the transit radius integral, quantify the slant-optical-depth amplification at the terminator limb, and analyze the degeneracies linking reference pressure, radius, mixing ratios, and gray cloud opacity. We present the Bayesian retrieval machinery — parametric forward models with nested sampling and Markov chain Monte Carlo, compared via Bayesian evidence — and apply it to the JWST era: the 26-sigma carbon dioxide detection in WASP-39b, NIRCam water and metallicity constraints, and the contested dimethyl sulfide biosignature claims on K2-18b. We close with a decision-theoretic ladder for biosignature assessment, arguing that no single molecular detection constitutes evidence of life without contextual, geochemical, and statistical vetting.

## 1 Intro

When a planet transits its host star, a minute fraction of the starlight filters through the thin skin of gas at the planet's limb. That skin — an annulus of atmosphere perhaps a hundred kilometers thick on a planet ten thousand kilometers across — imprints molecular absorption lines onto the stellar spectrum at the level of parts per million. The remarkable claim of modern exoplanet science is that from these parts-per-million wiggles we can recover the temperature structure, the abundances of water, methane, carbon dioxide, and ammonia, the presence of clouds and hazes, and, ultimately, hints of gases that life might produce. This thesis is about how that claim is justified: the physics that encodes atmospheric information into spectra, and the statistics that decodes it.

The field's trajectory has been one of steadily increasing information content. Secondary-eclipse photometry with *Spitzer* first revealed day-side emission from hot Jupiters; Hubble's WFC3 grism delivered water-band transmission spectra at 1.4 microns; and JWST now resolves the vibrational band structure of multiple molecules across 0.6–12 microns in a single transit [7]. Each advance has been accompanied by a parallel maturation of the *retrieval* paradigm introduced by Madhusudhan and Seager [1]: rather than asking whether a particular self-consistent model fits the data, retrieval asks what the data themselves demand of the atmosphere.

We proceed as follows. Section 2 develops the radiative-transfer background of transit and eclipse geometry. Section 3 formalizes the retrieval inverse problem and its Bayesian solution. Section 4 dissects four deep problems: the analytic structure of the transit radius integral, cloud–haze and reference-pressure degeneracies, the competing merits of nested sampling and MCMC, and the chemistry of biosignature gases. Section 5 confronts the machinery with JWST-era data, Section 6 states the limitations honestly, and Section 7 concludes with a framework for the search for life.

> **Thesis claim:** *Transmission and emission spectra constrain atmospheric properties only through a Bayesian inversion whose degeneracies are geometric and physical, not merely statistical; the path from spectra to biosignatures is therefore a ladder of excluded alternatives, not a single detection.*

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Transit geometry and the scale height

A transit light curve measures the wavelength-dependent fractional dimming of the star,

$$\delta(\lambda) = \left(\frac{R_p(\lambda)}{R_*}\right)^2,$$

where $R_p(\lambda)$ is the *effective* planetary radius. The atmosphere contributes through the pressure scale height,

$$H = \frac{k_B T}{\mu m_u g}.$$ For a hot Jupiter ($T \approx 1500$ K, $g \approx 10$ m s$^{-2}$, $\mu \approx 2.3$), $H \approx 500$ km against a planetary radius of $70{,}000$ km: the atmosphere is a film one part in 140 of the radius. Spectral features arise because $R_p(\lambda)$ varies by a few scale heights between molecular bands and continuum windows, producing transit-depth modulations of order

$$\Delta\delta \sim \frac{2 n H R_p}{R_*^2},$$

with $n \approx 2$–$5$ the number of scale heights probed. For a typical hot Jupiter around a Sun-like star this is $100$–$1000$ parts per million — the fundamental reason the field demands space-based photometry.

### 2.2 Slant optical depth and the transit radius integral

The essential physics is contained in the *slant* optical depth along a chord through the limb atmosphere at impact parameter $b$:

$$\tau_s(\lambda, b) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \kappa(\lambda)\, \rho(s)\, ds,$$

where $s$ is the path coordinate and $\kappa$ the mass extinction coefficient. For an exponential atmosphere the chord integral evaluates to

$$\tau_s(\lambda, b) \approx \tau_{v}(\lambda)\, e^{-(b - R_0)/H} \sqrt{\frac{2\pi R_0}{H}},$$

with $\tau_v$ the vertical optical depth at the reference radius $R_0$. The geometric factor $\sqrt{2\pi R_0 / H}$ — typically $30$–$60$ for giant planets — is the slant amplification that makes transmission spectroscopy sensitive to trace absorbers [6].

The effective transit radius follows from the annulus-blocking integral

$$R_p^2(\lambda) = R_0^2 + 2\int_{R_0}^{\infty} b\left(1 - e^{-\tau_s(\lambda, b)}\right) db,$$

which, for the isothermal, isobaric case, collapses to the celebrated logarithmic form

$$R_p(\lambda) \approx R_0 + H \ln\!\left(\frac{\kappa(\lambda)}{\kappa_0}\right).$$

### 2.3 Emission spectroscopy and secondary eclipse

At secondary eclipse the planet passes behind the star, and the drop in flux measures the planet's own emission plus reflected starlight:

$$\frac{F_p}{F_*} = \left(\frac{R_p}{R_*}\right)^2 \frac{B_\lambda(T_p)}{B_\lambda(T_*)} + A_g(\lambda)\left(\frac{R_p}{a}\right)^2 \Phi(\alpha),$$

with $B_\lambda$ the Planck function, $A_g$ the geometric albedo, $a$ the orbital separation, and $\Phi$ the phase function. Emission spectra probe the *dayside* temperature profile rather than the terminator: absorption features appear where temperature decreases with altitude, while emission features betray a thermal *inversion*.

### 2.4 Opacities, chemistry, and aerosols

The forward problem requires molecular cross-sections as functions of wavelength, temperature, and pressure — line lists such as HITRAN and ExoMol tabulate billions of transitions for H$_2$O, CH$_4$, CO$_2$, CO, NH$_3$, HCN, and increasingly exotic species. Collision-induced absorption (H$_2$–H$_2$, H$_2$–He) provides the quasi-continuum in hydrogen-dominated atmospheres. Chemistry enters in two flavors:

- **Thermochemical equilibrium**, where abundances follow from minimization of Gibbs free energy given metallicity and C/O ratio — a compact, physically motivated parameterization.
- **Free (disequilibrium) chemistry**, where each species' mixing ratio is an independent parameter, agnostic to equilibrium assumptions but vastly expanding the parameter space.

| Molecule | Key bands (microns) | Diagnostic role |
|---|---|---|
| H$_2$O | 1.4, 1.9, 2.7, 6.3 | Bulk oxygen carrier; metallicity tracer |
| CH$_4$ | 2.3, 3.3, 7.7 | Carbon chemistry; temperature indicator |
| CO$_2$ | 2.8, 4.3, 15 | Metallicity and C/O; JWST workhorse band |
| CO | 2.3, 4.7 | C/O ratio; disequilibrium tracer |
| NH$_3$ | 3.0, 6.1, 10.5 | Nitrogen chemistry; cool atmospheres |
| HCN | 3.0, 14 | Photochemistry; high C/O marker |
| DMS / DMDS | 3.4, 7–10 | Candidate biosignatures; contested |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The forward model

A retrieval forward model maps a parameter vector $\theta$ to a spectrum $M(\theta)$:

1. **Parametric pressure–temperature profile.** Flexible analytic forms (e.g., the Madhusudhan–Seager parameterization with free temperature nodes and inversion layers) that span the physically plausible space [1].
2. **Chemical parameterization.** Either equilibrium chemistry with free metallicity and C/O, or free constant-with-altitude mixing ratios $X_i$ for each molecule.
3. **Aerosol parameterization.** Cloud-top pressure, gray opacity, haze power-law slope and amplitude, or Mie particle properties.
4. **Radiative transfer.** Line-by-line or correlated-$k$ integration of the transit or eclipse geometry, including limb darkening of the star and instrument bandpasses. Correlated-$k$ and pre-tabulated cross-section grids are the standard speed–accuracy compromise.

```python
import numpy as np

def log_likelihood(theta, data, sigma, forward_model):
    """Gaussian log-likelihood for a transmission spectrum."""
    model = forward_model(theta)          # R_p^2 / R_*^2 at data wavelengths
    resid = (data - model) / sigma
    chi2 = np.sum(resid ** 2)
    norm = np.sum(np.log(2.0 * np.pi * sigma ** 2))
    return -0.5 * (chi2 + norm)

def log_posterior(theta, data, sigma, forward_model, log_prior):
    lp = log_prior(theta)
    if not np.isfinite(lp):
        return -np.inf
    return lp + log_likelihood(theta, data, sigma, forward_model)
```

### 3.2 Bayesian inference: nested sampling and MCMC

Retrieval is Bayesian inversion. Given data $D$ and model $\mathcal{M}$,

$$P(\theta \mid D, \mathcal{M}) = \frac{\mathcal{L}(D \mid \theta)\, \pi(\theta)}{\mathcal{Z}}, \qquad \mathcal{Z} = \int \mathcal{L}(D \mid \theta)\, \pi(\theta)\, d\theta,$$

where $\mathcal{Z}$ is the Bayesian evidence. Two sampling families dominate:

- **Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)**, typically the affine-invariant ensemble sampler, which excels at mapping posterior shapes in moderate dimensions but does not naturally compute $\mathcal{Z}$.
- **Nested sampling**, which transforms the evidence integral into a one-dimensional integral over prior mass, $\mathcal{Z} = \int_0^1 \mathcal{L}(X)\, dX$, maintaining a population of live points that climbs likelihood contours. It returns both $\mathcal{Z}$ and posterior samples, handles multimodal posteriors, and enables principled model comparison through Bayes factors $B_{10} = \mathcal{Z}_1 / \mathcal{Z}_0$.

Detection significances are quoted from $\ln B$: $\ln B > 5$ is conventionally "strong" evidence. This is the language in which modern claims — water at 5$\sigma$, DMS at 3$\sigma$ — are adjudicated [2][3].

```rust
// Skeleton of a nested-sampling iteration over N live points.
fn nested_step(live: &mut [Point], loglike: &dyn Fn(&Point) -> f64) -> (f64, Point) {
    // 1. Find the live point with the lowest likelihood L_min.
    let worst = live.iter().enumerate()
        .min_by(|a, b| a.1.ln_l.partial_cmp(&b.1.ln_l).unwrap()).unwrap().0;
    let discarded = live[worst].clone();
    // 2. Evidence weight from the shrinkage distribution.
    let log_width = ((live.len() as f64 - 1.0) / live.len() as f64).ln();
    // 3. Replace the worst point with a new draw from the prior with L > L_min.
    live[worst] = sample_constrained(loglike, discarded.ln_l);
    (log_width, discarded)
}
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The transit radius integral and its analytic structure

Return to the chord integral. The analytic treatment of Robinson [6] extends the classical formula with scattering and refraction, showing that a patch of the planetary disk appears opaque when the *slant* absorption optical depth, the slant scattering optical depth, or the refractive bending angle crosses its threshold — whichever comes first. Three consequences follow.

First, the $\sqrt{2\pi R_0/H}$ amplification means transmission spectra probe pressures roughly $\sqrt{2\pi R_0/H}$ times lower than emission spectra for the same opacity — typically millibars at the limb versus bars on the dayside. The two geometries are complementary, not redundant.

Third, refraction imposes a fundamental floor: rays bent by more than the angular radius of the star never reach the observer, so below a critical pressure the atmosphere is invisible regardless of opacity [6]. For temperate, small planets this refractive boundary can sit *above* the cloud deck, rendering the deep atmosphere fundamentally unobservable in transmission.

> **Theorem (transit radius, isothermal):** *For an isothermal atmosphere with wavelength-dependent opacity $\kappa(\lambda)$, the effective transit radius satisfies $R_p(\lambda) = R_0 + H\gamma_E + H\ln(\tau_0(\lambda))$ up to the Euler–Mascheroni constant $\gamma_E$, where $\tau_0$ is the reference slant optical depth. The spectrum's shape is therefore fixed by opacity ratios; its absolute level is fixed by the reference radius.*

### 4.2 Clouds, hazes, and the reference-pressure degeneracy

The most consequential degeneracy in transmission spectroscopy is the three-way trade between the reference radius $R_0$, the reference pressure $P_0$, and the molecular mixing ratios $X_i$. Because the spectrum constrains only the combination $R_0 + H\ln P_0$ (shifting $R_0$ up and $P_0$ down by $e^{\Delta R_0/H}$ leaves every chord integral invariant) and abundances enter only through opacity ratios against a continuum, one can inflate all mixing ratios while dropping the reference pressure and keep the spectrum nearly identical.

Gray clouds sharpen the problem: an opaque deck at pressure $P_c$ truncates all chords, replacing the deep-atmosphere information with a flat continuum. Hazes add a wavelength-dependent slope — often parameterized as $\sigma \propto \lambda^{-\alpha}$ — which can mimic or mask the Rayleigh scattering slope of a clear atmosphere, producing the notorious cloud–haze–metallicity degeneracy. Breaking it requires:

1. **Broad wavelength coverage**, so that multiple bands of the same molecule anchor its abundance independently of the continuum.
2. **High signal-to-noise in band wings**, where the pressure dependence of line broadening differs from gray opacity.
3. **Emission or phase-curve data**, which probe different pressures and break the terminator-only view.

The Haskell sketch below computes the scale height and the logarithmic transit-radius shift — the entire degeneracy in six lines:

```haskell
-- Scale height and isothermal transit-radius shift (SI units).
scaleHeight :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
scaleHeight temp mu gravity =
    let kB = 1.380649e-23; amu = 1.660539e-27
    in kB * temp / (mu * amu * gravity)

transitRadius :: Double -> Double -> Double -> Double -> Double
transitRadius r0 h kappa kappa0 = r0 + h * log (kappa / kappa0)

-- Degeneracy: raising r0 by dx while scaling kappa0 by e^(dx/h)
-- leaves every wavelength's radius invariant.
degenerateShift :: Double -> Double -> Double -> (Double, Double)
degenerateShift r0 h dx = (r0 + dx, dx / h)  -- (r0', ln-scaling of kappa0)
```

### 4.3 Nested sampling versus MCMC in high-dimensional retrievals

A modern free-chemistry retrieval carries 15–40 parameters: P–T nodes, a dozen molecular abundances, cloud and haze parameters, radius and reference pressure, stellar contamination terms, and instrument offsets. In this regime the samplers' strengths diverge.

Nested sampling's evidence computation is its superpower: comparing a model with DMS to one without is a Bayes factor, not a chi-squared difference, and therefore automatically penalizes the extra parameters' prior volume. Its live-point population also maps multimodal posteriors — crucial when, e.g., the data admit both a high-metallicity clear solution and a low-metallicity cloudy solution.

MCMC, particularly affine-invariant ensembles, is cheaper per independent sample in unimodal problems and simpler to diagnose with the Gelman–Rubin statistic $\hat{R}$, but it cannot compare models without additional thermodynamic integration, and it can strand walkers on a single mode for the entire run.

```tla+
---- MODULE Retrieval ----
EXTENDS Naturals, Reals, TLC
CONSTANTS Nparams
VARIABLES livePoints, logZ, logX, converged

Init == /\ livePoints \in [1..1000 -> [params : [1..Nparams -> Real],
                                       lnL : Real]]
        /\ logZ = 0.0 /\ logX = 0.0 /\ converged = FALSE

Next == \/ /\ ~converged
           /\ \E w \in 1..1000 :          \* worst live point
                 /\ \A i \in 1..1000 : livePoints[i].lnL >= livePoints[w].lnL
                 /\ logX' = logX + Ln((999.0) / 1000.0)
                 /\ logZ' = LogAdd(logZ, logX + livePoints[w].lnL)
                 /\ livePoints' = [livePoints EXCEPT ![w] = ConstrainedDraw(livePoints[w].lnL)]
                 /\ converged' = (logX + MaxLikelihood(livePoints) < logZ + Ln(0.01))
        \/ /\ converged /\ UNCHANGED <<livePoints, logZ, logX, converged>>

Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<livePoints, logZ, logX, converged>>
====
```

### 4.4 Biosignature gases: disequilibrium, false positives, and the K2-18b case study

A *biosignature* is an observable that requires life to explain — but no molecule is a biosignature in isolation. Earth's atmosphere is compelling not because it contains O$_2$ but because O$_2$ coexists with CH$_4$ at abundances $10^{10}$ times their thermochemical equilibrium value: a disequilibrium maintained, so far as we know, only by biology.

The framework must survive false positives. Abiotic O$_2$ accumulates through:

- **Photolysis of H$_2$O and CO$_2$** followed by hydrogen escape, especially on M-dwarf planets subjected to prolonged pre-main-sequence super-luminous phases;
- **Low non-condensable inventories**, where a runaway greenhouse leaves O$_2$ as the residual atmosphere;
- **Cometary and volcanic delivery** of sulfur and organics, now directly relevant: dimethyl sulfide has been detected in *abiotic cometary matter*, demonstrating that the canonical "DMS is uniquely biogenic" claim fails at the geochemical level [5].

This is the context for the K2-18b debate. The temperate sub-Neptune shows robust JWST detections of CH$_4$ and CO$_2$ in the near-infrared. Madhusudhan et al. reported 3$\sigma$ evidence for DMS and/or dimethyl disulfide (DMDS) in new 5–12 micron MIRI data, at abundances above 10 ppmv — extraordinary, given that Earth's DMS sits at parts-per-trillion [2]. Independent reanalyses counter that the MIRI spectrum is dominated by unresolved instrumental systematics: different wavelength binnings yield different planet spectra, 87.5% of retrievals with preferred binnings find no DMS, and the large mid-infrared features sit in tension with the smaller near-infrared ones [3]. As of this writing there is no statistically significant evidence for biosignatures on K2-18b — but the episode has stress-tested every link in the chain from detector systematics to Bayesian model comparison.

## 5 Empirical and Theoretical Results

**JWST transmission spectroscopy works at the predicted precision.** The Transiting Exoplanet Community Early Release Science program measured WASP-39b's 3–5.5 micron transmission spectrum with NIRSpec and detected the 4.3-micron CO$_2$ band at 26$\sigma$ — the first unambiguous CO$_2$ detection in an exoplanet atmosphere [7]. Complementary NIRCam observations from 2–4 microns detected gaseous water, bounded methane, and favored atmospheric metallicity 1–100 times solar with a substellar C/O ratio, pointing to substantial solid accretion during formation [8].

**Retrieval recovers known physics.** Applied to HD 189733b and HD 209458b, the Madhusudhan–Seager framework constrained day–night redistribution efficiency and confirmed H$_2$O, CO, CH$_4$, and CO$_2$ on both planets, including a dayside thermal inversion on HD 209458b — from photometry with a handful of channels [1].

**Model comparison adjudicates detections.** The K2-18b literature is now a case study in Bayesian hygiene. Competing reductions of identical JWST data produce Bayes factors for DMS ranging from $\ln B \approx 2$ (weak) to negative, depending on binning and systematics treatment [2][3][4].

Ordered summary of the empirical status:

1. **Confirmed:** CO$_2$, H$_2$O on hot Jupiters at high significance; CH$_4$ + CO$_2$ on K2-18b.
2. **Constrained:** metallicities to order-of-magnitude; C/O ratios to substellar versus superstellar; cloud-top pressures where features are muted.
3. **Contested:** DMS/DMDS on K2-18b; any thermal inversion requiring non-gray confirmation.
4. **Unobserved:** O$_2$–CH$_4$ disequilibrium pairs; phosphine; any second independent biosignature on the same planet.

## 6 Limitations

1. **One-dimensionality.** Retrievals assume a single terminator-averaged profile. Real terminators mix morning and evening limbs with different temperatures and cloud coverage, biasing retrieved abundances by factors of several in the worst cases.
2. **The reference-pressure wall.** Section 4.2's degeneracy is information theory, not a technical artifact. Transmission spectra fundamentally constrain *relative* abundances and the pressure scale, not absolute metallicity, unless emission or mass–radius data anchor the reference level.
3. **Stellar contamination.** Unocculted starspots and faculae imprint wavelength-dependent transit-depth offsets — the "transit light source effect" — that mimic hazes and molecular features, particularly for M-dwarf hosts.
4. **Cross-section accuracy.** Biosignature assessment lives or dies on laboratory data. The K2-18b debate exposed how poorly the infrared cross-sections of DMS, DMDS, and hundreds of candidate trace gases are known at relevant temperatures and pressures [2][4].
5. **Instrument systematics.** The MIRI reanalyses demonstrate that red noise and binning choices can manufacture or erase molecular features at the very significance levels where biosignature claims live [3].
6. **Prior dependence.** In low-information regimes the posterior is the prior wearing a data costume. Free-chemistry retrievals with log-uniform abundance priors from $10^{-12}$ to $10^{-1}$ can produce confidently wrong answers when the data constrain only band ratios.

## 7 Conclusion

Exoplanet atmospheric retrieval is the art of the principled inverse: a forward model honest about geometry, opacity, and ignorance, inverted through Bayesian machinery that reports probability distributions over answers rather than answers. Its central results are as much about *limits* as discoveries — the logarithmic compression of abundance information, the reference-pressure degeneracy, the refractive floor.

The search for biosignatures inherits this discipline. We propose the following evidentiary ladder, each rung of which must hold before the next is claimed:

> **The biosignature ladder:**
> 1. *Authentic signal* — the feature survives independent reductions and binning choices.
> 2. *Correct attribution* — the opacity is uniquely assigned; no abiotic molecule fits equally well.
> 3. *Abiotic exclusion* — photochemical, geochemical, and delivery false positives are quantitatively ruled out.
> 4. *Contextual habitability* — the planet can sustain liquid water and the inferred chemistry simultaneously.
> 5. *Independent confirmation* — a second molecule, a second instrument, or a second epoch corroborates.

The JWST era has given us CO$_2$ at 26$\sigma$ [7], metallicity constraints that rewrite formation histories [8], and a masterclass in how *not* to claim life from a single band [2][3][4][5]. The next decade — extremely large telescopes, high-resolution cross-correlation spectroscopy, and eventually direct imaging of temperate terrestrials — will test whether the ladder reaches the top.
