# Erasure Coding for Distributed Storage: Reed-Solomon, Locally Recoverable Codes, and Regenerating Codes in Ceph and HDFS

## Abstract

Distributed storage systems guard exabytes against disk, node, and rack failures by dispersing redundancy across independent failure domains. Triple replication is operationally simple but exacts a 3× capacity tax that dominates data-center cost at scale. Classical Reed–Solomon codes attain the Singleton bound and are therefore redundancy-optimal, yet they force the repair of a single failed block to read *k* surviving blocks — the entire data stripe — a phenomenon known as repair amplification. This thesis develops the theory and practice of modern erasure coding for storage. We formalize maximum-distance-separable codes through Vandermonde generator matrices over finite fields *GF(2^w)*; quantify the repair-degree/storage-overhead tradeoff; prove the locality–distance tradeoff governing locally recoverable codes; and characterize the storage–repair-bandwidth tradeoff for regenerating codes, deriving the minimum-storage-regenerating (MSR) and minimum-bandwidth-regenerating (MBR) operating points. Constructions are traced from the product-matrix framework and Azure Local Reconstruction Codes to production deployments in Ceph erasure-coded pools and HDFS erasure coding (HDFS-7285), with quantitative comparison of overhead, repair fan-in, and degraded-read cost across deployed profiles.

## 1 Introduction

Reliability in distributed storage has historically been purchased with replication. The Google File System and early versions of the Hadoop Distributed File System stored three full copies of every block, so that the loss of any two replicas still left the data intact. The arithmetic is brutal: 1 PB of logical data consumes 3 PB of raw disk, 3× the power, and 3× the rack footprint. As clusters grew from petabytes to exabytes — Facebook's analytics clusters, Microsoft's Azure Storage stamps — the 200% overhead of triplication became the dominant term in total cost of ownership [5].

Erasure coding offers an escape: split data into *k* fragments, compute *m* parity fragments, and tolerate any *m* failures with overhead *(k+m)/k* rather than 3. Reed–Solomon codes [1], introduced in 1960 for deep-space communication, became the default choice — deployed in RAID-6 arrays, OceanStore, and eventually at Facebook and in Ceph [7]. But a new problem surfaced at scale. Disk failures are not rare events; in a 100,000-disk fleet, several drives fail *every day*. Each failure triggers a repair that, under classical Reed–Solomon, must read *k* fragments to rebuild one — for a *k = 10* code, ten times the lost data crosses the network. The community's response unfolded along three axes: **locality**, reducing the number of fragments contacted during repair; **regenerating codes**, reducing the *amount* of data each contacted node sends; and **systems engineering**, embedding these codes in Ceph and HDFS with acceptable write-path semantics.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews finite fields, linear codes, and the Singleton bound. Section 3 fixes notation and the information-flow methodology used to reason about repair. Section 4 develops the three code families in depth: Reed–Solomon and MDS constructions, locally recoverable codes, regenerating codes, and their realization in Ceph and HDFS. Section 5 presents quantitative comparisons and formal tradeoff theorems with proof sketches. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

A linear *[n, k]* code over a finite field *F = GF(q)* is a *k*-dimensional subspace of *F^n*, described by a *k × n* generator matrix *G* or an *(n−k) × n* parity-check matrix *H*. The Hamming distance *d* of the code is the minimum weight of a nonzero codeword, and the Singleton bound states that

> **Theorem (Singleton bound):** For any *[n, k]* linear code, *d ≤ n − k + 1*.

Codes attaining equality are called *maximum distance separable* (MDS): any *k* of the *n* symbols suffice to recover the message, and any *n − k* erasures are tolerable — the strongest possible guarantee for a given redundancy [1].

Reed–Solomon codes are the canonical MDS family. The message is interpreted as the coefficients of a polynomial *f(x)* of degree less than *k* over *GF(q)*, and the codeword is the vector of evaluations *(f(α_1), …, f(α_n))* at *n* distinct field elements. Erasure decoding is then polynomial interpolation: any *k* surviving evaluations determine *f* uniquely. Practical storage systems work over *GF(2^8)* (byte symbols, field defined by a primitive polynomial such as *x^8 + x^4 + x^3 + x + 1*) or *GF(2^16)*, using logarithm/exponent tables to reduce field multiplication to modular addition.

Two performance concepts will recur throughout. The **storage overhead** is *n/k* (raw bytes per logical byte). The **repair degree** is the number of surviving nodes contacted to rebuild one failed fragment, and the **repair bandwidth** is the total data transferred. Classical *RS(k, m)* has overhead *(k+m)/k*, tolerates *m* failures, but has repair degree *k* and repair bandwidth equal to the full object — the inefficiency that motivated everything that follows [2][5].

| Scheme | Example | Overhead | Failures tolerated | Repair degree | Repair traffic |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3× replication | GFS, HDFS default | 3.00 | 2 | 1 | 1 block |
| RS(6,3) | HDFS default | 1.50 | 3 | 6 | 6 blocks |
| RS(10,4) | Facebook f4 | 1.40 | 4 | 10 | 10 blocks |
| Azure LRC(12,2,2) | WAS | 1.29 | 2–4 | 6 | ~6 blocks |
| XORbas LRC(10,6,5) | HDFS module | 1.40 | 4 | 5 | ~5 blocks |

## 3 Methodology

We adopt the standard stripe model. A logical object of *B* symbols is divided into *k* data fragments; encoding produces *n ≥ k* fragments of *α* symbols each, one stored per node. A data collector contacting any *k* nodes recovers the object (the *reconstruction property*). When a node fails, a newcomer contacts *d* surviving helpers (*k ≤ d ≤ n − 1*), each sending *β* symbols, for total repair bandwidth *γ = dβ*.

The central analytic instrument is the **information-flow graph** of Dimakis et al. [2]. The source *S* connects to each storage node *x_i* through an edge *x_i^in → x_i^out* of capacity *α* (the stored content). On failure, the newcomer *x_new* connects to *d* helpers with edges of capacity *β*. A data collector connects to any *k* active nodes with infinite-capacity edges. Applying the max-flow/min-cut theorem to this evolving graph yields the fundamental bound

> **Theorem (Storage–bandwidth bound [2]):** Any distributed storage system with the reconstruction property must satisfy *B ≤ Σ_{i=0}^{k−1} min(α, (d − i)β)*.

We distinguish **functional repair**, where the regenerated fragment need only preserve the system's information-theoretic properties, from **exact repair**, where the newcomer reproduces the lost fragment bit-for-bit — the variant systems actually deploy. We also distinguish **reconstruction** (full-object recovery by a client) from **degraded reads** (serving a read while some fragments are unavailable), since the latter dominates user-visible latency.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Reed–Solomon Codes and the MDS Construction

The workhorse of storage erasure coding is the *systematic* Reed–Solomon code. Let the *k* data symbols be *d = (d_1, …, d_k)*. Encoding computes *m* parity symbols *p = d · P* where *P* is a *k × m* Cauchy or Vandermonde matrix over *GF(2^w)*; the stored stripe is *(d, p)*. Decoding from any *k* surviving symbols inverts the corresponding *k × k* submatrix of the generator matrix.

> **Theorem (MDS property of Reed–Solomon [1]):** Every *k × k* submatrix of a Vandermonde matrix over a field is invertible, because its determinant is *Π_{i<j}(α_j − α_i) ≠ 0* for distinct evaluation points. Hence any *k* symbols determine the degree-*(k−1)* message polynomial, and the code attains the Singleton bound with equality.

The following Python sketch implements *RS(4,2)* encoding over *GF(2^8)* using log/exp tables, illustrating the byte-level arithmetic that libraries like Jerasure and Intel ISA-L accelerate with SIMD:

```python
PRIM = 0x11D  # x^8 + x^4 + x^3 + x + 1
exp = [0]*512; log = [0]*256
x = 1
for i in range(511):
    exp[i] = x; log[x] = i if i < 255 else log[x]
    x = (x << 1) ^ (PRIM if x & 0x80 else 0)

def gf_mul(a, b):
    return 0 if a == 0 or b == 0 else exp[log[a] + log[b]]

def rs_encode(data, vandermonde_rows):
    """data: k byte-lists; returns m parity byte-lists."""
    k = len(data); nbytes = len(data[0])
    parity = [[0]*nbytes for _ in vandermonde_rows]
    for r, row in enumerate(vandermonde_rows):
        for j in range(nbytes):
            acc = 0
            for i in range(k):
                acc ^= gf_mul(row[i], data[i][j])
            parity[r][j] = acc
    return parity

# Vandermonde rows: row r = (1, r+1, (r+1)^2, (r+1)^3) in GF(2^8)
```

In practice, nobody hand-rolls this. Ceph's default *jerasure* plugin implements `reed_sol_van` (Vandermonde), `reed_sol_r6_op`, `cauchy_orig`, and `cauchy_good` matrix techniques, while the *ISA* plugin and HDFS's native codec delegate to Intel's ISA-L library, which achieves multi-GB/s through AVX2/AVX-512 Galois-field kernels [7][8]. The MDS optimality of Reed–Solomon is precisely why it remains the default: for cold data where repairs are rare, no code stores fewer bytes per unit of fault tolerance.

The price is repair. Rebuilding one lost fragment of an *RS(k,m)* stripe reads *k* fragments — for Facebook's widely deployed *RS(10,4)*, 10× amplification. Since measurements show the vast majority of repairs are single-fragment failures, this motivated codes that repair from *fewer* than *k* fragments (locality) or transfer *less* than a full fragment per helper (regenerating codes).

### 4.2 Locality and Locally Recoverable Codes

A code symbol has **locality** *r* if it is a function of at most *r* other symbols; a code has locality *r* if every symbol does. Locality *r ≪ k* means single failures are repaired with *r* reads instead of *k*. Gopalan et al. established the fundamental tradeoff for linear codes:

> **Theorem (Locality–distance tradeoff):** An *[n, k]* linear code in which every symbol has locality *r* satisfies *d ≤ n − k − ⌈k/r⌉ + 2*. Papailiopoulos and Dimakis [4] generalized this to *all* (linear or nonlinear) codes via an information-flow gadget, proving the bound universal.

The flagship deployment is **Local Reconstruction Codes** in Windows Azure Storage [3], which won Best Paper at USENIX ATC 2012. Azure's *(12, 2, 2)*-LRC partitions 12 data fragments into two local groups of 6, adds one *local* parity per group (an XOR-like combination within the group), and two *global* parities computed across all 12 fragments:

- A single data-fragment failure is repaired from its 6 local-group siblings — **locality 6 instead of 12**.
- Overhead is *16/12 ≈ 1.33* (reported 1.29 with alignment optimizations), a 14% saving over *RS(6,3)*'s 1.5 while *exceeding* its durability: a data chunk survives three failures with certainty and four failures with 86% probability [3].
- Erasure coding runs **lazily** in the background: extents are written with triple replication, then re-encoded when sealed, keeping coding off the write critical path.

Facebook's **XORbas** (deployed in HDFS at Facebook) pushed the same idea further [5]. Built atop an *RS(10,4)* base (10 data + 4 parities = 14 blocks per 10), it adds three local parities over logarithmic-sized groups; an alignment condition *S_1 + S_2 + S_3 = 0* makes the third parity implied rather than stored. The measured result on production Hadoop clusters: approximately **2× reduction in repair disk I/O and network traffic** versus Reed–Solomon, at a 14% storage premium that the authors prove information-theoretically optimal for achieving locality 5 [5]. Locally recoverable codes thus trade a small, quantifiable storage surcharge for a large, permanent reduction in the network's background repair load.

### 4.3 Regenerating Codes: the Storage–Bandwidth Tradeoff

Where LRCs reduce the *number* of helpers, **regenerating codes** [2] reduce the *amount* each helper sends. The newcomer downloads *β < α* symbols from each of *d* helpers and still reconstructs a full *α*-symbol fragment. Minimizing the bound of Section 3 over the feasible *(α, γ)* region yields a smooth tradeoff curve with two extremal operating points:

> **Theorem (MSR and MBR points [2]):** For file size *B*, the minimum-storage (MSR) point is *(α_MSR, γ_MSR) = (B/k, Bd/(k(d − k + 1)))*, matching MDS storage efficiency while cutting repair bandwidth by *(d − k + 1)/d*... the minimum-bandwidth (MBR) point is *(α_MBR, γ_MBR) = (2Bd/(k(2d − k + 1)), 2B/(k(2d − k + 1)))*.

Concretely, an *MSR* code with *d = n − 1 = 13, k = 10* stores exactly as much as *RS(10,4)* but repairs a node by downloading only *β = α/(d−k+1) = α/4* from each of 13 helpers — total *3.25α* versus *10α* for Reed–Solomon, a 3× bandwidth saving at zero storage cost.

Explicit *exact*-repair constructions arrived with the **product-matrix framework** of Rashmi, Shah, and Kumar [6]. Each node *i* stores *c_i^T = ψ_i^T M*, where *ψ_i* is a row of an *n × d* encoding matrix *Ψ* and *M* is a *d × α* message matrix with carefully imposed symmetry. During repair, helper *j* sends the scalar *ψ_j^T M ψ_f* (for failed node *f*); the newcomer collects *d* such scalars and solves for *M ψ_f*, i.e., the exact lost content. The framework yields MBR codes for *all* parameters and MSR codes for *d ≥ 2k − 2* [6], with the elegant property that helpers perform no decoding — pure linear projection — enabling **repair-by-transfer** in some variants. The key analytic idea, *interference alignment* [6], arranges unwanted signal components from different helpers into a low-dimensional subspace so the desired fragment emerges cleanly, a technique borrowed from wireless communication theory.

The interior of the tradeoff curve is subtler: Shah et al. proved interior points are *not* achievable under exact repair with repair-by-transfer, and Tian characterized the exact-repair region of the *(4,3,3)* code as strictly smaller than the functional-repair region — a reminder that the flow-graph bound is necessary but not always sufficient once exactness is demanded.

### 4.4 From Theory to Deployment: Ceph and HDFS

**Ceph** exposes erasure coding through *erasure-coded pools* configured by a profile: `k` data chunks, `m` coding chunks, and a CRUSH failure domain [7]. The default profile (`k=2, m=1`, `plugin=jerasure`, `technique=reed_sol_van`) mirrors 2× replication at 1.5× cost; production cold-data pools commonly use `k=8, m=4`, tolerating four simultaneous OSD failures at 1.5× overhead across 12 hosts. Three plugins embody the theory of this thesis:

1. **jerasure** — classical Reed–Solomon/Cauchy/RAID-6 techniques; the MDS default.
2. **ISA** — Intel ISA-L accelerated Reed–Solomon for high-throughput encode/decode.
3. **LRC plugin** — implements locally recoverable codes with configurable locality *l*, directly descending from [3][4].

Operational constraints shape the deployment: erasure-coded pools forbid partial overwrites (writes are full-stripe), so Ceph pairs EC data pools with replicated metadata pools and targets them at RBD images, RGW object storage, and CephFS cold tiers rather than latency-sensitive workloads.

**HDFS** added erasure coding in HDFS-7285 (Hadoop 3.x), replacing triplication for cold data [8]. Files are striped into *block groups*: with policy *RS-6-3-1024k* (the default), each group holds 6 data blocks + 3 parity blocks striped in 1024 KB cells, cutting overhead from 3.0 to 1.5 while tolerating 3 failures. Supported policies include *RS-3-2-1024k*, *RS-6-3*, *RS-LEGACY-6-3-1024k*, and *XOR-2-1-1024k* (RAID-5-like). The NameNode manages block-group placement with rack awareness; codecs are pluggable through `io.erasurecode.codec.rs.rawcoders`, preferring native ISA-L implementations with pure-Java fallback. The architecture cleanly separates *policy* (k, m, cell size), *codec* (the mathematics of §4.1), and *placement* (failure-domain geometry) — the same separation of concerns that makes Ceph's plugin model work.

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

**Quantitative comparison.** The table in §2 summarizes the design space; the salient numbers from production literature are:

- *RS(10,4)* at Facebook: 1.4× overhead; single-block repair reads 10 blocks (10× amplification) [5].
- Azure *(12,2,2)*-LRC: 1.29× overhead, 14% below *RS(6,3)*; locality 6; survives 3 arbitrary failures with probability 1, 4 failures with probability 0.86 [3].
- XORbas *(10,6,5)*-LRC: ~2× lower repair I/O and traffic than *RS(10,4)*; +14% storage, proven optimal for locality 5 [5].
- MSR *(n=14, k=10, d=13)*: identical 1.4× storage to *RS(10,4)*, repair bandwidth *3.25α* vs *10α* — a 3.1× reduction derived directly from the MSR formula [2][6].
- Ceph *(8,4)* profile: 1.5× overhead, 4-failure tolerance, 12-OSD minimum [7]; HDFS *RS-6-3-1024k*: 1.5× overhead, 3-failure tolerance [8].

**Proof sketch: the storage–bandwidth bound.** Consider the flow graph of §3 after *k* successive failures and repairs, with the data collector attached to the *k* most recently repaired nodes. Order the *k* nodes by repair time; node *i* (0-indexed) connects to the source-side through at most *i* previously repaired nodes plus *(d − i)* original nodes. The min-cut separating *S* from the collector is therefore bounded by *Σ_{i=0}^{k−1} min(α, (d−i)β)*: the first term caps what a node can contribute by its stored content *α*, the second by the total repair traffic *(d−i)β* that flowed into the nodes it depends on. Max-flow/min-cut then forces *B ≤ Σ_{i=0}^{k−1} min(α, (d−i)β)*, with equality defining the optimal tradeoff curve whose endpoints are the MSR and MBR points [2].

**Proof sketch: MDS optimality of Reed–Solomon.** The *k × k* submatrix of the Vandermonde evaluation matrix has determinant *Π_{i<j}(α_j − α_i)*; with distinct evaluation points over a field this is nonzero, so every *k*-subset of coordinates is an information set. Thus *d = n − k + 1*, meeting the Singleton bound — no linear code with the same redundancy tolerates more erasures [1].

**Proof sketch: locality–distance tradeoff.** Suppose each symbol is recoverable from *r* others. Partition coordinates greedily into local groups; each group of size at most *r+1* contributes at least one parity constraint beyond the *n − k* global ones. Counting constraints gives *d ≤ n − k − ⌈k/r⌉ + 2* for linear codes; the flow-graph gadget of [4] extends the argument to nonlinear codes, showing locality exacts an unavoidable distance penalty.

## 6 Limitations

No code family dominates on every axis, and several theoretical gaps persist. Reed–Solomon's repair amplification (*k*× read for one lost fragment) makes it a poor choice for warm data with frequent single failures; its *O(k²)* decode per stripe (or *O(k log² k)* with FFT-based methods) also taxes degraded reads. Locally recoverable codes pay a permanent storage surcharge and, by the tradeoff of §5, surrender maximum distance — an LRC tolerates *fewer* worst-case erasure patterns than an MDS code of equal redundancy. Regenerating codes achieve their bandwidth savings only at the MSR/MBR extremes with known exact-repair constructions; product-matrix MSR codes require *d ≥ 2k − 2* [6], interior points of the exact-repair region remain largely unconstructible, and functional repair — while theoretically elegant — is undeployable because systems need bit-identical fragments for checksums and scrubbing.

Systems limitations are equally real. Ceph erasure-coded pools cannot service partial overwrites, restricting EC to immutable or cold data; HDFS erasure-coded files historically lacked append/*hsync* semantics, complicating adoption for streaming ingest. Galois-field arithmetic, while SIMD-accelerated, still costs CPU on the write path, and silent data corruption demands end-to-end checksums plus background scrubbing regardless of code strength. Finally, all analyses above assume independent, identically distributed failures; correlated failures — rack power loss, rolling upgrades, firmware bugs — violate the model and are handled in practice by cross-rack placement rather than by coding theory.

## 7 Conclusion

Erasure coding for distributed storage has matured from a single optimal point — the Reed–Solomon MDS code of 1960 [1] — into a design space parameterized by *what repair costs*. When capacity dominates, classical Reed–Solomon remains unbeaten: it attains the Singleton bound, and every deployed system from Ceph's jerasure plugin to HDFS's RS-6-3 policy defaults to it for cold data [7][8]. When *repair network traffic* dominates, the field offers two principled escapes with matching fundamental limits: locally recoverable codes, which bound the *number* of helpers via the locality–distance tradeoff and which Azure [3] and Facebook [5] proved at exabyte scale; and regenerating codes, which bound the *amount* each helper sends via the storage–bandwidth tradeoff, with exact-repair constructions from the product-matrix framework [2][6]. The trajectory is clear: as fleets grow and failures become continuous background events rather than rare emergencies, the codes that minimize the *steady-state repair tax* — not merely the static storage overhead — will define the next generation of storage infrastructure.
