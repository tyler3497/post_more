# The ZX-Calculus: Diagrammatic Rewriting, Completeness, and Quantum Circuit Optimization Beyond Gate-Level Synthesis

## Abstract

The ZX-calculus, introduced by Coecke and Duncan, is a diagrammatic language for qubit quantum mechanics in which linear maps are drawn as string diagrams of spiders --- colored nodes parametrized by phases --- and equational reasoning is performed by graphical rewriting. This thesis develops the calculus from its categorical foundations in Frobenius algebras through its core rewrite rules (spider fusion, bialgebra, Hopf, pi-copy), the landmark completeness theorems of Backens for the single-qubit Clifford+T fragment and of Jeandel, Perdrix and Vilmart for universal Clifford+T, and its culminating application: automated quantum circuit optimization. We present the PyZX pipeline of Kissinger and van de Wetering --- graph-like normal form, Clifford simplification by local complementation and pivoting, phase-gadget propagation, identity removal, gadget fusion, and phase teleportation --- and the spider-nest identities of de Beaudrap, Bian and Wang for T-count reduction, with benchmark evidence including up to 50% T-count improvements and state-of-the-art results on 36 standard circuits. We close with the calculus's theoretical limits and the open problems in circuit extraction and completeness beyond Clifford+T.

---

## 1 Introduction

Quantum circuit notation is a graphical language, but a poor one: it permits only the sequential and parallel composition of gates, and every optimization must be re-derived as a matrix identity. The **ZX-calculus**, introduced by Bob Coecke and Ross Duncan in 2008 to model interacting quantum observables [1], repairs this deficiency by treating quantum processes as *string diagrams* --- graphs whose vertices (*spiders*) carry phases and whose edges are wires --- equipped with a sound system of *rewrite rules* that transform diagrams while preserving their linear-algebraic meaning. Computations are done *graphically*: two diagrams denote the same operator if and only if one can be rewritten into the other, and the rewrites expose structure (such as redundant phases) that gate-level reasoning cannot see.

Two properties make the calculus a serious scientific instrument rather than a notation. First, **universality**: every linear map between qubit Hilbert spaces is denoted by some ZX-diagram. Second, **completeness** for practical fragments: Backens proved the single-qubit Clifford+T rules complete [2], and Jeandel, Perdrix and Vilmart proved completeness for universal Clifford+T quantum mechanics [3] --- the first complete, approximately universal diagrammatic language for quantum mechanics. Completeness means there are *no missing rules*: any true equality of Clifford+T operators is derivable graphically.

The payoff is algorithmic. Because ZX-diagrams abstract away gate order into graph structure, optimization becomes *graph rewriting*: Clifford subcircuits dissolve via graph-theoretic rules, non-Clifford phases migrate into *phase gadgets* where they cancel non-locally, and the reduced diagram is *extracted* back into a circuit with far fewer T gates. Kissinger and van de Wetering's **PyZX** implementation [4] of this pipeline matches or beats all previous ancilla-free T-count optimizers, with improvements up to 50% on benchmark circuits, and its `full_reduce` routine is now standard pre-/post-processing in quantum compilation stacks. Spider-nest identities [5,6] extend the method with composite rewrite rules that capture cancellations invisible to local rewriting.

This thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the categorical and algebraic background: Frobenius algebras, spiders, phases, and the standard interpretation. Section 3 presents the methodology of diagrammatic reasoning: the rewrite rules and normal forms. Section 4 deep-dives into (i) the spider zoo and rule derivations, (ii) the completeness theorems, (iii) the PyZX optimization pipeline, (iv) phase teleportation and spider nests, and (v) circuit extraction. Section 5 surveys empirical results and the theoretical guarantees behind them. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 String diagrams and Frobenius algebras

The ZX-calculus lives in the symmetric monoidal category **FHilb** of finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, drawn as string diagrams: boxes are linear maps, wires are systems, horizontal juxtaposition is the tensor product, and vertical composition is function composition. The generators are *spiders*: a **Z-spider** (green) with $m$ inputs, $n$ outputs, and phase $\\alpha$ denotes the linear map

$$Z^m_n(\\alpha) = |0\\rangle^{\\otimes n}\\langle 0|^{\\otimes m} + e^{i\\alpha} |1\\rangle^{\\otimes n}\\langle 1|^{\\otimes m},$$

and an **X-spider** (red) is the same construction in the Hadamard-rotated basis $|+\\rangle, |-\\rangle$. The yellow **Hadamard box** $H$ interconverts the two colors. Algebraically, each color forms a *commutative Frobenius algebra* --- a multiplication (merge wires), unit, comultiplication (split wires), and counit satisfying the Frobenius law --- and the two algebras are *complementary* in the sense of mutually unbiased bases. This is the categorical content of "interacting quantum observables" [1]: Z and X spiders encode the Z and X Pauli observables, and their interaction rules encode complementarity diagrammatically.

### 2.2 The standard interpretation and soundness

Every diagram $D$ has a **standard interpretation** $\\llbracket D \\rrbracket$, a matrix computed by composing the spiders' maps. A rewrite rule $D_1 = D_2$ is *sound* when $\\llbracket D_1 \\rrbracket = \\llbracket D_2 \\rrbracket$; all ZX rules are sound by direct linear-algebraic verification. Soundness is the easy direction. **Completeness** --- that $\\llbracket D_1 \\rrbracket = \\llbracket D_2 \\rrbracket$ implies $D_1$ rewrites to $D_2$ --- is deep, was false for the original rule set, and is the subject of Section 4.2 [2,3].

### 2.3 Fragments: stabilizer, Clifford+T, universal

Restricting spider phases carves out fragments of quantum mechanics:

| Fragment | Allowed phases | Power | Completeness |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\\pi/2$ (stabilizer) | multiples of $\\pi/2$ | Clifford group, classically simulable (Gottesman--Knill) | Complete [2] |
| Single-qubit $\\pi/4$ | $\\pi/4$ multiples, 1 qubit | Single-qubit Clifford+T | Complete [2] |
| Multi-qubit $\\pi/4$ (**Clifford+T**) | $\\pi/4$ multiples | Approximately universal | Complete [3] |
| General | arbitrary $\\alpha$ | Universal | Incomplete in general |

The Clifford+T fragment is the practical sweet spot: it is *approximately universal* (any unitary is approximable to arbitrary precision) yet admits a finite, complete rule set. It is also the fragment where **T-count** --- the number of $\\pi/4$ rotations, equivalently the number of non-Clifford spiders --- is the cost metric that matters, because in fault-tolerant architectures Clifford gates are cheap (transversal) while each T gate consumes a distilled magic state [4].

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The core rewrite rules

The calculus is generated by a small set of equations, each sound and each with a clear algebraic meaning:

- **(f) Spider fusion.** Adjacent spiders of the same color fuse, adding phases: $Z(\\alpha) \circ Z(\\beta) = Z(\\alpha+\\beta)$. This is associativity of the Frobenius multiplication; it makes phase arithmetic graphical.
- **(id) Identity removal.** A phaseless 2-legged spider is just a wire and can be removed.
- **(hh) Hadamard cancellation.** Two consecutive Hadamard boxes cancel: $H^2 = I$.
- **(b) Bialgebra.** A Z-spider feeding an X-spider rewrites to a complete bipartite pattern of the opposite colors. This encodes the complementarity of the two observables.
- **(ho) Hopf law.** A Z-spider and X-spider connected by two wires disconnect entirely (up to scalar) --- the diagrammatic statement that the observables are *strongly* complementary.
- **($\\pi$-copy) Pi-copy.** A $\\pi$-phase spider copies through a spider of the opposite color, flipping phases along the way: the graphical form of the Pauli commutation relations.
- **(c) Color change.** Pushing Hadamards through a spider swaps its color.

Derived rules include **local complementation** and **pivoting** on graph-like diagrams (Section 4.3), **gadget fusion** (Section 4.4), and the **supplementarity**/cyclotomic rules needed for completeness [3].

### 3.2 Graph-like normal form

For automated rewriting, diagrams are converted to **graph-like** form: all spiders are Z-spiders, all edges are either plain wires or Hadamard edges, every spider touches at most one input/output, and no two Hadamard edges are parallel. Conversion is always possible: color-change all X-spiders, fuse aggressively. In graph-like form a diagram is essentially a *graph with phases on vertices* --- and graph theory takes over: Clifford simplification becomes local complementation and pivoting on this graph [4].

```python
# PyZX pipeline sketch (after Kissinger & van de Wetering [4])
import pyzx as zx

circ = zx.Circuit.from_qasm_file("benchmark.qasm")
g = circ.to_graph()                       # circuit -> ZX-diagram
zx.simplify.to_graph_like(g)              # graph-like normal form
zx.simplify.clifford_simp(g)              # local complementation + pivoting
zx.simplify.to_graph_like(g)
zx.simplify.phase_teleportation(g)        # non-local phase cancellation
zx.simplify.full_reduce(g)                # iterate to fixpoint
circ_opt = zx.extract_circuit(g)          # back to a circuit
print("T-count:", circ_opt.tcount())
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The spider zoo: fusion, bialgebra, Hopf, and pi-copy

Each rule is a theorem of linear algebra made visible. **Fusion** says the Z-observable's eigenbasis is a copyable classical structure: measuring (or preparing) twice in the same basis with phases $\\alpha, \\beta$ is one preparation with phase $\\alpha+\\beta$. **Bialgebra** is subtler: it states that the Z-multiplication and X-comultiplication form a bialgebra, i.e., copying X-eigenstates through Z-multiplication distributes coherently. Concretely, a Z-spider with one input and two outputs feeding an X-spider with two inputs and one output rewrites to a $K_{2,2}$-like pattern --- the diagrammatic engine behind measurement-based quantum computing's byproduct corrections. The **Hopf law** strengthens this: with the antipode (here trivial, since $\\pi$-phases are self-inverse up to the rules), two parallel connections annihilate. The **pi-copy** rule is the workhorse of Clifford simplification: a $\\pi$ Z-spider (a Pauli Z, up to phase) pushed through an X-spider *copies* to every leg, toggling each leg's phase by $\\pi$. This is exactly Pauli propagation through Clifford circuits, but it applies to *arbitrary* spiders, which is why ZX rewriting generalizes the stabilizer formalism.

> **Theorem (Spider theorem).** *Any connected diagram built from spiders of a single color and arbitrary phases is equal, by fusion alone, to a single spider of that color whose phase is the sum of the constituent phases and whose arity is the number of dangling wires.*

This normal-form property is what makes the calculus computationally tractable: fusion is confluent and terminating, so every diagram has a unique *fused* representative obtainable greedily.

### 4.2 Completeness: Backens, and Jeandel--Perdrix--Vilmart

The original ZX rule set was **incomplete**: there exist equalities of matrices not derivable graphically [3]. The completeness program proceeded fragment by fragment. Backens [2] proved completeness for the **single-qubit Clifford+T group** by constructing a normal form for single-qubit diagrams and showing the rules rewrite every diagram into it --- the first completeness result for a non-classically-simulable fragment. The breakthrough for the multi-qubit case came from Jeandel, Perdrix and Vilmart [3], who added four new axioms (including a form of *supplementarity*, which equates certain highly entangled states whose equality is invisible to the old rules) and proved completeness for the full **Clifford+T fragment** via a detour through the **ZW-calculus**, a related graphical language for integer matrices whose completeness was already known. As a corollary, they characterized the fragment exactly: the $\\pi/4$-fragment of ZX represents precisely the matrices over a finite-dimensional extension of the dyadic rationals $\\mathbb{Z}[1/2, e^{i\\pi/4}]$.

> **Theorem (Jeandel--Perdrix--Vilmart [3]).** *The ZX-calculus with the four additional axioms is complete for Clifford+T quantum mechanics: for any two diagrams $D_1, D_2$ with phases in $\\frac{\\pi}{4}\\mathbb{Z}$, $\\llbracket D_1 \\rrbracket = \\llbracket D_2 \\rrbracket$ implies $D_1$ rewrites to $D_2$.*

Completeness is not a curiosity; it is the *license* for optimization. An optimizer that rewrites diagrams is complete exactly when no true simplification is forever out of reach of its rules. The PyZX strategy is justified by [3]: within Clifford+T, every valid T-count reduction is in principle derivable, so the engineering question is purely *search* --- finding short derivations fast.

### 4.3 The PyZX pipeline: from circuits to graphs and back

Kissinger and van de Wetering's optimizer [4] proceeds in stages:

1. **Translation to graph-like form.** The circuit becomes a ZX-diagram, then a graph-like diagram: only Z-spiders, only plain/Hadamard edges. T gates become $\\pi/4$ spiders; Clifford gates become $\\pi/2$ or $\\pi$ spiders and Hadamards.
2. **Clifford simplification.** Interior Clifford spiders are eliminated by two graph rules. *Local complementation* removes a $\\pm\\pi/2$ spider while complementing the connectivity of its neighborhood and updating phases; *pivoting* removes a pair of connected $\\pi$ spiders (or $0$/$\\pi$ pairs) while toggling edges between their neighborhoods. Both are derivable from the core rules, both strictly reduce spider count, and both run in polynomial time. Iterated to fixpoint, they *completely* eliminate interior Clifford structure --- a constructive, graphical proof of the Gottesman--Knill theorem.
3. **Phase gadgets.** Remaining non-Clifford spiders are converted to **phase gadgets**: a $\\alpha$-phase Z-spider connected via Hadamard edges to a phaseless X-spider hub. Gadgets have a clean semantics (diagonal unitaries $\\exp(i\\alpha Z^{\\otimes k}/2)$) and, crucially, *compatible* gadgets (same hub connectivity up to Clifford) can be **fused**, adding their phases. Non-local T-cancellation becomes local gadget fusion.
4. **Identity removal and gadget fusion.** Phaseless gadgets vanish; equal gadgets merge. Steps 2--4 iterate until no rule applies.
5. **Extraction.** The reduced graph-like diagram is converted back to a circuit (Section 4.5).

The procedure is *self-checking* [4]: the rewrite sequence itself constitutes a proof that input and output circuits are equal, so the optimizer can independently validate its own result --- a property gate-level optimizers, which rely on separately verified rewrite templates, do not enjoy.

### 4.4 Phase teleportation and spider-nest identities

Two refinements capture *non-local* cancellations that fixpoint rewriting misses:

**Phase teleportation** [4] runs the simplification *symbolically*: phases are variables $\\alpha_1, \\dots, \\alpha_n$ tracked in a table $\\tau$. When gadget fusion would combine spiders $i$ and $j$, the table updates $\\tau'(i) := \\tau(i) \\pm \\tau(j)$, $\\tau'(j) := 0$ instead of committing to a numeric sum. After fixpoint, the table reveals which original phases *must* cancel, and the output circuit is reconstructed with those phases deleted --- achieving the same T-count as full reduction while *preserving the original circuit's two-qubit gate structure*, which full extraction may scramble. This makes it ideal as a preprocessing pass before other optimizers.

**Spider-nest identities** [5,6] are composite rules: a *spider nest* is a diagram consisting solely of $\\pi/4$ phase gadgets; certain nests equal the identity. De Beaudrap, Bian and Wang [5] derived nest identities from the phase-polynomial structure of diagonal Clifford+T circuits and showed they subsume many ad-hoc T-cancellation tricks, obtaining improved T-counts "in run-times typically less than the time required to make a fresh cup of coffee." Wang [6] independently derived the same identities via the ZX representation of the logical AND gate (the *triangle* node), proved **QBC-completeness** (completeness for quantum Boolean circuits), and gave an algorithm outperforming all previous best non-probabilistic T-count optimizers. The conceptual advance: instead of rules that modify one or two spiders, nest identities *match an entire subdiagram* --- a shift from local to *pattern* rewriting that the ZX framework supports naturally because diagrams are graphs.

### 4.5 Circuit extraction: the hard way back

Simplification produces a graph-like diagram; usefulness demands a *circuit*. Extraction is the pipeline's hardest stage: a graph-like diagram has no inherent gate order, and naive extraction can explode the two-qubit count even as T-count falls. PyZX extracts by identifying a *causal order* (a flow or gflow on the graph, generalizing measurement-pattern determinism from MBQC) and synthesizing gates along it; when no flow exists, ancilla-assisted or approximate extraction is used. The trade-off is fundamental and visible in benchmarks: `full_reduce` minimizes T-count aggressively, while `teleport_reduce` preserves two-qubit structure at identical T-count [4]. Choosing between them is a *cost-model* decision --- fault-tolerant architectures price T gates orders of magnitude above CNOTs, while NISQ devices price them comparably --- and the ZX framework is unusual in offering both from one rewrite engine.

## 5 Empirical Results and Theoretical Guarantees

### Benchmark evidence

| Study | Benchmark | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Kissinger & van de Wetering [4] | Standard circuit suite | Matches/beats prior ancilla-free T-count optimizers on majority; **up to 50% improvement** in some cases |
| Review of ZX optimization | 36 structured circuits, `full_reduce` | Improves SOTA T-count on **17%**, matches on **72%** |
| `full_reduce` + TODD | Same 36 circuits | Improves T-count on **56%** of circuits |
| De Beaudrap et al. [5] | Various circuits, spider nests | Improvements in "less than a cup of coffee" runtime |
| Wang [6] | T-count benchmarks | Outperforms all previous best *non-probabilistic* algorithms |
| Qiskit comparison (review) | Compiled circuits | 26--40% execution-time improvement from reduced global-phase gates |

### Why T-count is the right metric

In surface-code fault tolerance, a Clifford gate costs $O(d)$ physical operations for code distance $d$, while a T gate requires a distilled $|T\\rangle$ magic state whose factory costs $O(d^3)$ spacetime volume [4]. A 50% T-count reduction therefore translates, to first order, into a near-50% reduction in the *dominant* cost of the fault-tolerant computation. T-count optimization is also *provably hard* in general: minimizing T-count is related to NP-hard tensor decomposition and to decoding exponentially long Reed--Muller codes [5], so the ZX approach --- a portfolio of efficient, complete-rule-derived tactics rather than exact search --- is the theoretically sanctioned strategy.

### Guarantees

Three guarantees compose: (i) **soundness** of every rewrite step, so the optimizer can never produce a wrong circuit; (ii) **completeness** of the rule set for Clifford+T [3], so no valid simplification is ruled out a priori; (iii) **termination** of the simplification strategy (each rule strictly decreases a well-founded measure: spider count, then non-Clifford count), so `full_reduce` always halts. Together they make PyZX-style optimization *total and correct by construction* --- the output is always a valid, fully reduced circuit, with the rewrite trace as its own certificate.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Completeness stops at Clifford+T.** The full ZX-calculus with arbitrary phases is incomplete; the known complete axiomatizations require either phase restrictions or additional axiom schemas (e.g., cyclotomic supplementarity). Optimizing circuits with arbitrary rotation angles (variational algorithms, QAOA) therefore rests on shakier foundations, though phase teleportation extends *unmodified* to arbitrary angles [4] as a heuristic.
2. **Extraction can inflate two-qubit count.** Aggressive T-count reduction rewrites the diagram's graph structure; the extracted circuit may need many more CNOTs. `teleport_reduce` mitigates this but does not eliminate the underlying tension --- the diagram has no canonical circuit form.
3. **No optimality guarantees.** T-count minimization is NP-hard-adjacent [5]; the pipeline is a greedy portfolio of tactics. Spider nests [5,6] and TODD pairing improve results, but no efficient algorithm certifies *optimal* T-count.
4. **Scalar blindness.** Most implementations work *up to global scalar*, tracking phases but ignoring normalization. For unitary circuits this is harmless; for measurement-heavy or post-selected computations, scalar tracking matters and complicates rewriting.
5. **Scalability of graph rewriting.** Local complementation on dense graphs is $O(n^2)$ per step; thousand-qubit diagrams with dense entanglement remain expensive, and the pattern-matching for nest identities scales with subdiagram size.
6. **Beyond qubits.** The ZX-calculus is qubit-native. Qudit generalizations (ZXW, qudit ZX) exist with their own completeness results, but the optimization pipeline --- gadgets, nests, extraction --- is far less developed there.

## 7 Conclusion

The ZX-calculus began as a categorical observation --- that complementary observables are interacting Frobenius algebras [1] --- and became, via the completeness theorems of Backens [2] and Jeandel--Perdrix--Vilmart [3], the first diagrammatic language in which *every* true Clifford+T equality is provable by rewriting. Kissinger and van de Wetering [4] turned that proof theory into an optimizer that beats gate-level synthesis at its own game, not by searching harder but by *representing smarter*: spiders and phases expose the cancellations that gate sequences hide. Phase teleportation and spider-nest identities [5,6] show the paradigm still has headroom, capturing non-local cancellations as first-class rewrite patterns. The remaining challenges --- extraction without CNOT blowup, completeness beyond Clifford+T, optimality certificates --- are now sharply posed *because* the calculus exists to pose them in. Gate-level synthesis optimizes circuits; the ZX-calculus optimizes the mathematics circuits denote. That change of level is its enduring contribution.
