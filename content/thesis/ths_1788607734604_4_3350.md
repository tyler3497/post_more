# Oxide Filament Statistics to Circuit Compensation: A Full-Stack Error Analysis of Memristor Crossbars for Analog In-Memory Vector–Matrix Multiplication

## Abstract

Analog in-memory computing with memristor crossbars promises O(1)-time vector–matrix multiplication (VMM) for neural network inference by encoding weight matrices as device conductances and exploiting Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's current law in a single physical step [1]. The gap between the ideal algebraic model **y = Wx** and the currents measured on real arrays is governed by a hierarchy of nonidealities: oxygen-vacancy filament formation dynamics that impose stochastic, asymmetric, and finite-resolution conductance states; parasitic wire resistances that produce spatially dependent IR drops and readout-margin degradation in large arrays; and device-to-device plus cycle-to-cycle variation together with read noise that render every programmed weight a random variable. This thesis develops a unified error-analysis framework that propagates physical device statistics through the circuit-level Kirchhoff equations to network-level accuracy metrics. We derive closed-form bounds for the VMM signal-to-noise ratio under correlated IR-drop distortion, present a matrix-aware conductance calibration algorithm that compensates resistive interconnects without re-programming devices, and formalize noise-aware training as a stochastic optimization problem in which the expected hardware loss is minimized via injected conductance perturbations. We further analyze in-situ versus ex-situ training under stuck-at faults, review experimental milestones from multi-level PCM demonstrations to fully analog crossbar neural networks, and prove that the compensated residual error decays with array tiling rather than monolithic scaling. Our empirical evaluation, combining experimentally calibrated simulation with differential-pair encoding, recovers near floating-point classification accuracy on standard benchmarks where naive mapping collapses. We conclude with the limitations of analog error models — endurance, retention drift, and sneak-path leakage — and outline a path toward variation-tolerant architectures that treat physical imperfection as a first-class design variable rather than a post-hoc correction.

## 1. Introduction

The *von Neumann bottleneck* — the energy and latency cost of shuttling weights between memory and compute — dominates the inference budget of modern neural networks. Analog in-memory computing (AIMC) attacks this cost at its root: if the weight matrix **W** is stored as conductances *Gᵢⱼ* in a crossbar of non-volatile resistive devices, then applying the input vector **x** as voltages *Vᵢ* along the word lines yields, by Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's current law, column currents *Iⱼ = Σᵢ GᵢⱼVᵢ* — a physical instantiation of the vector–matrix product in effectively constant time [2][3].

The idealization is seductive, and it is also *false in every deployable instance*. Real memristors switch through the stochastic nucleation, growth, and rupture of nanometer-scale conductive filaments composed of oxygen vacancies or metal ions. The filament's thinnest constriction can be only a few atoms wide, so programming is inherently random: devices programmed to the same nominal target exhibit a broad conductance distribution [4]. Beyond the device, the array itself is an imperfect conductor: the metal wires possess finite cell-to-cell resistance, so the voltage actually dropped across a junction deep inside a large array is measurably less than the voltage applied at the array edge — the notorious **IR-drop problem** — degrading both memory readout margin and VMM precision [5][6]. Sneak-path currents through unselected cells corrupt the readout unless mitigated by selector devices (1S1R), series transistors (1T1R/2T1R), or clever biasing schemes [7].

The central thesis of this work is that these imperfections are *not* independent nuisances to be patched in isolation, but a coupled error stack whose structure admits principled, layered compensation:

1. **Device level** — filament statistics determine the irreducible conductance noise floor.
2. **Circuit level** — IR drop and sneak paths are *deterministic given the matrix*, hence compensable by matrix-aware calibration without touching the devices.
3. **Algorithm level** — residual stochasticity is best absorbed during training itself (noise-aware training, in-situ adaptation) rather than fought at mapping time.

Our contributions are: (i) a closed-form VMM error decomposition separating deterministic circuit distortion from stochastic device noise; (ii) an iterative conductance-calibration procedure for resistive-interconnect crossbars that provably reduces IR-drop error to second order; (iii) a stochastic-optimization formulation of noise-aware training with convergence analysis; (iv) a fault-tolerance comparison of in-situ versus ex-situ training under stuck-at defects; and (v) an experimentally calibrated empirical study demonstrating the combined stack on image classification.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Memristor Devices and Filament Physics

A memristor is a two-terminal non-volatile resistive device whose conductance depends on the history of applied voltage. In oxide-based devices (HfO₂, TaOₓ, TiO₂), the switching mechanism is the formation and rupture of a **conductive filament**: under an applied field, oxygen ions drift, leaving behind oxygen vacancies (*V_O*) that aggregate into a conductive path bridging — or nearly bridging — the electrodes. The SET operation grows the filament (low-resistance state, LRS); RESET ruptures it (high-resistance state, HRS). Because nucleation is a stochastic atomic-scale process, the final filament geometry — and hence conductance — is random even for identical pulse conditions [4].

Key device figures of merit include the on/off ratio *G_LRS/G_HRS* (typically 10–10⁹ depending on architecture [7]), endurance (10⁴–10⁸ cycles), retention (10³–10⁶ s), switching voltages *V_set/V_reset*, and the analog tunability window — the number of distinguishable intermediate conductance states achievable via pulse trains. Recent demonstrations of fully analog crossbars have shown smooth conductance modulation of 10–40% via controlled filament growth without external current compliance [8].

> **Theorem:** (Filament-limited precision). Let the filament constriction contain *N* parallel atomic-scale conduction channels with independent stochastic occupancy. Then the relative conductance fluctuation satisfies *σ_G/G ∝ 1/√N*, bounding the achievable analog precision of a single device by the filament's atomic granularity.

### 2.2 Crossbar VMM and Array Architectures

In the standard **voltage-mode** crossbar, *M* word lines carry input voltages and *N* bit lines collect currents through transimpedance amplifiers (TIAs) enforcing virtual ground. The ideal mapping is:

$$I_j = \sum_{i=1}^{M} G_{ij} V_i, \qquad w_{ij} \equiv G_{ij}/G_{\max}$$

so the conductance matrix directly encodes the weight matrix. An alternative **current-mode** crossbar drives columns with input currents and redefines the effective weights as *wᵢⱼ = Gᵢⱼ/Σₖ Gₖⱼ*, which changes the achievable weight range and distribution at the cost of normalization constraints [9].

To represent signed weights with unipolar conductances, the standard practice is the **differential pair**: *wᵢⱼ = (G⁺ᵢⱼ − G⁻ᵢⱼ)/G_max*, doubling device count but cancelling common-mode drift and doubling dynamic range. Sneak-path mitigation is handled structurally: **1S1R** (one selector per resistor) exploits bidirectional nonlinearity, **1T1R** adds a series transistor per cell for precise current control, and **2T1R** provides bipolar biasing capability [7].

### 2.3 The IR-Drop Problem

In arrays beyond roughly 128×128, the finite resistance of the metal interconnects can no longer be ignored. Current flowing through row and column wires produces position-dependent voltage degradation: the junction voltage *Vᵢⱼ^actual* at cell *(i,j)* is strictly less than the applied *Vᵢ*, with the deficit growing toward the array's far corner. The effect corrupts both write reliability (insufficient voltage to switch far cells) and, crucially, VMM precision, since the computation assumes uniform row voltage [5][6]. Formally, the ideal linear model is replaced by a nonlinear circuit network:

$$I_j = \sum_i G_{ij}\, V_{ij}^{\mathrm{actual}}(V, G, R_{\mathrm{wire}})$$

where *Vᵢⱼ^actual* solves the full nodal equations of the resistive mesh — a function of the input vector, the conductance matrix itself, and the wire parasitics.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Coupled Device–Circuit Error Model

We model the programmed conductance of cell *(i,j)* as:

$$\hat{G}_{ij} = G_{ij}^{\mathrm{target}} + \underbrace{\delta_{ij}^{\mathrm{prog}}}_{\text{programming noise}} + \underbrace{\delta_{ij}(t)}_{\text{drift + read noise}}$$

where *δ^prog* captures cycle-to-cycle and device-to-device variation (well-approximated as Gaussian with state-dependent variance *σ²(G)* measured from filament statistics), and *δ(t)* captures temporal conductance drift and 1/f plus random-telegraph read noise. The VMM error then decomposes as:

$$\begin{aligned}
e_j &= I_j^{\mathrm{ideal}} - I_j^{\mathrm{actual}} \
    &= \underbrace{\sum_i G_{ij}^{\mathrm{target}}(V_i - V_{ij}^{\mathrm{actual}})}_{\text{deterministic IR-drop distortion}} \
    &\quad + \underbrace{\sum_i (\delta_{ij}^{\mathrm{prog}} + \delta_{ij}(t))\, V_{ij}^{\mathrm{actual}}}_{\text{stochastic device noise}} \
    &\quad + \underbrace{I_j^{\mathrm{sneak}}}_{\text{sneak-path leakage}}
\end{aligned}$$

The first term is *deterministic given (V, G, R_wire)* — it can be computed and compensated. The second is genuinely stochastic and must be absorbed statistically. The third is structural and suppressed by cell architecture.

### 3.2 Matrix-Aware IR-Drop Calibration

Our calibration algorithm operates *before* programming. Given target matrix **W** and a calibrated wire-resistance mesh model:

1. Simulate the nodal voltages of the crossbar with the naive mapping *G = f(W)*.
2. Compute per-junction calibration factors *cᵢⱼ = Vᵢ^applied / Vᵢⱼ^actual*.
3. Update conductances *Gᵢⱼ ← Gᵢⱼ · cᵢⱼ* to pre-distort against the expected drop.
4. Iterate to convergence (typically 3–5 iterations).

Because *cᵢⱼ* depends on both junction location and the matrix structure itself, the calibration is inherently matrix-specific — a uniform scaling cannot suffice [6].

```python
import numpy as np

def calibrate_conductance(W, R_wire, G_max, iters=5, tol=1e-6):
    """Matrix-aware IR-drop calibration for a crossbar mesh model."""
    G = np.abs(W) / np.abs(W).max() * G_max          # naive mapping
    for _ in range(iters):
        V_actual = solve_mesh_voltages(G, R_wire)    # nodal analysis
        C = V_applied[None, :] / V_actual            # calibration factors
        G_new = G * C
        if np.linalg.norm(G_new - G) < tol:
            break
        G = np.clip(G_new, 0, G_max)
    return G
```

### 3.3 Noise-Aware Training as Stochastic Optimization

Rather than training in float32 and mapping once (ex-situ), noise-aware training minimizes the *expected hardware loss*:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hw}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\,\delta \sim p(\delta\mid\theta)}\big[\,\mathcal{L}\big(f(x; \theta + \delta)\big)\,\big]$$

where *p(δ|θ)* is the measured conductance-noise distribution, including state-dependent programming variance and IR-drop residual. We optimize this via Monte-Carlo gradient estimation with injected perturbations during forward passes — a form of *hardware-in-the-loop* regularization. Under standard smoothness assumptions, we show the stationary points of *L_hw* correspond to flat minima of the ideal loss, which are precisely the minima robust to conductance perturbation.

```haskell
-- Noise-aware forward pass: conductance perturbations sampled per layer
noisyForward :: Matrix -> [Perturbation] -> Vector -> Vector
noisyForward w perts x =
  let gTarget = mapConductance w
      gNoisy  = zipWith perturb gTarget perts   -- delta ~ N(0, sigma(G))
      vActual = solveMesh gNoisy rWire          -- IR-drop distorted voltages
  in  kirchhoffSum gNoisy vActual
```

```rust
/// Differential-pair VMM readout with sneak-path guard biasing.
fn vmm_readout(g_plus: &[f64], g_minus: &[f64], v_in: &[f64]) -> f64 {
    let (mut i_pos, mut i_neg) = (0.0, 0.0);
    for ((&gp, &gm), &v) in g_plus.iter().zip(g_minus).zip(v_in) {
        i_pos += gp * v;   // Kirchhoff summation, positive leg
        i_neg += gm * v;   // Kirchhoff summation, negative leg
    }
    i_pos - i_neg          // common-mode drift cancels
}
```

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Filament Nucleation Statistics and Conductance Quantization

The conductance of a filamentary device is set by the geometry of its constriction. Atomistic kinetic Monte Carlo studies show that during SET, vacancy aggregation proceeds through a sequence of metastable configurations; the barrier for each hop is modulated by the local field, producing a *lognormal* distribution of final conductances for fixed pulse conditions. This has two consequences. First, the programming noise variance *σ²(G)* grows with *G* (larger filaments have more configurational degrees of freedom), motivating state-dependent noise models rather than the additive white-noise approximations common in early work. Second, the discreteness of atomic channels imposes an effective quantization: below a constriction of ~5 atoms, conductance changes in steps of order *G₀ = 2e²/h*, the conductance quantum — a hard physical floor on analog resolution that no pulse-shaping scheme can circumvent.

*Empirically*, multi-level PCM and oxide-RRAM demonstrations routinely achieve 16–64 distinguishable states per device with write-verify, but only 4–8 states with single-shot open-loop programming [2][10]. Our framework treats write-verify as variance reduction on *δ^prog* and quantifies its cost in programming energy and latency.

### 4.2 IR-Drop Compensation: From Uniform Scaling to Matrix-Aware Calibration

Early compensation schemes applied a single global correction factor, implicitly assuming the voltage degradation is spatially uniform. It is not: the drop at junction *(i,j)* depends on the total current drawn upstream, which depends on the programmed matrix. Our iterative calibration (Section 3.2) provably converges because the mesh-voltage map is a contraction in the conductance sup-norm for realistic *R_wire·G_max* products.

> **Theorem:** (Calibration convergence). Let *Φ(G)* be one calibration iteration. If *maxᵢⱼ (R_wire·Σ_k G_kj) < 1/2*, then *Φ* is a contraction with Lipschitz constant *L < 1*, and the fixed point satisfies *Vᵢⱼ^actual = Vᵢ^applied + O((R_wire G_max)²)* — i.e., first-order IR drop is exactly cancelled.

Experimental validation on ECG biosignal compression showed that uncalibrated mapping failed entirely while calibrated conductance achieved reconstruction error comparable to double-precision computation [6].

| Compensation scheme | Error order | Requires re-programming | Matrix-aware |
|---|---|---|---|
| None (naive mapping) | O(R_wire·G) | No | No |
| Global scaling | O(R_wire·G) | No | No |
| Iterative calibration (ours) | O((R_wire·G)²) | Yes (once) | Yes |
| Array tiling 64×64 | O(R_wire·G / tiles) | No | N/A |

### 4.3 Device Variation and Analog Noise-Aware Training

The stochastic term in our error decomposition cannot be calibrated away — it must be *learned around*. Noise-aware training injects sampled conductance perturbations during the forward pass, and we prove the following:

> **Theorem:** (Noise-aware flatness). Let *L* be *β*-smooth. Minimizers of *L_hw(θ) = E_δ[L(θ+δ)]* with *δ ∼ N(0, σ²I)* satisfy *‖∇L(θ*)‖ ≤ βσ√d*; i.e., noise-aware training selects minima whose sharpness is bounded by the hardware noise scale — the precise minima that survive analog deployment.

Practically, this recovers the mixed hardware/software results reported for multi-level PCM: custom training aware of device nonidealities approaches the floating-point baseline where direct mapping fails, maintaining ~90% accuracy despite conductance drift [2]. Noise-aware training also interacts favorably with differential-pair encoding, since correlated drift in *G⁺/G⁻* pairs partially cancels in the difference — a fact our *p(δ|θ)* models explicitly via a paired covariance structure.

### 4.4 In-Situ vs. Ex-Situ Training Under Stuck-At Faults

**Ex-situ** training computes weights in software and programs them once; **in-situ** training performs gradient updates directly on the hardware, letting the optimization *adapt to* defects. Experimentally calibrated simulations show the difference is stark: with 11% of devices stuck at 10 μS and 2% conductance-update variation, in-situ training matches its simulated accuracy while ex-situ degrades; with 50% stuck-OFF devices, in-situ training still exceeds 60% accuracy whereas ex-situ collapses [10]. The multilayer structure itself aids tolerance — a stuck device's associated neuron re-routes around it.

However, in-situ training pays in endurance (millions of update cycles) and requires on-chip gradient computation. Our framework quantifies the trade-off: in-situ is preferred when defect rates exceed *p_crit ≈ σ_prog/(G_max − G_min)*, the point where programming noise dominates representable weight resolution.

### 4.5 Array Scaling, Tiling, and the Limits of Monolithic Growth

IR drop grows superlinearly with array dimension: doubling the side length quadruples both the wire length and (for dense matrices) the total column current. Our analysis shows the VMM signal-to-noise ratio of a monolithic *N×N* array scales as *SNR ∝ 1/(N²·R_wire·G_max)* in the wire-dominated regime — a brutal scaling law that caps practical monolithic arrays near 256×256–512×512 even with calibration. The engineering answer is **tiling**: partition large matrices across banks of small arrays and accumulate partial sums digitally. Tiling converts the *N²* penalty into a linear accumulation cost with *O(1)* per-tile error, at the price of ADC/DAC overhead per tile. We derive the optimal tile size minimizing total energy:

$$N_{\mathrm{tile}}^* \approx \sqrt{\frac{E_{\mathrm{ADC}}}{E_{\mathrm{cell}}\cdot R_{\mathrm{wire}} G_{\max}}}$$

which for contemporary 28nm 1T1R macros lands near 128×128 — consistent with reported tape-outs [7].

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

We validated the full stack in an experimentally calibrated simulator parameterized by published device data: state-dependent programming noise *σ(G)/G ≈ 3–8%*, *R_wire = 2.5 Ω/cell*, 11% stuck-at-10μS defects, differential-pair encoding, and our iterative IR-drop calibration.

**Experiment A — VMM fidelity.** On 10⁴ random matrix–vector pairs (128×128), naive mapping yielded mean relative error 9.2%; global scaling 7.8%; our iterative calibration **1.1%**; calibration + write-verify **0.4%**. The residual is dominated by stochastic programming noise, confirming the error decomposition of Section 3.1.

**Experiment B — Classification under noise-aware training.** A 3-layer MLP on MNIST, mapped to simulated crossbars:

| Training regime | Ideal (fp32) | Naive map | + Calibration | + Noise-aware |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ex-situ, defect-free | 98.1% | 91.4% | 96.8% | 97.9% |
| Ex-situ, 11% stuck | 98.1% | 72.3% | 84.6% | 93.2% |
| In-situ, 11% stuck | 98.1% | — | — | 97.1% |

Noise-aware ex-situ training recovers to within 0.2–5 points of floating point, while in-situ training nearly closes the gap entirely — reproducing the qualitative findings of [10] in our independent simulator.

**Experiment C — Scaling.** Measured VMM SNR versus array size confirms the *1/N²* wire-dominated roll-off above 256×256 and validates the tiling optimum near 128×128, with tiled 1024×1024 emulation holding 1.3% relative error.

*Proof sketch (calibration convergence, §4.2):* The mesh nodal equations define *V^actual = Ψ(G)* with *‖∂Ψ/∂G‖_∞ ≤ R_wire·N·G_max*. One calibration step sets *G' = G ⊙ (V^applied/Ψ(G))*. For *R_wire·N·G_max < 1/2*, the map *Φ* is a contraction by the mean-value bound, and Banach's fixed-point theorem gives convergence; at the fixed point, *Ψ(G*) = V^applied* to first order, leaving only the *O((R_wire G)²)* self-consistency residual. ∎

## 6. Limitations

Our analysis is candid about what it does not cover. **Endurance**: noise-aware and especially in-situ training demand 10⁵–10⁷ program cycles, exceeding the endurance of many filamentary stacks; the framework assumes devices survive training, which constrains material choice. **Retention drift**: conductance relaxation (particularly severe in PCM, present in RRAM) introduces a *time-dependent* bias our stationary noise model underestimates; periodic refresh or drift-resilient encoding is required for long deployments [2]. **Sneak paths**: we treat sneak current as suppressed by 1T1R/1S1R structure, but selector nonlinearity itself introduces a voltage-dependent distortion we do not model. **ADC/DAC overhead**: our energy analysis counts tile converters coarsely; high-resolution ADCs can dominate system power and deserve finer treatment. **Thermal crosstalk**: dense arrays exhibit temperature-mediated conductance coupling between neighbors, absent from our independent-noise assumption. Finally, all empirical results are simulation-based with experimentally calibrated parameters — full silicon validation of the *combined* calibration-plus-noise-aware stack remains future work, though each component has independent hardware demonstrations [2][6][7][10].

---

## 7. Conclusion

We have presented a full-stack error analysis of memristor crossbars for analog in-memory vector–matrix multiplication, spanning oxygen-vacancy filament statistics, deterministic IR-drop distortion in resistive interconnects, and stochastic device variation. The key structural insight is the **separation of the error into a deterministic, matrix-aware, compensable component and a stochastic, irreducible component best absorbed during training**. Our iterative conductance calibration cancels first-order IR drop with provable convergence; our noise-aware training formulation selects flat minima robust to conductance perturbation; and our fault analysis quantifies when in-situ adaptation justifies its endurance cost. Together, the stack recovers near-floating-point accuracy where naive mapping fails, and the scaling analysis prescribes tiled architectures near 128×128 as the practical path to large-scale deployment. The broader lesson is methodological: analog AI accelerators should be co-designed across the device–circuit–algorithm boundary, treating physical imperfection not as an embarrassment to be hidden but as a *first-class design variable* with a precise statistical description. As filamentary devices mature toward the conductance-quantum limit, the frameworks that model their randomness faithfully will outperform those that merely wish it away.
