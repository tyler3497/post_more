# Lattice-Based Post-Quantum Cryptography: Module-LWE, Kyber/Dilithium, and the Number Theoretic Transform

## Abstract

The impending arrival of cryptographically relevant quantum computers has compelled a global migration away from RSA, finite-field Diffie–Hellman, and elliptic-curve cryptography, all of which fall to Shor's polynomial-time algorithm for integer factorization and discrete logarithms. Lattice-based cryptography has emerged as the leading replacement family, anchored by the Learning with Errors problem introduced by Regev and its algebraic variants. This article gives a mathematically rigorous, implementation-aware treatment of **Module Learning with Errors**, the hardness foundation on which NIST standardized CRYSTALS-Kyber as **ML-KEM** (FIPS 203) and CRYSTALS-Dilithium as **ML-DSA** (FIPS 204) in August 2024. We develop the algebraic architecture of module lattices over the cyclotomic ring Z_q[x] modulo x^n+1, trace the worst-case to average-case reduction lineage from Regev through Lyubashevsky–Peikert–Regev to Langlois–Stehlé, and dissect the Kyber key-encapsulation mechanism and Dilithium Fiat–Shamir signature at the level of their defining algorithms. A full section is devoted to the **Number Theoretic Transform**, the discrete-Fourier analog over finite fields that makes polynomial arithmetic in these schemes fast enough for deployment, including NTT-friendly prime selection and the negative-wrapped convolution trick used by both standards. We close with concrete security estimates, side-channel and failure-fidelity caveats, and the open problems that remain before lattice cryptography can claim maturity comparable to the elliptic-curve era it displaces.

##1 Intro

In 1994, Peter Shor showed that a quantum computer can factor integers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time [1]. This single result underwrote an existential deadline for public-key cryptography: every widely deployed key-exchange and signature scheme — RSA, Diffie–Hellman, ECDH, ECDSA, EdDSA — rests on exactly the problems Shor solves. The National Institute of Standards and Technology launched its Post-Quantum Cryptography standardization project in 2016 to find replacements believed to resist quantum attack, and in August 2024 published its first three standards: **FIPS 203** (ML-KEM, the Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber), **FIPS 204** (ML-DSA, the Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, the hash-based backup) [2][3].

Of these, the two lattice standards carry the heaviest load: ML-KEM is the primary key-establishment mechanism for TLS, messaging, and VPNs, while ML-DSA is the primary general-purpose signature scheme. Both rest on a single hardness foundation: the **Module Learning with Errors** (Module-LWE) problem, together with its sibling Module-SIS for signatures. Module lattices interpolate between the plain lattices of Regev's original LWE — flexible but slow — and the ideal lattices of Ring-LWE — blazingly fast but structurally rigid [4][5][6]. The module rank k provides a continuous security knob, allowing parameter sets that trade a modest efficiency cost against the worry that too much algebraic structure aids the adversary.

This article has three objectives. First, to present the algebraic foundations of module lattices and Module-LWE with full mathematical precision, including the reduction lineage that justifies confidence in their hardness. Second, to open the hoods of ML-KEM and ML-DSA, explaining the Kyber IND-CCA construction and the Dilithium Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts signature at the level at which an implementer or cryptanalyst must understand them. Third, to explain the computational engine that makes all of this practical: the **Number Theoretic Transform**, without which polynomial multiplication in rings of dimension 256 or 512 would be prohibitively expensive for constrained devices. Along the way we give concrete parameters, sizes, and security estimates drawn from the standards and the literature.

##2 Background

### Lattices and hard problems

A *lattice* L is a discrete additive subgroup of R^n, generated by a basis of n linearly independent vectors. The **Shortest Vector Problem** (SVP) asks to find the shortest non-zero vector of L; the **Shortest Independent Vectors Problem** (SIVP) asks for n linearly independent vectors whose lengths are bounded by the n-th successive minimum. Ajtai's seminal 1996 result gave the first worst-case to average-case reduction: if one can solve random instances of his lattice function family on average, one can solve SVP in the worst case [7]. This reduction is the founding philosophical move of lattice cryptography: cryptographic instances may be *generated* with structure (as samples), while security reduces to *unstructured* worst-case geometry that no algorithm is known to break even in quantum polynomial time.

> Theorem: (Ajtai, 1996) For suitable polynomial n and modulus q, solving the average-case Short Integer Solution problem implies a polynomial-time solution to worst-case approximate SVP in arbitrary n-dimensional lattices.

### Learning with Errors

Regev's LWE problem turns Ajtai's template into encryption-friendly form. Let q be a modulus, n a dimension, and chi a narrow discrete error distribution over Z_q. The *search-LWE* problem is: given samples of the form (a, b) with a drawn uniformly from Z_q^n and b equal to the inner product of a with a secret s plus error e drawn from chi, recover s [8]. The *decision-LWE* variant asks to distinguish such samples from uniform random pairs. For error rate alpha q greater than two times the square root of n, Regev proved a quantum reduction from worst-case approximate SVP on n-dimensional lattices to decision-LWE. The concrete parameters commonly used satisfy q on the order of n squared and error width proportional to alpha times q.

### Ring and Module variants

Plain LWE needs O(n squared) storage and O(n squared) arithmetic per sample — too slow for high-throughput systems. Lyubashevsky, Peikert, and Regev introduced **Ring-LWE** (2010), replacing the vector space Z_q^n with a polynomial quotient ring R_q = Z_q[x] modulo a cyclotomic polynomial such as x^n+1 [5]. One ring element encodes n coefficients, and multiplication of ring elements is a single quasi-linear operation. The price is algebraic structure: the underlying lattice is an *ideal* lattice, a rank-one module, and one must worry whether ideals admit attacks unavailable against general lattices. No such attack has succeeded against cryptographically deployed parameters, but the concern motivated the interpolation.

Langlois and Stehlé defined **Module-LWE** (2012): work with R_q^k, free modules of rank k over the ring [6]. Setting k = 1 recovers Ring-LWE and letting the ring be Z_q itself recovers plain LWE, so modules unify the landscape. The module rank k gives a tunable dial: Kyber-512 uses k = 2, Kyber-768 uses k = 3, Kyber-1024 uses k = 4, each with the same ring of dimension n = 256. Their reduction shows that Module-LWE over a rank-k module is at least as hard as worst-case lattice problems in dimension k times n, with approximation factors matching the plain-LWE lineage.

The decisional and search variants are: given a uniform matrix A in R_q of shape k by k (Kyber) or k by l (Dilithium) and a vector t = A s + e with short secret s and short error e, distinguish or recover. Both standards rely on the decisional form for their CPA/CCA security arguments.

##3 Methodology

Our treatment proceeds from first principles to engineering. We extract all parameter values directly from the published standards FIPS 203 and FIPS 204 [2][3] and the underlying CRYSTALS specification documents [9][10]. Security estimates are reported as NIST security categories (1 through 5, calibrated against the cost of breaking AES or SHA-3 at stated strengths) and as concrete **core-SVP** bit estimates: the logarithm base two of the gate count required by the best known lattice attacks, typically parameterized by the BKZ block size beta needed to achieve the root Hermite factor delta_0 that breaks the instance.

For performance figures we cite the widely reproduced benchmark corpus: reference C implementations, AVX2-optimized builds, and the pqm4 suite for ARM Cortex-M4 [11][12]. The NTT analysis follows the standard treatment of the discrete Fourier transform over finite fields, including the negative-wrapped convolution formulation that eliminates explicit zero-padding [13]. Throughout, inline citations map to a References section of verifiable primary sources: the FIPS documents, the NIST round-3 submissions, and the foundational papers of the field.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The algebraic machinery of Module-LWE

Fix a power-of-two n and an NTT-friendly prime q. Define R = Z[x] modulo x^n+1 and R_q = R modulo q, the ring of polynomials of degree below n with coefficients modulo q. A **module** of rank k over R_q is the set R_q^k with componentwise addition and scalar multiplication by ring elements; geometrically it is a lattice of dimension k times n over the integers.

- **Secret and error spaces.** Secrets are sampled as vectors s in R_q^k with small coefficients — Kyber uses centered binomial distributions eta equal to 3, 2, or 2 depending on the parameter set; Dilithium samples coefficients uniformly from a small symmetric range around zero.
- **Public randomness.** The matrix A is expanded deterministically from a short seed via SHAKE-128 or SHAKE-256 (XOF mode), so public keys need only carry the seed plus the noisy vector t.
- **The core equation.** The public key relation is t = A s + e, with inner products taken in R_q. Polynomial products inside are computed by the NTT, discussed in section 4.4.

The **decisional Module-LWE assumption** states that (A, A s + e) is computationally indistinguishable from (A, u) with u uniform in R_q^k.

A Haskell-flavored encoding of the problem's type structure makes the algebraic layering explicit:

```haskell
-- Cyclotomic quotient ring R_q = Z_q[x] / (x^n + 1)
newtype Poly (n :: Nat) (q :: Nat) = Poly { coeffs :: Vector q }

-- A rank-k module over R_q
newtype Module (k :: Nat) (n :: Nat) (q :: Nat) = Module { comps :: Vector (Poly n q) }

-- Module-LWE: distinguish (A, A·s + e) from (A, uniform)
mlweSample :: Module k n q -> (Matrix k k (Poly n q), Module k n q)
```

### 4.2 CRYSTALS-Kyber and ML-KEM

Kyber's core is an IND-CPA public-key encryption scheme, **Kyber.CPAPKE**, lifted to an IND-CCA key-encapsulation mechanism via a variant of the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform with explicit rejection [9][2].

1. **Key generation.** Sample secret s and error e from the centered binomial distribution, expand A from seed rho, and set t = A s + e. The public key is the pair of seed and t; the secret key is s (plus bookkeeping material for the FO transform).
2. **Encapsulation.** Sample a fresh ephemeral secret r and errors e_1, e_2, and compute u = A^T r + e_1 and v = t^T r + e_2 + Decompress(message). The ciphertext is the compressed pair (u, v); the shared secret is derived by hashing the message and ciphertext.
3. **Decapsulation.** Recover message = Compress(v − s^T u), then *re-encrypt* the recovered message and compare against the received ciphertext; mismatch triggers output of an implicit pseudorandom key rather than failure, closing chosen-ciphertext oracles.

Compression is the clever bit: coefficients are rounded to d_u and d_v bits, so ciphertext size shrinks dramatically while the introduced rounding noise stays within the decryption tolerance.

| Scheme | Module rank k | pk (bytes) | ct (bytes) | NIST category |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-KEM-512 | 2 | 800 | 768 | 1 (AES-128) |
| ML-KEM-768 | 3 | 1184 | 1088 | 3 (AES-192) |
| ML-KEM-1024 | 4 | 1568 | 1568 | 5 (AES-256) |

ML-KEM-768 is NIST's recommended default: security category 3 at key and ciphertext sizes only slightly larger than classic ECDH transcripts, with decapsulation typically under 100k cycles on modern x86 cores [2][12].

```python
import random

def centered_binomial(eta: int) -> int:
    """Sample from B_eta - B'_eta: coefficients in {-eta..eta}."""
    a = sum(random.getrandbits(1) for _ in range(eta))
    b = sum(random.getrandbits(1) for _ in range(eta))
    return a - b

# Kyber-768 uses eta = 2: coefficients land in {-2,-1,0,1,2}
samples = [centered_binomial(2) for _ in range(10_000)]
assert max(abs(s) for s in samples) <= 2
```

### 4.3 CRYSTALS-Dilithium and ML-DSA

Dilithium builds signatures from the Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts paradigm.

1. **Signing.** Sample a masking vector y with coefficients uniform in a large range. Compute w = A y and extract its high-order bits w_1. Hash the message with w_1 to obtain a sparse challenge polynomial c with exactly tau nonzero coefficients of plus or minus one. Form the candidate z = y + c s_1. If z or the low bits of w − c s_2 fall outside prescribed bounds, **abort and restart** with fresh y — this rejection sampling guarantees the signature distribution is independent of the secret. Otherwise compress hints h that let the verifier recover w_1 from A z − c t, and output (z, h, c).
2. **Verification.** Check that z's coefficients are bounded, recompute w_1' from A z − c t_1 times 2^d plus the hints, and accept iff the hash matches and the hint count is small.

The abort loop is the scheme's distinctive feature: it replaces the Gaussian sampling of earlier lattice signatures (GPV, BLISS) with uniform sampling plus rejection, eliminating the timing side channels that plagued discrete-Gaussian implementations.

| Scheme | (k, l) | pk (bytes) | sig (bytes) | NIST category |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| ML-DSA-65 | (6, 5) | 1952 | 3293 | 3 |
| ML-DSA-87 | (8, 7) | 2592 | 4595 | 5 |

Dilithium's modulus q = 8380417 = 2^23 − 2^13 + 1 admits a 256-point NTT and supports the bit-decomposition tricks (high/low bits, hint packing) on which compact verification depends.

```rust
fn sign_attempt(sk: &SecretKey, msg: &[u8]) -> Option<Signature> {
    let y = sample_mask_vector();                 // uniform in a wide range
    let w = mat_vec_mul(&A, &y);
    let c = hash_to_challenge(msg, &high_bits(&w)); // sparse, tau +-1 coeffs
    let z = add(&y, &poly_vec_mul(&c, &sk.s1));
    if !infinity_norm_ok(&z, GAMMA1 - BETA) { return None; } // abort: restart
    let w_minus = sub(&w, &poly_vec_mul(&c, &sk.s2));
    if !low_bits_ok(&w_minus) { return None; }               // abort: restart
    Some(make_hint_signature(z, w_minus, c))
}
```

### 4.4 The Number Theoretic Transform

All of the above is practical only because polynomial multiplication in R_q runs in quasi-linear time.

- **Why it matters.** Pointwise multiplication of NTT-domain vectors corresponds to *cyclic* convolution. Because our rings quotient by x^n + 1 (negacyclic), the negative-wrapped convolution trick evaluates with a twisted root psi with psi^2 = omega, folding the reduction into the transform itself — no separate modular reduction pass is needed.
- **Prime selection.** Kyber's q = 3329 admits a full 256-point NTT directly. Dilithium's q = 8380417 likewise admits a 256-point NTT, and its size additionally leaves headroom for the coefficient growth produced by challenge-times-secret products before any reduction.
- **Cost profile.** On a Cortex-M4, a forward NTT of length 256 costs on the order of a few thousand cycles; polynomial multiplication that would take n squared = 65536 modular multiplications naively drops to roughly n log n ≈ 2048 butterfly operations plus pointwise products [11].

---

The butterfly — the atomic unit — computes, for twiddle factor zeta:

```python
def butterfly(a: int, b: int, zeta: int, q: int) -> tuple[int, int]:
    t = (zeta * b) % q
    return (a + t) % q, (a - t) % q
```

Stacked in log2(n) = 8 stages, these butterflies form the complete network, and the inverse transform differs only by inverse twiddles and a final multiplication by n^{-1} modulo q.

##5 Empirical/Proofs

### Security reductions

The chain of trust is reductionist. Regev's quantum reduction ties LWE to worst-case GapSVP and SIVP [8]; LPR extend the framework to Ring-LWE with a quantum reduction from worst-case ideal-lattice problems [5]; Langlois–Stehlé give classical and quantum reductions from Module-LWE to worst-case problems on module lattices of dimension k times n [6]. For signatures, Dilithium's unforgeability reduces to Module-LWE plus Module-SIS in the (quantum) random-oracle model, with the Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts analysis following Lyubashevsky's identification-scheme framework [10].

### Concrete cryptanalysis: core-SVP estimates

The best known attacks combine lattice reduction (BKZ with block size beta) with sieving or enumeration for the SVP oracle. Security is quoted as the log2 gate count of the cheapest attack. Representative estimates from the literature and the NIST submissions:

| Target | BKZ block size beta | Core-SVP bits (classical) |
|---|---|---|
| ML-KEM-512 (Kyber512) | ≈ 400 | ≈ 118 |
| ML-KEM-768 (Kyber768) | ≈ 600 | ≈ 183 |
| ML-KEM-1024 (Kyber1024) | ≈ 870 | ≈ 256 |
| ML-DSA-65 (Dilithium3) | ≈ 520 | ≈ 160 |

These comfortably exceed the NIST category thresholds, and quantum speedups of sieving (Grover-accelerated) shave only a modest constant factor, already accounted for in the category definitions [9][10]. Peikert's decade survey remains the definitive map of the reduction landscape and its remaining gaps [14].

### Performance evidence

Optimized implementations demonstrate deployment readiness: AVX2 builds of Kyber-768 decapsulate in well under 100k cycles, Dilithium-3 signs in a few hundred thousand cycles, and pqm4's Cortex-M4 ports run both schemes on microcontrollers with tens of kilobytes of stack [11][12]. NTT implementations using Montgomery and Barrett reduction avoid division entirely, and the transform dominates the cycle profile — precisely why the standards' prime choices are performance features, not accidents.

> Theorem: (Correctness of negative-wrapped convolution) Let psi be a primitive 2n-th root of unity modulo q with psi^2 = omega. Then the negacyclic product c = a · b in Z_q[x] modulo x^n+1 satisfies NTT^{-1}(pointwise(NTT_psi(a), NTT_psi(b))) = c, where NTT_psi uses the twisted evaluation points psi^{2i+1}.

##6 Limitations

Lattice confidence is deep but not absolute. The **algebraic structure** that makes Ring- and Module-LWE fast is exactly what distinguishes them from plain LWE; while no attack has exploited the module structure at deployed parameters, the margin is a matter of expert consensus rather than proof, and conservative deployments (higher k) trade efficiency for distance from the ideal-lattice extreme [5][6].

**Side channels** are the sharpest practical concern. NTT butterfly networks have data-dependent memory access in naive implementations; timing and power analysis against Kyber's NTT and Dilithium's rejection-sampling loop have been demonstrated in the literature, and masked implementations carry significant overhead [15]. Constant-time discipline is mandatory but subtle — particularly the variable iteration count of signing aborts, which leaks only public information in principle but complicates worst-case timing analysis.

Further caveats: Kyber has a nonzero **decryption-failure** probability (around 2^{-160} for the standardized sets), which the FO transform renders harmless for IND-CCA security but which must be accounted for in formal proofs [2]; **key reuse** across protocols is safe under the standard's explicit-rejection variant but was a genuine attack surface in early misuse; signature and key **sizes** remain large relative to ECDSA, complicating DNS, embedded, and blockchain deployments; and the schemes provide no **forward secrecy** by themselves — that property must come from the protocol layer, as in TLS 1.3's ephemeral key exchange.

##7 Conclusion

Module-LWE has carried lattice cryptography from theory to standard. By interpolating between plain and ideal lattices, it gave NIST two standards — ML-KEM and ML-DSA — that are simultaneously efficient enough to deploy (thanks in large part to the NTT-friendly primes and quasi-linear polynomial arithmetic dissected here) and conservative enough to trust (thanks to the reduction lineage from Regev through LPR to Langlois–Stehlé). The engineering is real: cycle counts on both server silicon and microcontrollers prove these are drop-in-capable replacements for ECDH and ECDSA in most protocols.

What remains is the long, unglamorous work of deployment: constant-time and masked implementations, hybrid classical/post-quantum handshakes during the transition, formal verification of the NTT and sampling code, and continued cryptanalytic pressure on module lattices — the only process that has ever truly validated a hardness assumption. The mathematics says these problems are hard; the next decade will find out whether we were right.
