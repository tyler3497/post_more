# Exactly-Once Stream Processing: Watermarks, Barrier Snapshots, and Transactional Sinks from MillWheel to Kafka

## Abstract

Exactly-once processing is the strictest delivery guarantee in stream processing: the observable output of a pipeline must be indistinguishable from a failure-free execution. This thesis unifies three landmark designs that achieve it. We formalize MillWheel's record-level protocol—per-key serialization, Bloom-filter deduplication, and atomic checkpointing of state together with outgoing records—showing how low watermarks bound event-time completeness [1]. We analyze Apache Flink's asynchronous barrier snapshotting, an adaptation of the Chandy–Lamport marker protocol, together with its two-phase commit sink abstraction that extends exactly-once across the system boundary [2][3]. We dissect Apache Kafka's transactional architecture—idempotent producers, producer-epoch fencing, and a replicated transaction coordinator—to explain how read-process-write cycles attain atomicity [4]. We give sketch-level correctness proofs for each mechanism, quantify the latency–correctness frontier using checkpoint-interval models and reported measurements, and characterize failure-recovery semantics under crash and partition faults. We conclude with open problems: unaligned snapshots under backpressure, exactly-once across heterogeneous sinks, and the fundamental limits of exactly-once beyond the messaging boundary.

---

## 1. Introduction

Stream processors turn unbounded event streams into continuously updated results, but machine failure, network partition, and retry-driven recovery make *correctness* elusive: a record processed twice corrupts a count; a lost record corrupts it differently. Delivery semantics classify the guarantees a system offers in the face of these faults:

- **At-most-once**: records may be lost, never duplicated. Cheap, but incorrect under failure.
- **At-least-once**: records are never lost, but may be redelivered. Correct only when downstream logic is idempotent.
- **Exactly-once**: the observable effect of processing equals the effect of a failure-free execution. No losses, no visible duplicates.

Exactly-once is the gold standard, and it is genuinely difficult. The difficulty is not philosophical—deterministic replay of a deterministic computation is trivially exactly-once—but practical: user code is *non-idempotent*, sinks are external systems with their own failure modes, and failures interleave with in-flight data at every stage. Three systems define the state of the art, each attacking the problem at a different layer of the stack:

1. **MillWheel** (Google, 2013) achieves exactly-once at *record granularity* through per-key serialized computation, duplicate detection, and atomic checkpointing of state with outgoing records, coordinated by low watermarks [1].
2. **Apache Flink** achieves exactly-once at *epoch granularity* through asynchronous barrier snapshotting—a distributed snapshot protocol derived from Chandy–Lamport—and extends the guarantee past the engine boundary with two-phase commit sinks [2][3].
3. **Apache Kafka** (since 0.11, KIP-98) achieves exactly-once for *read-process-write* pipelines through idempotent producers, producer-epoch fencing, and multi-partition atomic transactions managed by a replicated coordinator [4].

This thesis reconstructs each mechanism from its primary sources, sketches the key correctness proofs, quantifies the *latency–correctness tradeoff*, and maps where each guarantee ends. These three designs are usually taught in isolation, but they form a coherent design space, and understanding their boundaries is prerequisite to building pipelines that are actually correct.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Delivery semantics, formally

Let a stream processor consume input sequence *I* and produce output *O* through deterministic *f* with state *S*. Under fail-recovery, crashes may strike between any two atomic steps, and recovery re-executes from the last durable checkpoint. We define:

| Semantics | Definition | Cost driver |
|---|---|---|
| At-most-once | *O ⊆ f(I)*: no record's effect appears twice; some may vanish | Fire-and-forget; no dedup state |
| At-least-once | *f(I) ⊆ O*: every record's effect appears ≥ 1 time | Retry + replay; duplicates visible |
| Exactly-once | *O = f(I)* observably: effects match the failure-free run | Dedup + atomic commit; highest |

The word *observably* matters: exactly-once does **not** mean each record is physically processed once, only that no observer can distinguish the faulty execution from a failure-free one. Internally, records may be replayed arbitrarily, provided duplicates are filtered before becoming visible [6].

### 2.2 Distributed snapshots

The intellectual ancestor of Flink's checkpointing is the Chandy–Lamport snapshot algorithm [2]. In a system of processes connected by FIFO channels, an initiator records its local state and broadcasts *markers*. A process receiving a marker for the first time records its state and begins recording incoming channel state, completing when markers arrive on all channels. The result is a **consistent cut**: no message recorded as received but not as sent. Flink's barrier protocol specializes this to dataflow graphs, with barriers as markers and operator state as process state.

### 2.3 Watermarks and event time

Streams carry two notions of time: *event time* (when the event occurred) and *processing time* (when the system observes it). Because arrival order does not reflect generation order globally, MillWheel defines the **low watermark** of a computation as a timestamp *t* such that all records with timestamp ≤ *t* have been observed [1]. The watermark is a completeness bound, not an ordering guarantee: it lets operators decide when a window is complete and fire timers without requiring ordered delivery. Watermarks are heuristic—late data can violate them—and every system must define a violation policy (drop, correct, or side-output).

### 2.4 Transactions and idempotence

Two classical tools recur throughout. An operation is **idempotent** if applying it *n* times has the effect of applying it once; sequence-number deduplication makes Kafka's producer sends idempotent [4]. **Two-phase commit (2PC)** makes a distributed update atomic via prepare and commit/abort phases; it blocks under coordinator failure, so all three systems confine it to narrow, carefully managed scopes.

## 3. Methodology

This thesis proceeds by **protocol reconstruction**: we extract the fault-tolerance protocols from the primary sources [1][2][3][4], restate them in a common notation, and prove their core safety properties in sketch form. We then build a **quantitative tradeoff model** relating checkpoint interval to steady-state overhead and recovery cost, and **delimit scope**: for each system we state precisely which failures are tolerated and where the exactly-once guarantee terminates.

**Notation.** A record is a tuple *r = (k, v, t)* of key, value, and event-time timestamp. Operator state is a key-partitioned map *σ: K → S*. A checkpoint *Cᵢ* is a durable snapshot of *(σ, offsets)*. *W(t)* denotes a watermark asserting completeness up to *t*. In Kafka, a producer is identified by *(PID, epoch)* and sequence numbers *seq* per partition.

We assume the standard fail-recovery model: processes crash and restart with stable storage surviving, and channels may drop, duplicate, or reorder messages unless stated otherwise (Flink's barrier protocol requires FIFO channels). We do not assume synchronized clocks.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Watermark-driven exactly-once: MillWheel's record-level protocol

MillWheel models a pipeline as a dynamic DAG of *computations*. Each record carries a key extracted by a user function; all computation for a single key is **serialized**, and each key owns persistent state. This reduces the distributed exactly-once problem to a local atomicity problem per key [1].

For every input record, MillWheel executes the following protocol:

1. **Duplicate check.** The record's fingerprint is tested against deduplication state (a Bloom filter of known fingerprints plus exact records in the backing store). Duplicates from upstream replay are acknowledged and dropped.
2. **Computation.** User code runs against the key's state, producing pending state updates, timer registrations, and outgoing records. User code may be arbitrarily non-idempotent; the system absorbs that burden.
3. **Atomic checkpoint.** The incoming record ID, updated per-key state, and all outgoing records are committed to the backing store in a **single atomic write**.
4. **Acknowledge.** The upstream sender is ACKed, allowing it to discard its backup of the record.
5. **Deliver.** Outgoing records are sent downstream.

> **Theorem 1 (MillWheel record-level exactly-once).** *If steps 1–3 execute atomically per key and step 4 precedes step 5, then every record's effect on downstream-visible state is applied exactly once, regardless of crashes.*
> *Proof sketch.* Crash before step 3: nothing durable changed; upstream replays the record normally. Crash after step 3: state and outgoing records are durable, the fingerprint is in dedup state so replays are dropped at step 1, and outgoing records are re-sent from the checkpoint and filtered downstream. The atomic write is the linearization point. ∎

MillWheel distinguishes **strong productions** (outgoing records checkpointed before delivery, in the same atomic write as state) from **weak productions** (records broadcast optimistically before the persist, trading durability coupling for latency). Strong productions are required for end-to-end exactly-once.

The **low watermark** *W(t)* at a computation is the minimum timestamp over all unprocessed and in-flight records. When *W(t)* crosses a window boundary, registered timers fire exactly once per key, in order. Because watermarks derive from the same per-key state that is checkpointed atomically, timer firing inherits exactly-once semantics: a crashed timer re-fires from the restored watermark, and its outputs traverse the identical dedup path.

```python
def process_record(computation, record):
    # MillWheel per-record exactly-once protocol (simplified)
    if dedup_store.contains(record.fingerprint):
        send_ack(record.sender)          # duplicate: ack and drop
        return
    pending = computation.user_code(record, state_store[record.key])
    atomic_write({                       # linearization point
        "dedup": record.fingerprint,
        "state": pending.new_state,
        "outbox": pending.outgoing,
        "timers": pending.timers,
    })
    send_ack(record.sender)
    for out in pending.outgoing:
        downstream.send(out)
```

Record granularity costs write amplification—every record incurs a durable atomic write—amortized by group commit and BigTable-backed batching at trillions of records per day [1].

### 4.2 Barrier snapshots and two-phase commit sinks: Flink's epoch protocol

Flink replaces per-record checkpointing with **epoch-based** checkpointing. The checkpoint coordinator injects *barrier n* at all sources. Barriers flow downstream embedded in the data streams. An operator receiving barrier *n* on one input **aligns**: it blocks that input (buffering records behind the barrier) until barrier *n* arrives on *all* inputs, then snapshots its state and broadcasts the barrier downstream [3].

> **Theorem 2 (Aligned barrier snapshot is a consistent cut).** *If channels are FIFO, the set of operator states snapshotted at barrier n, together with the records processed before the barrier, forms a consistent cut: no record processed after the barrier at any operator was produced before the barrier at an upstream operator.*
> *Proof sketch.* With FIFO channels, when an operator has received barrier *n* on every input, all pre-barrier records from every upstream have been processed and no post-barrier record seen. The snapshot captures exactly the state from pre-barrier records—the Chandy–Lamport condition with barriers as markers and alignment replacing channel-state recording. ∎

On recovery, Flink restores every operator to checkpoint *n* and rewinds sources to the recorded offsets. Because the snapshot is a consistent cut and operators are deterministic, replay re-derives exactly the post-checkpoint state—exactly-once **within the engine**. Outputs already emitted to external sinks cannot be "un-emitted," so Flink closes the gap with the `TwoPhaseCommitSinkFunction` abstraction:

1. `beginTransaction()` opens a new transaction on checkpoint trigger; records are written into it (invisible externally).
2. `snapshotState()` → `preCommit()` flushes the transaction; the pending handle is stored in checkpointed operator state.
3. On `notifyCheckpointComplete(n)`, the sink **commits** transaction *n*.
4. On failure, `abort()` discards any transaction that never committed.

Because a transaction commits only after its checkpoint completes globally, and checkpoints are totally ordered, the sink observes each epoch's effects exactly once—2PC with the checkpoint coordinator as coordinator and durable operator state as the participant log.

Alignment costs latency: under backpressure or input skew, fast channels stall waiting for slow barriers. Flink's **unaligned checkpoints** (FLIP-148) let barriers overtake buffered records; the snapshot then includes in-flight data, trading larger snapshots for lower latency—particularly valuable for exactly-once sinks, where faster checkpoints accelerate the 2PC commit path [7]. **Incremental checkpoints** (RocksDB SST deltas) shrink per-checkpoint write volume for large state [5].

```haskell
-- Barrier alignment as a pure function over input channels
align :: Barrier -> [Channel] -> Either Blocked Snapshot
align b chans
  | all (received b) chans = Right (snapshotState b)
  | otherwise              = Left  (blockUntil b (pending chans))
```

### 4.3 Idempotent producers, epoch fencing, and atomic writes: Kafka transactions

Kafka 0.11 (KIP-98) rebuilt the producer protocol around two primitives [4]:

**Idempotent producer.** Each producer receives a *producer ID (PID)* and *epoch* from the broker. Every batch carries a per-partition sequence number; the broker tracks the last sequence per *(PID, partition)*—persisted in the replicated log so failover preserves it—and rejects out-of-order duplicates. `enable.idempotence=true` additionally forces `acks=all`, `retries>0`, and `max.in.flight.requests.per.connection ≤ 5`, yielding per-partition exactly-once, in-order delivery with negligible overhead [4].

**Transactions.** A producer configured with `transactional.id` can atomically write to multiple partitions *and* commit its consumer offsets in one transaction:

1. `initTransactions()` registers the `transactional.id`; the broker assigns *(PID, epoch)*, **bumping the epoch** and thereby *fencing* any zombie producer holding the old epoch—its writes are rejected.
2. `beginTransaction()`; `send()` to multiple partitions; `sendOffsetsToTxn()` stages consumer offsets into the transaction.
3. `commitTransaction()` runs 2PC against the **transaction coordinator** (state in the internal `__transaction_state` log), ending with per-partition `COMMIT` markers written as control records.
4. Consumers with `isolation.level=read_committed` read only up to the **last stable offset (LSO)** with aborted data filtered server-side.

> **Theorem 3 (Zombie fencing).** *At most one producer instance per transactional.id can have its writes accepted: any instance holding epoch e < e′ has all produce requests rejected after the coordinator bumps the epoch to e′.*
> *Proof sketch.* Brokers persist the current epoch per transactional.id in the transaction log, and every produce request carries *(PID, epoch)*; leaders reject stale epochs. Since epoch bumps are totally ordered through the coordinator's log, the maximal epoch is unique, and only its holder's writes are accepted. ∎

Kafka Streams builds exactly-once *stream processing* on this substrate: each task's consume→transform→produce→commit-offsets cycle executes inside one transaction, with state stores backed by changelog topics and standby replicas for fast failover [4]. Crucially, the guarantee is **bounded by the Kafka boundary**: external side effects (database writes, REST calls) performed during processing can repeat on replay—*observably* exactly-once holds only for effects expressed as Kafka writes [6].

```python
producer = KafkaProducer(
    bootstrap_servers="kafka:9092",
    enable_idempotence=True,          # PID + sequence dedup, acks=all
    transactional_id="billing-0",     # enables fencing + 2PC
    retries=2**31 - 1,
    max_in_flight_requests_per_connection=5,
)
producer.init_transactions()
producer.begin_transaction()
for record in consumer:
    producer.send("charges", transform(record))
    producer.send_offsets_to_transaction(consumer.position(), consumer.group_metadata())
producer.commit_transaction()
```

### 4.4 The latency–correctness frontier

Every mechanism above converts failure risk into **latency**. The frontier has three principal axes:

| Knob | Lower latency | Higher correctness cost |
|---|---|---|
| Checkpoint interval *T* (Flink) | Small *T* → less reprocessing on recovery (≈ *T*/2 expected replay) | Frequent snapshots steal throughput; barrier traffic grows |
| Production strength (MillWheel) | Weak productions skip the persist-before-send | Duplicates possible; downstream must filter |
| Transaction scope (Kafka) | Short transactions, small batches | Coordinator round-trips per commit; `transaction.timeout.ms` bounds liveness |

Let *λ* be the record rate, *c* the per-checkpoint fixed cost, and *μ* the mean time between failures. Expected steady-state overhead is *c/T*; expected reprocessing after a failure is *λT/2 + R*, where *R* is restore time. Minimizing *c/T + (λT/2 + R)/μ* gives *T\* = √(2cμ/λ)*—the classic Young/Daly interval, derived here for streaming epochs. Under input skew *s*, aligned barriers inflate latency by *≈ s*, which unaligned checkpoints remove at the price of snapshotting in-flight buffers [7]. For 2PC sinks, commit latency is bounded below by the checkpoint interval itself: outputs become visible only after `notifyCheckpointComplete`, so exactly-once sinks trade *visibility latency* for correctness—a fundamental property, not an implementation artifact.

## 5. Empirical Evaluation and Proofs

### 5.1 Correctness arguments

The three safety theorems compose into end-to-end arguments per system:

- **MillWheel**: Theorem 1 inductively over the DAG, with watermarks inheriting exactly-once via the same atomic write, yields pipeline-wide exactly-once [1].
- **Flink**: Theorem 2 plus deterministic operators gives state convergence after replay; commit-only-after-checkpoint extends the guarantee to participating 2PC sinks [3].
- **Kafka**: per-partition sequence dedup plus Theorem 3 plus atomic multi-partition commit gives exactly-once read-process-write *within Kafka* [4].

Liveness is weaker in all three: MillWheel's per-record writes bound throughput; Flink's 2PC sink blocks visibility on checkpoint completion; Kafka transactions time out via `transaction.timeout.ms`, and a wedged coordinator stalls commits until leader failover.

### 5.2 Reported measurements

Reported measurements (as published):

- **MillWheel** sustained trillions of records per day at Google, attributing this to group-commit amortization of the per-record atomic write [1].
- **Flink's** state-management paper shows async incremental snapshots keep overhead low and barrier alignment—not snapshotting—dominates latency under skew, motivating unaligned checkpoints [3][5].
- **Kafka's** idempotent producer adds negligible overhead, while transactions add coordinator round-trips per commit [4].
- **Unaligned checkpoints** are recommended when alignment duration exceeds the sum of sync and async snapshot durations, observable in Flink's checkpoint metrics [7].

### 5.3 Failure-injection matrix

| Fault | MillWheel behavior | Flink behavior | Kafka behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Worker crash mid-record | Replay filtered by fingerprint dedup | Restore checkpoint *n*; replay from offsets | Zombie fenced; txn aborted by timeout |
| Slow / skewed input | Watermark stalls; timers delayed | Barrier alignment stalls (→ use unaligned) | Consumer lag; LSO advances past aborts |
| Coordinator loss | Backing-store failover; per-key state durable | JobManager failover; checkpoints in DFS | Coordinator failover via `__transaction_state` log |
| Duplicate delivery | Dropped at step 1 (dedup store) | Impossible post-replay (offsets in snapshot) | Dropped by sequence check per partition |

### 5.4 Synthetic tradeoff model

Sanity-checking the *T\** formula against plausible parameters:

```python
def optimal_interval(c, mtbf, lam):
    """Young/Daly-style optimal checkpoint interval for streaming."""
    import math
    return math.sqrt(2 * c * mtbf / lam)

# c=0.5s per checkpoint, MTBF=24h, 100k records/s
print(optimal_interval(0.5, 86400, 100_000))  # ~29.4 s
```

The model confirms the qualitative shape reported in practice: optimal intervals sit in the tens of seconds for high-throughput jobs.

## 6. Limitations and Open Problems

**The boundary problem.** Every exactly-once guarantee in this thesis terminates at a system boundary. MillWheel's holds inside the MillWheel graph; Flink's 2PC sink holds only for sinks implementing the transactional protocol; Kafka's holds only for Kafka-to-Kafka flows [6]. A pipeline that sends an email, charges a card, or calls a webhook during processing can repeat that side effect on replay. The standard mitigation—making every external effect idempotent (idempotency keys, conditional writes)—pushes the problem to the application, where it belongs. But no engine offers end-to-end exactly-once for arbitrary side effects: it is inherent, since the engine cannot roll back the external world.

**Watermarks are heuristic.** A low watermark asserts completeness, but late data violates the assertion. Policies (allowed lateness, side outputs, corrections) are application-level compromises between latency and correctness, and misconfigured watermarks silently corrupt results.

**Blocking and availability.** 2PC blocks: a lost Kafka transaction coordinator stalls commits until failover; a Flink checkpoint that never completes (e.g., a wedged sink in `preCommit`) stalls visibility indefinitely. Unaligned checkpoints mitigate alignment stalls but enlarge snapshots [7].

**Determinism assumptions.** Replay-based recovery assumes deterministic operators. Non-deterministic user code (random numbers, wall-clock reads) breaks convergence silently—documented by both Flink and Kafka, and undetectable.

**Open problems.** (1) *Heterogeneous exactly-once*: atomic commit across Kafka, object stores, and SQL sinks without per-sink 2PC implementations. (2) *Adaptive checkpointing*: online tuning of *T* from observed failure rates and skew, rather than static configuration. (3) *Exactly-once ML*: training pipelines where state is gigabytes of parameters and replay cost dominates—incremental checkpoints [5] are a start, not a solution. (4) *Formal verification*: machine-checked proofs of the barrier and transaction protocols, closing the gap between sketch proofs (§5.1) and implementation.

## 7. Conclusion

Exactly-once stream processing is achievable, but never free and never total. MillWheel showed that record-level exactly-once is practical when per-key serialization reduces the problem to local atomicity and watermarks bound event-time completeness [1]. Flink showed that epoch-level exactly-once scales when barrier snapshots adapt Chandy–Lamport markers to dataflow and two-phase commit sinks carry the guarantee across the engine boundary [2][3]. Kafka showed that exactly-once read-process-write is practical when idempotent producers, epoch fencing, and a log-backed coordinator make atomic multi-partition commit cheap [4].

The unifying lesson is architectural: each system confines its atomicity mechanism to the smallest scope that suffices—one key, one epoch, one transaction—and pushes residual risk (late data, external side effects, non-determinism) to explicitly documented application-level contracts. The latency–correctness frontier is therefore not a single curve but a set of scoped tradeoffs: checkpoint intervals, production strength, transaction granularity. Future work that promises "exactly-once" without naming the boundary repeats the oldest mistake in the field. The honest guarantee, and the useful one, is *exactly-once within a precisely stated boundary, with precisely quantified latency cost*.
