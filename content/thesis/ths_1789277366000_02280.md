# Neuromorphic Computing with Spiking Neural Networks: Memristor Synaptic Crossbars, Surrogate-Gradient Learning, and Event-Driven Inference Hardware at the Edge

## Abstract

The von Neumann bottleneck — the separation of memory and compute — makes always-on edge AI prohibitively expensive: a data-center query over a large model dissipates millijoules where a brain performs equivalent inference for microjoules. This thesis develops the case for neuromorphic computing as the principled alternative: spiking neural networks (SNNs) whose binary, asynchronous events mirror cortical communication; memristive synaptic crossbars that collapse weight storage and multiplication into one physical substrate via Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws; surrogate-gradient learning that rescues backpropagation from the spike threshold's non-differentiability; and event-driven silicon — Intel's Loihi 2, the SpiNNaker2 many-core platform, the analog BrainScaleS-2 substrate — that turns sparsity into energy proportionality. We derive the surrogate-gradient estimator, formalize crossbar matrix–vector multiplication under device non-idealities, compare the three hardware families, and quantify the energy argument: Loihi at 270 µJ per keyword-spotting inference versus 29.8 mJ for a GPU (109×) [7], and a 370M-parameter MatMul-free language model on Loihi 2 at 2.8 mJ per prefill token [10]. We close with device non-idealities, training costs, and the roadmap to memristor-first systems.

## 1 Introduction

Modern deep learning is an exercise in moving numbers. Every multiply–accumulate fetches a weight from DRAM, ships it across an interconnect, computes, and writes back — billions of times per inference. Data movement dwarfs arithmetic: a 32-bit DRAM access dissipates roughly three orders of magnitude more energy than the floating-point operation it feeds. This is the **von Neumann bottleneck**, and it is why always-on, battery-powered intelligence remains out of reach for edge devices [1].

Biology suggests a radically different contract. The human brain, with approximately 86 billion neurons and 10^14 synapses, operates on a power budget of roughly 20 W — four orders of magnitude below a GPU cluster of comparable representational capacity. Its efficiency rests on three pillars:

1. **Colocation of memory and computation.** Synaptic weights are stored *in* the physical junctions between neurons; there is no separate memory hierarchy to traverse.
2. **Event-driven, sparse communication.** Neurons transmit information as brief, all-or-nothing *action potentials* (spikes). A quiescent neuron consumes essentially nothing; energy is expended only when information flows.
3. **Massive parallelism with local plasticity.** Learning is a local phenomenon — spike-timing-dependent plasticity modifies synapses using only pre- and post-synaptic activity — without a global backward pass.

**Neuromorphic computing** is the engineering discipline that transposes these principles into silicon. Its contemporary renaissance is driven by the convergence of four developments, each of which this thesis treats as a load-bearing pillar:

- **Spiking neural networks (SNNs)** as a computational model with well-understood dynamics — the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron, its generalizations, and their discrete-time formulations.
- **Memristive devices** — resistive RAM (RRAM), phase-change memory (PCM), and ferroelectric tunnel junctions — as analog synaptic elements whose conductance encodes weights and whose crossbar organization implements matrix–vector multiplication *in place*.
- **Surrogate-gradient learning**, the mathematical device that makes deep SNNs trainable with gradient descent despite the Heaviside nonlinearity of the spike mechanism.
- **Event-driven inference hardware** — digital (Loihi 2, SpiNNaker2), mixed-signal (BrainScaleS-2), and in-memory (memristor crossbars) — with published energy benchmarks against GPUs.

The thesis is organized as follows: §2 background (neuron models, plasticity, hardware taxonomy), §3 methodology (surrogate-gradient derivation, crossbar mapping, hardware-in-the-loop training), §4 the deep dive (memristor synapses, surrogate-gradient theory, event-driven architectures, energy benchmarks), §5 empirical results and formal statements, §6 limitations, §7 conclusion.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron

The workhorse of neuromorphic modeling is the **leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF)** neuron, whose subthreshold dynamics obey

$$\tau_m \frac{du}{dt} = -(u - u_{\mathrm{rest}}) + R I(t),$$

where $u(t)$ is the membrane potential, $\tau_m$ the membrane time constant, and $I(t)$ the synaptic input current. When $u$ crosses the threshold $\vartheta$, the neuron emits a spike — modeled as a Dirac impulse $S(t) = \sum_k \delta(t - t_k)$ — and resets to $u_{\mathrm{reset}}$. In discrete time with step $\Delta t$, the dynamics become the recurrence

$$u[t+1] = \beta u[t] + (1-\beta)\left(u_{\mathrm{rest}} + R I[t]\right) - S[t]\,\vartheta, \qquad S[t] = H\!\left(u[t] - \vartheta\right),$$

with leak factor $\beta = \exp(-\Delta t/\tau_m)$ and $H$ the Heaviside step. This casts the SNN as a *recurrent neural network with binary activations* — precisely what makes backpropagation-through-time applicable once the non-differentiability of $H$ is handled [4].

> **Definition 2.1 (Spike train).** A spike train is a finite sequence of event times $\{t_1, \ldots, t_K\} \subset [0, T]$ or, equivalently, a binary function $S : \{1,\ldots,T\} \to \{0,1\}$. Information in an SNN is carried by *which* neurons spike and *when* — the **rate code** (firing frequency) and the **temporal code** (precise spike timing) being the two classical hypotheses.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The surrogate-gradient estimator







```python

class SurrogateSpike(torch.autograd.Function):
    """Heaviside in forward, fast-sigmoid derivative in backward."""
    @staticmethod
    def forward(ctx, u, theta, beta):
        ctx.save_for_backward(u, theta, torch.tensor(beta))
        return (u >= theta).float()
    @staticmethod
    def backward(ctx, grad_out):
        u, theta, beta = ctx.saved_tensors
        # surrogate derivative of fast sigmoid: 1 / (1 + beta*|x|)^2
        x = u - theta
        grad_u = grad_out / (1.0 + beta.item() * x.abs()).pow(2)
        return grad_u, None, None

def lif_step(u, I, w, theta=1.0, beta_mem=0.9, beta_sg=10.0):
    """One discrete-time LIF step with soft reset."""
    u = beta_mem * u + (1.0 - beta_mem) * (w @ I)   # leak + integrate
    s = SurrogateSpike.apply(u, theta, beta_sg)      # fire (surrogate grad)
    u = u - s * theta                               # reset by subtraction
    return u, s
```

> **Theorem 3.1 (Surrogate gradients are not conservative).** Gygax and Zenke [4] prove that the surrogate-gradient vector field is *not* the gradient of any scalar surrogate loss: there exist closed loops in weight space along which the line integral of the surrogate "gradient" is non-zero. Surrogate learning is therefore not gradient descent on a well-defined objective — it is a *heuristic dynamical system* that happens to work. This is not a defect to be patched but a fact to be understood: the empirical success of the method (next section) coexists with the absence of a clean optimization-theoretic foundation.

### 3.2 Mapping SNN layers onto memristor crossbars


1. **Quantize** weights to the device's attainable conductance levels (typically 4–64 levels depending on the stack [5][6]).
2. **Decompose** $w_{ij} = G^+_{ij} - G^-_{ij}$ with $G^\pm_{ij} \ge 0$, programming two adjacent crossbar columns per logical weight.
3. **Encode inputs as voltages**: rate-coded spikes become pulse trains whose duty cycle encodes activation; the column current integrates to the postsynaptic sum.
4. **Calibrate for non-idealities**: wire resistance (IR drop), sneak-path currents through unselected cells, device-to-device variability, and conductance drift are absorbed by a hardware-aware retraining pass (see §3.3).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Memristor Synapses: Device Physics, Crossbar Organization, and the Non-Ideality Budget


**Device physics.** In oxide RRAM (e.g., HfO_x_), a "set" voltage drives oxygen vacancies into a conductive filament (lower resistance); a "reset" pulse ruptures it. Partial pulses produce *analog* intermediate states — the physical basis of LTP/LTD emulation. Pt/a-HfSiO_x_/TaN devices report a 10^3 memory window, 10^3-cycle endurance, and multilevel control via current compliance [5].

**Crossbar organization and sneak paths.** The naive 1R crossbar suffers *sneak currents* — parasitic parallel paths through unselected cells that grow superlinearly with array size. The standard remedy is the **1T1R** cell (access transistor per cell), at the cost of area; IBM's 256×256 PCM 1T1R arrays with on-chip integrate-and-fire neurons proved the approach at scale. Differential-pair encoding doubles column count but is the standard solution to the sign problem [5].

**The non-ideality budget.** A realistic crossbar MVM deviates from $I = GV$ through:

- *IR drop*: finite wire resistance makes the effective voltage at cell $(i,j)$ depend on position; the error scales as $O(N^2 R_{\mathrm{wire}} / R_{\mathrm{cell}})$ for an $N \times N$ array.
- *Conductance drift and noise*: filament relaxation and read noise inject multiplicative and additive perturbations.
- *Stuck cells and limited endurance*: defective cells must be mapped around; endurance of 10^6–10^12 cycles constrains on-chip learning.
- *Quantization*: 4–6 effective bits of conductance resolution versus 32-bit software floats.


```rust
/// Device-aware crossbar matrix-vector multiply with differential pairs.
pub struct Crossbar {
    g_pos: Vec<Vec<f64>>, // G+ conductances (Siemens)
    g_neg: Vec<Vec<f64>>, // G- conductances (Siemens)
    r_wire: f64,          // series wire resistance per cell (Ohms)
    levels: usize,         // discrete conductance levels
}

impl Crossbar {
    pub fn mvm(&self, v: &[f64]) -> Vec<f64> {
        let n = v.len();
        let mut i_out = vec![0.0; n];
        for j in 0..n {
            let mut acc = 0.0;
            for i in 0..n {
                // quantize conductance to device levels
                let gp = quantize(self.g_pos[i][j], self.levels);
                let gn = quantize(self.g_neg[i][j], self.levels);
                // first-order IR-drop: effective voltage sags with row index
                let v_eff = v[i] / (1.0 + (i as f64) * self.r_wire * (gp + gn));
                acc += (gp - gn) * v_eff; // Kirchhoff summation at the column
            }
            i_out[j] = acc;
        }
        i_out
    }
}

fn quantize(g: f64, levels: usize) -> f64 {
    let q = (g * (levels as f64 - 1.0)).round() / (levels as f64 - 1.0);
    q.clamp(0.0, 1.0)
}
```


### 4.2 Surrogate-Gradient Learning: Theory, Variants, and the Robustness Phenomenon





```haskell
-- Discrete-time LIF layer unrolled over T steps; surrogate derivative
-- carried as an explicit function argument for clarity.



-- fast sigmoid surrogate derivative, steepness beta

lifFold :: Double -> Double -> (Double -> Double)
        -> Vec -> [Vec] -> ([Vec], LIFState)
lifFold theta leak sd weights inputs =
    foldl step ([], LIFState (repeat 0.0) []) inputs
  where
    step (ss, st) inp =
        let u'  = zipWith (\u i -> leak * u + (1 - leak) * i)
                          (membrane st) (matVec weights inp)
            s   = map (\u -> if u >= theta then 1.0 else 0.0) u'
            -- surrogate derivative evaluated at each neuron, for the
            -- backward pass: dS/du <- sd (u - theta)
            _dsdu = map (\u -> sd (u - theta)) u'
            u'' = zipWith (\u si -> u - si * theta) u' s  -- reset
        in (s : ss, LIFState u'' s)
    matVec w x = map (sum . zipWith (*) x) w
```

> **Theorem 4.1 (Robustness of the surrogate shape).** *Across a family of surrogate derivatives $\{\sigma'_k\}$ with matched peak height and width, the test accuracy of BPTT-trained SNNs on temporal benchmarks varies by less than the run-to-run variance of any single choice* [4].

### 4.3 Event-Driven Inference Hardware: Loihi 2, SpiNNaker2, BrainScaleS-2



**SpiNNaker2 — the software-defined many-core.** Neurons are implemented *in software* on 152 ARM Cortex-M4F cores per chip, each with MAC/convolution/exp accelerators and per-core dynamic voltage-frequency scaling [9]. The spike-optimized asynchronous packet fabric is inherited from SpiNNaker 1 (whose million-core machine simulated ~1% of the human brain in real time); SpiNNaker2 targets 10× the cores at constant power, scaling to 10-million-core, 10-billion-neuron systems delivering ~0.3 exaops of conventional AI throughput [9]. Generality costs per-event CPU overhead; the reward is arbitrary neuron models, learning rules, and event-based algorithms (e.g., event-based GRUs) on one substrate.

**BrainScaleS-2 — the analog accelerator.** LIF neurons are *physical analog circuits*: membrane capacitors integrate currents at 1,000–10,000× biological speed. A digital plasticity processor closes the loop, and the hardware-in-the-loop protocol of Cramer et al. [3] — analog forward pass, surrogate-gradient backward pass in software — reached 70k patterns/s below 300 mW with ~20 spikes per inference. Analog computation is exquisitely efficient but pays in variability: every chip differs slightly, and training must absorb the mismatch.

| Dimension | Loihi 2 | SpiNNaker2 | BrainScaleS-2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neuron model | Programmable digital, graded spikes | Arbitrary (software) | Analog LIF, accelerated |
| Learning | On-chip 3-factor engine | Software / host | Hardware-in-the-loop |
| Time model | Asynchronous event-driven | 1 ms tick / event-based | 10³–10⁴× accelerated analog |
| Strength | Efficiency + programmability | Generality, scale | Speed, energy per op |
| Weakness | Fixed digital datapath | Per-event CPU overhead | Device mismatch, small scale |

*Why GPUs lose at batch one.* GPU efficiency comes from amortizing weight fetches over large batches and dense tensor cores; at batch size 1, arithmetic intensity collapses and the device is memory-bandwidth-bound, burning hundreds of milliwatts to stream weights for a single token. The neuromorphic chip, with weights pinned in local memory and computation gated on events, has no such amortization requirement.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation and Formal Statements

> **Theorem 5.1 (Crossbar MVM in O(1) time).** *Let $G \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ be a conductance matrix programmed into an ideal crossbar and $V \in \mathbb{R}^N$ an input voltage vector. Then the vector of column currents $I = GV$ is physically realized in a single read step, independent of $N$, up to the settling time of the readout circuitry.* **Proof sketch.** By Ohm's law each cell $(i,j)$ contributes current $G_{ij}V_i$; by Kirchhoff's current law the bitline sums these contributions instantaneously. The computation is performed by the physics of the array, not by a sequential ALU. ∎
>
> *Remark.* The qualifier — *ideal* — carries the engineering content of §4.1: IR drop, sneak paths, and quantization convert the O(1) ideal into an approximation managed by hardware-aware training [5].

> **Theorem 5.2 (Energy proportionality of event-driven inference).** *For an SNN mapped to an event-driven substrate where each spike incurs fixed energy $E_s$ and quiescent neurons incur leakage $E_0 \approx 0$, the inference energy satisfies $E \approx E_s \cdot \mathbb{E}[\text{spike count}]$, independent of the total synapse count.* This is the formal content of the keyword-spotting scaling result: 50× larger networks at 1.3× the power [7].

**Measured results (literature).**

1. *Surrogate-gradient training, BrainScaleS-2 hardware-in-the-loop:* software-equivalent accuracy on 16×16 MNIST/Fashion-MNIST, 70k patterns/s, <300 mW, ~20 spikes/input after activity regularization [3].
2. *Keyword spotting, Loihi vs. GPU:* 270 µJ vs. 29.8 mJ per inference (109×), equal accuracy, advantage growing with network size [7].
3. *Keyword spotting, SpiNNaker2 prototype vs. Loihi:* 7.1 µJ vs. 37 µJ per inference (de-overheaded) [8].
4. *MatMul-free LM (370M), Loihi 2:* 2.8 mJ/prefill-token, 59 mJ/generation-token single-chip; multi-chip scaling to 24 chips characterized [10].
5. *Memristor synapse device (Pt/a-HfSiO_x_/TaN):* 10^3 memory window, 10^4 s retention, 10^3-cycle endurance, 94.6% MNIST in simulation [5].

## 6 Limitations and Future Work

An honest thesis must weigh what neuromorphic computing cannot yet do.

1. **Device non-idealities remain the central obstacle.** Conductance drift, limited endurance (10^3 cycles in some HfO_x_ stacks [5] versus 10^12+ for SRAM), device-to-device variability, and sneak paths mean Theorem 5.1's O(1) MVM is an idealization. Deployments today require hardware-aware retraining, and on-chip learning at scale is still largely aspirational.
2. **Training is still von Neumann.** Surrogate-gradient BPTT runs on GPUs; the neuromorphic chip is (modulo Loihi 2's learning engine and BrainScaleS-2's plasticity processor) an *inference* substrate. The energy cost of training is not addressed by current hardware.
3. **The software gap.** As Intel's Mike Davies observed, "there hasn't been the emergence of a single software framework as you see in the deep learning world" [2]. Lava, Nengo, Sinabs, snnTorch, and SpikingJelly fragment the ecosystem; models are not portable across platforms the way PyTorch models are portable across GPUs.
4. **Benchmark realism.** Keyword spotting and 16×16 MNIST are existence proofs, not evidence of general superiority. The field needs standardized, application-scale benchmarks — matched accuracy, latency, *and* energy — before the 109× claims can guide procurement.
5. **Temporal coding is under-exploited.** Most deployed SNNs use rate codes, discarding the precise spike timing biology relies on; surrogate-gradient training of genuinely temporal codes remains brittle.

**Future work** points in three directions. First, *memristor-first architectures* co-designing the learning rule with device physics — e.g., three-factor rules whose updates are local enough to be implemented as pulse overlaps on the crossbar. Second, *algorithm–hardware co-design*: the MatMul-free result suggests models designed *for* the hardware (ternary weights, addition-only attention) are the highest-leverage direction. Third, *standardized energy benchmarking* — a "MLPerf for spikes" — without which the 109× claims remain contested.

## 7 Conclusion

Neuromorphic computing with SNNs is now an engineering discipline with working silicon, trainable models, and measured energy advantages. The four pillars reinforce one another: **spiking neurons** supply the sparse event abstraction; **surrogate gradients** make deep SNNs trainable via automatic differentiation, on the theoretical footing clarified by Gygax and Zenke [4]; **memristor crossbars** collapse weight storage and multiplication into array physics [5][6]; and **event-driven hardware** — Loihi 2's asynchronous cores [1][2], SpiNNaker2's software-defined fabric [9], BrainScaleS-2's accelerated analog neurons [3] — converts sparsity into energy proportionality: 109× over GPUs on keyword spotting [7], millijoule-per-token language models [10].

What remains is ecosystem maturity: device reliability, a unified software stack, training *on* (not just *for*) the substrate, and honest application-scale benchmarks. The brain's 20 W is the existence proof that the physics allows it. The task of the next decade is to make the engineering follow.
