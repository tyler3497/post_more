# Superoptimization: Denotational Program Equivalence and Stochastic Search for Optimal Machine Code

## Abstract

Superoptimization treats code generation as a search problem: among all machine programs denotationally equivalent to a target, find one minimizing a performance cost model. This thesis develops the mathematical foundations and engineering practice of the field, from Henry Massalin's 1987 exhaustive enumerative superoptimizer [1] to stochastic superoptimization via Markov chain Monte Carlo in STOKE [2], and synthesizing superoptimizers such as Souper [3]. We formalize programs as elements of a denotational semantic domain in which equivalence becomes a decidable relation over loop-free fragments, discharged by symbolic execution to SMT solvers or approximated by bounded testing. We derive the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance rule governing STOKE's rewrite sampler, analyze proposal distributions including learned neural policies [4], and compare static, dynamic, and learned cost models. Empirical results show stochastic search matching or beating gcc -O3, icc -O3, and expert hand-written assembly, and synthesized optimizations shipping in production compilers. We close with the completeness, scalability, and floating-point limitations that bound what superoptimizers can certify.

##1 Introduction

Conventional compilers optimize by applying hand-written transformation rules in a fixed or heuristic order. Each rule is locally sound, and the pipeline is fast, but nothing guarantees that the final instruction sequence is anywhere near optimal for the target microarchitecture. Henry Massalin's 1987 paper proposed the radical alternative: given a target function, enumerate *all* instruction sequences in order of increasing length and return the first one provably equivalent to the target [1]. By construction, the result is the smallest program in the enumerated space. Massalin's superoptimizer was infeasible for sequences longer than a handful of instructions, yet it established the field's defining tradeoff: *completeness versus scale*. Denali later traded completeness for goal-directedness, using algebraic matching to reduce the undecidable part of equivalence to a propositional core discharged by SAT [5].

The modern stochastic turn reformulates superoptimization as sampling from a Boltzmann distribution over programs. STOKE encodes correctness and performance as terms in a cost function and explores the space with Markov chain Monte Carlo, sacrificing completeness for the ability to consider far larger programs [2]. Souper applies the same idea at the LLVM IR level, synthesizing peephole-style rewrites with an SMT solver in the loop [3]. Learned proposal distributions [4] and large language models that propose candidate rewrites [6] now push the frontier further.

This thesis unifies these strands through *denotational program equivalence*: two programs are interchangeable exactly when they denote the same function on machine state. Section 2 reviews the landscape, Section 3 lays out the methodology, Section 4 develops the deep theory, Section 5 reports empirical results and proofs, and Sections 6 and 7 discuss limitations and conclusions.

##2 Background

### 2.1 Peephole Optimization versus Superoptimization

Peephole optimizers match small windows of instructions against rewrite patterns discovered by compiler engineers. They are fast and predictable but fundamentally limited: the set of patterns is whatever a human wrote down, and pattern interaction is ad hoc. Superoptimization instead *derives* rewrites from a specification of equivalence. The classical pipeline has three components [7]:

1. **Enumerator**: produces candidate programs in some order, by brute force [1], by stochastic sampling [2], or by constraint-driven synthesis [3].
2. **Equivalence oracle**: decides whether a candidate denotes the same function as the target, via Boolean testing [1], symbolic execution plus SAT or SMT [2][5], or a test suite [2][6].
3. **Cost model**: ranks equivalent candidates by predicted or measured performance.

### 2.2 A Taxonomy of Approaches

| Approach | Search strategy | Equivalence check | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Massalin enumerative [1] | Exhaustive by length | Boolean minterm test | Up to 3 instructions |
| Denali goal-directed [5] | Algebraic matching | SAT solving | Basic blocks |
| STOKE stochastic [2] | MCMC sampling | SMT + test cases | Loop-free x86-64 |
| Souper synthesizing [3] | CEGIS enumeration | SMT solver | LLVM IR fragments |
| Learned proposals [4] | RL-guided sampling | Same as STOKE | Hacker's Delight |
| LLM-guided [6] | Neural generation | Test suite | x86-64 assembly |

### 2.3 Denotational Framing

Let a machine state be a tuple of registers, flags, and memory. The *denotation* of an instruction sequence P, written with double semantic brackets, is the partial function from initial to final states it computes. Two sequences P and P-prime are equivalent, written P equiv P-prime, when their denotations agree on all states of interest. Equivalence is undecidable for general programs, but for loop-free fragments over bitvectors it reduces to a formula in the theory of bitvectors and arrays, which modern SMT solvers decide efficiently. This decidability is the entire foundation on which scalable superoptimization rests [2][3][5].

> **Theorem:** *Soundness of SMT discharge.* If a bitvector SMT solver reports that the formula denoting disequality of P and P-prime is unsatisfiable, then P and P-prime are observationally equivalent on every reachable machine state.

The proof is immediate from the soundness of the symbolic encoding: each machine instruction is modeled as a pure function over symbolic bitvector state, so unsatisfiability of the disequality constraint means no concrete state distinguishes the two programs.

##3 Methodology

Our methodology follows the three-pillar structure common to all modern superoptimizers: a denotational equivalence oracle, a stochastic or enumerative search procedure, and a cost model combining correctness and performance.

### 3.1 The Cost Function

STOKE formulates superoptimization as minimizing a cost function over candidate rewrites R given a target T [2]:

```python
def cost(rewrite, target, testcases):
    eq = equivalence_penalty(rewrite, target, testcases)  # correctness term
    perf = performance(rewrite)                          # speed/size term
    return eq + perf
```

The equivalence penalty is zero exactly when the rewrite passes all test cases, and the performance term rewards faster or shorter code. Because the target's own cost is finite, the sampler can always fall back to the original program, so search never regresses.

### 3.2 The Search Procedure

From a current rewrite, a *move* proposes a modification: replace a random instruction, swap two instructions, change an operand, or insert or delete an instruction. The proposal is accepted with the Metropolis-Hastings probability, which we derive in Section 4.2. Incorrect rewrites are accepted with nonzero probability at high temperature, allowing the sampler to escape local minima; as the temperature cools, the chain concentrates on low-cost programs.

### 3.3 Synthesis as Counterexample-Guided Search

Souper inverts the pipeline: it extracts an optimization *opportunity* (a left-hand side) from LLVM IR, then enumerates right-hand sides of increasing size, asking an SMT solver whether each is equivalent under the path conditions [3]. Counterexamples prune the enumeration. This is counterexample-guided inductive synthesis specialized to peephole-shaped rewrites, and it discovered optimizations that shipped in production compilers.

```rust
// Schematic cost term combining static and dynamic signals
fn total_cost(eq_pass: bool, latency: f64, size: usize) -> f64 {
    let correctness = if eq_pass { 0.0 } else { 1e9 };
    correctness + 0.7 * latency + 0.3 * size as f64
}
```

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Denotational Semantics of Loop-Free Machine Code

Consider a register machine with a finite register file and a flags word. In Haskell-style notation, the denotation of straight-line code is a state transformer:

```haskell
type Reg  = Int
type Word = Integer        -- unbounded here; bitvectors in the solver
type State = (Map Reg Word, Flags, Memory)

denote :: [Instr] -> State -> State
denote []     s = s
denote (i:is) s = denote is (step i s)
```

`step` interprets a single instruction: an add updates the destination register and the carry, zero, sign, and overflow flags according to the x86 specification. Memory is modeled as an array theory term, so loads and stores become select and store operations. Observational equivalence is then:

> **Definition:** *Denotational equivalence.* Programs P and Q are equivalent, P ~ Q, when for every initial state s in the domain of interest, denote P s and denote Q s agree on all *live* outputs: the registers, flags, and memory locations the surrounding code actually reads.

The live-output restriction is critical. A rewrite that clobbers a dead register or leaves flags undefined is still acceptable, and this is where superoptimizers find their most surprising wins: sequences that a compiler would reject as semantically different are equivalent *modulo liveness*. STOKE computes liveness from the target's context and constrains the equivalence query accordingly [2].

Symbolic execution lifts `denote` to symbolic states. Each input register becomes a fresh bitvector variable; each instruction becomes a term constructor. The equivalence query is the negation of output equality, and an SMT solver decides it. For loop-free fragments this is complete relative to the instruction model; modeling errors, such as an incorrect flag semantics, are the dominant source of unsoundness in practice [2][5].

### 4.2 Stochastic Search: Metropolis-Hastings over Rewrites

Let the proposal distribution q(R-prime | R) give the probability of proposing rewrite R-prime from R. Let the target distribution be the Boltzmann distribution p(R) proportional to exp(-beta * cost(R)), with inverse temperature beta. The Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability is:

> **Theorem:** *Detailed balance.* Accepting a proposal R-prime from R with probability alpha = min(1, (p(R-prime) q(R | R-prime)) / (p(R) q(R-prime | R))) makes the Markov chain reversible with stationary distribution p.

When the proposal distribution is symmetric, as in STOKE's uniform move set, this reduces to alpha = min(1, exp(-beta (cost(R-prime) - cost(R)))): strictly improving moves are always accepted, and worsening moves are accepted with probability decaying in the cost increase. Because STOKE's move set includes instruction deletion and replacement, the chain is ergodic over the finite space of rewrites up to a length bound, and classical simulated-annealing theory applies: with a logarithmic cooling schedule, the chain converges in probability to the global cost minimum.

The uniform proposal distribution is the method's weakness: most random moves are useless. Bunel et al. learn a proposal distribution with reinforcement learning, using unbiased gradient estimators of the expected improvement, and show substantial gains on Hacker's Delight benchmarks [4]. The learned policy exploits program structure, proposing operand changes correlated with the target's dataflow rather than uniform noise. More recently, large language models have been used as proposal mechanisms, generating candidate x86-64 rewrites that are then validated against test suites [6].

A minimal sampler in Python illustrates the core loop:

```python
import math, random

def mcmc(target, cost_fn, propose, steps, beta0=1.0):
    current = target
    best = target
    for t in range(steps):
        beta = beta0 * math.log(2 + t)      # logarithmic cooling
        cand = propose(current)             # random rewrite move
        delta = cost_fn(cand) - cost_fn(current)
        if delta <= 0 or random.random() < math.exp(-beta * delta):
            current = cand
            if cost_fn(current) < cost_fn(best):
                best = current
    return best
```

### 4.3 Equivalence Oracles: Tests, Validators, and SMT

No single equivalence check dominates; production superoptimizers layer them by cost:

1. **Test cases.** A rewrite is executed on concrete inputs and its live outputs compared against the target. Fast but unsound: passing tests do not imply equivalence. STOKE uses tests as a cheap filter before invoking the solver [2], and LLM-guided approaches rely on test suites as the primary oracle [6].
2. **Translation validation.** A symbolic validator constructs the disequality formula and discharges it with an SMT solver. Sound relative to the instruction model, but expensive; STOKE invokes it sparingly, on promising candidates [2].
3. **Bounded model checking.** For fragments with bounded loops, unrolling plus SMT gives a decision procedure up to the bound. Beyond the bound, the answer is unknown rather than true.

Denali's division of labor is instructive: algebraic matching handles the undecidable quantifier over inputs, and the remaining propositional problem goes to a SAT solver [5]. The matcher finds all ways of computing the result; the solver selects the fastest, accounting for common subexpressions and machine constraints. This separation lets each component be the best available tool for its subproblem.

A TLA+ sketch of the oracle contract makes the layering precise:

```tla
--------------------------- MODULE SuperoptOracle ---------------------------
VARIABLES target, candidate, verdict

TestFilter ==
    /\ verdict = "unknown"
    /\ verdict' \in {"pass", "fail"}

SMTValidate ==
    /\ verdict = "pass"
    /\ verdict' \in {"equivalent", "counterexample"}

Next == TestFilter \/ SMTValidate

Soundness == (verdict = "equivalent") => (candidate ~ target)
=============================================================================
```

The invariant `Soundness` captures the methodology's contract: only the SMT layer may promote a candidate to equivalent, and it does so only when the solver proves the disequality formula unsatisfiable.

### 4.4 Cost Models and Performance Prediction

A superoptimizer is only as good as its cost model. Three families exist:

- **Static heuristics.** Instruction count, total latency from tables, or micro-op counts. Cheap and differentiable enough for search, but blind to out-of-order execution, port contention, and cache effects.
- **Measured performance.** Timing the candidate on the actual machine. Faithful but slow and noisy; STOKE uses it to validate finalists rather than to guide every step [2].
- **Learned models.** Neural predictors of runtime trained on measured data, offering a middle ground. Early results suggest they can steer search toward microarchitecturally favorable sequences that static tables miss.

> **Theorem:** *Cost-model regret bound (informal).* If the cost model errs by at most epsilon on every candidate and the search returns the cost-minimal rewrite, the returned program is within epsilon of optimal under true performance.

The bound is trivial but clarifying: all of superoptimization's empirical risk concentrates in the gap between the cost model and the silicon. This is why STOKE's headline results are stated against *measured* performance of gcc -O3 and icc -O3 outputs rather than against any static metric [2].

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 STOKE: Matching Production Compilers and Experts

Beginning from binaries compiled by LLVM at -O0 for 64-bit x86, STOKE produced programs matching or outperforming gcc -O3 and icc -O3, and in some cases expert hand-written assembly [2]. The canonical example is a Montgomery multiplication kernel where STOKE discovered a 25-instruction sequence beating both production compilers and the expert's version, by exploiting flag-setting side effects of arithmetic instructions that compilers conservatively model as clobbers. These are exactly the denotational-equivalence-modulo-liveness wins predicted by the theory in Section 4.1.

### 5.2 Souper: Synthesis at the IR Level

Souper enumerated right-hand sides for LLVM IR fragments and verified them with an SMT solver, discovering peephole-style optimizations that were implemented by hand in shipping versions of both LLVM and Microsoft Visual C++ [3]. Used as a fully automated pass, it compiled a Clang binary about 3 MB (4.4 percent) smaller than the baseline. Because Souper works on target-independent IR, it cannot discover machine-specific sequences; STOKE and Souper are therefore complementary rather than competing [3].

### 5.3 Learned and LLM-Guided Search

Replacing STOKE's uniform proposal distribution with a reinforcement-learned policy significantly improved optimization quality on automatically generated and Hacker's Delight benchmarks [4]. The learned proposals concentrate probability mass on moves that historically improved similar programs, effectively amortizing search experience across targets. SuperCoder extends this direction with large language models proposing x86-64 rewrites, validating candidates by assembling and running them against test suites, and reporting speedups over gcc -O3 baselines [6]. The LLM acts as a strongly structured prior over the program space, while the test suite and timing harness supply the ground-truth oracle the model itself cannot provide.

### 5.4 Convergence Guarantees

> **Theorem:** *Simulated annealing convergence.* Let the state space be finite, the chain ergodic, and the cooling schedule satisfy T(t) >= c / log(1 + t) for a sufficiently large constant c depending on the maximum escape depth. Then the chain converges in probability to the set of global cost minima.

This classical result justifies the stochastic formulation: unlike greedy peephole application, which gets stuck in local optima, a properly cooled MCMC sampler explores globally. The price is the loss of Massalin's completeness guarantee [1]: stochastic search may simply never visit the optimal rewrite within the time budget. In practice, the tradeoff favors stochasticity for any fragment beyond a few instructions [2].

> **Theorem:** *Denali completeness of the propositional core.* Once algebraic matching fixes the correspondence between term structure and machine instructions, the remaining optimization problem is NP-complete and is solved exactly by the SAT solver [5].

##6 Limitations

1. **Completeness is sacrificed.** Enumerative superoptimization is complete but scales to only a few instructions [1]; stochastic search scales further but offers no completeness guarantee [2]. A missed optimum is silent.
2. **SMT scalability.** Equivalence queries grow with fragment length and memory operations; solver timeouts cap the fragments that can be certified. Array-theory reasoning over memory remains the bottleneck.
3. **Loops and control flow.** The decidable core covers loop-free fragments. Extending denotational equivalence to loops requires invariants or bounded unrolling, neither of which integrates cleanly with rewrite sampling.
4. **Floating point.** IEEE-754 semantics, NaN propagation, and rounding modes make equivalence checking over floating-point code far harder; most superoptimizers restrict themselves to integer and bitvector operations.
5. **Unsound oracles.** Test-suite-only validation [6] can certify incorrect rewrites. Even SMT-based validation is only as sound as the instruction semantics model; a modeling bug silently miscompiles.
6. **Cost-model fidelity.** Static cost models misrank candidates on modern out-of-order cores; measured timing is faithful but too slow to guide fine-grained search, leaving a fidelity-versus-speed tradeoff unresolved.
7. **Search budget.** Superoptimization remains a compile-time luxury: minutes to hours per function. It is economical for hot kernels and standard libraries, not for whole programs.

---

##7 Conclusion

Superoptimization reframes compilation as search over a denotational equivalence class. Massalin showed the idea in its purest form: enumerate everything, keep the smallest equivalent program [1]. Denali showed how to split the problem so that SAT solvers handle the combinatorial core [5]. STOKE showed that stochastic search over a cost function combining test-based and SMT-based equivalence with performance terms reaches scales where it beats production compilers and human experts [2]. Souper showed that the same machinery synthesizes IR-level optimizations that ship in real compilers [3], learned proposals showed that search itself can be learned [4], and language models now supply structured priors over the rewrite space [6].

The unifying invariant is *denotational program equivalence*: a mathematically precise relation, decidable over loop-free bitvector fragments, that lets a machine decide when two programs mean the same thing and then hunt relentlessly for the fastest way to mean it. The open problems, loops, floating point, faithful cost models, and proof-producing solvers, define the next decade of the field.
