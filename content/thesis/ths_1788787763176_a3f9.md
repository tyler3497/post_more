# Wiesner's Quantum Money and Uncloneable Cryptography: Quantum Lightning, Copy-Protection of Point Functions, One-Shot Signatures, and Certified Deletion

## Abstract

We survey the theoretical foundations of uncloneable cryptography, a discipline whose roots lie in Stephen Wiesner's 1970s conjugate-coding quantum money and which has grown into a rich taxonomy of primitives: public-key quantum money from hidden subspaces, quantum lightning, quantum copy-protection of point functions and compute-and-compare programs, one-shot signatures, and encryption with certified deletion. All derive their power from the quantum no-cloning theorem, the monogamy of entanglement, and conjugate-coding techniques. We formalize security definitions, reconstruct the Aaronson–Christiano subspace-state construction and its inner-product adversary lower bound, trace Zhandry's quantum lightning strengthening, analyze the pirate–freeloader game defining copy-protection, and connect one-shot signatures to certified deletion. We prove unforgeability of Wiesner's private-key scheme against non-adaptive attacks, exhibit adaptive online attacks and their patches, and conclude with noise tolerance, everlasting security, and the open frontier of standard-model copy-protection for general programs.

## 1. Introduction

In the late 1960s, Stephen Wiesner proposed a scheme that quantum cryptography's standard historiography treats as its genesis: *quantum money*, a currency whose banknotes are unforgeable as a consequence of the laws of physics rather than computational hardness [1]. The instrument of unforgeability is *conjugate coding* — encoding classical bits into quantum states drawn from complementary, mutually unbiased bases, such that any measurement extracting information in one basis irrecoverably destroys information encodable in the other. The manuscript, rejected by the journals of its era as too speculative, was finally published in 1983 [1], after its sibling idea had already catalyzed quantum key distribution.

The half-century since has transformed Wiesner's isolated curiosity into **uncloneable cryptography**, a coherent research program asking a single question with many faces: *what can we build when physical law, not mathematics, forbids copying?* The answers now include:

- **Private-key quantum money**: Wiesner's original scheme, secure against non-adaptive counterfeiters but vulnerable to *online* attacks in which a counterfeiter interacts adaptively with the verifying bank [2].
- **Public-key quantum money**: anyone may verify, only the mint may issue. The landmark construction of Aaronson and Christiano from *hidden subspaces* gives unconditional security relative to classical oracles [2].
- **Quantum lightning** (Zhandry): a strict strengthening of quantum money in which *not even the scheme's inventor* can produce two valid banknotes with the same serial number [3].
- **Quantum copy-protection**: encoding a program as a quantum state that can be executed on arbitrary inputs yet cannot be pirated into two working copies, a task impossible classically. Formalized by Aaronson in 2009 [4] and advanced to provable QROM security for compute-and-compare programs by Ananth et al. [5].
- **One-shot signatures**: quantum signing keys that permit signing exactly one message and then *self-destruct*, enabling signatures unforgeable without any ledger [8].
- **Encryption with certified deletion** (Broadbent–Islam): quantum ciphertexts whose recipient can produce a *classical certificate* proving the plaintext has been irreversibly destroyed [7].

> **Theorem (Informal):** Under the quantum no-cloning theorem, a uniform family of primitives — money, lightning, copy-protected programs, one-shot signatures, certifiably deletable ciphertexts — achieves security properties that are provably unattainable by any classical means. All reduce, in essence, to the adversary's inability to clone or to simultaneously extract conjugate-coded information.

This thesis develops the shared mathematical substrate of these primitives, reconstructs the principal constructions and lower-bound techniques, and maps the open frontier.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The No-Cloning Theorem and Conjugate Coding

The **no-cloning theorem** states that no completely positive trace-preserving map $\Lambda$ satisfies $\Lambda(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|) = |\psi\rangle\langle\psi| \otimes |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$ for all states $|\psi\rangle$. Cloning *unknown* quantum states is physically impossible. **Conjugate coding** [1] weaponizes this: fix two orthonormal bases of a qubit that are mutually unbiased — the rectilinear basis $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ and the diagonal basis $\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$ with $|\pm\rangle = (|0\rangle \pm |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$. A classical bit string $x \in \{0,1\}^n$ together with a basis string $\theta \in \{0,1\}^n$ determines the *Wiesner state*

$$|x^\theta\rangle = H^{\theta_1}|x_1\rangle \otimes \cdots \otimes H^{\theta_n}|x_n\rangle,$$

where $H$ is the Hadamard gate. Measuring in the basis specified by $\theta$ recovers $x$ perfectly; measuring in any *other* basis yields statistically useless outcomes and disturbs the state. An adversary ignorant of $\theta$ that attempts to copy or learn $x$ faces the *monogamy of entanglement*: a single $n$-qubit system cannot be split into two registers each of which reveals $x$ given $\theta$. The optimal strategy wins the associated monogamy game with probability exactly $(1/2 + 1/(2\sqrt{2}))^n$ — the BB84 bound.

### 2.2 A Taxonomy of Uncloneable Primitives

| Primitive | Quantum object | Unforgeability target | Verifier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Private-key money (Wiesner) | Wiesner state $|x^\theta\rangle$ + serial | Counterfeiting a banknote | Bank only (needs $\theta$) |
| Public-key money (Aaronson–Christiano) | Subspace state $|A\rangle$ + serial | Counterfeiting a banknote | Public (membership oracles) |
| Quantum lightning (Zhandry) | Bolt $|\text{storm}_s\rangle$ | Producing two bolts, same serial | Public |
| Copy-protection | Quantum program $\rho_C$ | Splitting into two working copies | Public (evaluation) |
| One-shot signatures | Quantum signing key $|sk\rangle$ | Signing two distinct messages | Public |
| Certified deletion | Quantum ciphertext $|ct\rangle$ | Recovering plaintext after deletion cert | Public/Private cert check |

### 2.3 Security Models and Oracles

Security is formalized as *games* between a challenger and a quantum polynomial-time (QPT) adversary. Two idealizations recur:

1. **Classical oracle model**: parties query classical membership oracles (e.g., for a subspace $A$ and its dual $A^\perp$). Unconditional security proofs here use the *quantum adversary method*.
2. **Quantum oracle model**: all parties share black-box access to a fixed unitary $U$; Aaronson [4] shows public-key quantum money and copy-protection exist relative to such an oracle.
3. **Standard model with iO**: instantiating oracles by *indistinguishability obfuscation* yields computational security, as in Zhandry's iO-based public-key money [3] and the CLLZ hidden-coset program family [6].

## 3. Methodology

Our method is *reductive and constructive*: we take the uncloneability of Wiesner states (a consequence of monogamy of entanglement) as the atomic security fact, and show how every primitive in the taxonomy is assembled from it by one of three design patterns.

1. **Conjugate-coded authentication.** Mint a random basis string $\theta$; verification is measurement in $\theta$. This yields private-key money [1], one-shot signatures [8], and certified-deletion ciphertexts [7].
2. **Subspace-state public verification.** Replace the secret basis by *publicly testable membership* in a hidden subspace $A$ and its dual $A^\perp$; the Fourier transform interchanges them, giving a public projection onto $|A\rangle$ [2].
3. **Bolt-to-certificate reductions.** Strengthen "the bank cannot clone" to "*nobody* can duplicate, including the minter" by requiring that even producing two accepting states breaks a computational assumption — quantum lightning [3].

For each construction we state the security game, sketch the reduction, and identify the exact hardness assumption invoked (information-theoretic, oracle-model, or iO/LWE-based).

> **Remark (everlasting security):** A distinctive feature of this literature is that some schemes are *information-theoretically* secure against unbounded adversaries that interact only classically with oracles, with computational assumptions invoked solely to instantiate the oracles publicly. The security of the *functionality* can thus outlive the hardness assumptions used to publish it.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Wiesner's Private-Key Money and the Online Attack

**Mint.** The bank samples a serial number $s$, a random basis string $\theta \in \{0,1\}^n$, and random bits $x \in \{0,1\}^n$, prepares the banknote $(s, |x^\theta\rangle)$, and stores $(s, \theta, x)$ privately.

**Verify.** Presented with $(s, \rho)$, the bank measures each qubit of $\rho$ in the basis dictated by $\theta$; it accepts iff all outcomes equal $x$.

Unforgeability against a *non-adaptive* adversary — one who never queries the bank — follows from monogamy: any counterfeiter outputting two states that both pass verification must split $|x^\theta\rangle$ into two winning registers, impossible beyond the BB84 bound, which is negligible in $n$.

The *online attack* breaks the naive scheme in a devastatingly simple way: the adversary submits a candidate banknote, and the bank *returns the bill* after validation, correcting "minor deviations" to account for noise. By submitting a banknote with one qubit perturbed and observing whether the bank returns it corrected, the counterfeiter learns that qubit's basis and bit — and iterating, reconstructs the entire note [2]. Aaronson and Christiano's patch is the first private-key scheme secure against *adaptive* interaction with the bank: the bank *never returns the bill*, and verification is performed via the same two-test projective measurement used in their public-key scheme. A crucial insight: noise tolerance and return-the-bill behavior are fundamentally in tension with security [2].

```python
# Wiesner minting and verification (idealized simulation)
def mint(n):
    theta = random_bits(n)          # secret basis choices
    x     = random_bits(n)          # secret bits
    serial = uuid4().hex
    note = [prep(x_i, theta_i) for x_i, theta_i in zip(x, theta)]
    return serial, note, (theta, x)

def verify(note, theta, x):
    # measure each qubit in its secret basis; accept iff all match
    return all(measure(q, b) == bit for q, b, bit in zip(note, theta, x))
```

### 4.2 Hidden Subspaces: Public-Key Money from Subspace States

Aaronson and Christiano's breakthrough [2] observes that a Wiesner state $|x^\theta\rangle$ is a *product* state with a secret description, whereas a **subspace state**

$$|A\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|A|}} \sum_{v \in A} |v\rangle$$

for a random $n/2$-dimensional subspace $A \le \mathbb{F}_2^n$ is an *entangled* state whose description can be *public* without enabling cloning. The banknote is $(s, |A_s\rangle)$; the serial $s$ encodes classical membership oracles for $A_s$ and $A_s^\perp$. Verification applies the oracle for $A_s$, then the quantum Fourier transform $H^{\otimes n}$ — which maps $|A\rangle$ to $|A^\perp\rangle$ — then the oracle for $A_s^\perp$, then a second Fourier transform, implementing a rank-one projection onto $|A\rangle$ [2]. Only two tests are needed: one in the standard basis, one in the Hadamard basis, exactly matching Wiesner's complementary-observables intuition.

The security theorem is a query-complexity lower bound:

> **Theorem (Aaronson–Christiano [2]):** Any quantum algorithm mapping $|A\rangle \mapsto |A\rangle^{\otimes 2}$ with membership oracles for $A$ and $A^\perp$ requires $\Omega(2^{n/4})$ queries. Consequently the oracle scheme is unconditionally secure.

The proof introduces the **inner-product adversary method**, a new variant of Ambainis's adversary technique: run a single counterfeiting algorithm in parallel against two distinct subspaces $A, A'$, and track the inner product of the algorithm's output states; a successful cloner must *distinguish* $A$ from $A'$, forcing many oracle queries [2].

### 4.3 Quantum Lightning: Nobody May Strike Twice

Quantum money demands that no one except the mint can create banknotes. **Quantum lightning** [3] demands strictly more: *not even the mint* can produce two valid bolts sharing a serial number. Formally, a scheme provides algorithms $\mathsf{Storm}$ (produces a bolt $|\text{storm}\rangle$ and serial $s$) and $\mathsf{Ver}$; security requires that no QPT adversary, given the public parameters *and the storm code itself*, can output two bolts both verifying under the *same* serial. The reduction paradigm is striking:

> **Theorem (Zhandry [3], informal):** A quantum-money adversary either (1) duplicates a bolt under one serial — breaking lightning security directly — or (2) forges a bolt under a *new* serial with the mint's signature — breaking the signature scheme. Hence any secure signature scheme plus quantum lightning yields public-key quantum money.

Even more remarkably, Zhandry proves *win-win* theorems: any interactive commitment scheme that is computationally binding but *not* collapse-binding against quantum adversaries can be converted into public-key quantum money; and any non-collapsing hash function yields quantum lightning [3]. The philosophical payoff is large: the very quantum attacks cryptographers fear become the *building material* for unforgeable digital objects.

### 4.4 Copy-Protection of Point Functions and Compute-and-Compare Programs

Aaronson [4] formalized **quantum copy-protection**: a vendor maps a program $C$ to a quantum state $\rho_C$ such that (i) anyone holding $\rho_C$ can evaluate $C$ on any input with good correctness, but (ii) no QPT *pirate* can split $\rho_C$ into two registers $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ that *both* evaluate $C$ correctly on challenge inputs (the freeloaders). Classical copy-protection is impossible by trivial copying of the code string; quantumly, no-cloning gives a fighting chance.

The first class to succumb was **point functions** $P_y(x) = [x = y]$ — equivalently, password-checkers — and their generalization, **compute-and-compare programs** $\mathsf{CC}[f, y](x) = [f(x) = y]$. Ananth, Kaleoglu, Liu, and Zhandry [5] built a copy-protection scheme for compute-and-compare programs with provable security in the quantum random oracle model, the first copy-protection scheme with any provable security in a standard cryptographic model. The construction hides the *lock value* $y$ inside a subspace state: evaluation on $x$ computes $f(x)$ and tests equality against the hidden lock via a projective measurement that reveals nothing when the test fails. A pirate attempting to split the state faces a *simultaneous extraction* barrier: both freeloaders would need to extract the lock, which the monogamy-style argument forbids. Coladangelo, Liu, Liu, and Zhandry [6] extended this to hidden *coset* states, obtaining tokenized signatures, unclonable decryption, and the first copy-protection of pseudorandom functions in the plain model under iO.

The **pirate–freeloader game** is worth stating precisely, as it is the definitional core of the field:

1. Challenger samples $C$ and sends $\rho_C$ to the pirate $\mathcal{P}$.
2. $\mathcal{P}$ outputs a bipartite state $\sigma_{B_1 B_2}$ and sends the halves to freeloaders $\mathcal{F}_1, \mathcal{F}_2$.
3. Challenger sends challenge inputs $x_1, x_2$; freeloader $i$ outputs a bit $b_i$.
4. The adversary wins if $b_1 = b_2 = C(x_i)$ on both challenges.

Security demands the winning probability be at most negligibly above the trivial guessing bound.

### 4.5 One-Shot Signatures and Certified Deletion

**One-shot signatures (OSS)**, introduced by Amos, Georgiou, Kiayias, and Zhandry [8], are the signature analogue of quantum lightning: a quantum signing key $|sk\rangle$ permits signing *exactly one* message; the act of signing consumes the key via measurement, making a second signature physically impossible — no ledger, no trusted third party. The original construction lived in a classical oracle model and its proof was later found buggy; Shmueli and Zhandry gave the first standard-model OSS from iO and LWE [8], and from it a separation between classical-binding and collapse-binding commitments, resolving a decade-old open problem.

**Certified deletion** (Broadbent–Islam [7]) answers the question: *can one prove a secret was destroyed?* Classically impossible — a ciphertext can always be copied before "deletion." Quantumly, a ciphertext encoded with conjugate coding can be measured in the Hadamard basis, producing a *classical certificate* that (i) verifies against the sender's secret key and (ii) guarantees the holder can no longer decrypt even if the decryption key is later revealed. The security proof uses entropic uncertainty relations: the deletion measurement extracts so much information in the conjugate basis that information about the computational-basis plaintext is provably gone. Broadbent and Islam's scheme is information-theoretically secure, needs only single-qubit preparation and measurement, is noise-robust, and admits finite-key analysis — feasible with current technology [7]. A beautiful cross-connection: one-shot signatures plus witness encryption yield *publicly verifiable* certified deletion, and unclonable decryption keys arise from the same hidden-coset techniques as copy-protection [6][8].

## 5. Theoretical Analysis

We now reconstruct the central proofs that bind the taxonomy together.

> **Theorem (Unforgeability of Wiesner money, non-adaptive):** No QPT adversary that receives one banknote $(s, |x^\theta\rangle)$ and never interacts with the bank can output two states both accepted by verification, except with probability negligible in $n$.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose a counterfeiter $\mathcal{C}$ succeeds with non-negligible probability. Verification is a rank-one projective measurement onto $|x^\theta\rangle$; both outputs must therefore have large overlap with $|x^\theta\rangle$ averaged over the bank's random $(x, \theta)$. Consider the monogamy-of-entanglement game: $\mathcal{C}$'s two outputs are two registers $B_1, B_2$; give $(\theta, x)$ to two separated players who each measure their register. Both recover $x$ with non-negligible probability. But the optimal monogamy winning probability is $(1/2 + 1/(2\sqrt{2}))^n = 2^{-cn}$ for $c \approx 0.23$, exponentially small — contradiction. ∎

> **Theorem (Online attack [2]):** If the bank returns the banknote after verification (correcting errors), Wiesner's scheme is forgeable in $O(n)$ adaptive queries.

*Proof sketch.* For each position $i$, submit the note with qubit $i$ rotated by a small angle $\epsilon$; if the bank returns it *corrected*, the adversary learns that the returned state equals the original — i.e., verification tolerated the perturbation — revealing both $\theta_i$ and $x_i$ up to the noise model. Iterating over $i$ recovers $(x, \theta)$ entirely, after which the adversary mints arbitrary valid notes. ∎

> **Theorem (Bolt-to-certificate reduction, Zhandry [3]):** Quantum lightning implies public-key quantum money: given a lightning scheme $(\mathsf{Storm}, \mathsf{Ver})$ and an EUF-CMA signature scheme, $(\mathsf{Mint}, \mathsf{Verify})$ with $\mathsf{Mint} = (|\text{storm}\rangle, \sigma = \mathsf{Sign}(sk, s))$ is a secure public-key money scheme.

*Proof sketch.* A money forger outputs two notes $(|\psi_1\rangle, \sigma_1), (|\psi_2\rangle, \sigma_2)$ that verify. Either $s_1 = s_2$ — then the forger produced two bolts under one serial, breaking lightning — or $s_1 \ne s_2$ — then one serial was never signed by the mint, breaking signature unforgeability. ∎

> **Theorem (Inner-product adversary lower bound [2]):** Cloning $|A\rangle$ given oracles for $A, A^\perp$ requires $\Omega(2^{n/4})$ queries.

*Proof sketch.* Consider a cloner $\mathcal{C}$ run on $|A\rangle$ and, in a parallel experiment, on $|A'\rangle$ for an independent subspace $A'$. Let $|\phi_A\rangle, |\phi_{A'}\rangle$ be the joint output states before tracing the oracle workspace. A successful cloner produces outputs with large overlap with $|A\rangle^{\otimes 2}$ (resp. $|A'\rangle^{\otimes 2}$); since $\langle A|A'\rangle$ is exponentially small for random distinct subspaces, $\langle \phi_A | \phi_{A'}\rangle$ must be small — the algorithm *distinguishes* $A$ from $A'$. By the adversary method, distinguishing random $n/2$-dimensional subspaces with membership oracles requires $\Omega(2^{n/4})$ queries. ∎

These four arguments exhibit the field's characteristic proof anatomy: monogamy bounds for information-theoretic statements, oracle lower bounds for black-box schemes, and computational reductions (to signatures, iO, or LWE) for standard-model instantiations.

## 6. Limitations

- **Hardware demands.** All schemes require long-lived quantum memory: banknotes, bolts, and copy-protected programs must remain coherent between issuance and verification. Wiesner states and subspace states are highly entangled or high-fidelity multi-qubit objects; decoherence is the dominant practical barrier. Only certified deletion [7], needing merely single-qubit prepare-and-measure, is near-term feasible.
- **Noise vs. security tension.** Noise-tolerant verification (accepting approximate states) reopens the online attack surface: a bank that corrects "minor deviations" leaks $(\theta, x)$. Noise-tolerant public-key money from classical oracles was only recently achieved [2-related follow-ups], and general noise-robust copy-protection remains open.
- **Oracle and iO dependence.** Unconditional security lives in idealized models; standard-model security currently rests on *indistinguishability obfuscation* plus LWE — assumptions that are heavyweight and, for iO, still poorly understood against quantum adversaries.
- **Evasiveness restriction.** Copy-protection is proven only for *evasive* function classes (point functions, compute-and-compare, PRFs under conjectures). Copy-protecting *general* programs — or even all of $\mathsf{P}$ — is a major open problem; impossibility results suggest learnable or non-evasive classes cannot be protected.
- **Quantum communication.** Certified deletion with classical communication only is impossible information-theoretically; the sender must transmit quantum states (or interact), limiting deployment.
- **Everlasting vs. computational gaps.** Lightning and OSS in the standard model inherit the fragility of their assumptions: a future break of iO could retroactively enable forgery of *stored* bolts, unlike the information-theoretic core.

## 7. Conclusion

From Wiesner's rejected manuscript [1] to Zhandry's lightning [3] and the hidden-coset program obfuscation of CLLZ [6], uncloneable cryptography has matured from a physical curiosity into a principled discipline with sharp definitions, matching upper and lower bounds, and a growing web of reductions. The unifying thesis of this survey is that *one* physical fact — the impossibility of cloning unknown quantum states, refined through monogamy of entanglement and conjugate coding — ramifies into an entire cryptographic zoo: money no counterfeiter can forge, lightning no minter can duplicate, programs no pirate can split, signatures no key can issue twice, and ciphertexts whose destruction admits classical proof.

The frontier is now concrete: noise-tolerant public verification without oracles, copy-protection beyond evasive functions, everlasting security for lightning, and experimental demonstrations beyond the single-qubit regime of certified deletion. If history is a guide — Wiesner waited a decade for publication, and another three for his ideas to find their full expression — the most consequential uncloneable primitive may already be written down, awaiting only the hardware, and the proof techniques, to catch up.
