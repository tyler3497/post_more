# The Natural Gradient on Dually Flat Statistical Manifolds: Fisher Information, Dual Affine Connections, and the Geometry of Exponential-Family Optimization

## Abstract

A parametric statistical model is not merely a set of parameter vectors: it is a differentiable manifold whose points are probability distributions, and it carries a canonical Riemannian metric — the Fisher information. This thesis develops the differential-geometric foundation of statistical inference known as *information geometry*, and derives its most consequential computational payoff, the natural gradient. We begin with Rao's Fisher–Rao metric and Cencov's uniqueness theorem, which singles out the Fisher information as the only geometry of a statistical manifold coherent under sufficient statistics. We then construct Amari's one-parameter family of $\alpha$-connections and the dualistic structure $(M, g, \nabla, \nabla^{*})$, proving the dual flatness theorem and the generalized Pythagorean theorem for canonical divergences. Exponential families are shown to be the canonical dually flat manifolds: natural and expectation parameters are related by Legendre duality, and the Kullback–Leibler divergence is realized exactly as a Bregman divergence. Finally, we derive the natural gradient as steepest descent under the Fisher metric, establish its parameterization invariance and asymptotic Fisher efficiency, and connect it to contemporary second-order optimization through the generalized Gauss–Newton equivalence.

## 1 Introduction

In statistical estimation and machine learning, one optimizes over a parameter space $\Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ while the object of interest — the probability distribution $p(x;\theta)$ — lives elsewhere entirely. Classical gradient descent treats $\Theta$ as Euclidean, so a step $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla L(\theta)$ depends on the arbitrary choice of coordinates: reparameterize the model and the trajectory changes, even though the underlying distributions are unchanged. This coordinate dependence is a category error: the parameters are coordinates on a manifold $\mathcal{P} = \{p_\theta\}$, and optimization over $\mathcal{P}$ demands the manifold's own notion of steepest descent [1].

Information geometry supplies that notion. Introduced by Rao [3], axiomatized by Cencov [4], and developed into a full differential-geometric program by Amari [1][2], it equips the statistical manifold with the Fisher information as a Riemannian metric $g$ and, crucially, with a *pair* of affine connections $\nabla$ and $\nabla^{*}$ dual with respect to $g$ — the structural reason why maximum-likelihood estimation, the EM algorithm, and variational inference possess the elegant convergence and projection properties they do.

This thesis develops the dualistic geometry of statistical manifolds — the Fisher metric, Cencov's uniqueness theorem, the $\alpha$-family of connections, dually flat manifolds, and Legendre duality on exponential families — and shows how this geometry produces the natural gradient, analyzed both as geometry (steepest descent, parameterization invariance, Fisher efficiency) and as modern optimization (the generalized Gauss–Newton equivalence, approximations, and limitations) [5][6].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Statistical manifolds and the Fisher information

Let $\mathcal{P} = \{p_\theta(x) : \theta \in \Theta\}$ be a regular parametric family of probability densities on a sample space $\mathcal{X}$, with $\Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ open. The *score function* is the gradient of the log-likelihood,

$$\ell(x;\theta) = \log p(x;\theta), \qquad s_i(x;\theta) = \partial_i \ell(x;\theta) = \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta^i}\log p(x;\theta).$$

Under mild regularity (differentiation under the integral sign), the score has mean zero: $\mathbb{E}_\theta[s_i] = 0$. Its covariance defines the **Fisher information matrix**

$$g_{ij}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[\partial_i \ell \cdot \partial_j \ell\right] = -\mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[\partial_i \partial_j \ell\right], \tag{1}$$

the second equality being the *information identity*. Rao [3] recognized that $(g_{ij})$ is not merely a covariance: it is a Riemannian metric tensor on $\Theta$, turning the parameter domain into a Riemannian manifold whose geometry is intrinsic to the distributions, not to the coordinates.

The metric acquires its meaning from the Kullback–Leibler divergence. Expanding around $\theta$,

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\theta \,\|\, p_{\theta + d\theta}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\, g_{ij}(\theta)\, d\theta^i d\theta^j + o(\|d\theta\|^2). \tag{2}$$

Thus the Fisher information is the infinitesimal form of KL divergence: it measures how *distinguishable* nearby distributions are. The Cramér–Rao bound — $\mathrm{Var}_\theta(\hat{T}) \succeq g(\theta)^{-1}$ for any unbiased estimator $\hat{T}$ of $\theta$ — is the same object viewed from estimation theory: the metric governing distinguishability simultaneously bounds estimability [3].

### 2.2 Exponential and mixture families

Two classes of models recur throughout information geometry, each flat in one of the two distinguished connections:

- **Exponential families.** $p(x;\theta) = \exp\!\left(\theta^i F_i(x) - \psi(\theta)\right)$, with natural parameters $\theta$, sufficient statistics $F$, and log-partition function $\psi(\theta)$. Here $g_{ij}(\theta) = \partial_i \partial_j \psi(\theta)$: the Fisher metric is the Hessian of a convex potential.
- **Mixture families.** $p(x;\eta) = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \eta^i q_i(x) + \big(1 - \sum_i \eta^i\big) q_0(x)$, affine in the *mixture parameters* $\eta$.

The complementary affine structures of these two classes foreshadow the dual connections of Section 3.

### 2.3 Cencov's uniqueness theorem

Many Riemannian metrics can be placed on $\Theta$; why privilege (1)? Cencov [4] answered with a characterization of striking strength: the Fisher–Rao metric is the *unique* Riemannian metric (up to a positive constant) on statistical manifolds that is invariant under *Markov morphisms* — i.e., under congruent embeddings induced by sufficient statistics or, more generally, by stochastic maps (Markov kernels) between sample spaces. Any other metric would assign different lengths to statistically equivalent paths, a form of incoherence: two experimenters observing the same phenomenon through different sufficient statistics would disagree about distances between models.

Chentsov's program also identified the unique invariant symmetric $3$-tensor, the **Amari–Chentsov tensor**

$$T_{ijk}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[\partial_i \ell \cdot \partial_j \ell \cdot \partial_k \ell\right], \tag{3}$$

which, together with $g$, encodes the entire one-parameter family of $\alpha$-connections [7][12].

| Object | Definition | Invariance |
|---|---|---|
| Fisher metric $g_{ij}$ | $\mathbb{E}_\theta[\partial_i\ell\,\partial_j\ell]$ | Unique under Markov morphisms (Cencov) |
| Amari–Chentsov tensor $T_{ijk}$ | $\mathbb{E}_\theta[\partial_i\ell\,\partial_j\ell\,\partial_k\ell]$ | Unique invariant symmetric 3-tensor |
| KL divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ | $\mathbb{E}_\theta[\log(p_\theta/p_{\theta'})]$ | Infinitesimal form is $g$ |
| $\alpha$-connection $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$ | $\Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{ijk} = \Gamma^{(0)}_{ijk} - \tfrac{\alpha}{2}T_{ijk}$ | Dual pair: $(\alpha, -\alpha)$ |

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Dual connections

A Riemannian manifold carries a canonical connection, the Levi-Civita connection $\nabla^{(0)}$. Information geometry instead works with a *dualistic structure* $(M, g, \nabla, \nabla^{*})$: a Riemannian metric $g$ together with a *pair* of torsion-free affine connections satisfying

$$X\langle Y, Z\rangle = \langle \nabla_X Y, Z\rangle + \langle Y, \nabla^{*}_X Z\rangle, \qquad \forall\, X, Y, Z, \tag{4}$$

where $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle = g(\cdot,\cdot)$. Parallel transport by $\nabla$ preserves inner products when paired with transport by $\nabla^{*}$; given $g$ and any torsion-free $\nabla$, (4) determines $\nabla^{*}$ uniquely. The Levi-Civita connection is the self-dual case $\nabla = \nabla^{*}$.

On a statistical manifold, there is no reason to privilege the self-dual choice. The distinguished connections arise from embeddings into the space of positive measures: the **exponential connection** $\nabla^{(e)} = \nabla^{(1)}$, whose geodesics are exponential families, and the **mixture connection** $\nabla^{(m)} = \nabla^{(-1)}$, whose geodesics are mixture families. They are dual with respect to $g$, and the $\alpha$-family interpolates:

$$\Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{ijk} = \Gamma^{(0)}_{ijk} - \frac{\alpha}{2}\, T_{ijk}, \qquad \alpha \in \mathbb{R}, \tag{5}$$

so that $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$ and $\nabla^{(-\alpha)}$ are dual. The cases $\alpha = 0$ (Levi-Civita), $\alpha = 1$ ($e$-connection), and $\alpha = -1$ ($m$-connection) are the workhorses of the theory [2][7].

### 3.2 From divergence to duality

Dualistic structures can be *induced* by a divergence function. Given $D(p\|q)$ with $D(p\|p) = 0$ and vanishing first derivatives on the diagonal, Eguchi's construction recovers $g$ and the connection pair $(\nabla, \nabla^{*})$ from mixed third derivatives of $D$ evaluated on the diagonal. For the KL divergence this yields exactly the Fisher metric and the $e$/$m$-connection pair; for a **Bregman divergence** $B_\psi$ generated by a strictly convex $\psi$, it yields a *dually flat* manifold — both connections flat, with global affine coordinates linked by the Legendre transform $\eta = \nabla\psi(\theta)$ [7][11].

### 3.3 Deriving the natural gradient

Consider minimizing a loss $L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}[-\log p_\theta(y|x)]$ over a statistical manifold. Steepest descent in *distribution* space — rather than in arbitrary parameter coordinates — solves

$$\tilde{\nabla}L(\theta) = \arg\min_{\|d\|_g = \epsilon} L(\theta + d), \tag{6}$$

where $\|d\|_g^2 = d^i g_{ij} d^j$ is the Fisher norm, equivalently the KL divergence between the current and perturbed model by (2). A Lagrange-multiplier computation gives the celebrated formula [1]:

$$\boxed{\quad \tilde{\nabla}L(\theta) = G(\theta)^{-1} \nabla L(\theta), \quad G = (g_{ij}) \quad} \tag{7}$$

the **natural gradient**. It is steepest descent with respect to the information geometry rather than the Euclidean geometry of an arbitrary parameterization. The reference implementation below captures the essence for a small model:

```python
import numpy as np

def natural_gradient_step(theta, grad, fisher, lr=0.1, damping=1e-3):
    """One natural-gradient step: theta <- theta - lr * G^{-1} grad.
    Damping (Tikhonov) stabilizes inversion when G is ill-conditioned,
    exactly as motivated by Martens' second-order analysis [5]."""
    G = fisher(theta) + damping * np.eye(theta.size)
    nat_grad = np.linalg.solve(G, grad(theta))
    return theta - lr * nat_grad

def empirical_fisher(theta, xs, logp_grad):
    """Empirical Fisher: average of outer products of per-sample score
    vectors. NOTE: this is NOT the true Fisher (see Sec. 6 and [9])."""
    S = np.stack([logp_grad(x, theta) for x in xs])  # (n, d)
    return (S.T @ S) / len(xs)
```

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Fisher metric and its privileged status

Cencov's theorem [4] converts a convention into a necessity. A sufficient statistic $T$ induces a *congruent embedding* $\mathcal{P}_\mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{P}_\mathcal{Y}$ losing no information, and any geometrically meaningful distance must be preserved under it. Cencov proved that only (a positive multiple of) the Fisher metric is invariant under all such Markov morphisms. The Amari–Chentsov tensor (3) is likewise the unique invariant symmetric $3$-tensor, and together $(g, T)$ generate every $\alpha$-connection via (5) [7].

This uniqueness has a practical corollary: any preconditioner claiming statistical legitimacy must approximate the Fisher information, not an arbitrary curvature. Newton's Hessian lacks this invariance — Martens [5] shows the Hessian is *not* even approximately parameterization invariant, whereas the Fisher matrix is. The three canonical connections contrast as follows:

| $\alpha$ | Connection $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$ | Flat model class | Geodesics |
|---|---|---|---|
| $+1$ | exponential ($e$-) | exponential families | $\log p_t = (1-t)\log p_0 + t\log p_1 - \psi_t$ |
| $0$ | Levi-Civita (metric) | — (self-dual) | Fisher–Rao geodesics |
| $-1$ | mixture ($m$-) | mixture families | $p_t = (1-t)p_0 + t p_1$ |

### 4.2 Dual flatness and the $\alpha$-family of curvatures

A statistical manifold is **$\alpha$-flat** if $\nabla^{(\alpha)}$ has vanishing curvature, admitting affine coordinates in which its geodesics are straight lines. The *dual flatness theorem* (Amari [2]) states that $\alpha$-flatness implies $(-\alpha)$-flatness: a manifold flat in the $e$-connection is automatically flat in the $m$-connection, with a *second* affine coordinate system. A manifold flat in both $\nabla$ and $\nabla^{*}$ is **dually flat**, and possesses two global affine coordinate systems $\theta$ ($\nabla$-affine) and $\eta$ ($\nabla^{*}$-affine), a pair of convex potentials $\psi(\theta)$, $\varphi(\eta)$ related by the **Legendre transform** $\varphi(\eta) = \sup_\theta \{\theta \cdot \eta - \psi(\theta)\}$ with $\eta = \nabla\psi(\theta)$ and $\theta = \nabla\varphi(\eta)$, the metric as a Hessian in *either* chart ($g_{ij}(\theta) = \partial_i\partial_j \psi(\theta)$, $g^{ij}(\eta) = \partial^i\partial^j \varphi(\eta)$), and a canonical divergence $D(p\|q) = \psi(\theta_p) + \varphi(\eta_q) - \theta_p \cdot \eta_q$ that reduces to the Bregman divergence for $B_\psi$-induced structures.

The curvature tensors satisfy $R^{(\alpha)} = -R^{(-\alpha)}$-type symmetries, so the $\alpha$-family encodes a single underlying geometry viewed through complementary affine lenses [11][12].

### 4.3 Exponential families as dually flat manifolds

The exponential family $p(x;\theta) = \exp(\theta \cdot F(x) - \psi(\theta))$ is the canonical example. Its expectation parameters $\eta = \mathbb{E}_\theta[F(X)] = \nabla\psi(\theta)$ are the $\nabla^{(m)}$-affine coordinates, while $\theta$ are $\nabla^{(e)}$-affine. The KL divergence between two members is exactly the Bregman divergence of $\psi$:

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta} \,\|\, p_{\theta'}) = B_\psi(\theta' \,\|\, \theta) = \psi(\theta') - \psi(\theta) - \langle \eta, \theta' - \theta\rangle. \tag{8}$$

This identity is the engine of the *generalized Pythagorean theorem*. Let $\mathcal{M}$ be an $m$-autoparallel submanifold (e.g., a moment-constrained model) and let $\hat{q}$ be the $m$-projection of $p$ onto $\mathcal{M}$ (the member of $\mathcal{M}$ minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|\cdot)$). Then for every $q \in \mathcal{M}$:

> **Theorem (Pythagorean, Amari [2]):** *On a dually flat manifold, with $\hat{q} = \arg\min_{q \in \mathcal{M}} D(p\|q)$ the $m$-projection onto an $m$-autoparallel submanifold $\mathcal{M}$,*
> $$D(p\|q) = D(p\|\hat{q}) + D(\hat{q}\|q) \qquad \forall\, q \in \mathcal{M}.$$
> *The $m$-geodesic from $p$ to $\hat{q}$ meets $\mathcal{M}$ orthogonally in the Fisher metric.*

Two algorithmic consequences follow immediately. Maximum-likelihood estimation on an exponential family is $m$-projection of the empirical distribution onto the model — the MLE matches moments, $\eta_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \bar{F}$, in a single $m$-projection. The EM algorithm alternates $e$- and $m$-projections (alternating KL minimization), and the Pythagorean decomposition is what makes its monotonic convergence transparent [12].

| Family | Natural params $\theta$ | Expectation params $\eta$ | Potential $\psi(\theta)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bernoulli | $\log \tfrac{\mu}{1-\mu}$ | $\mu$ | $\log(1 + e^\theta)$ |
| Poisson | $\log \lambda$ | $\lambda$ | $e^\theta$ |
| Gaussian ($\sigma$ fixed) | $\mu/\sigma^2$ | $\mu$ | $\theta^2\sigma^2/2$ |
| Multinomial | $\log \tfrac{\pi_i}{\pi_0}$ | $\pi_i$ | $\log(1 + \sum e^{\theta_i})$ |

### 4.4 The natural gradient: invariance, efficiency, and second-order form

**Parameterization invariance.** Let $\eta = \eta(\theta)$ be a reparameterization with Jacobian $J^i_j = \partial \eta^i / \partial \theta^j$. The Fisher matrices and gradients transform as $G_\eta = (J^{-1})^\top G_\theta J^{-1}$ and $\nabla_\eta L = (J^{-1})^\top \nabla_\theta L$, whence

$$G_\eta^{-1} \nabla_\eta L = J\, G_\theta^{-1} \nabla_\theta L, \tag{9}$$

i.e., the natural-gradient *vector* pushes forward correctly: it is the same tangent vector to the distribution manifold in both charts. Vanilla gradient descent enjoys no such property — the Hessian, in particular, fails even approximate invariance [5]. In the continuum limit, natural gradient descent follows a well-defined curve in distribution space regardless of parameterization.

**Fisher efficiency.** Amari [1] proved that online natural-gradient learning is *Fisher efficient*: its asymptotic covariance attains the Cramér–Rao bound, $\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\theta}_t) \sim (t\,G)^{-1}$. No first-order method sharing only gradient information can do better asymptotically.

**Generalized Gauss–Newton equivalence.** Martens [5] reframes the natural gradient as approximate second-order optimization: for negative log-likelihood losses of exponential-family output models (squared error with Gaussian outputs, cross-entropy with softmax outputs), the Fisher information *coincides* with the generalized Gauss–Newton (GGN) matrix. NGD is therefore an approximate Newton method whose curvature matrix is always positive semidefinite, parameterization invariant, and better-behaved than the Hessian near saddles — which also explains why practical implementations (K-FAC and descendants) use Kronecker-factored blocks as structured GGN approximations [6].

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Proof sketch of the Pythagorean theorem

Write the canonical divergence in dual coordinates as $D(p\|q) = \psi(\theta_p) + \varphi(\eta_q) - \theta_p \cdot \eta_q$. The first-order optimality of the $m$-projection $\hat{q}$ makes the $m$-geodesic $p \to \hat{q}$ (straight in $\eta$-coordinates) orthogonal to the $m$-autoparallel submanifold $\mathcal{M}$ in the Fisher metric. Expanding $D(p\|q) - D(p\|\hat{q}) - D(\hat{q}\|q)$, the cross terms cancel by this orthogonality, leaving the decomposition; full proof in Amari [2, Thm. 3.9] and [12].

> **Proposition (Geometric Cramér–Rao):** *For an unbiased estimator $\hat{\theta}$ taking values in $\Theta$, $\mathrm{Cov}_\theta(\hat{\theta}) - G(\theta)^{-1} \succeq 0$. The bound is saturated iff the estimator's influence function is $G^{-1}$ times the score — i.e., iff estimation proceeds along the natural gradient direction.*

### 5.2 Convergence behavior: natural gradient vs. gradient descent

Martens [5] analyzes stochastic natural gradient descent on convex quadratics $L(\theta) = \tfrac{1}{2}\theta^\top A \theta - b^\top \theta$ in closed form. Where ordinary SGD's convergence degrades with the condition number $\kappa(A)$, the natural gradient effectively whitens the geometry, inheriting the favorable convergence of exact second-order methods while remaining well-defined where the Hessian is indefinite. The key practical upshot of [5] is a design discipline: damping ($G + \lambda I$) and trust-region control are not hacks but the correct way to handle the discrepancy between the local quadratic model and the true loss, exactly as in Newton methods.

A minimal numerical demonstration on logistic regression illustrates the geometry: measuring progress in *KL divergence to the optimum* rather than in parameter distance, natural gradient descent traces a nearly straight line in distribution space, while vanilla gradient descent zigzags along the valley of an ill-conditioned likelihood. The two trajectories coincide only when $G \propto I$ — i.e., when the parameterization is already Fisher-orthonormal.

### 5.3 The empirical Fisher controversy

A widely used shortcut replaces the true Fisher $G(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[ss^\top]$ with the *empirical* Fisher $\hat{G}(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i s(x_i;\theta)s(x_i;\theta)^\top$, averaging per-sample gradient outer products at the *observed* labels. Kunstner, Hennig, and Balles [9] demonstrate that this substitution is theoretically unsound: the empirical Fisher need not converge to the true Fisher even at the optimum, and can produce preconditioners that actively harm optimization — including on the generalized-linear models where the true Fisher is well-behaved. Several "natural gradient" implementations are thus distinct algorithms whose good behavior, when present, comes from mechanisms other than information geometry.

### 5.4 Structure of the Fisher in deep networks

Amari, Karakida, and Oizumi [10] analyze the Fisher information matrix of random deep networks via mean-field theory. Their central result: as layer width $n \to \infty$, the off-diagonal blocks of $G$ corresponding to different layers are $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ — negligibly small — so $G$ is well-approximated by a layer-wise block-diagonal matrix; within a layer, unit-wise blocks are similarly negligible. This justifies unit-wise and block-diagonal natural gradient methods [6] and Kronecker-factored approximations (K-FAC), which exploit the further factorization of each block into input- and output-covariance factors. Ollivier [6] extends this program with four algorithms spanning the scalability spectrum, each preserving invariance under data and network re-representations — the practical payoff of taking the Riemannian viewpoint seriously.

## 6 Limitations

1. **Curved and singular models.** Dual flatness belongs to exponential and mixture families, not to statistical manifolds in general. Neural networks and latent-variable models are *curved* — nonzero $\alpha$-curvature, no global affine coordinates, and only approximate Pythagorean decompositions. Singular models (non-identifiable parameters) are worse: the Fisher matrix degenerates, the Cramér–Rao bound breaks down, and Watanabe's singular learning theory replaces the $d/2$ of BIC with the real log-canonical threshold. The natural gradient's guarantees assume regularity throughout.

2. **Computational cost.** Forming and inverting $G$ costs $O(d^3)$ naively and $O(d^2)$ in memory — infeasible for large models. All practical deployments (diagonal, block-diagonal, K-FAC, or subsampled approximations) sacrifice exactness, and with it, exact parameterization invariance: the invariance result (9) holds for the true Fisher, and approximations inherit it only approximately [5].

3. **Empirical vs. true Fisher.** As established in [9], the empirical Fisher is not a consistent estimator of the Fisher matrix away from realizability, and "natural gradient" implementations built on it may not implement natural gradient descent at all. Practitioners should treat the distinction as load-bearing, not terminological.

4. **Damping is mandatory.** The Fisher metric describes infinitesimal geometry; finite steps require regularization. Martens [5] shows undamped NGD can diverge on non-convex problems for the same reason undamped Newton diverges.

5. **Scope of the divergence geometry.** Results proved for KL/Bregman divergences (Pythagoras, flat projections) do not transfer automatically to Rényi, $f$-divergences, or Wasserstein geometries, each of which induces different connection geometry [7].

## 7 Conclusion

Information geometry reframes statistics as geometry: models are manifolds, the Fisher information is their essentially unique metric, and the dual pair of exponential and mixture connections encodes the affine structures that make exponential families computationally tractable. On dually flat manifolds — the exponential families at the heart of classical statistics and of every softmax or Gaussian output layer in deep learning — Legendre duality unifies natural and expectation parameters, the KL divergence becomes a Bregman divergence, and estimation decomposes into orthogonal projections with a Pythagorean theorem.

The natural gradient is this geometry's algorithmic dividend: steepest descent under the Fisher metric, parameterization invariant, Fisher efficient, and — via the generalized Gauss–Newton equivalence — a principled approximate Newton method. Its modern incarnations, from K-FAC to Ollivier's invariant algorithms, are best understood not as optimization tricks but as structure-preserving discretizations of a Riemannian flow.

The caveats are real: curvature outside exponential families, singular models, the cubic cost of exact inversion, and the empirical-Fisher trap all circumscribe the theory's reach. But the central lesson stands: whenever the object being optimized is a probability distribution, the parameter space is the wrong space in which to measure a step — and information geometry is the right one.
