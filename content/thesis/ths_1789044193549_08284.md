# Post-Quantum Digital Signatures: A Comparative Analysis of CRYSTALS-Dilithium (ML-DSA), FALCON (FN-DSA), and SPHINCS+ (SLH-DSA) Across Lattice and Hash-Based Assumptions

## Abstract

Shor's algorithm renders RSA, DSA, and ECDSA forgeable by a cryptographically relevant quantum computer, invalidating the authentication infrastructure of TLS, code signing, and digital identity. This thesis compares the three NIST post-quantum signature standards — CRYSTALS-Dilithium (FIPS 204, ML-DSA) [1][2], FALCON (FIPS 206 draft, FN-DSA) [4][5], and SPHINCS+ (FIPS 205, SLH-DSA) — spanning two hardness paradigms: structured lattices (Module-LWE/SIS, NTRU) and symmetric primitives (hash security alone). We dissect Dilithium's Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts construction and its rejection-sampling security argument, FALCON's GPV hash-and-sign framework with fast Fourier trapdoor sampling, and SPHINCS+'s stateless hypertree built from WOTS+ one-time and FORS few-time signatures with a tight tweakable-hash reduction. A unified evaluation compares signature sizes, key sizes, signing latency, side-channel surface, and assumption strength. We conclude that no single scheme dominates: Dilithium is the balanced default, FALCON serves size-constrained protocols, and SPHINCS+ is the assumption-diverse hedge — and that hybrid classical–PQC deployment with crypto-agility is the only defensible migration path.

## 1 Introduction

Digital signatures are the *trust anchors* of modern computing: every HTTPS certificate chain, OS update, package release, and cryptocurrency transaction reduces to unforgeability under chosen-message attack. For three decades that unforgeability has rested on **integer factorization** (RSA) and the **discrete logarithm problem** (DSA, ECDSA, EdDSA) [8].

Shor's 1994 quantum algorithm gives polynomial-time quantum solutions to both problems, so a sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer renders RSA-2048 and P-256 ECDSA forgeable *retroactively* — signatures recorded today can be broken tomorrow. Since keys and certificates live for years, migration cannot wait for the hardware; NIST's PQC standardization project, launched in 2016, is the institutional response [8].

For **key encapsulation**, NIST selected CRYSTALS-Kyber (FIPS 203, ML-KEM). For **signatures**, it selected three schemes covering two mathematical families [8]:

1. **CRYSTALS-Dilithium** → *FIPS 204: Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (ML-DSA)* — the primary standard [1][2].
2. **FALCON** → *FIPS 206: FFT-over-NTRU-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (FN-DSA)* — the compact-signature alternative [4][5][9].
3. **SPHINCS+** → *FIPS 205: Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (SLH-DSA)* — the conservative backup built on an entirely different assumption [6][7].

Three schemes exist for **cryptographic diversification**: lattice schemes are fast but concentrate assumption risk; hash-based schemes are slower and bulkier but rest on the hash function — the most battle-tested primitive in cryptography. We compare the schemes along three axes:

- **Construction**: Fiat–Shamir-with-aborts (Dilithium) vs. GPV hash-and-sign with fast Fourier sampling (FALCON) vs. hypertree/WOTS+/FORS hash composition (SPHINCS+).
- **Cost model**: public-key size, signature size, and signing/verification latency.
- **Assumption strength and implementation risk**: structured-lattice problems vs. hash-function properties, and the very different side-channel profiles of Gaussian sampling, rejection sampling, and hash trees.

The rest of this thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the quantum threat model, lattice problems, and hash-based signature history. Section 3 describes our comparative methodology. Section 4 presents the deep technical analysis of each scheme. Section 5 consolidates empirical performance data and sketches the security reductions. Section 6 discusses limitations and open problems. Section 7 concludes with deployment guidance.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The Quantum Threat to Classical Signatures

Shor's algorithm solves integer factorization and discrete logarithms in *O((log N)³)* time; RSA, DSA, ECDSA, and EdDSA all collapse. Grover's algorithm offers only a quadratic speedup against symmetric primitives — remediable by doubling key and digest sizes. Hence **signatures require entirely new mathematics** while symmetric cryptography needs only parameter inflation.

NIST defines five **security categories** for PQC candidates, benchmarked against the resources needed to break AES-128 (Level 1), SHA-256/SHA3-256 collisions (Level 2), AES-192 (Level 3), SHA3-384 collisions (Level 4), and AES-256 (Level 5) [1]. All three signature schemes analyzed here target these levels with three parameter sets each.

### 2.2 Lattices and Hard Problems

A *lattice* is a discrete additive subgroup of ℝⁿ. The canonical hard problems are:

- **Short Integer Solution (SIS)**: given a random matrix **A** ∈ ℤ_q^{n×m}, find a short nonzero vector **z** with **Az = 0 mod q**.
- **Learning With Errors (LWE)**: distinguish (**A**, **As + e**) from uniform, where **s** is secret and **e** is small noise.

Cryptography uses *structured* variants: **Module-LWE / Module-SIS** over *R_q = ℤ_q[X]/(Xⁿ + 1)* with module rank *k, ℓ* (Dilithium), and **NTRU** over the same ring with a hidden short basis (FALCON) [2][4]. The ring structure enables NTT/FFT *O(n log n)* multiplication and ~2.5× smaller public keys than unstructured lattices [2].

### 2.3 Hash-Based Signatures: From Lamport to SPHINCS+

Hash-based signatures predate public-key cryptography itself: Lamport's 1979 one-time signature (OTS) signs one message per key pair. Merkle's 1989 tree construction aggregates 2^h OTS key pairs under a single root, but is **stateful** — reusing a leaf destroys security, so the signer must track state across invocations, which is operationally fragile for distributed servers and backups.

The breakthrough of SPHINCS (2015) and SPHINCS+ (2017/2019) [6] is **statelessness**: the signer picks a leaf *pseudorandomly* per message from a hypertree of up to 2^64 leaves, so reuse is negligible. Security reduces solely to hash-function properties — the most conservative assumption available [6][7].

### 2.4 Prior Standardization Outcomes

In July 2022 NIST announced the first PQC selections [8]; on **August 13, 2024** it published FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA), with FALCON's FIPS 206 in development [1][8]. In March 2025 NIST selected HQC as a backup KEM, but no signature backup beyond SPHINCS+ exists — underscoring the hash-based leg's importance [8].

| Scheme | Standard | Family | Hardness assumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| CRYSTALS-Dilithium | FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) [1] | Module lattice | Module-LWE + Module-SIS |
| FALCON | FIPS 206 (FN-DSA, draft) [5] | NTRU lattice | SIS over NTRU lattices |
| SPHINCS+ | FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) | Stateless hash-based | Hash collision / second-preimage resistance [6] |
| RSA / ECDSA | FIPS 186-5 | Factoring / DLP | Broken by Shor's algorithm |
## 3 Methodology

Our comparison is grounded in the **primary specifications and security analyses** rather than marketing material: the FIPS 204 standard [1], the CRYSTALS-Dilithium TCHES paper and ePrint report [2][3], the FALCON specification and supporting documentation [4][9], the SPHINCS+ framework paper [6], and NIST's standardization documentation [8]. The methodology has four components:

1. **Constructional analysis.** We reconstruct each scheme's algorithms from the specifications: sampling distributions, rejection conditions, and verification identities.
2. **Parameter systematization.** We tabulate standardized parameter sets with byte-exact sizes and NIST security-level mappings.
3. **Empirical consolidation.** We normalize published reference/AVX2 benchmarks into a single comparison.
4. **Assumption and risk audit.** We evaluate hardness assumptions, reduction tightness (including Dilithium's QROM result [3]), and implementation hazards — FALCON's floating-point FFT sampling, Dilithium's rejection-sampling leakage, and the state-management problem SPHINCS+ eliminates.

> **Theorem (Informal reduction structure):** Each scheme's EUF-CMA security (SUF-CMA for ML-DSA [1]) reduces to its underlying problem — Module-LWE/SIS for Dilithium [2][3], SIS over NTRU lattices for FALCON [4], hash collision/second-preimage resistance for SPHINCS+ [6] — in the (quantum) random-oracle model. SPHINCS+'s reduction is tight; the lattice proofs lose factors to forking and the Fiat–Shamir transform.

All claims about parameter values and sizes are taken from the standards and specifications cited; where multiple variants exist, we report the NIST-standardized ones.
## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 CRYSTALS-Dilithium: Fiat–Shamir with Aborts over Module Lattices

CRYSTALS-Dilithium [2][3] is the **primary** NIST signature standard (ML-DSA) [1], operating over *R_q = ℤ_q[X]/(X^256 + 1)* with *q = 83,80,417* — a prime chosen for fast NTT polynomial multiplication [2]. All arithmetic is module-linear algebra over *R_q*.

**Key generation.** Expand a public matrix **A** ∈ *R_q^{k×ℓ}* from a 32-byte seed *ρ*, sample short secrets **s₁**, **s₂**, and compute **t = As₁ + s₂**. Only the high bits *t₁* of **t = t₁·2^d + t₀** are published, shrinking the public key to *(ρ, t₁)* [1][2].

**Signing: Fiat–Shamir with aborts.** The signer repeatedly:

1. Samples a masking vector **y** with coefficients uniformly in *[-γ₁, γ₁]*.
2. Computes **w = Ay**, extracts high bits **w₁**, and derives the challenge *c = H(μ ‖ w₁)*, where *μ* binds the message (plus per-signature randomness *ρ′* in the randomized variant).
3. Computes the response **z = y + c·s₁**.
4. **Rejection sampling:** if ‖**z**‖∞ ≥ *γ₁ − β*, the low bits of **w − c·s₂** are too large, or the hint count exceeds *ω*, discard and restart.

> **Theorem (Lyubashevsky's rejection sampling):** Conditioned on acceptance, **z** is statistically independent of **s₁** — signatures leak *zero* information about the secret key, however many are observed [2][3].

**Verification** recomputes *w₁′* from **Az − c·t₁·2^d** with hints, re-derives *c̃ = H(μ ‖ w₁′)*, and accepts iff *c̃ = c* and ‖**z**‖∞ < *γ₁ − β* [1].

Unlike earlier lattice signatures (BLISS), Dilithium uses **no discrete Gaussian sampling anywhere** — Gaussians enabled cache-timing attacks on BLISS, while uniform sampling plus aborts is trivially constant-time [2].

The three standardized parameter sets are:

| Parameter set | *(k, ℓ)* | *η* | Public key | Signature | NIST level |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ML-DSA-44 | (4, 4) | 2 | 1,312 B | 2,420 B | 2 |
| ML-DSA-65 | (6, 5) | 4 | 1,952 B | 3,309 B | 3 |
| ML-DSA-87 | (8, 7) | 2 | 2,592 B | 4,627 B | 5 |

The standard also defines **HashML-DSA**, a domain-separated pre-hash variant [1]. Security is *strong* EUF-CMA (SUF-CMA) [1], reducing to Module-LWE, Module-SIS, and **SelfTargetMSIS**, whose quantum hardness follows from a QROM reduction via quantum reprogramming [3].

```python
def dilithium_sign(sk, msg, A, gamma1, beta):
    s1, s2, t0, rho = sk
    mu = H(tr || msg)
    while True:                            # "with aborts" loop
        y  = sample_uniform(-gamma1, gamma1)
        w1 = high_bits(A @ y)
        c  = H(mu + w1)
        z  = y + c * s1
        if norm_inf(z) >= gamma1 - beta:
            continue                       # reject: leaks nothing
        r0 = low_bits(A @ y - c * s2)
        if norm_inf(r0) >= gamma2 - beta:
            continue
        h  = make_hint(-c * t0, A @ y - c*s2 + c*t0)
        if count_ones(h) > omega:
            continue
        return (z, h, c)
```

### 4.2 FALCON: Hash-and-Sign over NTRU Lattices via Fast Fourier Sampling

FALCON (*Fast-Fourier Lattice-based Compact Signatures over NTRU*) [4][9] instantiates the **Gentry–Peikert–Vaikuntanathan (GPV) hash-and-sign framework**: the public key describes a lattice, the secret key is a *short trapdoor basis*, and signing samples a lattice vector near a hashed target point [9].

**The NTRU lattice.** The secret key consists of four short polynomials *f, g, F, G ∈ ℤ[X]/(X^n + 1)* satisfying the **NTRU equation** [4][5]:

$$fG - gF = q \pmod{X^n + 1}, \qquad h = g \cdot f^{-1} \pmod q$$

with *n ∈ {512, 1024}*, *q = 12,289*. The public key is the single polynomial *h*; the private basis *B = [[g, −f],[G, −F]]* is a trapdoor for *{ (s₁, s₂) : s₁ + s₂·h = 0 mod q }* [5].

**Signing by trapdoor sampling.** Hash the message with a random salt to a target *c*, then use the secret basis to sample short *(s₁, s₂)* with *s₁ = c − s₂·h* via **fast Fourier sampling** — a recursive LDL*-based Gaussian sampler over the cyclotomic tower running in *O(n log n)* rather than the *O(n²)* of naive GPV sampling [4][9]. The signature *(salt, s₂)* compresses to ~666 bytes for Falcon-512.

> **Theorem (GPV / FALCON security sketch):** If the sampler's output is statistically close to the ideal discrete Gaussian, signatures are *simulatable without the trapdoor*; unforgeability then reduces to SIS over NTRU lattices [4][9].

**Verification** recomputes *s₁ = c − s₂·h mod q* and checks ‖(s₁, s₂)‖² ≤ β² — essentially one NTT-domain multiplication [4].

FALCON's headline advantage is **compactness**: Falcon-512 keys are 897 bytes and signatures average ~690 bytes (variable-length), roughly *3–5× smaller* than Dilithium at comparable security [5]. The price is implementation complexity: the sampler needs **double-precision floating-point FFTs**, which complicate constant-time code, have enabled FPU side-channel attacks, and exclude FPU-less embedded targets [4]. FALCON is the specialist's choice — ideal for bandwidth-constrained protocols (certificates, DNSSEC) but demanding careful engineering [9].

| Parameter set | *n* | *q* | Public key | Signature (avg) | NIST level |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Falcon-512 | 512 | 12,289 | 897 B | ~666 B | 1 |
| Falcon-1024 | 1024 | 12,289 | 1,793 B | ~1,280 B | 5 |

### 4.3 SPHINCS+: Stateless Hash-Based Signatures from Hypertrees, WOTS+, and FORS

SPHINCS+ [6] is the deliberate *outlier*: its security rests on **nothing but the hash function** — no lattices, no number theory — only that SHA-256/SHAKE-256 behave like random oracles for second-preimage and PRF properties [6][7].

**Architecture.** A SPHINCS+ key pair is a **hypertree**: *d* layers of XMSS Merkle trees totaling 2^h leaves, each leaf a **WOTS+** one-time key pair. The hypertree root is the public key — just *n* bytes (32 for the 128-bit sets) [6][7].

**Signing.** To sign a message *M*:

1. Derive a pseudorandom leaf index *idx* and randomness *R* from a secret seed and *M* (**statelessness**: random, not sequential, leaf choice).
2. Sign *M*'s digest with **FORS** (*Forest of Random Subsets*): the digest selects *k* of *t* secret values in each of *k* trees; security holds while a FORS key signs few messages [6].
3. Authenticate the FORS public key with the WOTS+ key at leaf *idx* of the bottom XMSS layer.
4. Chain WOTS+ signatures up the *d* layers to the hypertree root [6].

**Verification** recomputes FORS, then walks the *d* authentication paths, checking *O(h)* hashes — fast, though signing costs *tens of thousands* of hash evaluations.

> **Theorem (Tweakable-hash security reduction):** SPHINCS+'s *tweakable hash functions* give a unified, tight proof: EUF-CMA security reduces tightly to the hash's multi-target collision and pseudorandomness properties, with parameters derived directly from the bound [6].

Parameters trade speed against size — *s* (small) vs. *f* (fast), three security levels, three hash families:

| Parameter set | Public key | Signature | Sign ops | NIST level |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SPHINCS+-128s | 32 B | 7,856 B | ~2^h/2 slow | 1 |
| SPHINCS+-128f | 32 B | 17,088 B | fast | 1 |
| SPHINCS+-192s | 48 B | 16,224 B | slow | 3 |
| SPHINCS+-256f | 64 B | 49,856 B | fast | 5 |

Signatures span kilobytes to tens of kilobytes — 5–20× Dilithium's — but the assumption profile is unmatched: even a catastrophic lattice break leaves SPHINCS+ standing, which is why NIST standardized it as the backup [7][8].

## 5 Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Performance Consolidation

Published benchmarks (reference C and AVX2 implementations from the NIST submission packages and subsequent studies [2][4][6]) paint a consistent picture on a modern x86-64 core:

- **Key generation** is a one-time cost: Dilithium's NTT expansion takes well under a millisecond; FALCON's NTRU key generation (solving *fG − gF = q*) costs tens of milliseconds; SPHINCS+ needs a handful of hashes [4][6].
- **Signing throughput** favors lattices decisively: Dilithium signs in *hundreds of microseconds* (AVX2), with rejection adding small expected overhead (tuned to ≈ 4–7 repetitions) [2]; FALCON-512 stays sub-millisecond [4][9]. SPHINCS+ is *orders of magnitude slower* — the "f" variants perform ~10⁵ hash compressions per signature, "s" variants far more [6].
- **Verification** is cheap everywhere — one or two polynomial multiplications or *O(h)* hashes, all well under 100 µs optimized.

The binding constraint is therefore **not CPU but bytes**: Dilithium-65, FALCON-512, and SPHINCS+-128f certificate chains differ by an order of magnitude in handshake size — which is why TLS groups pair ML-DSA with ECDSA in hybrids while reserving FALCON for size-sensitive profiles [8].

### 5.2 Security Reductions: A Sketch

**Dilithium.** EUF-CMA reduces to Module-LWE (hiding the secret in *t = As₁ + s₂*), Module-SIS (forging implies a short solution), and **SelfTargetMSIS** (challenge binding) [2]. The Kiltz–Lyubashevsky–Schaffner ROM proof was extended to the **QROM** by reducing SelfTargetMSIS from MLWE via quantum reprogramming and rewinding, at the cost of larger parameters under the *q = 1 mod 2n* NTT constraint [3].

**FALCON.** The fast Fourier sampler's output is within negligible statistical distance of the ideal discrete Gaussian, so the signing oracle is simulatable from the public key alone; any forger yields a short NTRU-lattice vector, breaking SIS [4][9].

**SPHINCS+.** The **tweakable hash function** abstraction yields a *tight* reduction: forging implies breaking a well-defined hash property (multi-target collision or decisional second-preimage resistance), with bounds computed directly from parameters — no forking-lemma looseness [6].

### 5.3 Deployment Evidence

Adoption is already underway:

1. **Hybrid TLS/SSH**: OpenSSH 9.0+ ships `sntrup761x25519-sha512`; IETF drafts standardize ML-DSA OIDs and hybrid X.509 certificates [8][10].
2. **Firmware signing**: SPHINCS+'s tiny public keys and statelessness suit offline root keys, where signing is rare and verification ubiquitous [6][7].
3. **Constrained protocols**: FALCON's sub-kilobyte signatures lead for DNSSEC and embedded PKI, pending hardened constant-time implementations [4][9].

## 6 Limitations

**Assumption concentration.** Two of three schemes rest on structured lattices; an algebraic (non-quantum) lattice breakthrough could damage Dilithium and FALCON together. Only SPHINCS+ is assumption-diverse — the strongest argument for its standardization despite its size [6][8].

**Implementation hazards are asymmetric.** Dilithium was *designed* for constant-time code: uniform sampling, no Gaussians, no floating point [2]. FALCON's double-precision FFT sampler has already produced FPU side-channel attacks; constant-time FALCON remains expert work, and FPU-less microcontrollers are effectively excluded [4]. SPHINCS+ is essentially a hash pipeline — the easiest to implement securely — though fault injection on its deterministic signing path deserves attention.

**Size and ecosystem friction.** Even the smallest PQC signatures dwarf ECDSA's 64 bytes: Dilithium-44's 2,420-byte signatures stress MTU-constrained protocols, X.509 chains, and blockchains. Protocol redesign — fragmentation handling, certificate compression — is an unavoidable migration cost.

**Proof gaps.** Dilithium's QROM proof [3] needs parameter inflation (*q = 1 mod 2n*) beyond the deployed sets, or settles for a ROM-only argument. FIPS 206 is still a draft, and FALCON's sampler side-channel resistance is active research [4][9]. SPHINCS+'s tight reduction is the gold standard, but still assumes ideal hash behavior against quantum adversaries [6].

**No agility yet.** Most deployed stacks hard-code one signature algorithm. The standards provide the primitives, but *crypto-agility* — negotiating algorithms, hybrid classical+PQC signatures, and algorithm identifiers in protocols — lags behind and is where real-world migration will succeed or fail [8][10].

## 7 Conclusion

NIST's three post-quantum signature standards are a **portfolio**, not competitors: CRYSTALS-Dilithium (ML-DSA, FIPS 204) [1][2] is the workhorse — fast, simple, constant-time-friendly, finalized; FALCON (FN-DSA, FIPS 206 draft) [4][5] is the compact specialist for bandwidth-starved protocols, bought with floating-point implementation complexity; SPHINCS+ (SLH-DSA, FIPS 205) [6][7] is the conservative anchor whose security asks nothing of number theory and everything of the hash function. The schemes differ less in raw security than in *what they trust and what they cost*.

Three recommendations follow. **First**, default new systems to ML-DSA-65, the balanced choice with the simplest secure implementation story [1]. **Second**, deploy FALCON-512 only where signature size is the binding constraint *and* where the implementation team can audit floating-point sampling code [4][9]. **Third**, protect long-lived trust anchors — root CAs, firmware signing keys — with SPHINCS+, whose assumption profile is the only hedge against a structural break in lattice cryptography [6][7]. Above all, build for **crypto-agility and hybrid signatures** now: the quantum timeline is uncertain, but the migration timeline is not — it starts today [8].
