##1 Introduction

Science is full of quantities we cannot directly measure. A physician observes blood pressure in treated and untreated patients; she wants the counterfactual quantity — how *this* patient's pressure would respond to *this* drug. An economist observes that education correlates with earnings; she wants the earnings that would result from *forcing* an additional year of schooling. These are interventional questions, and they are underdetermined by data alone. No table of observations, however large, can settle them without extra assumptions, because interventional distributions live in a strictly richer causal layer than observational ones [4].

Structural causal models supply the missing assumptions in a compact, transparent form: a directed acyclic graph (DAG) encoding which variables causally influence which others, plus functional equations describing the mechanisms. The *do*-operator, $do(X = x)$, formalizes an intervention as a surgical modification of the model — severing the natural mechanisms that determine $X$ and fixing it to $x$. The identification problem is then: given the graph and the observational distribution $P$, can the interventional quantity $P(y \mid do(x))$ be uniquely computed, and if so, by what formula?

For two decades this problem was attacked piecemeal, with one graphical criterion after another — the back-door criterion, the front-door criterion, the conditions of Galles and Pearl — each sufficient but none complete. A criterion is *sound* when it never certifies a wrong formula; it is *complete* when it certifies a formula for *every* identifiable quantity. Completeness was finally achieved in 2006, from two directions simultaneously: do-calculus itself was shown complete by two independent teams [2][3], and a recursive algorithm — the **ID algorithm** — was constructed whose failure modes produce *hedges*, graphical structures that witness non-identifiability [2]. This thesis unifies these developments into a single narrative, from the semantics of intervention to the algorithmic boundary of identifiability.

##2 Background

### Structural causal models

A **structural causal model** (SCM) $M$ is a tuple $\langle U, V, F, P(u) \rangle$, where $U$ is a set of exogenous (background) variables with distribution $P(u)$, $V$ is a set of endogenous (observed) variables, and $F$ is a set of functions, one per $V_i$, mapping the exogenous variables and the other endogenous variables into $V_i$:

$$V_i = f_i(pa_i, u_i), \quad i = 1, \ldots, n,$$

where $pa_i \subseteq V \setminus \{V_i\}$ are the *causal parents* of $V_i$. Each SCM induces a causal diagram $G$: a directed edge $X \to Y$ exists whenever $X$ appears as an argument of $f_Y$. We assume $G$ is acyclic. The joint distribution over $V$ factorizes along the graph:

$$P(v) = \prod_i P(v_i \mid pa_i), \tag{1}$$

the familiar Markov factorization. When the exogenous variables are mutually independent, the model is *Markovian*; when they may be arbitrarily correlated, it is *semi-Markovian*. Correlated exogenous variables that share a child pair are represented graphically by a **bidirected arc** $X \dashleftarrow\!\!\dashrightarrow Y$ between observed variables, encoding a hidden common cause [5].

### Interventions and the truncated factorization

An intervention $do(X = x)$ replaces the mechanism for $X$ with the constant $x$, leaving every other mechanism untouched. The post-intervention distribution is given by the **truncated factorization**:

$$P(v \mid do(x)) = \prod_{i: V_i \notin X} P(v_i \mid pa_i), \tag{2}$$

evaluated at $X = x$, with the factors $P(x_i \mid pa_i)$ for intervened variables simply removed. Marginalizing out the rest yields the causal effect $P(y \mid do(x))$, sometimes written $P_x(y)$. Equation (2) is definitional: it says what an intervention *is*, not how to compute it from data. When all variables are observed, computing $P_x(y)$ from (2) is straightforward; when the graph contains bidirected arcs — unmeasured confounding — the factors $P(v_i \mid pa_i)$ may be unidentifiable from the joint distribution, and (2) becomes a tantalizing formula with unknown pieces. This is where do-calculus enters [1][6].

### Graphical separation: d-separation

All three do-calculus rules are conditioned on graphical separation statements evaluated in *mutilated* graphs. Recall that $X$ and $Y$ are **d-separated** by $Z$ in $G$, written $(X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z)_G$, when every path between $X$ and $Y$ is blocked: either a non-collider on the path is in $Z$, or a collider on the path has no descendant in $Z$. The graph $G_{\overline{X}}$ deletes all arrows *entering* $X$; $G_{\underline{X}}$ deletes all arrows *emerging from* $X$. These mutilations correspond exactly to the graphical consequences of interventions, since $do(x)$ cuts the incoming arrows of $X$ and prevents $X$ from carrying observational information downstream [5][6][7].

| Mutilated graph | Edges deleted | Causal interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| $G_{\overline{X}}$ | Arrows entering $X$ | Post-intervention graph after $do(x)$ |
| $G_{\underline{X}}$ | Arrows leaving $X$ | $X$ observed but not transmitting influence |
| $G_{\overline{X}\,\underline{Z}}$ | Entering $X$, leaving $Z$ | $X$ intervened, $Z$ observed as evidence |

##3 Methodology

### The three rules of do-calculus

Pearl's do-calculus (Pearl, 1995) is a syntactic system for transforming expressions that contain $do$-operators into equivalent expressions [1][6]. Applied to a causal diagram $G$ with disjoint variable sets $X, Y, Z, W$:

> **Theorem 1 (Do-calculus [1]).** Let $G$ be the causal diagram of an SCM. The following rules hold for all disjoint sets $X, Y, Z, W$:

> - **Rule 1 — Insertion/deletion of observations:**
> $$P(y \mid do(x), z, w) = P(y \mid do(x), w) \quad \text{if } (Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\overline{X}}}.$$
> - **Rule 2 — Action/observation exchange:**
> $$P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), z, w) \quad \text{if } (Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\overline{X}\,\underline{Z}}}.$$
> - **Rule 3 — Insertion/deletion of actions:**
> $$P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), w) \quad \text{if } (Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z \mid X, W)_{G_{\overline{X}\,\overline{Z(W)}}}.$$

Here $Z(W)$ denotes the subset of $Z$ that are not ancestors of any node in $W$ in $G_{\overline{X}}$. Rule 1 says an observation $Z$ can be dropped when it is d-separated from the outcome in the post-intervention graph. Rule 2 allows swapping an intervention for an observation when $Z$ exerts no confounding influence on $Y$ once its outgoing arrows are cut. Rule 3 is the subtlest: an intervention on $Z$ can be erased entirely when $Z$ has no causal path to $Y$ other than through the intervened $X$ (the precise condition is the d-separation in $G_{\overline{X}\,\overline{Z(W)}}$). These rules, together with ordinary probability manipulations (marginalization, conditioning, Bayes), constitute a complete proof system for identification, as we shall see [2][3].

A quantity $P(y \mid do(x))$ is **identifiable** from $P$ in $G$ if it is uniquely determined by $P$ in every SCM compatible with $G$. The rules are *sound*: every reduction they license is valid in all such models. The remarkable fact is that they are also *complete*: if a quantity is identifiable, some sequence of the three rules reduces it to a $do$-free expression [2][3].

### Two classical corollaries: back-door and front-door

Before the general algorithm, consider how do-calculus recovers the two classical adjustment criteria. Suppose a set $Z$ satisfies the **back-door criterion** relative to $(X, Y)$: no node in $Z$ is a descendant of $X$, and $Z$ blocks every back-door path from $X$ to $Y$. Then

$$P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid x, z)\,P(z), \tag{3}$$

the familiar adjustment formula. The do-calculus derivation is instructive. Begin with $P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid do(x), z)\,P(z \mid do(x))$. Since $Z$ contains no descendants of $X$, Rule 3 gives $P(z \mid do(x)) = P(z)$. Since $Z$ blocks all back-door paths, $(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp X \mid Z)_{G_{\underline{X}}}$ holds, so Rule 2 converts $P(y \mid do(x), z)$ to $P(y \mid x, z)$. Equation (3) follows.

The **front-door criterion** handles a case the back-door criterion cannot: unmeasured confounding between $X$ and $Y$, with a mediator $M$ intercepting all directed paths from $X$ to $Y$, no unblocked back-door path from $X$ to $M$, and all back-door paths from $M$ to $Y$ blocked by $X$. Two chained applications of Rule 2 and Rule 3 yield

$$P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid x)\sum_{x'} P(y \mid x', m)\,P(x'), \tag{4}$$

a formula Pearl called "unwittingly" discovered by the calculus: no one would have guessed the effect is identified, yet the rules derive it mechanically [5]. Both criteria are special cases of the general theory — they are *sufficient* conditions, while do-calculus plus the ID algorithm gives conditions that are also *necessary*.

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 C-components and the Q-factorization

The ID algorithm's key insight is that identification decomposes along **c-components** (confounded components): maximal sets of nodes connected by bidirected paths, representing the reach of unmeasured confounding [2]. For any set $S \subseteq V$, define the **Q-factor**

$$Q[S](v) = P_{v \setminus s}(s),$$

the post-intervention distribution of $S$ under intervention on all remaining variables. Tian and Pearl showed that the observational distribution factorizes as a product of the Q-factors of the c-components of $G$:

$$P(v) = \prod_i Q[S_i](v), \tag{5}$$

where $S_1, \ldots, S_k$ are the c-components [2]. Each $Q[S_i]$ is itself identifiable from $P$ — a consequence of Rule 3, since intervening on $V \setminus S_i$ can be shown do-free. This decomposition is the engine of the algorithm: the hard problem $P_x(y)$ is broken into independent subproblems, one per c-component of the relevant ancestral subgraph, and each subproblem is either solved recursively or abandoned with a hedge witness.

```python
def ID(y, x, P, G):
    """Return an expression for P_x(y) in terms of P, or FAIL with a hedge.
    y: outcome assignment, x: intervention assignment, G: causal diagram."""
    V = topological_order(G)
    A = ancestors(y, G)                      # restrict to ancestors of Y
    if V != A:
        return ID(y, x & A, P(A), G[A])
    W = (V - x) - ancestors(y, G[V - x])
    if W:                                    # drop non-ancestors of Y
        return ID(y, x | W, P, G)
    C = c_components(G[V - x])               # decompose
    if len(C) > 1:                           # product of subproblems
        return sum_{V - (y | x)} prod_i ID(s_i, V - s_i, P, G)
    S = first(C)                             # single c-component case
    if S == C_of(G):                         # no confounding cut
        return sum_{S - y} prod_{V_i in S} P(v_i | v_{<i})
    if exists S' with C_of(G) < S' < S:       # hedge found
        return FAIL(F=G[S], F'=G[S'])
    S_prime = {v in S : v in ancestors(y, G)} # recurse on ancestors
    return ID(y, x & S_prime, prod_{V_i in S_prime} P(V_i | V_{<i}), G[S_prime])
```

Each line either reduces the problem or identifies a structural obstruction; exactly one branch fires at every recursive call, and termination is guaranteed because every recursion strictly shrinks the graph [2].

### 4.2 The ID algorithm as a sequence of do-calculus steps

A striking feature of ID is that every one of its recursive steps can be simulated by the three rules of do-calculus plus probability manipulations [2]. Line 1 (restricting to ancestors of $Y$) is Rule 3: interventions on non-ancestors of $Y$ cannot affect $Y$ and may be deleted. Line 2 is another Rule 3 application. Line 3's decomposition is licensed by the Q-factorization (5), itself a Rule 3 consequence. Line 4 handles the unconfounded case by direct factorization. Line 5 is the failure case. Line 6 recurses. Because ID is complete — it identifies exactly the identifiable quantities — and every ID step is a do-calculus derivation, do-calculus inherits completeness. This reduction, carried out in detail by Shpitser and Pearl, is the conceptual bridge between the syntactic calculus and the recursive algorithm: ID is do-calculus made mechanical and directed [2][6].

### 4.3 Hedges: the anatomy of non-identifiability

When ID fails, it does not merely report failure — it returns a **hedge**, a graphical certificate of non-identifiability [2]. Let $F, F'$ be $R$-rooted **C-forests** (subgraphs where every node has at most one child and $R$ is the root set), with $F' \subseteq F$, $F \cap X \neq \emptyset$, $F' \cap X = \emptyset$, and $R \subseteq An(Y)_{G_{\overline{X}}}$. Then $(F, F')$ forms a hedge for $P_x(y)$.

> **Theorem 2 (Hedge criterion [2]).** $P_x(y)$ is identifiable from $P$ in $G$ if and only if there is no hedge for $P_{x'}(y')$ in $G$, for any $X' \subseteq X$ and $Y' \subseteq Y$.

The canonical example is the **bow graph**: $X \to Y$ together with a bidirected arc $X \dashleftarrow\!\!\dashrightarrow Y$. The bow is the simplest hedge, and it witnesses the non-identifiability of the direct effect: two models with identical observational distributions — one in which $X$ strongly causes $Y$ with no confounding, another in which the association is pure confounding with no causal effect — can be constructed, so no formula can separate them. The proof of Theorem 2 generalizes this parity construction: given a hedge, build two binary models that agree on all of $P$ but disagree on $P_x(y)$, using the nested forests to propagate a bit-parity signal that intervention disrupts but observation cannot detect [2]. The **napkin graph** — a chain $W \to X \to Y$ with bidirected arcs $W \dashleftarrow\!\!\dashrightarrow Y$ and $X \dashleftarrow\!\!\dashrightarrow Y$ — provides another textbook hedge, showing that even seemingly identified structures collapse when confounding nests correctly.

### 4.4 From unconditional to conditional: the IDC extension

Real queries are often conditional: $P_x(y \mid z)$, the effect of $X$ on $Y$ within the subpopulation $Z = z$. Shpitser and Pearl extended ID to this **conditional identification** problem with the IDC algorithm [2]. The core idea is a reduction to the unconditional case via Rule 2 of do-calculus: whenever some $Z' \in Z$ satisfies $(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp Z' \mid X, Z \setminus \{Z'\})_{G_{\overline{X}\,\underline{Z'}}}$, the conditioning event can be absorbed into the intervention, $P_x(y \mid z', w) = P_{x,z'}(y \mid w)$, and recursion continues with the enlarged intervention set. When no such $Z'$ exists, IDC calls ID on the joint query $P_x(y, z)$ and renormalizes. The hedge criterion extends accordingly, and completeness of do-calculus carries over to conditional queries — the full interventional layer $P_x(y \mid z)$ is characterized [2][4].

##5 Empirical Results / Proofs

The completeness theorems are constructive, and their content is best appreciated through worked derivations that the algorithms perform automatically. Consider the graph $X \to M \to Y$ with a bidirected arc $X \dashleftarrow\!\!\dashrightarrow Y$ (unobserved confounding of treatment and outcome) and an observed covariate $Z$ with $Z \to X$ and $Z \to Y$ (blocking the $X \dashleftarrow\!\!\dashrightarrow Y$ confounding observationally). Feeding $P_x(y)$ and this graph to ID, the algorithm proceeds as follows: ancestors of $Y$ are all nodes; no non-ancestors can be dropped; the c-components of $G[V \setminus \{X\}]$ are $\{M\}$, $\{Y\}$, and $\{Z\}$-containing pieces that separate the problem into independent factors; recursion on each factor yields the closed form

$$P_x(y) = \sum_{z, m} P(y \mid x, m, z)\,P(m \mid x, z)\,P(z),$$

which is precisely the front-door-style estimand augmented by the back-door adjustment on $Z$ — derived without any human insight, purely by recursive decomposition. Every step is a licensed do-calculus move, so the expression is guaranteed correct in every compatible SCM [2].

> **Theorem 3 (Soundness and completeness of ID [2]).** ID always terminates. Whenever it returns an expression for $P_x(y)$, the expression is correct in every SCM inducing $G$ and $P$. Whenever $P_x(y)$ is identifiable from $P$ in $G$, ID returns such an expression; otherwise it returns a hedge witnessing non-identifiability.

> **Theorem 4 (Completeness of do-calculus [2][3]).** The three rules of do-calculus, together with standard probability manipulations, are complete for identifying all effects of the form $P_x(y)$ and $P_x(y \mid z)$: a quantity is identifiable if and only if it can be reduced to a $do$-free expression by the rules.

Huang and Valtorta's independent proof of Theorem 4 proceeds through a complete identifiability algorithm of their own design, showing that each of its steps likewise corresponds to do-calculus rule applications [3]. The two completeness results — one routed through ID and hedges, the other through a direct complete algorithm — agree, and both were recognized at UAI 2006, where the two papers shared the best student paper award [2][3]. The JMLR synthesis of Shpitser and Pearl then extended the characterization to the full causal hierarchy: observational, interventional, and counterfactual queries, each with its complete algorithm and its graphical non-identifiability witness [4].

##6 Limitations

Completeness is a statement *relative to the graph*. The theorems assume the causal diagram is correctly specified — that every arrow represents a genuine causal mechanism and every missing arrow a genuine absence. In practice, graphs are hypothesized, learned from data under strong assumptions, or elicited from domain experts; misspecification can invalidate every downstream conclusion, and the algorithms offer no protection against it. Identification is not estimation: even when $P_x(y)$ is identified, the estimand may involve high-dimensional sums or inverse-probability weights with catastrophic finite-sample behavior.

Furthermore, the theory characterizes identification from the *full* joint distribution $P(v)$. When only marginals are available, or when selection bias conditions the sample on a collider (the *s-recoverability* problem), the completeness results do not apply, and additional machinery is needed [4]. The do-calculus is also non-constructive as a search procedure: completeness guarantees a derivation *exists*, but blindly searching the space of rule applications is infeasible — which is precisely why the ID algorithm, with its polynomial-time recursion on c-components, is the practical vehicle [2][6]. Finally, parametric structure can identify what the nonparametric theory declares impossible: linearity, monotonicity, or instrumental-variable assumptions can break hedges, so non-identifiability in the graphical sense is non-identifiability *without further assumptions*, not an absolute barrier.

---

##7 Conclusion

The journey from observation to intervention is now a solved problem in the nonparametric setting. Structural causal models give interventions a precise semantics through the truncated factorization; do-calculus turns that semantics into a three-rule syntactic system; the ID algorithm mechanizes the system as a recursion on c-components; and hedges certify exactly where the boundary lies, with completeness theorems guaranteeing that nothing identifiable escapes and nothing non-identifiable is certified [1][2][3][4]. The practical upshot is a disciplined workflow: write down the causal graph, pose the interventional query, run the algorithm, and either receive an estimand with a correctness guarantee or a hedge explaining *why* the data cannot answer the question. That hedge is itself valuable — it tells the analyst exactly which unmeasured confounder to measure next. Causal identification is thus not only a body of theorems but a guide to experimental design: it maps the frontier between what observation can teach us and what only intervention can reveal.
