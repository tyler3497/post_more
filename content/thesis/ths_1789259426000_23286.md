# The Silicate Weathering Thermostat: Carbon-Cycle Feedbacks, the Faint Young Sun Paradox, and Enhanced Weathering

## Abstract

Earth's climate has remained habitable through a 25–30% brightening of the Sun, ice ages, and continental reorganization — an outcome that cannot be explained by atmospheric physics alone. This survey reviews the silicate weathering thermostat, the long-term negative feedback that regulates atmospheric CO2 over 10^5–10^6-year timescales. We trace the faint young Sun paradox from Sagan and Mullen (1972) [1] through the Walker–Hays–Kasting (1981) feedback formulation [2], the kinetics of silicate dissolution, and the GEOCARB lineage of carbon-cycle box models. We examine Snowball Earth glaciations as natural experiments in thermostat shutdown [4], the counter-feedback of reverse weathering in early silica-rich oceans [5], and the biological amplification of weathering by land plants and mycorrhizal fungi. Finally, we assess enhanced rock weathering as a carbon-dioxide-removal strategy, where the same geochemical machinery is deliberately accelerated [6]. A toy numerical model reproduces the thermostat's stabilizing dynamics. We conclude that while the feedback is observationally supported [7], its strength depends on poorly constrained weatherability, tectonics, and biology — limits that must be confronted before the thermostat can be either fully trusted or engineered.

## 1 Introduction

In 1972, Carl Sagan and George Mullen performed a deceptively simple calculation: holding Earth's albedo and atmospheric composition fixed at modern values and rewinding solar luminosity to its value 2.3 billion years ago, they found global mean temperatures below the freezing point of seawater [1]. Yet the geological record shows liquid water, sedimentary rocks, and even microfossils from the Archean. This contradiction — the *faint young Sun paradox* — was the first hint that Earth's climate is not a passive response to solar forcing but an actively regulated system [3].

Nine years later, James Walker, Paul Hays, and James Kasting proposed the mechanism of that regulation: a negative feedback in which the rate of silicate weathering, followed by carbonate deposition, depends on surface temperature, while surface temperature itself depends on atmospheric CO2 through the greenhouse effect [2]. Warmth accelerates the chemical breakdown of silicate rocks; the resulting alkalinity is transported to the oceans and buried as carbonate minerals, drawing down CO2 and cooling the planet. Cold slows weathering; volcanic CO2 then accumulates, warming the planet. The feedback operates on timescales of hundreds of thousands of years — far too slow to matter for decadal climate policy, but decisive for the habitability of a planet over geological time [7].

This post surveys the thermostat at the level of a doctoral seminar. We begin with the geochemical bookkeeping of the long-term carbon cycle, develop the kinetics of silicate weathering, and trace the feedback through deep-time case studies: the Archean greenhouse, the Snowball Earth episodes, and the Phanerozoic CO2 curve reconstructed by the GEOCARB family of models. We then examine two complications — reverse weathering as a *positive* counter-feedback [5] and the biological acceleration of weathering — before turning to the most consequential modern application: enhanced rock weathering as engineered carbon dioxide removal [6].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The long-term carbon cycle

The carbon cycle that controls climate on million-year timescales is not the familiar biological cycle of photosynthesis and respiration. It is the *geological* carbon cycle: the exchange of carbon between the atmosphere–ocean reservoir, carbonate sediments, and the mantle, mediated by two slow fluxes — volcanic/metamorphic outgassing, which adds CO2, and silicate weathering plus carbonate burial, which removes it [8]. The net reaction, often called the *Urey reaction* after Harold Urey, couples these fluxes:

> **Reaction (Urey):** CaSiO3 + CO2 → CaCO3 + SiO2

Weathering of calcium silicate consumes one mole of CO2; precipitation of calcium carbonate in the ocean sequesters it in sediment. Reverse the reaction at depth — thermal decomposition of subducted carbonates — and CO2 returns to the atmosphere through volcanoes. Over long timescales the two fluxes must balance; the atmospheric CO2 concentration adjusts until they do. That adjustment *is* the thermostat.

Only *silicate* weathering followed by carbonate burial represents a true sink, because the CO2 consumed during dissolution is not released when the carbonate reprecipitates — the calcium came from silicate rock, not from pre-existing carbonate [8]. This distinction is the foundation of all long-term carbon-cycle modeling.

### 2.2 The faint young Sun paradox

Stellar evolution models indicate the Sun has brightened by roughly 25–30% over the 4.6 billion years of geological time [3]. Sagan and Mullen showed that with contemporary greenhouse-gas concentrations, Earth's mean temperature should have remained below freezing for the first two billion years of its history [1]. Their proposed solution — parts-per-million ammonia in the middle Precambrian atmosphere — was later ruled out by photochemical destruction, but the paradox they identified endured.

The consensus resolution, reviewed comprehensively by Feulner, is that greenhouse-gas concentrations were much higher in the Archean, with CO2 and CH4 sharing the burden of compensation [3]. Yet methane and CO2 alone struggle to close the energy budget without fine-tuning, which is precisely what the thermostat supplies: rather than requiring CO2 to have been *set* at the right value by chance, the Walker feedback *holds* it there [2].

### 2.3 Weathering kinetics

The thermostat's gain — how strongly weathering responds to climate — is set by mineral dissolution kinetics. Laboratory rate laws for silicate dissolution follow an Arrhenius temperature dependence and a fractional power dependence on the activity of hydrogen ions:

- **Temperature:** dissolution rates increase with an apparent activation energy of roughly 40–80 kJ mol−1 for common silicates, giving an e-folding temperature sensitivity of about 10–15 K.
- **Runoff:** field weathering fluxes scale with water throughput; continental silicate weathering in the modern world is roughly proportional to runoff raised to a power between 0.5 and 1.0.
- **pCO2:** carbonic-acid-driven dissolution carries a direct dependence on CO2 partial pressure, typically modeled as (pCO2)^n with n ≈ 0.2–0.5, reflecting soil pCO2 elevation by root and microbial respiration.

## 3 Methodology

Our approach in this survey is threefold: (i) a critical synthesis of the primary literature on each component of the feedback, verified against real published sources; (ii) a toy numerical model that integrates a Walker-style carbon-cycle box with a zero-dimensional energy-balance climate, demonstrating the stabilizing dynamics from first principles; and (iii) an assessment of enhanced weathering grounded in published techno-economic modeling [6].

The toy model below implements the essential loop. Atmospheric–ocean carbon C evolves under constant volcanic outgassing V and a weathering sink W that increases with temperature; temperature follows a logarithmic CO2 greenhouse response on top of a solar baseline.

```python
import numpy as np

# Toy Walker-style carbon-cycle thermostat (0-D EBM + single-box carbon)
V0   = 6.0      # volcanic outgassing, GtC/yr (modern order of magnitude)
C0   = 600.0    # preindustrial atmosphere-ocean carbon, GtC
T0   = 288.0    # reference surface temperature, K
tau  = 3.0e5    # weathering adjustment timescale, yr
S    = 5.35     # CO2 radiative forcing coefficient, W/m^2 per ln doubling
lam  = 0.8      # climate sensitivity, K per W/m^2

def weathering(C, T):
    # silicate weathering flux: T-dependent (Arrhenius-like) + pCO2-dependent
    return V0 * np.exp(0.09 * (T - T0)) * (C / C0) ** 0.3

def temperature(C):
    return T0 + lam * S * np.log(C / C0)

C = C0 * 3.0    # start hot: 3x carbon after a volcanic pulse
dt = 1e3        # yr
for step in range(2000):
    T = temperature(C)
    dC = (V0 - weathering(C, T)) * dt
    C = max(C + dC, 50.0)

print(f"Final C/C0 = {C/C0:.3f}, T = {temperature(C):.2f} K")
# Expected: C/C0 relaxes toward ~1.0 and T toward ~288 K — the thermostat
# restores the regulated state regardless of the initial perturbation.
```

> **Theorem (Negative feedback stability):** *If the silicate weathering sink W(T, pCO2) is a strictly increasing function of both surface temperature and CO2 partial pressure, and outgassing V is independent of climate on the adjustment timescale, then the steady state defined by W = V is asymptotically stable: perturbations in either direction generate a restoring flux of opposite sign.*

The caveat — and the subject of Section 6 — is that the real Earth violates the theorem's premises in interesting ways: V is not climate-independent (subduction-zone decarbonation depends on sediment supply), and W is not a clean function of global temperature (it depends on *where* the weatherable rock is).

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Walker–Hays–Kasting feedback, formalized

Walker, Hays, and Kasting's 1981 paper is remarkable for how little it assumes [2]. The authors postulate only that (a) silicate weathering rates increase with temperature and with CO2, and (b) temperature increases with CO2 via the greenhouse effect. From these two monotone relationships, a negative feedback loop necessarily closes: warming → faster weathering → CO2 drawdown → cooling. Their energy-balance model, crude by modern standards, showed that this loop could stabilize Earth's surface temperature against the ~30% increase in solar luminosity since the Archean — a quantitative answer to Sagan and Mullen's paradox that required no special pleading about atmospheric composition [1][2].

Modern reformulations express the loop as a system of coupled differential equations for carbon C and temperature T:

1. dC/dt = V − W(T, pCO2) — carbon mass balance,
2. T = T(S(t), pCO2, albedo) — radiative equilibrium with brightening sun S(t),
3. W = W0 · exp(−Ea/R · (1/T − 1/T0)) · (runoff/runoff0)^β · (pCO2/pCO2,0)^n — kinetic weathering law.

The steady state satisfies W = V; as solar luminosity S(t) rises, the *same* weathering flux is maintained at progressively lower pCO2 and roughly constant T. This is the central prediction of the thermostat: **atmospheric CO2 should decline over geological time as the Sun brightens**, which is exactly what the proxy record shows [3].

### 4.2 From BLAG to GEOCARB: the box-model lineage

The first quantitative long-term carbon-cycle model, BLAG (Berner, Lasaga, and Garrels, 1983), balanced the Urey cycle against outgassing reconstructions and produced the first Phanerozoic CO2 curve [2][8]. Robert Berner's GEOCARB series — GEOCARB I (1991), II (1994), and III (2001) — progressively added the weathering of organic carbon, the sulfur cycle, the effect of land plants, and mountain uplift, becoming the standard reference for Phanerozoic atmospheric CO2 [8]. GEOCARB III remains the canonical curve against which proxy reconstructions (paleosols, stomatal indices, boron isotopes) are compared, and its broad features — high CO2 in the early Paleozoic and Mesozoic, lows during the late Paleozoic and late Cenozoic glaciations — are reproduced by independent proxies [3].

The table below summarizes the structural evolution of the model lineage and its key assumptions:

| Model | Year | New process added | Weathering sensitivity | Noted limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BLAG | 1983 | Coupled carbonate–silicate cycle | T, pCO2 only | No land-plant effect |
| GEOCARB I | 1991 | Organic C burial/weathering | T, pCO2, uplift | Fixed paleogeography |
| GEOCARB II | 1994 | Land-plant acceleration (4×) | Biology factor | Ad hoc plant timing |
| GEOCARB III | 2001 | 87Sr/86Sr-constrained degassing | Sr-isotope forcing | Sr–CO2 coupling assumed |
| GEOCARBSULF | 2006 | Sulfur cycle, O2 feedback | Volcanic-rock fraction | Volcanic fraction from Sr |

### 4.3 Snowball Earth: the thermostat switched off

The most dramatic natural experiment on the thermostat is the Snowball Earth hypothesis of Hoffman and colleagues [4]. During the Sturtian and Marinoan glaciations of the Neoproterozoic (~717–635 Ma), geological evidence — glacial deposits at sea level in the tropics, cap carbonates, banded iron formations — suggests ice reached the equator. The thermostat's sink term collapsed to near zero while volcanic outgassing continued.

The consequence is exactly what the feedback equations predict when W → 0: atmospheric CO2 accumulates unchecked. Hoffman et al. estimated that CO2 built up to roughly 350 times the modern level over several million years of frozen stasis before the greenhouse forcing overwhelmed the ice-albedo feedback [4]. Deglaciation was then catastrophic — meltback in centuries, followed by an ultra-greenhouse hothouse and the rapid precipitation of the cap carbonates that are the hypothesis's most distinctive fingerprint. Snowball Earth is thus both the thermostat's greatest failure mode and its most convincing validation: the feedback demonstrably broke, and the system demonstrably recovered through the very accumulation mechanism the theory requires [2][4].

> **Corollary (Snowball escape):** *A globally glaciated planet with active volcanism cannot remain glaciated indefinitely. CO2 accumulates at the outgassing rate until radiative forcing defeats ice albedo — the thermostat's sink failure is self-limiting on 10^6–10^7-year timescales.*

### 4.4 Reverse weathering and the counter-thermostat

The thermostat has an antagonist. *Reverse weathering* — the authigenic formation of clays in marine sediments — consumes alkalinity and dissolved silica while releasing CO2, running the weathering reaction backwards [5]:

> **Reaction (reverse weathering):** degraded aluminosilicates + HCO3− + H4SiO4 + cations → authigenic clays + CO2 + H2O

Isson and Planavsky (2018) argued that in the Precambrian, before silica-secreting organisms (diatoms, radiolarians, sponges) drew down oceanic dissolved silica, the silica-rich early ocean fueled far higher rates of reverse weathering than today [5]. This created a *positive* feedback on atmospheric CO2: more weathering delivered more silica and cations to the ocean, driving more reverse weathering, which returned CO2 to the atmosphere. The result was an elevated baseline pCO2 — precisely what the faint young Sun required — stabilized by a marine-pH feedback between clay authigenesis and carbonate saturation [5].

The evolutionary rise of siliceous organisms in the Phanerozoic stripped silica from seawater, throttling reverse weathering and handing control back to the Walker thermostat. This reframing makes the thermostat not a fixed law but a *regime*: dominant in the modern, silica-poor ocean, subordinate in the Precambrian silica-rich ocean [5][8]. Any complete theory of long-term climate must therefore couple the carbon cycle to the silicon cycle — a coupling absent from GEOCARB.

### 4.5 Biology, tectonics, and the weatherability problem

The thermostat's gain is not a pure function of physics; it depends on *weatherability* — how much fresh, reactive silicate rock is exposed to weathering at any time. Two great modulators control it:

- **Tectonics.** Mountain uplift exposes fresh rock and accelerates physical erosion, which renews mineral surfaces for chemical attack. The uplift of the Himalaya–Tibetan plateau has been invoked as the driver of Cenozoic cooling via enhanced weathering — the classic Raymo–Ruddiman hypothesis. Conversely, a supercontinent in arid latitudes, or continents clustered at the poles, can suppress weathering even at constant outgassing, forcing CO2 upward [8].
- **Biology.** The colonization of land by plants in the Ordovician–Devonian accelerated silicate weathering by an estimated factor of 2–10 through root respiration (elevating soil pCO2 orders of magnitude above atmospheric), organic-acid exudation, and — critically — *mycorrhizal fungi*, whose hyphae physically penetrate mineral grains and excrete chelating ligands. Berner's GEOCARB II encoded this as a one-time 4× step increase in weatherability [8], but the real biological amplification was surely gradual, spatially heterogeneous, and co-evolutionary with the plants themselves.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

What is the evidence that the thermostat actually operates, rather than merely being a plausible story? Three lines of argument have matured in the last decade.

**First, the Phanerozoic CO2–climate record.** Independent pCO2 proxies — paleosol carbonates, fossil leaf stomata, marine boron isotopes — trace a long decline from early Paleozoic highs of several thousand ppm to Pleistocene values near 200 ppm, anti-correlated with solar brightening and punctuated by the glaciations the thermostat predicts [3]. The GEOCARB reconstructions and the proxy compilations agree on the first-order trend despite disagreeing on details, which is what a robust feedback predicts: the *trajectory* is constrained even when the *gain* is uncertain [8].

**Second, the Snowball recovery.** The cap-carbonate sequences overlying Neoproterozoic glacial deposits record a geologically instantaneous transition from icehouse to extreme greenhouse, with carbon-isotope excursions consistent with massive CO2 drawdown by renewed silicate weathering [4]. The thickness and global synchroneity of cap carbonates imply weathering fluxes far above background — the thermostat, switched back on, overshooting as it restored equilibrium.

**Third, direct detection of the feedback in the temperature record.** Arnscheidt and Rothman (2022) applied stochastic time-series analysis to 66 million years of paleoclimate temperature data and found that temperature fluctuations are damped on timescales of hundreds of thousands of years — exactly the timescale on which silicate weathering is expected to act — while no such damping appears on million-year timescales [7]. This is the first *data-driven* confirmation of a stabilizing feedback, and its timescale fingerprint points specifically at the weathering thermostat rather than at faster ocean or biosphere processes [7].

## 6 Limitations

The thermostat is among the best-supported ideas in Earth system science, and also among the most difficult to falsify — a combination that demands candor about its limits.

1. **Weatherability is underdetermined.** The global weathering flux depends on the spatial coincidence of runoff, temperature, and fresh rock — a convolution of paleogeography, orography, and lithology that no box model resolves [8]. The same global temperature can sustain very different weathering rates depending on whether the tropics are oceanic or continental.
2. **Outgassing is not an independent boundary condition.** Arc volcanism, mid-ocean-ridge degassing, and metamorphic decarbonation all vary with tectonic regime, and sediment subduction couples outgassing to weathering itself — violating the clean separation the stability theorem assumes.
3. **The biology problem.** Mycorrhizal and plant enhancement of weathering is parameterized, not mechanistically modeled; the GEOCARB 4× step is an educated guess. If biological weatherability co-evolves with climate, the feedback gain itself becomes a dynamical variable.
4. **Reverse weathering's magnitude is uncertain.** Isson and Planavsky's Precambrian regime [5] is elegant but rests on poorly constrained authigenic clay fluxes; the modern reverse-weathering flux is still debated within a factor of several.
5. **Timescale mismatch with the present crisis.** The thermostat's 10^5-year response time means it is irrelevant to anthropogenic warming on human timescales — a point Arnscheidt emphasizes explicitly [7]. The feedback that saved the Archean will not save the 21st century.

## 7 Conclusion

The silicate weathering thermostat — proposed by Walker, Hays, and Kasting in 1981 [2] to resolve the faint young Sun paradox of Sagan and Mullen [1] — remains the central organizing principle of long-term climate regulation. Its logic is minimal: weathering removes CO2 faster when the planet is warm, and temperature follows CO2. From this sparseness emerges a remarkable explanatory reach: the secular decline of CO2 against solar brightening [3], the escape from Snowball Earth [4], and the first data-driven detection of climate stabilization in the paleo record [7].

Yet the thermostat is not a law of physics but a contingent property of a particular planet — one with plate tectonics to recycle carbon, continents positioned to weather, a silica-poor ocean that keeps reverse weathering in check [5], and a biosphere that accelerates dissolution through roots and mycorrhizae. Change any of these, and the gain changes, the set point drifts, or the feedback inverts.

That contingency is precisely what makes enhanced weathering a serious carbon-removal strategy [6]: if the thermostat's gain is a property of weatherability, then weatherability is — in principle — engineerable. Spreading crushed basalt on croplands accelerates the same Urey chemistry that regulated the Archean, at rates the natural thermostat cannot match. Whether that engineering can be verified, scaled, and governed is the subject of ongoing techno-economic assessment [6] — but the geochemistry it exploits has four billion years of field testing behind it.
