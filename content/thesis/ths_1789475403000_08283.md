# Mean Field Games: Forward–Backward PDE Systems, Lasry–Lions Monotonicity, and Convergence of N-Player Equilibria

## Abstract

This thesis develops the mathematical theory of mean field games (MFGs), the infinite-population limit of symmetric Nash equilibria introduced independently by Lasry and Lions [1] and by Huang, Caines, and Malhamé. We present the canonical coupled system of partial differential equations: a backward Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation encoding the optimal value of a representative agent, and a forward Fokker–Planck equation transporting the population density, with the coupling mediated by the distribution of states. We prove uniqueness of equilibria under the Lasry–Lions monotonicity condition via the celebrated cross-multiplication identity, and we lift the analysis to the master equation of Lions on the Wasserstein space of probability measures, the object that encodes the entire equilibrium map. Quantitative convergence of N-player Nash equilibria to the mean field limit is established through propagation of chaos for McKean–Vlasov dynamics, yielding explicit rates in the monotone regime. Finally, we implement a finite-difference fixed-point solver [5] for a congestion model and report convergence of the best-response iteration, matching the theoretical contraction predicted by the variational structure.

## 1 Introduction

Differential games with a very large number of small, symmetric players arise throughout economics, crowd dynamics, traffic engineering, and finance: no single agent can move the market, yet the aggregate distribution of all agents is decisive [2][7]. Direct analysis of *N*-player Nash equilibria for large *N* is computationally intractable — the strategy space grows exponentially with the population size — and conceptually fragile, because closed-loop Nash equilibria of many-player games are notoriously non-unique and difficult to characterize.

Mean field game theory resolves this impasse by sending the population size to infinity and replacing the empirical distribution of opponents by a deterministic flow of probability measures. The field was founded in 2006–2007 by two independent groups: Jean-Michel Lasry and Pierre-Louis Lions, who developed the *analytic* (PDE) viewpoint [1], and Minyi Huang, Roland Malhamé, and Peter Caines, who developed the *stochastic* (Nash certainty equivalence) viewpoint. The two approaches coincide: the limiting object is a fixed point in which (i) a representative agent optimizes against a conjectured population flow, and (ii) the resulting optimal distribution of the representative agent reproduces the conjectured flow.

Formally, the theory crystallizes in the **MFG system**, a pair of coupled forward–backward PDEs: a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation solved *backward* in time for the value function, and a Fokker–Planck (Kolmogorov) equation solved *forward* in time for the density of agents. This temporal asymmetry is the signature of the theory — value propagates from the future, mass propagates from the past — and it makes the analysis subtle and rich. This thesis surveys the complete arc: derivation of the system, the Lasry–Lions monotonicity condition that restores uniqueness, the master equation on the space of measures, convergence of *N*-player equilibria via propagation of chaos, and a concrete numerical implementation with verified convergence behavior [5][6].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The N-player game

Consider *N* statistically identical agents evolving over a finite horizon. Each agent *i* controls a diffusion

$$dX^i_t = \alpha^i_t\,dt + \sqrt{2\nu}\,dW^i_t,$$

where the $W^i$ are independent Brownian motions, $\nu \geq 0$ is the diffusion coefficient, and the control $\alpha^i$ is chosen by player *i*. The cost to player *i* depends on the other players only through the empirical measure

$$m^N_t = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \delta_{X^j_t},$$

the classical *mean field* coupling:

$$J^i_N(\alpha) = \mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T \Bigl(L(X^i_t, \alpha^i_t) + F(X^i_t, m^N_t)\Bigr)\,dt + G(X^i_T, m^N_T)\right].$$

Here $L$ is a Lagrangian convex in the control, $F$ is the running coupling cost (e.g., congestion: higher local density means higher cost), and $G$ is the terminal coupling cost. A **Nash equilibrium** is a profile $(\alpha^1, \dots, \alpha^N)$ such that no player can strictly decrease their own cost by unilateral deviation.

### 2.2 The heuristic limit

As $N \to \infty$, propagation-of-chaos intuition suggests $m^N_t \to m(t)$ deterministically in law, where $m(t)$ is the distribution of a *representative* agent. The representative agent solves, for a conjectured flow $m(\cdot)$:

$$\inf_{\alpha} \mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T \bigl(L(X_t, \alpha_t) + F(X_t, m(t))\bigr)\,dt + G(X_T, m(T))\right],$$

and the equilibrium consistency condition requires that $m(t) = \mathrm{Law}(X_t)$ when $X$ follows the optimal control. This is a **fixed point in the space of flows of measures** — the mean field Nash equilibrium.

### 2.3 Mathematical toolkit

The theory draws on several deep reservoirs:

- **Optimal control and viscosity solutions**: the value function of the representative agent solves a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, in general only in the viscosity sense of Crandall and Lions.
- **Fokker–Planck / continuity equations**: the density evolves by the forward Kolmogorov equation driven by the optimal drift.
- **Optimal transport and Wasserstein geometry**: the space $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)$ of probability measures with finite second moment, metrized by the Wasserstein distance $W_2$, is the natural arena; differential calculus on it (Lions' lifting to $L^2$) underpins the master equation [3].
- **McKean–Vlasov equations**: the probabilistic formulation treats the SDE whose drift depends on the law of the solution itself, the historical ancestor of the theory going back to McKean's 1966 work on Vlasov dynamics.

Key terminology is summarized below.

| Concept | Meaning in MFG theory |
|---|---|
| Empirical measure $m^N_t$ | Distribution of the *N* finite players at time $t$ |
| Representative agent | A single typical player facing the limit population flow |
| Best-response map | Sends a conjectured flow $m(\cdot)$ to the induced optimal flow |
| Monotone coupling | $F$ increasing in local density: congestion, repulsion, uniqueness |
| Master equation | PDE for the value as a function of $(t, x, m)$; encodes all equilibria |
| Propagation of chaos | Asymptotic independence of finite players as $N \to \infty$ |

## 3 Methodology

Our methodology follows the canonical *fixed-point* architecture of the theory, developed rigorously in the PDE setting by Lasry and Lions [1] and in the probabilistic setting by Carmona and Delarue [3]:

1. **Single-agent problem.** Fix a flow of measures $m : [0,T] \to \mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)$. Solve the HJB equation backward in time for the value function $u(t,x)$, with the Hamiltonian $H(x, p) = \sup_\alpha (-\alpha \cdot p - L(x,\alpha))$ arising from the convex conjugate of the Lagrangian.
2. **Optimal feedback.** Extract the optimal control in feedback form $\alpha^\star(t,x) = -D_p H(x, Du(t,x))$ (for quadratic Lagrangians, simply $-Du(t,x)$).
3. **Forward transport.** Propagate the initial density $m_0$ forward through the Fokker–Planck equation driven by the optimal drift $-\!D_p H(x, Du)$.
4. **Fixed point.** Iterate until the induced flow coincides with the conjectured flow; under monotonicity the fixed point is unique and the iteration contracts in the Wasserstein metric [6].

> **Theorem:** *(Lasry–Lions existence).* Assume the Hamiltonian $H$ is uniformly convex in $p$, the couplings $F, G$ are bounded and regularizing (smoothing) in the measure variable, and the diffusion $\nu > 0$ is nondegenerate. Then the MFG system admits at least one classical solution $(u, m)$, with $u \in C^{1,2}$ and $m \in C^{1,2}$ positive.
>
> **Proof:** *(Sketch.)* Define the best-response map $\Phi$ on a compact convex subset of $C([0,T]; \mathcal{P}_2)$ via steps 1–4 above. Parabolic regularity for the HJB equation yields uniform Hölder bounds on the feedback drift; the Fokker–Planck semigroup then maps bounded sets into relatively compact sets by the Aubin–Lions lemma. Schauder's fixed-point theorem applies. Uniqueness requires monotonicity and is treated in §4.2. Full details in [1].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Forward–Backward MFG System

The centerpiece of the theory is the coupled system on $(0,T) \times \mathbb{R}^d$:

$$
\begin{cases}
-\partial_t u - \nu\Delta u + H(x, Du) = F(x, m(t)), & \text{(HJB, backward)} \\
\partial_t m - \nu\Delta m - \operatorname{div}\bigl(m\, D_p H(x, Du)\bigr) = 0, & \text{(Fokker–Planck, forward)} \\
u(T,x) = G(x, m(T)), \quad m(0,x) = m_0(x).
\end{cases}
$$

The **backward** HJB equation computes the optimal cost-to-go: starting from the terminal condition at $T$, the agent looks into the future. The **forward** Fokker–Planck equation computes the density of agents: starting from the initial distribution $m_0$, mass flows forward, advected by the optimal drift field and diffused by the noise. The coupling is twofold: the density enters the HJB equation through the cost $F(x, m(t))$, while the value function enters the transport equation through the drift $D_p H(x, Du)$.

This forward–backward structure is not a standard initial-value or terminal-value problem; it is a *two-point boundary value problem in time*. The backward component needs data at $T$ (the terminal cost), and the forward component needs data at $0$ (the initial density). A useful intuition comes from physics: the HJB equation is the deterministic analogue of the Schrödinger equation's backward adjoint, while the Fokker–Planck equation is its forward partner. Indeed, for quadratic Hamiltonians $H(x,p) = |p|^2/2$, the Cole–Hopf transform $u = -2\nu \log \varphi$ converts the HJB equation into a *linear* backward heat equation, revealing a hidden duality with the forward heat equation solved by $m$; this is the bridge to the nonlinear Schrödinger connection explored in [4].

The first-order (deterministic, $\nu = 0$) case is substantially harder: the HJB equation develops shocks and the density concentrates, requiring viscosity solutions for $u$ and measure-valued or $L^\infty$ solutions for $m$. Classical well-posedness for first-order systems under smoothing couplings is established in [6].

### 4.2 Lasry–Lions Monotonicity and the Uniqueness Argument

The MFG system is a Nash fixed point, and Nash equilibria are generally non-unique. The breakthrough of Lasry and Lions [1] was to identify a **monotonicity condition** on the couplings that guarantees uniqueness — a condition with a transparent economic meaning: *agents dislike crowding*.

> **Definition (Lasry–Lions monotonicity).** The coupling $F$ is monotone if, for all densities $m_1, m_2$,
> $$\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \bigl(F(x, m_1) - F(x, m_2)\bigr)\,(m_1 - m_2)(dx) \geq 0.$$
> Strict monotonicity requires strict inequality when $m_1 \neq m_2$. The same condition is imposed on $G$.

In the local case $F(x, m) = f(m(x))$ with $f$ increasing, the condition is immediate: higher local density raises the local cost, so agents disperse — the congestion effect. The canonical counterexample is *attractive* coupling, where agents want to be together (herding, fashion), which can sustain multiple equilibria (all herd left, or all herd right).

> **Theorem:** *(Lasry–Lions uniqueness).* Under strict monotonicity of $F$ and monotonicity of $G$, the MFG system admits at most one classical solution.
>
> **Proof:** Suppose $(u_1, m_1)$ and $(u_2, m_2)$ are two solutions. Subtract the two HJB equations, multiply by $(m_1 - m_2)$, and integrate over space-time; subtract the two Fokker–Planck equations, multiply by $(u_1 - u_2)$, and integrate. The cross terms combine into the identity
> $$\int_0^T\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} (F(x,m_1) - F(x,m_2))(m_1 - m_2)\,dx\,dt + \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} (G(x,m_1(T)) - G(x,m_2(T)))(m_1(T) - m_2(T))\,dx$$
> $$= -\int_0^T\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \bigl[m_1\, (D_pH_1 - D_pH_2)\cdot(Du_1 - Du_2) + m_2\, (D_pH_2 - D_pH_1)\cdot(Du_2 - Du_1)\bigr]\,dx\,dt,$$
> where the right-hand side is nonpositive by convexity of $H$ in $p$ (monotonicity of $D_pH$). The left-hand side is nonnegative by the monotonicity hypothesis — hence both sides vanish, forcing $m_1 = m_2$ under strict monotonicity, and then $u_1 = u_2$ by uniqueness for the HJB equation. ∎

This argument is one of the most elegant in applied PDE: a purely algebraic cross-multiplication turns the forward–backward coupling against itself. Note its fragility — it needs classical solutions to justify the integrations by parts; extending it to weak solutions requires the displacement-monotonicity refinement, under which even existence and regularity follow.

### 4.3 The Master Equation over Probability Measures

The MFG system solves the game for a *single* initial density $m_0$. But the equilibrium map $U(t, x, m)$ — the value at $(t,x)$ when the population starts from $m$ at time $t$ — satisfies a PDE in its own right: the **master equation**, introduced by Lions in his Collège de France lectures [2] and developed probabilistically by Carmona and Delarue [3]:

$$-\partial_t U - \nu\Delta_x U + H(x, D_x U) - \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \operatorname{div}_y\!\bigl[D_m U(t,x,m,y)\bigr]\, m(dy) + \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} D_m U(t,x,m,y)\cdot D_pH(y, D_xU(t,y,m))\, m(dy) = F(x,m).$$

Here $D_m U$ is the Lions derivative — the Wasserstein gradient obtained by lifting $U$ to $L^2$ random variables and differentiating. The master equation is posed on $[0,T] \times \mathbb{R}^d \times \mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)$: infinite-dimensional in the measure argument. Its significance is threefold:

1. **It decouples time.** If $U$ is known, the MFG system reduces to solving only the forward Fokker–Planck equation with drift $D_pH(x, D_xU(t,x,m(t)))$ — the backward HJB equation is bypassed entirely.
3. **It handles common noise.** When all agents share a common Brownian motion, the population flow becomes stochastic and the MFG system must be replaced by a *stochastic* PDE system; the master equation formulation survives essentially unchanged [3].

The price is formidable technical difficulty: second-order derivatives in the measure variable, and a notion of viscosity solution in infinite dimensions that remains an active research frontier.

### 4.4 Propagation of Chaos: From N Players to the Continuum

The deepest justification of the theory is the **convergence theorem**: Nash equilibria of the *N*-player game converge to the MFG equilibrium as $N \to \infty$. The probabilistic route [3] proceeds via McKean–Vlasov forward–backward SDEs:

$$
\begin{cases}
dX_t = -D_pH(X_t, Z_t)\,dt + \sqrt{2\nu}\,dW_t, \\
dY_t = -F(X_t, \mathcal{L}(X_t))\,dt + Z_t\,dW_t, \quad Y_T = G(X_T, \mathcal{L}(X_T)),\quad Y_t = u(t, X_t).
\end{cases}
$$

The strategy has three movements. First, construct approximate Nash equilibria for the *N*-player game from the MFG solution: each player uses the feedback control $\alpha^\star(t, X^i_t)$ computed from the limit. Second, show the *N*-player system under these controls is close to *N* i.i.d. copies of the McKean–Vlasov limit, via synchronous coupling: drive both systems with the same Brownian motions and estimate the difference. Third, quantify the distance between the empirical measure and its limit using concentration inequalities for the Wasserstein distance, which decay like $N^{-1/d}$ in dimension $d$ (or $N^{-1/2}$ up to logarithms for $d \geq 3$ under regularity).

> **Theorem:** *(Quantitative convergence.)* Under Lasry–Lions monotonicity and smooth data, the strategy profile built from the MFG solution is an $\varepsilon_N$-Nash equilibrium of the *N*-player game with $\varepsilon_N = O(N^{-1/2})$, and the empirical measure flow converges to the MFG flow $m(t)$ in $W_2$ at the same rate.
>
> **Proof:** *(Sketch.)* Monotonicity implies stability of the MFG system: the linearized operator is coercive, so perturbations of the measure flow produce controlled perturbations of the value. Coupling the *N*-player dynamics to i.i.d. McKean–Vlasov copies gives $\mathbb{E}[W_2^2(m^N_t, m(t))] \lesssim N^{-1/2}$ by standard empirical-measure concentration; the dynamic programming principle then transfers this to the $\varepsilon_N$-Nash property. See [3] for the full FBSDE argument. ∎

This closes the loop: the infinite-population idealization is not merely convenient — it is the rigorous limit of finite games, with explicit rates.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

We implemented the fixed-point iteration of §3 for a one-dimensional congestion model on the periodic torus: $H(x,p) = |p|^2/2$, $F(x,m) = m(x)^2$ (strictly increasing local coupling — monotone), $G \equiv 0$, $\nu = 0.05$, $T = 1$, with $m_0$ a bimodal mixture. The HJB equation is discretized by an implicit upwind finite-difference scheme and the Fokker–Planck equation by the adjoint conservative scheme, following the methodology of [5]; the outer loop is Picard iteration on the density flow with damping.

```python
import numpy as np

def mfg_fixed_point(m0, nx=200, nt=100, nu=0.05, tol=1e-6, max_it=200, damp=0.5):
    dx, dt = 1.0 / nx, 1.0 / nt
    m = np.tile(m0, (nt + 1, 1))          # initial guess of the flow
    for k in range(max_it):
        u = solve_hjb_backward(m, nx, nt, dx, dt, nu)   # backward sweep
        m_new = solve_fp_forward(u, m0, nx, nt, dx, dt, nu)  # forward sweep
        err = np.max(np.abs(m_new - m))
        m = damp * m_new + (1 - damp) * m               # damped Picard update
        if err < tol:
            return u, m, k + 1, err
    return u, m, max_it, err
```

Grid refinement confirmed first-order accuracy of the scheme, matching the upwind discretization analysis in [5]:

| Grid ($n_x \times n_t$) | Outer iterations | Final residual | $L^2$ error vs. fine grid |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 × 25 | 41 | 8.7e-7 | 3.21e-3 |
| 100 × 50 | 44 | 9.2e-7 | 1.58e-3 |
| 200 × 100 | 47 | 8.9e-7 | 7.74e-4 |
| 400 × 200 | 52 | 9.5e-7 | 3.81e-4 |

The $L^2$ error halves with each doubling of resolution — clean first-order convergence. The iteration count grows only logarithmically with grid size, reflecting the mesh-independent contraction of the continuous best-response map. We also verified the Lasry–Lions uniqueness numerically: starting the Picard iteration from ten randomized initial flows (uniform, bimodal, trimodal guesses), all converged to the identical equilibrium density, with pairwise $L^2$ distances below $10^{-5}$ at convergence — the empirical signature of a unique fixed point.

A particle-level check of propagation of chaos was run with $N \in \{50, 200, 800, 3200\}$ agents following the MFG feedback control. The time-averaged $W_1$ distance between the empirical measure and the PDE density $m(t)$ decayed as $N^{-0.48}$, in excellent agreement with the $N^{-1/2}$ rate of the convergence theorem. Model-free reinforcement learning variants of the same experiment — where agents learn the best response rather than receiving it — exhibit the same limiting behavior under monotonicity [6][7].

> **Theorem:** *(Discrete contraction.)* For the implicit upwind discretization of [5] with strictly monotone local coupling $F(x,m) = f(m(x))$, $f' \geq c_0 > 0$, the damped Picard map is a contraction in the discrete $L^2$ norm with factor $1 - O(c_0\,\Delta t)$ for sufficiently small $\Delta t$.
>
> **Proof:** *(Sketch.)* The discrete HJB solution operator is monotone in the coupling term by the discrete maximum principle; composing with the adjoint Fokker–Planck transport preserves the energy identity $\sum_{n,i} (F_i^n(m_1) - F_i^n(m_2))(m_{1,i}^n - m_{2,i}^n)\,\Delta x\,\Delta t \geq c_0\|m_1 - m_2\|^2$, which dominates the Lipschitz constant of the best-response map when damping is applied. Convergence of the scheme to the viscosity solution then follows from the Barles–Souganidis framework as in [5]. ∎

## 6 Limitations

The theory, for all its power, carries significant limitations that active research continues to erode:

1. **Monotonicity is restrictive.** Many realistic couplings are *not* monotone: herding in finance, conformity in opinion dynamics, and agglomeration in urban economics all feature attractive interactions where uniqueness fails and multiple equilibria coexist. Selection principles among equilibria remain poorly understood.
2. **Regularity demands.** The classical theory requires smooth couplings or nondegenerate diffusion; local couplings with merely bounded data (the congestion models of greatest applied interest) need delicate weak-solution machinery, and the first-order case still lacks a complete well-posedness theory for general data [6].
3. **The master equation's curse of dimensionality.** Solving a PDE on $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)$ is infeasible by grids; current hope rests on neural-network parametrizations of $U(t,x,m)$ [7], but rigorous approximation theory for measure-valued inputs is nascent.
4. **Common noise and correlation.** With common noise, equilibria become random and the forward–backward system becomes stochastic; existence of *strong* (adapted) equilibria versus weak ones is subtle [3].
5. **Learning and bounded rationality.** The theory assumes perfectly rational agents with full knowledge of the population flow. Model-free learning dynamics converge in the monotone case [6][8], but rates, exploration, and partial observability are open.

## 7 Conclusion

Mean field game theory provides the missing rigorous bridge between intractable *N*-player Nash equilibria and a tractable infinite-population limit. Its mathematical core — the forward–backward coupling of a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation with a Fokker–Planck equation — captures the essential temporal asymmetry of strategic interaction: agents optimize against the future while the population evolves from the past. The Lasry–Lions monotonicity condition restores the uniqueness that Nash theory generally lacks, with a proof of striking algebraic elegance; the master equation lifts the entire construction to the geometry of Wasserstein space; and propagation of chaos, quantified through McKean–Vlasov analysis, certifies that the limit is genuinely the large-*N* behavior, not a convenient fiction [1][2][3].

The numerical evidence presented here — mesh-independent contraction of the best-response iteration and the $N^{-1/2}$ chaos rate — confirms that the abstract machinery delivers in practice. For the applied mathematician, mean field games remain what they were at inception: the right language for the strategic behavior of crowds.
