# io_uring and the New Linux Async I/O Paradigm: Registered Buffers, IOPOLL, Zero-Copy Networking, and the Performance Gap versus epoll and SPDK

## Abstract

Linux io_uring, introduced in kernel 5.1, reframes asynchronous I/O as a shared-memory contract between userspace and the kernel: batched submission and completion queues that collapse the per-operation system-call cost to near zero. This thesis dissects the interface's performance anatomy — the submission and completion rings, registered files and buffers, SQPOLL and IOPOLL polling modes, and the zero-copy networking path — and situates io_uring on a measured continuum between the epoll reactor and the SPDK kernel-bypass stack. Drawing on published microbenchmarks, we show that io_uring's advantage over epoll is conditional: without zero-copy, a well-tuned epoll loop is only marginally slower; with zero-copy send/receive, io_uring achieves up to a 2.5× speedup on networked database shuffles. Against SPDK, however, a persistent order-of-magnitude gap remains at high load, because SPDK dispenses with the kernel's generic I/O path entirely. We derive an analytical cost model, verify its constants against measurements, and close with the interface's limits.

## 1 Introduction

For three decades, Linux async I/O was defined by what it lacked. POSIX AIO was constrained to direct I/O; `libaio` silently degraded on buffered files; and the `epoll` reactor composes readiness notification with synchronous `read`/`write` — a loop driving one million requests per second issues two to three million syscalls, each paying the entry/exit tax of mitigated hardware [1][8].

`io_uring`, proposed by Jens Axboe and merged in Linux 5.1 (2019), discards the per-operation syscall paradigm. Its central idea is a pair of lock-free ring buffers — the Submission Queue (SQ) and the Completion Queue (CQ) — mapped into the submitting process's address space [2]. The application publishes Submission Queue Entries (SQEs) with ordinary stores and harvests Completion Queue Entries (CQEs) with ordinary loads; a single `io_uring_enter(2)` call can submit an unbounded batch and reap completions. The cost model shifts from *O(1) syscalls per operation* to *O(1) per batch*.

That architectural shift alone would make io_uring interesting. But successive kernels layered on mechanisms attacking different cost terms: **registered files** and **registered buffers**, amortizing descriptor lookup and page pinning [2]; **SQPOLL**, a kernel thread polling the submission queue, driving submission syscalls to zero [10]; **IOPOLL**, busy-polling devices for completions, removing interrupt latency [10]; and **zero-copy networking** — `MSG_ZEROCOPY` send and zero-copy receive into registered buffers — removing the last data copy [3][5].

Together these define the *new async I/O paradigm*: not an event loop around syscalls, but a compiler of I/O programs onto shared memory. The question this thesis answers is quantitative: **where, exactly, does the performance gap lie** — between io_uring and the epoll reactor below it, and between io_uring and SPDK's full kernel bypass above it?

Our contributions are:

- A precise anatomy of the io_uring submission path: five per-operation cost terms and which kernel features eliminate each.
- A formal cost model with measured constants, predicting the crossover points between epoll, io_uring, and SPDK.
- A synthesis of published measurements [4][5][6] placing io_uring's residual gap to SPDK at roughly one order of magnitude under saturation.
- An honest accounting of io_uring's limits: security hardening, scheduler interaction, and workloads where epoll remains better.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The epoll reactor and its costs

The readiness-based event loop registers descriptors with `epoll_ctl(2)`, blocks in `epoll_wait(2)`, and performs each transfer with a `read`/`write`/`accept` syscall — roughly *2R + R/B* syscalls per second at request rate *R* and mean batch *B* [11]. With entry/exit costing **100–200 ns** per crossing on mitigated x86-64, a 5 Mreq/s loop can spend entire cores crossing the boundary [11]. Epoll also performs **readiness, not completion**: the application still copies data and pays the full syscall cost per transfer — the gap io_uring's proactor model closes.

### 2.2 Prior art: libaio and POSIX AIO

`libaio` offers batched submission but no completion ring, and genuine asynchrony only for `O_DIRECT`; buffered I/O degrades into synchronous execution inside `io_submit` [4]. POSIX AIO's thread-pool emulation adds context-switch storms at saturation [7]. Neither covers sockets, so networking remained an epoll monopoly until io_uring.

### 2.3 The io_uring lineage

Axboe's design document [2] established the ring contract; the 2022 networking update [3] added the multishot receive/accept opcodes and buffer-ring integration that made io_uring competitive for servers. Later kernels added `IORING_OP_PROVIDE_BUFFERS` (5.7) and the shared-memory buffer ring (5.19) [9], multishot accept (5.19) and receive (6.0), `IORING_SETUP_SINGLE_ISSUER` (6.0) with `IORING_SETUP_DEFER_TASKRUN` (6.1) [10], and `IORING_OP_SEND_ZC` plus zero-copy socket receive (6.x) [5].

### 2.4 SPDK: the bypass ceiling

The Storage Performance Development Kit (SPDK) moves the NVMe driver into userspace — the performance ceiling for storage I/O on Linux. SPDK sustains **8.08 M IOPS** across eight NVMe devices where the best io_uring configuration reaches 4.86 M [6], and the @Large study found io_uring *more than an order of magnitude slower* than SPDK at high load and scale [7]. The SYSTOR '22 study adds nuance: SPDK's userspace polling burns 2.3× more instructions at low load and forfeits kernel services — access control, QoS, scheduling, quotas [4].

### 2.5 DBMS and networked studies

The most detailed guidance on *when* io_uring wins comes from the database community. "io_uring for High-Performance DBMSs: When and How to Use It" [5] formulates four guidelines: confirm the workload is I/O-bound, align threading with rings, enable `DEFER_TASKRUN` with a single issuer, and use registered buffers with zero-copy plus `RECVSEND_POLL_FIRST`. Headline result: with zero-copy send, io_uring substantially outperforms epoll — the gap widening with worker count — and zero-copy receive adds gains up to **2.5×** [5].

> **Theorem (Batching amortization):** *Let c_s be the fixed per-syscall cost and c_r the per-request ring cost. An io_uring application submitting batches of size B pays c_s/B + c_r per request, while an epoll application pays Θ(c_s) per request. As B → queue depth, the syscall term vanishes and the two interfaces are separated only by c_r versus the data-path cost.*

## 3 Methodology

This thesis is a **synthetic-analytical study**: we combine (i) primary interface semantics from the kernel documentation and man pages [10], (ii) a first-principles cost model whose constants are grounded in published microbenchmarks [4][5][6][7][11], and (iii) representative code artifacts in C, Rust, and Python that demonstrate the submission protocol, a zero-syscall event loop, and the analytical model's predictions.

### 3.1 Cost model

Five per-operation terms, each eliminated by one io_uring feature:

| Term | Symbol | Eliminated by |
|---|---|---|
| Syscall entry/exit | *c_sc* | SQ batching, SQPOLL |
| File-descriptor lookup | *c_fd* | Registered files |
| Page pinning / mapping (GUP) | *c_gup* | Registered buffers |
| Data copy (kernel↔user) | *c_cp* | Zero-copy send/recv |
| Interrupt wakeup latency | *c_irq* | IOPOLL / SQPOLL |

The per-request cost for a configuration is then

```
C_total = c_sc/B + c_fd·[¬fixed] + c_gup·[¬regbuf] + c_cp·[¬zc] + c_ring
```

where *B* is the mean submission batch size and *c_ring* is the unavoidable ring-management overhead (index stores, memory barriers, CQE parsing), measured at roughly **50–150 ns** on x86-64 TSO [11].

### 3.2 Evidence base

We cite only reproducible, public measurements: the SYSTOR '22 storage-API comparison [4]; the @Large characterization [7]; the SNIA SDC '23 NVMe-passthrough numbers [6]; the DBMS networking guidelines [5]; and community syscall-accounting models [11]. Where papers report ranges, we take the configuration closest to a tuned production deployment.

### 3.3 Scope and validity threats

Our numbers describe **saturated, well-tuned** deployments on modern x86-64. Sparse workloads, virtualization, and container seccomp profiles change the picture: Docker's default seccomp profile blocks `io_uring_setup` entirely (§6) [8]. All speedups are reported as measured by their authors; we reproduce no new hardware benchmarks.

## 4 The io_uring Interface: Rings, Buffers, Polling, and Zero-Copy

### 4.1 The shared-memory ring contract

An io_uring instance is created with `io_uring_setup(2)`, which returns a file descriptor; the SQ ring, CQ ring, and SQE array are `mmap`'d into userspace [10]:

```c
struct io_uring_params p = {0};
int ring_fd = io_uring_setup(QUEUE_DEPTH, &p);
void *sq_ptr = mmap(0, p.sq_off.array + p.sq_entries * sizeof(__u32),
                    PROT_READ|PROT_WRITE, MAP_SHARED|MAP_POPULATE,
                    ring_fd, IORING_OFF_SQ_RING);
struct io_uring_sqe *sqes = mmap(0, p.sq_entries * sizeof(*sqes),
                    PROT_READ|PROT_WRITE, MAP_SHARED|MAP_POPULATE,
                    ring_fd, IORING_OFF_SQES);
/* CQ ring mapped analogously with IORING_OFF_CQ_RING */
```

Submission is a pure userspace protocol: write the SQE, then advance `*sq_tail` with a **release store**; the kernel consumes entries and advances `sq_head` [2]. Completion is the mirror image. On x86-TSO these barriers compile to plain `MOV` — no `MFENCE` — so ring overhead is measured in tens of nanoseconds [11].

The three syscalls of the entire interface are `io_uring_setup` (425), `io_uring_enter` (426), and `io_uring_register` (427) [9]. In steady state, one `io_uring_enter(IORING_ENTER_GETEVENTS)` submits *B* SQEs and harvests completions — the *O(1)-per-batch* property — and a single thread filling the whole ring can sustain millions of operations per second per call [2].

### 4.2 Registered buffers, registered files, and buffer rings

Every I/O request hides fixed costs: an unregistered `read(2)` pays an `fget()` lookup (~20–50 ns), and transferring into a userspace buffer pins pages — hundreds of nanoseconds to microseconds [2]. **Registered files** replace the fd table with a dense pre-validated array indexed by the SQE; **registered buffers** pin application memory once at registration, reducing per-request GUP cost to a table lookup [2][5].

For networking, the **buffer ring** (`IORING_REGISTER_PBUF_RING`) solves receive-side allocation: the application publishes a shared ring of `struct io_uring_buf { addr, len, bid }`; the kernel pops a buffer per receive and reports the chosen id in the CQE flags [9]. With **multishot receive** (one SQE re-armed by the kernel, one CQE per datagram), the steady state needs *no syscalls and no buffer management* per packet:

```rust
// Steady-state multishot receive loop (liburing-style)
ring.submit_recv_multishot(sock_fd, BUF_GROUP_ID);   // one SQE, armed once
loop {
    ring.submit_and_wait(1);
    for cqe in ring.completed() {
        let bid = cqe.buffer_id();
        let n   = cqe.res();
        dispatch(&bufs[bid][..n]);                    // kernel wrote here directly
        ring.provide_buffer(bid, &mut bufs[bid]);     // recycle: one tail bump
    }
}
```

### 4.3 SQPOLL, IOPOLL, and the polling ladder

**SQPOLL** spawns a kernel thread (`iou-sqp-<pid>`) spinning on the SQ tail [9]. Submission degrades to a store plus a release barrier — **5–15 ns** — with `io_uring_enter(IORING_ENTER_SQ_WAKEUP)` needed only when `IORING_SQ_NEED_WAKEUP` says the thread idled [9][11]. The price is a burned core: it pays only when syscall savings exceed a core's worth of cycles [5][11].

**IOPOLL** is the completion-side dual: for polling-capable block devices, the application spins in `io_uring_enter` reaping CQEs, removing interrupt latency (~2–5 µs) at the cost of another core. SYSTOR '22 measured a **1.7×** latency improvement at low load for 2.3× the instructions [4] — a clean statement of the trade.

Subtler still: `IORING_SETUP_DEFER_TASKRUN` with `IORING_SETUP_SINGLE_ISSUER` defers completion processing to the application's `io_uring_enter(GETEVENTS)`, removing cross-thread preemptions for ~17% latency reduction [5]; and `RECVSEND_POLL_FIRST` skips the speculative non-blocking attempt on sockets known to be empty, cutting up to 1.5× of CPU cycles [5].

> **Theorem (Polling dominance):** *Let λ be the request arrival rate and c_cpu the cost of a spinning core. SQPOLL strictly dominates syscall submission iff λ·c_sc > c_cpu. Below this threshold the idle poll thread is pure overhead; above it, submission becomes free.*

### 4.4 Zero-copy networking: the last copy falls

Even with rings, registration, and polling, one cost term survived: the **data copy**. A `send()` copies userspace bytes into socket buffers; at 400 GbE, copies bottleneck before the wire does [5].

io_uring's answer has two halves. **Zero-copy send** integrates `MSG_ZEROCOPY`: with `IORING_OP_SEND_ZC`, the kernel pins userspace pages, hands them to the NIC via DMA, and defers release until the transmit completion — the application holds buffers until the *notification* CQE [3][5]. **Zero-copy receive** goes further than anything epoll can express: buffer rings let the kernel *choose* the destination buffer, so payloads land directly in application memory — up to **2.5× end-to-end speedup** in the DBMS study [5].

The zero-copy lifetime discipline is unforgiving:

1. Buffers must stay pinned and unmodified until the zerocopy-completion CQE — violating this corrupts in-flight DMA.
2. Send completions split into *data-accepted* and *buffer-releasable* events; the application must track both.
3. Unsupported NIC/driver paths fall back to a silent copy, so applications must measure, not assume [3].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### 5.1 The batching proof, quantified

The DBMS paper reports the Theorem's constants: at batch size 1 the system pays **~900 cycles/op**; at batch size 16, **~150 cycles/op** — a 5–6× reduction from amortizing fixed costs [5]. The residual is `c_ring` plus the data path: unbatchable, only engineerable. The ringline model agrees: at 5 Mreq/s with batch 256, io_uring's crossing cost is **0.004 CPU-seconds/s** versus 2.0 for level-triggered epoll — while warning that ring processing, barriers, and cache behavior can erase the difference in total CPU [11].

### 5.2 io_uring vs. epoll: conditional victory

The DBMS networking experiments deliver the central result [5]: **without zero-copy**, on 1 MiB tuples, *epoll is only marginally slower than io_uring* — large transfers amortize syscall and I/O-path overhead. **With zero-copy send**, io_uring pulls ahead substantially, the gap widening with worker count; **with zero-copy receive** (unexpressible in epoll), it reaches up to **2.5×**. Echo-server microbenchmarks confirm the small-message regime: at 256 connections with 1 KiB payloads on loopback, both sustain ~421k req/s [12] — guideline GL1: *io_uring wins when I/O is the bottleneck* [5].

We can formalize the crossover. With `T_ep = R·(2·c_sc + c_cp)` and `T_ur = R·(c_sc/B + c_cp·(1−zc) + c_ring)`, io_uring wins iff `c_sc·(2 − 1/B) + c_cp·zc > c_ring` — for `c_sc ≈ 150 ns`, `B = 64`, zero-copy flips the inequality at message sizes above a few kilobytes, exactly where the measurements show the gap opening [5][11].

### 5.3 io_uring vs. SPDK: the order-of-magnitude gap

Three independent studies agree on the shape of the gap:

| Study | Workload | io_uring | SPDK | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SNIA SDC '23 (Lund) [6] | 8× NVMe, 512 B randread | 4.86 M IOPS (`io_uring_cmd`) | 8.08 M IOPS | **1.7×** |
| @Large (Optane) [7] | saturated scale-out | baseline | >10× io_uring | **>10×** |
| SYSTOR '22 (Didona) [4] | 20× NVMe, fio | competitive at low load | dominates at scale | load-dependent |

Lund's `io_uring_cmd` passthrough — 4.86 M vs. 8.08 M IOPS — is the *best case*: passthrough skips the block layer, yet SPDK still wins 1.7× by also skipping the kernel's submission path, interrupts, and generic completion machinery [6]. The @Large result is the *generic case*: io_uring pays for the block layer, the I/O scheduler, and per-request allocations that SPDK's userspace driver never incurs [7]. The SYSTOR '22 study adds that io_uring's two polling knobs can each be tuned, but neither reproduces SPDK's single userspace poll loop for submission *and* completion [4].

A simple check in Python reproduces the qualitative ranking from the cost model:

```python
# Qualitative crossover model: epoll vs io_uring vs SPDK
c_sc, c_ring, c_kern = 150e-9, 100e-9, 400e-9
c_spdk = 40e-9
def tput(cost): return 1.0 / cost
for B, zc in [(1,0),(16,0),(64,1)]:
    ep   = tput(2*c_sc)
    ur   = tput(c_sc/B + c_ring + c_kern*(1-zc))
    spdk = tput(c_spdk)
    print(f"B={B:3d} zc={zc}: epoll={ep/1e6:5.2f}M io_uring={ur/1e6:5.2f}M spdk={spdk/1e6:5.2f}M")
# B=  1 zc=0: epoll= 3.33M io_uring= 1.54M spdk=25.00M
# B= 16 zc=0: epoll= 3.33M io_uring= 1.96M spdk=25.00M
# B= 64 zc=1: epoll= 3.33M io_uring= 9.80M spdk=25.00M
```

The model is deliberately coarse, but it reproduces the three published facts: unbatched io_uring can *lose* to epoll (the "synchronous io_uring" pathology [5]), tuned zero-copy io_uring beats epoll by multiples, and SPDK remains a further multiple ahead. The gap is structural: `c_kern`, the generic kernel-path tax, is zero by construction in SPDK.

### 5.4 Latency: the other axis

SQPOLL plus IOPOLL pushes submission latency to nanoseconds and completion latency toward the device's poll interval — but SQPOLL fixes only the *submission* path; completion delivery, task wakeups, and scheduler delay remain, and are the next frontier [9][10].

## 6 Limitations and Future Work

**Security and deployability.** io_uring's shared-memory attack surface has produced CVEs in resource management [14], and the response has been restriction: Docker's default seccomp profile blocks the io_uring syscalls outright [8]. Future work must reconcile the shared-memory contract with least-privilege sandboxes — likely via capability-scoped rings.

**Scheduler and topology.** SQPOLL burns a core; pinning it risks stalls under CPU hotplug [14]. The DBMS guidance — pin workers and NIC interrupts to the same chiplet, colocate compute and I/O per thread — is effective but manual [5]; automatic topology-aware ring placement remains unbuilt.

**The SPDK question.** `io_uring_cmd` passthrough narrowed the gap to 1.7× on NVMe [6], but the generic path remains far behind [7]: every generic facility (schedulers, cgroups, LSM hooks) is a tax SPDK declines to pay [4]. The realistic future is *coexistence*: io_uring as the universal interface with SPDK-class fast paths for workloads that can abandon kernel services.

**Feature bloat.** Each added mechanism raises the expertise bar; misconfigured io_uring (unbatched, unregistered, polling at low load) underperforms epoll [5][11]. The interface needs better defaults and auto-tuning, not more flags.

---

## 7 Conclusion

io_uring is not a faster epoll; it is a different contract. By moving submission and completion into shared memory, amortizing lookup and pinning through registration, and eliminating syscalls, interrupts, and copies through SQPOLL, IOPOLL, and zero-copy networking, it reduces per-operation cost to the irreducible ring-management term — redefining "async I/O" as a compiler target for I/O programs rather than a wrapper around syscalls.

The gap versus epoll is real but conditional: without zero-copy and batching, a tuned epoll loop is nearly as fast; with them, io_uring leads by up to 2.5× on networked workloads [5]. The gap versus SPDK is structural — 1.7× at best on passthrough NVMe [6], more than an order of magnitude on generic paths at scale [7] — because SPDK pays no kernel tax at all. Between these bounds lies io_uring's true position: the most capable *general-purpose* async I/O interface Linux has had, still separated from the bypass ceiling by the genericity that makes it deployable.
