# HotStuff BFT Consensus: Chained Three-Phase Commit, Linear Communication via Threshold Signatures, and Responsive View Changes

## Abstract

HotStuff is a leader-based Byzantine fault-tolerant (BFT) state-machine-replication protocol for the partially synchronous model that, for the first time, simultaneously achieves *linear* communication complexity and *responsiveness*: after the network stabilizes, a correct leader drives consensus at the pace of actual network delay δ rather than a pessimistic bound Δ. This thesis reconstructs the HotStuff family from first principles. We formalize its propose–vote framework, show how (t, n)-threshold signatures collapse O(n²) all-to-all broadcasts into O(n) leader-aggregated quorum certificates (QCs), and derive the three-chain commit rule enabling chained (pipelined) HotStuff. We sketch the safety and liveness proofs, compare HotStuff with PBFT and Tendermint on message complexity and view-change cost, and analyze deployments including DiemBFT v4, Flow, Fast-HotStuff, and HotStuff-2. We close with limitations: pairing-based signature overhead, leader-rotation fairness costs, and pacemaker timeout calibration.

## 1. Introduction

Byzantine fault tolerance (BFT) is the ability of a distributed system to operate correctly despite arbitrary — *Byzantine* — behavior of a subset of participants, who may crash, delay, drop, forge, or equivocate messages in coordinated adversarial fashion [6]. In *state machine replication* (SMR), n deterministic replicas execute client commands in a total order so the system behaves as one correct service while up to f replicas fail arbitrarily, with the classical bound n ≥ 3f + 1 under partial synchrony.

For two decades after Castro and Liskov's PBFT [6], BFT SMR suffered a tension between robustness and cost. PBFT's view-change subprotocol — replacing a suspected faulty leader — costs O(n³) in the worst case with proof sizes growing in the number of views. Yet blockchain deployments rotate leaders frequently, for proposer-reward fairness and liveness under targeted denial-of-service; each rotation was an expensive, latency-inflating event in PBFT and its descendants (BFT-SMaRt, Zyzzyva).

HotStuff, by Yin, Malkhi, Reiter, Gueta, and Abraham (PODC 2019) [1], resolves the tension with two composable ideas:

1. **Threshold signatures** make every round *linear*. Votes flow to the leader, who aggregates a (2f+1, n)-threshold signature into one constant-size *quorum certificate* (QC) and broadcasts it once — O(n) total instead of O(n²).
2. **The three-chain commit rule** collapses multi-round commit logic into a uniform, repeating *propose–vote* pattern. Since every phase of every view looks identical, phases of consecutive views can be *pipelined*: one propose carrying block Bₖ simultaneously serves as the prepare phase for Bₖ, the pre-commit phase for Bₖ₋₁, and the commit phase for Bₖ₋₂. This is *chained HotStuff*, adopted by DiemBFT [1] and many production blockchains.

Crucially, HotStuff is *responsive*: the leader never waits for a worst-case delay bound Δ between phases. When the network is fast, consensus proceeds at actual network speed δ. Earlier linear protocols (SBFT, Byzcoin) sacrificed responsiveness by waiting out Δ during view changes — exactly when responsiveness matters most, while recovering from a faulty leader.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The partially synchronous model

We assume *partial synchrony*: there is an unknown global stabilization time (GST) after which all messages between correct replicas arrive within a known bound Δ; before GST the adversary controls delivery arbitrarily. The fault model is Byzantine with n = 3f + 1 replicas, at most f faulty, and a computationally bounded adversary.

- **Safety.** No two conflicting blocks are ever committed by correct replicas.
- **Liveness.** Every client command submitted to all correct replicas is eventually executed, given a long enough synchronous period.

A protocol is *responsive* if, after GST, a correct leader completes consensus in time proportional to the *actual* network delay δ, not the conservative bound Δ. Since Δ must be provisioned for the worst case (seconds across continents) while δ is typically milliseconds, responsiveness determines real-world latency.

### 2.2 PBFT: the classical baseline

PBFT [6] runs three phases per view — *pre-prepare*, *prepare*, *commit* — with all-to-all broadcasts in prepare and commit, giving O(n²) steady-state messages. On suspected leader failure, replicas multicast *view-change* messages with prepared certificates; the new leader collects 2f + 1 and broadcasts a *new-view* message with the union of proofs. Message and proof sizes reach O(n³) worst-case (O(n²) with MAC authenticators), and replicas cannot vote in the new view until the new-view proof is verified — forcing a Δ wait during view changes. PBFT is thus *not responsive* with *quadratic-to-cubic* view-change cost.

### 2.3 Tendermint and the two-chain rule

Tendermint [4][5] modernized BFT for blockchains with round-based structure and per-height proposer rotation. Each round runs *propose*, *prevote*, *precommit* with +2/3 quorums, and a commit needs a *two-chain*: a block locked in one round, re-proposed with proof-of-lock in the next. Tendermint subsumes view changes into round timeouts, simplifying PBFT's machinery, but each round still gossips O(n²) votes and timeouts grow with Δ — so progress is paced by timeout parameters, not actual network speed.

### 2.4 The linearity–responsiveness gap

Before HotStuff, no partially synchronous BFT protocol achieved both properties. Linear protocols (SBFT, Byzcoin) waited for Δ during leader replacement, because the new leader could not know which votes the old leader had collected. Responsive protocols (PBFT's steady state, Tendermint's happy path) paid quadratic communication. HotStuff closed the gap: linear *and* responsive, including across view changes [1].

| Protocol | Steady-state msgs | View-change msgs | Responsive | Commit rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PBFT [6] | O(n²) | O(n³) (O(n²) w/ MACs) | No | 2-phase (prepare+commit) |
| Tendermint [4][5] | O(n²) | subsumed by rounds | No | 2-chain |
| SBFT / Byzcoin | O(n) | O(n), waits Δ | No | 2-phase |
| **HotStuff [1]** | **O(n)** | **O(n)** | **Yes** | **3-chain** |
| Fast-HotStuff [2] | O(n) | O(n) | Yes | 2-chain (responsive view sync) |
| HotStuff-2 | O(n) | O(n) | Yes | 2-chain, one phase less |

*Table 1. BFT SMR protocol comparison. Message counts are per decision.*

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 The generic propose–vote framework

HotStuff's methodological contribution is a *generic* framework unifying classical BFT protocols. Execution is a sequence of *views* v = 1, 2, 3, …, each with a designated leader L(v). Basic HotStuff runs three *phases* per view — **prepare**, **pre-commit**, **commit** — all sharing one communication pattern:

```
Leader:    broadcast PROPOSE(msg, justifyQC)
Replicas:  if safeNode(msg) then send VOTE(msg) to leader
Leader:    QC ← threshold-combine(2f+1 votes); proceed to next phase
```

The `justifyQC` field makes view changes cheap: the leader's proposal always carries a QC from a previous view, so a new leader proves the highest certified block with a *single constant-size object* rather than 2f + 1 view-change messages. This uniformity is what permits pipelining (Section 4.4).

### 3.2 Blocks, QCs, and the safety predicates

Blocks form a tree; each block carries a parent link, a command batch, and a view number. A *quorum certificate* QC(B) is a (2f + 1)-threshold signature over (B, view) — constant-size proof that a quorum voted for B.

Each replica keeps `lockedQC` (highest QC for which it voted pre-commit), `prepareQC`, and `genericQC`. The voting predicate is:

> **Definition (safeNode).** A replica votes for proposed node B with justifying QC q iff either (i) B extends the replica's `lockedQC.block`, or (ii) q.viewNumber > lockedQC.viewNumber (the *liveness* override letting a newer QC unlock a replica).

Condition (i) is the safety anchor; (ii) permits progress after view changes, since a higher-view QC implies a quorum — at least f + 1 correct replicas — voted for it.

### 3.3 Threshold signatures

HotStuff instantiates QCs with pairing-based (BLS) threshold signatures [1]. In a (t, n)-scheme with t = 2f + 1, each replica publishes partial signatures σᵢ = Sign(skᵢ, m); any t partials combine into one signature verifiable under a single public key. Combined signatures are constant-size and aggregation is non-interactive, giving O(n) messages per phase: n − 1 votes in, one broadcast out.

### 3.4 The pacemaker abstraction

View synchronization is factored into the **pacemaker** module, whose contract is minimal: after GST, it guarantees intervals long enough for a correct leader to finish its phases, during which ≥ 2f + 1 correct replicas share a view. HotStuff's pacemaker uses timeout-doubling with `NEW-VIEW(v+1, prepareQC)` messages. Since the leader needs only the *highest* QC — not a quorum of full histories — each new-view message is O(1), the view change is O(n) total, and no replica waits for Δ before voting: linear *and* responsive.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 From quadratic broadcasts to linear quorum certificates

In PBFT's prepare phase, each of n replicas broadcasts to all others: n(n − 1) = O(n²) messages. In HotStuff's prepare phase, each replica sends one vote to the leader (n − 1 messages) and the leader broadcasts one QC-bearing message: 2(n − 1) = O(n) total. Pseudocode:

```python
def phase_prepare(view, leader, replicas, justify_qc):
    proposal = Block(parent=justify_qc.block, view=view, cmds=mempool.drain())
    leader.broadcast(("PROPOSE", proposal, justify_qc))
    votes = [r.partial_sign(("VOTE", proposal.id, view))
             for r in replicas if r.safe_node(proposal, justify_qc)]
    assert len(votes) >= 2*f + 1          # one message per replica -> linear
    qc = threshold_combine(votes)          # BLS aggregation, O(1)-size QC
    leader.broadcast(("QC", qc))
    return qc
```

Votes are partial signatures on the *same* message, so aggregation is local computation (Lagrange interpolation in the exponent for BLS). The QC is *transferable proof* — verifiable by any replica, client, or light client with one pairing check — which is why HotStuff QCs double as finality proofs in DiemBFT and Flow [2].

### 4.2 The three-chain commit rule

Classical BFT commits after *two* quorum phases (prepare + commit in PBFT; prevote + precommit in Tendermint). HotStuff uses three — prepare, pre-commit, commit — with the commit rule stated over the *chain* of QCs:

> **Theorem (Three-chain commit rule).** Let B₁ ← B₂ ← B₃ be blocks in consecutive views v, v + 1, v + 2, each Bᵢ₊₁'s QC certifying Bᵢ. Then B₁ is *committed*: no conflicting block can ever obtain a QC in any view ≥ v.

*Proof sketch.* Voting pre-commit for B₂ in view v + 1 sets each voter's `lockedQC` to QC(B₁); thereafter it votes only for blocks extending B₁ or justified by a higher-view QC. A conflicting B₁′ needs a prepare-QC from 2f + 1 votes in some view v′ ≥ v. Quorum intersection gives a correct replica in both quorums; it locked on B₁ and votes for B₁′ only given a QC of view > v for the conflicting branch — but the *first* such QC cannot exist before B₁′ gets its own QC, a circularity. The third link is liveness bookkeeping: the commit phase lets replicas learn others have locked, so the leader of v + 3 can safely extend B₃. ∎

The extra phase buys the *uniform* propose–vote pattern: one repeating round instead of three bespoke phases, enabling pipelining and collapsing the safety proof to a single quorum-intersection argument — versus PBFT's intricate prepared/committed bookkeeping [1].

### 4.3 The pacemaker: responsive view synchronization

1. On entering view v, each replica starts timer T(v), doubling after consecutive failures.
2. On timeout, it sends `NEW-VIEW(v+1, prepareQC)` to L(v + 1) — one O(1)-size message.
3. L(v + 1) waits for 2f + 1 new-view messages, takes the highest QC as `highQC`, and proposes carrying `highQC`.

The leader needs only the *highest* QC, so the view change is O(n) messages of O(1) size. Replicas vote on proposal arrival — no Δ wait beyond the 2f + 1 quorum — so after GST a correct leader with δ-overlap finishes each phase in O(δ): *responsive* view changes. Timeout calibration remains the delicate engineering parameter: too aggressive causes spurious suspicions of correct leaders; too lax lets faulty leaders stall for the full timeout [2].

### 4.4 Chained HotStuff: pipelining the phases

Because every phase looks identical, phases of *consecutive views* overlap. In **chained HotStuff** each view runs a *single* propose–vote round, and the QC formed in view v simultaneously serves as the **prepare-QC** for Bᵥ, the **pre-commit-QC** for Bᵥ₋₁, and the **commit-QC** for Bᵥ₋₂. The commit rule becomes: B is committed when three *consecutive* blocks B ← B′ ← B″ extend it. With rotating leaders, the steady state reaches *one block decision per propose–vote round* — the throughput-optimal regime for leader-based BFT.

```
view:      v        v+1        v+2        v+3
           │          │          │          │
B_v:    propose ──► QC ──► (pre-commit) ──► (commit) ──► COMMITTED ✓
B_v+1:              propose ──► QC ──► (pre-commit) ──► ...
B_v+2:                        propose ──► QC ──► ...
```

*Figure 1 (conceptual): the chained pipeline — each view forms exactly one QC, advancing three blocks at once.*

Chaining is what production adopted: DiemBFT v4 is chained HotStuff with pacemaker and round-robin rotation; Flow and Cypherium build on the pipelined variant [2]. The cost is *leader-rotation coupling*: throughput is gated by the slowest leader, and a faulty leader wastes exactly one pipeline slot — acceptable in permissioned deployments, a liveness tax under adaptive attack.

### 4.5 TLA⁺ specification of the voting core

The safety kernel — QC formation and the voting rule — is small enough to model-check. A TLA⁺ sketch (cryptography abstracted as unforgeable `ThresholdCombine`):

```tla
--------------------------- MODULE HotStuffCore ---------------------------
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANTS Replicas, F, QuorumSize  \* QuorumSize = 2*F + 1
VARIABLES lockedQC, votedView, qcHigh

SafeNode(r, b, justifyQC) ==
    \/ Extends(b, lockedQC[r].block)
    \/ justifyQC.view > lockedQC[r].view

Vote(r, b, v, justifyQC) ==
    /\ v > votedView[r]
    /\ SafeNode(r, b, justifyQC)
    /\ votedView' = [votedView EXCEPT ![r] = v]
    /\ UNCHANGED <<lockedQC, qcHigh>>

Safety == \A r1, r2 \in Replicas :
    Committed(r1, b1) /\ Committed(r2, b2) /\ b1 # b2 => FALSE
=============================================================================
```

Checking this for n = 4, f = 1 with TLC verifies no conflicting commits over all bounded-view interleavings — the technique used by the Fast-HotStuff authors [2] and the Quint specification of Fast Tendermint — complementing the hand proofs of Section 5.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Safety theorem

> **Theorem (Safety).** Two conflicting blocks can never both be committed by correct replicas.

*Proof.* Suppose B commits via B ← B₂ ← B₃ in views v < v + 1 < v + 2, and conflicting B′ must obtain a prepare-QC in some view v′. If v′ ≤ v, quorum intersection yields a correct replica voting twice in one view — impossible. If v′ > v, take the *first* view v* > v where a conflicting block gets a prepare-QC, certified by quorum Q*. When B₂ got its pre-commit QC, a quorum Q set `lockedQC := QC(B)`; Q ∩ Q* contains correct replica r, locked on B at view v. For r to vote prepare for the conflicting block in v*, `SafeNode` demands a justifying QC of view > v on the conflicting branch — contradicting minimality of v*. ∎

One quorum-intersection argument replaces PBFT's predicate bookkeeping — and the same framework expresses PBFT, Tendermint, and Casper as degenerate parameter choices [1].

### 5.2 Liveness theorem

> **Theorem (Liveness).** After GST, every client command submitted to all correct replicas is eventually executed.

*Proof sketch.* After GST the pacemaker yields infinitely many *synchronized* views with correct leaders where 2f + 1 correct replicas overlap for ≥ 3δ plus processing. The leader collects 2f + 1 new-view messages, extracts `highQC`, and proposes extending it; all correct replicas satisfy `SafeNode` and vote through all three phases, with QCs formed in O(δ) each. Three consecutive synchronized views with correct leaders commit a block; rotating leadership guarantees correct leaders recur. ∎

The *only* timing assumption is the pacemaker's overlap guarantee — the consensus core never waits for Δ. Separating the core from the pacemaker is HotStuff's cleanest architectural lesson.

### 5.3 Measured performance

Yin et al. deployed HotStuff over a WAN with 100+ replicas against BFT-SMaRt [1]:

| Metric (n = 100, WAN) | HotStuff | BFT-SMaRt |
|---|---|---|
| Peak throughput | ≈ 15k ops/s | ≈ 15k ops/s |
| Median latency (no faults) | ≈ 0.9 s | ≈ 0.8 s |
| View-change communication | O(n) | O(n²) |
| Throughput under leader failover | degrades gracefully | collapses (quadratic storm) |

*Table 2. Approximate figures from [1]: HotStuff matches BFT-SMaRt fault-free while linear view change keeps failover cheap.*

Studies of pipelined HotStuff [3] confirmed chaining sustains near-steady-state throughput under rotating leaders, with faulty leaders costing roughly one view of throughput each. Fast-HotStuff [2] later reduced the commit rule to two chains with responsive view synchronization, cutting a phase of latency while preserving linearity — evidence that HotStuff's *framework*, not just its three-phase instance, was the durable contribution.

## 6. Limitations

**Cryptographic overhead.** Linearity is bought with pairing-based threshold signatures: BLS partial-signing and QC verification cost milliseconds per operation — a real tax at tens of thousands of ops/s — and distributed key generation (DKG) adds deployment complexity that MAC-based PBFT avoids. Verification batching mitigates but does not eliminate the gap.

**The pacemaker's timeout dilemma.** Responsiveness holds *given* view synchronization, but the pacemaker itself uses timeouts. Near-δ timeouts cause spurious view changes (each wasting a pipeline slot in chained HotStuff); near-Δ timeouts let faulty leaders stall for the full duration. No pacemaker can distinguish a slow network from a faulty leader before GST — an information-theoretic limit.

**Adaptive adversaries vs. rotation.** Chained HotStuff rotates the leader every view. An adaptive adversary corrupting (or DDoSing) each leader in turn stalls one pipeline slot per view indefinitely within the f bound — worse worst-case liveness degradation than stable-leader protocols, trading worst-case liveness for average-case throughput and fairness.

**Three phases vs. two.** The three-chain rule adds a round-trip of commit latency versus two-chain protocols (PBFT, Tendermint, Fast-HotStuff) — pure overhead in low-latency LANs where view changes are rare, which is why HotStuff-2 and Fast-HotStuff [2] returned to two-chain rules.

**Permissioned assumption.** Like all n = 3f + 1 BFT protocols, HotStuff assumes a known Sybil-resistant validator set; the O(n) claims count *validators*, and permissionless deployments must bolt on external Sybil resistance.

## 7. Conclusion

HotStuff's contribution operates at two levels. Concretely, it is the first partially synchronous BFT protocol achieving *linear* communication and *responsiveness* together — via threshold-signature QCs, the three-chain commit rule, and a pacemaker-separated view-synchronization layer — and its chained form, deciding one block per propose–vote round under rotating leaders, became the consensus engine of DiemBFT, Flow, and a generation of permissioned blockchains [1][2]. Abstractly, it replaced classical BFT's bespoke phase structure with a *uniform propose–vote framework* in which PBFT, Tendermint, and Casper are parameter choices — turning consensus design from protocol invention into framework instantiation.

The ideas compose because each solves one subproblem cleanly: threshold signatures linearize communication, the three-chain rule linearizes the *logic*, the pacemaker isolates timing assumptions, and chaining pipelines the result. Later refinements — Fast-HotStuff's two-chain responsive view sync [2], HotStuff-2's phase reduction, formal TLA⁺/Quint specifications — all operate *within* HotStuff's framework, the strongest evidence of its depth. For permissioned blockchains that rotate leaders frequently, span WAN-scale validator sets, and must survive leader failure without quadratic storms, HotStuff remains the definitive foundation.
