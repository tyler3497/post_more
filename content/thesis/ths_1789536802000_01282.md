# Neural Tangent Kernels and the Lazy Training Regime: Kernel Regression Equivalence in Infinite-Width Limits, Finite-Width Corrections via Tensor Programs, and Spectral Bias in Gradient Descent Dynamics

## Abstract

We survey and unify three landmark developments in the mathematical theory of deep learning: the *Neural Tangent Kernel* (NTK) of Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler, the *lazy training* phenomenon of Chizat, Oyallon, and Bach, and the *spectral bias* of gradient descent. We derive the NTK as the kernel of the first-order linearization of a neural network around its random initialization, prove that in the infinite-width limit it converges to a deterministic kernel and remains constant throughout training, and show that lazy training of any sufficiently over-parameterized differentiable program is exactly kernel regression in the associated reproducing kernel Hilbert space. We present the Tensor Programs framework of Yang as the rigorous engine behind the architectural universality of NTK limits, characterize finite-width corrections that vanish at predictable rates in the width, and formalize the dichotomy between the kernel regime and the feature-learning regime. Finally, we prove that gradient flow under a fixed kernel learns a target function along kernel principal components at rates governed by the kernel eigenspectrum, which explains the empirically observed spectral bias toward low-frequency, smooth functions.

## 1 Introduction

Deep learning presents a paradox that classical statistical learning theory cannot easily digest: highly non-convex optimization problems with millions of parameters, trained by simple gradient-based methods, reliably converge to solutions that generalize. The last decade of theoretical work has converged on a productive strategy for resolving this paradox — study *limits* in which the analysis becomes tractable, and then characterize the *corrections* that finite models incur. The most influential such limit is the infinite-width limit of neural networks.

Two facts make this limit remarkable. First, at initialization, an infinitely wide neural network is a Gaussian process, a classical object whose prior and posterior are completely understood [1]. Second, and far less obvious, the *training dynamics* of an infinitely wide network are also classical: gradient descent in parameter space becomes *kernel gradient descent* in function space with respect to a fixed kernel, the Neural Tangent Kernel [1]. The non-convex parameter-space problem is thereby replaced by a convex function-space problem.

This thesis develops the full mathematical picture. We begin in **Section 2** with the background on Gaussian process limits and kernel methods that the NTK theory builds upon. **Section 3** derives the NTK from first principles and introduces the lazy training phenomenon, in which a model behaves as its own linearization around initialization. **Section 4** is the deep technical core: the recursive computation of the NTK, the exact equivalence between lazy training and kernel regression, the Tensor Programs machinery that extends NTK results to arbitrary architectures, finite-width corrections and the feature-learning dichotomy, and the spectral structure of learning dynamics. **Section 5** states the key theorems with proof sketches and summarizes the empirical record. **Section 6** discusses limitations, and **Section 7** concludes.

> **Definition:** The *lazy training regime* is the regime in which the parameters of a model move by a vanishingly small amount during training, while the model function itself moves by an order-one amount, so that the model is well approximated by its first-order Taylor expansion around the initialization for the entire trajectory [2].

## 2 Background

### Gaussian processes at initialization

The story begins before any training occurs.

The Gaussian process viewpoint already explains several phenomena: the distribution of functions expressible at initialization, the role of the activation function in shaping the prior covariance, and the behavior of Bayesian neural networks in the wide limit.

### Kernel methods and the representer theorem

A positive semidefinite kernel $K$ on an input space defines a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) $\mathcal{H}_K$ in which function evaluation is continuous.

$$\min_{f \in \mathcal{H}_K} \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - f(x_i))^2 + \lambda \lVert f \rVert_{\mathcal{H}_K}^2,$$

admits by the representer theorem a solution of the form $f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i K(x, x_i)$, reducing an infinite-dimensional problem to an $n$-dimensional one.

### Gradient flow and positive definiteness

We analyze training in continuous time via gradient flow, $\dot{\theta}_t = -\eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t)$, the infinitesimal-step limit of gradient descent.

## 3 Methodology

### Deriving the kernel dynamics

Let $f(\theta; x)$ denote a neural network with parameters $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p$ and input $x$, trained on data $(x_i, y_i)$ for $i$ ranging over the training set, with the squared loss $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_i (f(\theta; x_i) - y_i)^2$.

$$\frac{d\theta_t}{dt} = -\eta \sum_{i} \big(f(\theta_t; x_i) - y_i\big)\, \nabla_\theta f(\theta_t; x_i).$$

Applying the chain rule to the network function at an arbitrary input $x$ yields the *function-space* dynamics:

$$\frac{d}{dt} f(\theta_t; x) = -\eta \sum_{i} \hat{\Theta}_t(x, x_i)\, \big(f(\theta_t; x_i) - y_i\big),$$

where the **empirical neural tangent kernel** is defined as the Gram matrix of parameter gradients,

$$\hat{\Theta}_t(x, x') := \big\langle \nabla_\theta f(\theta_t; x),\, \nabla_\theta f(\theta_t; x') \big\rangle.$$

By construction $\hat{\Theta}_t$ is positive semidefinite.

```python
import numpy as np

def ntk_relu_gram(X, depth, sigma_w=np.sqrt(2.0), sigma_b=0.0):
    """Analytic NTK Gram matrix for a ReLU MLP (Jacot et al. recursion)."""
    n = X.shape[0]
    Sigma = (sigma_w**2) * (X @ X.T) / X.shape[1] + sigma_b**2
    Theta = Sigma.copy()
    for _ in range(1, depth):
        diag = np.sqrt(np.diag(Sigma))
        corr = Sigma / np.outer(diag, diag)
        corr = np.clip(corr, -1.0, 1.0)
        # Arccosine kernel moments for ReLU
        Sigma = (sigma_w**2) * np.outer(diag, diag) / (2*np.pi) * (
            np.sqrt(1 - corr**2) + (np.pi - np.arccos(corr)) * corr
        ) + sigma_b**2
        dSigma = (sigma_w**2) / (2*np.pi) * (np.pi - np.arccos(corr))
        Theta = Sigma + Theta * dSigma
    return Theta

# Kernel gradient flow has a closed form: f_t = y - exp(-eta * Theta * t) @ (y - f0)
```

### The linearization and the lazy scaling

Consider the first-order Taylor expansion of the network around its initialization $\theta_0$:

$$f^{\text{lin}}(\theta; x) := f(\theta_0; x) + \big\langle \nabla_\theta f(\theta_0; x),\, \theta - \theta_0 \big\rangle.$$

*Lazy training* is the phenomenon in which the true trajectory $f(\theta_t; \cdot)$ stays uniformly close to the linearized trajectory $f^{\text{lin}}(\theta_t; \cdot)$ for all time [2].

The practical consequence is a clean methodological split: to study a network in the lazy regime, one may replace the network by its linearization and inherit the entire kernel-methods toolkit — closed-form dynamics, generalization bounds via RKHS norms, and spectral analysis of the kernel Gram matrix.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The NTK recursion and its deterministic limit

For a depth-$L$ fully connected network, the analytic NTK admits a layer-wise recursion.

$$\Theta^{(1)} = \Sigma^{(1)}, \qquad \Theta^{(l)} = \Sigma^{(l)} + \Theta^{(l-1)} \odot \dot{\Sigma}^{(l)} \quad \text{for } l \geq 2.$$

Each step of the recursion has a clear interpretation: $\Sigma^{(l)}$ captures how the *function value* covariance propagates, while the multiplicative term captures how *gradient* information backpropagates through the layer.

The recursion is not merely a theoretical device: it is the algorithm by which analytic NTKs are computed in practice, and the Python implementation above evaluates it in closed form for ReLU networks via the arccosine kernel moments.

### 4.2 Lazy training is kernel regression

Chizat, Oyallon, and Bach formalized lazy training beyond neural networks, as a general phenomenon in differentiable programming [2].

This equivalence reframes the empirical mystery.

> **Theorem:** *(Informal lazy-training equivalence.)* Consider a differentiable model $f(\theta; \cdot)$ with $m$ parameters, initialized at $\theta_0$, trained by gradient flow on the squared loss under a scaling such that $\lVert \theta_t - \theta_0 \rVert = O(m^{-1/2})$ uniformly in $t$ while $\lVert f(\theta_t; \cdot) - f(\theta_0; \cdot) \rVert = \Theta(1)$. Then $\sup_t \lVert f(\theta_t; \cdot) - f^{\mathrm{lin}}(\theta_t; \cdot) \rVert \to 0$ as $m \to \infty$, and the limit dynamics coincide with kernel gradient descent under the tangent kernel at $\theta_0$ [2].

### 4.3 Tensor Programs and architectural universality

The original NTK analysis covered fully connected networks; extending it to convolutions, recurrence, attention, residual connections, batch normalization, and weight sharing required a general proof technology. Yang's **Tensor Programs** framework provides exactly that [4]. A Tensor Program is a formal language of random-variable-generating operations — matrix multiplication by Gaussian matrices, coordinate-wise nonlinearities, and averaging — equipped with a *Master Theorem* stating that every scalar computed by such a program converges almost surely to a deterministic limit characterized by Gaussian moment recursions.

Within this framework, Yang proved that the NTK of a randomly initialized network of *any architecture* expressible as a Tensor Program converges to a deterministic limit as widths grow [4]. The proof isolates the role of the *gradient independence assumption* (GIA) — the heuristic that a weight matrix used in forward propagation may be treated as independent of its transpose used in backpropagation — and identifies a verifiable condition, the *Simple GIA Check*, under which GIA-based calculations are correct, together with counterexamples where it fails [4]. A companion result establishes that during training, networks in the NTK parameterization follow kernel gradient descent with the infinite-width NTK for essentially arbitrary architectures, completing the architectural universality of the theory [6].

### 4.4 Finite-width corrections and the feature-learning dichotomy

Infinite-width limits are approximations, and a mature theory must quantify the approximation error. At finite width $n$, the empirical NTK fluctuates around its limit as $\hat{\Theta} = \Theta + O(n^{-1/2})$, and these fluctuations feed back into the dynamics: the kernel is no longer exactly constant, parameter motion is no longer exactly confined, and the linearization error is nonzero but controlled. The corrections vanish at predictable rates in the width, which is why the kernel picture becomes quantitatively accurate for very wide networks while remaining only qualitative at practical widths.

More fundamentally, Yang and Hu showed that the kernel regime is not the only possible infinite-width behavior [5]. They classified a natural space of network parameterizations generalizing the standard, NTK, and mean-field parameterizations, and proved a *dichotomy*: any parameterization in this space either admits *feature learning* — hidden representations evolve nontrivially in the limit — or has training dynamics given by kernel gradient descent, but never both. The maximal update parameterization ($\mu$P) achieves feature learning in the limit, and its infinite-width limits can be computed exactly via Tensor Programs, outperforming NTK baselines on tasks that depend crucially on representation learning, such as Word2Vec and few-shot learning [5].

The dichotomy reframes the debate about the relevance of NTK theory: the kernel regime is *one* infinite-width limit among several, selected by the parameterization. Standard parameterization at large width sits near the kernel regime; $\mu$P sits in the feature-learning regime. Finite-width networks interpolate between the two, which explains why NTK predictions are sometimes quantitatively accurate and sometimes qualitatively wrong.

The framework has since been extended beyond SGD: the same dichotomy between feature learning and kernel behavior holds for adaptive optimizers such as Adam, albeit with a nonlinear notion of kernel, using an extended Tensor Program language capable of expressing how adaptive optimizers process gradients into updates [8].

### 4.5 Spectral bias and the eigenspectrum of learning

Even within the kernel regime, not all target functions are learned equally fast. Under kernel gradient flow with a fixed kernel, the residual vector $r_t = f_t(X) - y$ on the training data obeys the linear ODE $\dot{r}_t = -\eta \, \Theta \, r_t$, with solution $r_t = e^{-\eta \Theta t} r_0$. Expanding in the eigenbasis of the Gram matrix $\Theta = \sum_k \lambda_k u_k u_k^\top$ gives

$$r_t = \sum_k e^{-\eta \lambda_k t} \, \langle u_k, r_0 \rangle \, u_k.$$

Each principal component of the residual decays exponentially at a rate proportional to its eigenvalue: *convergence is fastest along the largest kernel principal components* [1]. This is the precise mathematical form of spectral bias in the kernel regime, and it immediately suggests a theoretical motivation for early stopping — halt training when the large-eigenvalue components are fit but before the small-eigenvalue components begin fitting noise [1].

The Fourier-analytic counterpart was established empirically by Rahaman et al., who showed that deep ReLU networks are biased toward low-frequency functions: the magnitude of frequency component $k$ of the network function decays at least as fast as $O(k^{-2})$, with width helping polynomially and depth helping exponentially in modeling higher frequencies [7]. Low frequencies are learned first, are more robust to parameter perturbation, and occupy a larger volume of parameter space; representing high-frequency functions requires fine-tuning of parameters [7]. The NTK eigenspectrum and the Fourier spectrum are two views of the same phenomenon: gradient-based training has an intrinsic simplicity bias, fitting smooth, low-complexity structure before high-frequency detail.

> **Lemma:** *(Spectral decomposition of kernel gradient flow.)* Let $\Theta$ be the fixed NTK Gram matrix on the training data with eigenpairs $(\lambda_k, u_k)$ ordered $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq 0$. Under gradient flow on the squared loss, the projection of the residual onto $u_k$ evolves as $\langle u_k, r_t \rangle = e^{-\eta \lambda_k t} \langle u_k, r_0 \rangle$. In particular, the relative convergence rate along $u_k$ versus $u_j$ is exactly $\lambda_k / \lambda_j$ [1].

## 5 Empirical Results and Formal Proofs

### Formal statements

We now state the two central theorems precisely, with proof sketches.

> **Theorem:** *(NTK convergence and constancy, Jacot et al.)* Consider a fully connected network of depth $L$ with Lipschitz activation, initialized with standard Gaussian weights. As the hidden widths $n_1, \dots, n_{L-1} \to \infty$ sequentially, the empirical NTK $\hat{\Theta}_t$ converges in probability to the deterministic kernel $\Theta^{(L)}$ given by the recursion of Section 4.1, *uniformly* over the training trajectory: $\sup_{t \geq 0} \lVert \hat{\Theta}_t - \Theta^{(L)} \rVert \to 0$ in probability. Consequently the network function follows kernel gradient descent with the fixed kernel $\Theta^{(L)}$ [1].

*Proof sketch.* The argument has two halves. **Convergence at initialization** proceeds by induction on layers: assuming the pre-activations at layer $l-1$ are jointly Gaussian with covariance $\Sigma^{(l-1)}$, the empirical averages defining $\hat{\Theta}^{(l)}$ converge by the law of large numbers to their expectations, which evaluate to the recursion. **Constancy during training** bounds the parameter motion: for the squared loss, $\lVert \theta_t - \theta_0 \rVert \leq \lVert r_0 \rVert / \sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(\Theta)} \cdot n^{-1/2}$-type estimates show parameters move $O(n^{-1/2})$, and a Hessian bound $\lVert \nabla^2 f \rVert = O(n^{-1/2})$ then implies the Jacobian — and hence the NTK — varies by $O(n^{-1/2})$, vanishing in the limit [1].

> **Theorem:** *(Lazy training equivalence, Chizat et al.)* Under the scaling described in Section 4.2, gradient flow on the nonlinear model and gradient flow on its linearization around the initialization remain uniformly close, with the gap vanishing as the parameter count grows. The limit trajectory is kernel gradient descent in the RKHS of the tangent kernel [2].

The extension to stochastic gradient descent was carried out by Lee et al., who showed that the training dynamics become a stochastic differential equation driven by the NTK, reducing to a simple mean-reverting process for the squared loss, and who analyzed how depth, initialization scale, and activation choice shape the NTK and hence the dynamics [3].

### Empirical record

1. **Concentration of the empirical NTK.** Jacot et al. verified numerically that the empirical NTK of wide networks concentrates around the analytic limit and that kernel-regression predictions with the analytic NTK track the trained network's outputs [1].
2. **Lazy versus feature-learning performance.** Chizat et al. demonstrated that pushing standard vision CNNs into the lazy regime degrades test performance, while Yang and Hu showed that infinite-width *feature-learning* limits outperform NTK baselines on representation-sensitive tasks, with finite-width networks approaching the feature-learning limit as width grows [2][5].
3. **Spectral bias in practice.** Rahaman et al. confirmed across architectures and datasets that low-frequency components of target functions are fit first and that high frequencies require disproportionate parameter tuning, matching the eigenvalue-decay prediction of kernel gradient flow [7].
4. **Architectural generality.** Reference implementations of infinite-width NTKs for recurrent networks, Transformers, and batch normalization corroborate the Tensor Programs predictions beyond MLPs [4].

```python
import numpy as np

def kernel_gradient_flow(Theta, y, f0, eta, T, steps=1000):
    """Closed-form training dynamics under a fixed kernel (spectral view)."""
    lam, U = np.linalg.eigh(Theta)
    r0 = (f0 - y)
    coeffs = U.T @ r0
    t = np.linspace(0, T, steps)
    # Each eigen-component decays as exp(-eta * lambda_k * t)
    traj = (U * np.exp(-eta * lam[None, :] * t[:, None])) @ coeffs
    return t, y[None, :] + traj  # predictions on training points over time
```

## 6 Limitations

The theory surveyed here is powerful but bounded, and its boundaries are as instructive as its successes.

First, the most practically relevant limitation is the one Chizat et al. identified: the lazy regime does not describe how deep networks are actually trained to state-of-the-art performance, and forcing models into it hurts [2]. The NTK is therefore best understood as characterizing *one* training regime — the appropriate one for very wide networks under NTK parameterization — rather than as a general theory of deep learning.

Second, the sharpest results assume the squared loss and full-batch gradient flow. Extensions to SGD exist [3] and to adaptive optimizers [8], but the analysis of cross-entropy loss, batch normalization in training mode, and data augmentation within the kernel framework remains less complete.

Third, the theory leans on positive definiteness of the limiting NTK on the training data. Degenerate cases — duplicate inputs, pathological activations, or architectures where the Simple GIA Check fails — can break the convergence guarantees or make GIA-based computations incorrect [4].

Fourth, infinite-width limits can be quantitatively misleading at practical widths and depths. Finite-width corrections vanish asymptotically, but the constants and the depth-to-width interplay matter: very deep networks at fixed width can behave quite differently from their infinite-width idealizations, and the NTK parameterization itself is rarely used in practice.

Finally, spectral-bias results are cleanest in the kernel regime or in one-dimensional Fourier settings; a fully general, non-asymptotic characterization of the simplicity bias of feature-learning networks remains open [7].

## 7 Conclusion

The Neural Tangent Kernel, lazy training, and spectral bias together form a coherent mathematical narrative about gradient-based learning in over-parameterized models. The NTK shows that infinite-width training is kernel gradient descent with an explicit, computable kernel [1]. Lazy training shows that this kernel picture extends to any differentiable program under the right scaling — and simultaneously warns that the kernel regime is not the regime in which deep learning wins [2]. Tensor Programs lift the entire theory to arbitrary architectures and delineate, via the feature-learning dichotomy, exactly when the kernel picture applies and when it must give way to representation learning [4][5][6]. Spectral bias, finally, explains *what* is learned *when*: the eigenspectrum of the kernel dictates a curriculum from smooth, low-frequency structure to high-frequency detail [1][7].

The frontier is now clear. The kernel regime is solved; the feature-learning regime is only beginning to yield exact results. Extending the Tensor Programs calculus to describe feature learning in realistic architectures — attention, normalization, adaptive optimization [8] — and connecting the resulting infinite-width feature-learning limits to the generalization behavior of practical networks is the central open program in the mathematical theory of deep learning.
