# Verifying Quantum Computational Advantage: Linear Cross-Entropy Benchmarking for Random Circuit Sampling, Porter-Thomas Fidelity, Classical Spoofing, and Superconducting Noise Models

## Abstract

## 1. Introduction

In October 2019, Google's quantum AI team announced that their 53-qubit superconducting processor, *Sycamore*, had performed a specific computational task — sampling the output distribution of a random quantum circuit — in approximately 200 seconds, estimating that the equivalent classical simulation would require roughly 10,000 years on the Summit supercomputer [1]. This claim, dubbed *quantum supremacy* by John Preskill in 2012 [6], ignited one of the most consequential debates in modern computer science: not merely whether the experiment was correct, but what it *means* to verify a computation that is, by construction, too hard to check directly.

> **Definition 1.1 (The verification problem).** Given a fixed $n$-qubit circuit $C$ drawn from a random ensemble $\mathcal{E}$ and $N$ bitstring samples $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^N$ produced by an untrusted device $D$, determine whether $D$ samples from the ideal distribution $P_C(x) = |\langle x | C | 0^n \rangle|^2$ with non-negligible fidelity, using only classical resources that scale sub-exponentially in $n$ for the verification step, assuming access to a classical oracle for the values $P_C(x_i)$ at the *sampled* bitstrings.

This thesis is organized around four pillars of the verification problem: (i) the random circuit sampling task and its complexity foundations; (ii) the Porter–Thomas statistical fingerprint of quantum chaos; (iii) the XEB estimator, its fidelity correspondence, and its vulnerability to classical spoofing via tensor-network contraction; and (iv) the physical noise models of superconducting qubit arrays that bound achievable fidelity. We treat each with full mathematical rigor.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Random circuit sampling

The hardness case for RCS rests on the conjecture that no polynomial-time classical algorithm can sample from a distribution multiplicatively close to $P_C$. Bouland, Fefferman, Nirkhe, and Vazirani [9] provided the key complexity-theoretic evidence: assuming the *non-collapse of the polynomial hierarchy*, no efficient classical algorithm can sample from the output distribution of a random circuit to within total variation distance $1/\mathrm{poly}(n)$. Aaronson and Arkhipov's earlier BosonSampling proposal [10] established the paradigm; RCS is its universal-gate cousin, and it is arguably harder to spoof because universal circuits have no Gaussian structure to exploit.

### 2.2 The XEB estimator

Let $P_C(x)$ denote the ideal output probability of bitstring $x$. Given samples $\{x_i\}$ from an experimental device, the **linear cross-entropy benchmarking fidelity** is defined [1][8] as

$$\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}} \;=\; 2^n \left\langle P_C(x_i) \right\rangle_{i=1}^N - 1 \;=\; \frac{2^n}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} P_C(x_i) - 1.$$

The calibration points are immediate: if the device samples *perfectly* from $P_C$, then under the Porter–Thomas law (see §4.2), $\mathbb{E}[P_C(x_i)] = \sum_x P_C(x)^2 \approx 2/2^n$, giving $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}} \approx 1$. If the device outputs *uniformly random* bitstrings, $\mathbb{E}[P_C(x_i)] = 1/2^n$ and $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}} \approx 0$ [1][8]. Values between 0 and 1 interpolate linearly with the mixture weight of the ideal distribution in the device's output, which is why XEB functions as a *fidelity proxy*.

> **Theorem (XEB–fidelity correspondence, informal).** *For a noisy device whose error channel is well-approximated by a global depolarizing channel of fidelity $\phi$, the expected XEB score satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}}] = \phi + o(1)$, provided the ideal circuit ensemble forms an approximate 2-design and the circuit is sufficiently deep to thermalize the output distribution to Porter–Thomas form [7][5].*

## 3. Methodology

Our methodology is analytical and comparative: we derive the XEB estimator from first principles of random matrix theory, construct the standard noise models for superconducting hardware, analyze the principal classical spoofing algorithms as adversarial benchmarks, and evaluate the empirical record against these models. The work proceeds in four methodological steps:

1. **Circuit ensemble analysis.** We characterize the random circuit families used in supremacy experiments — gate sets, coupler patterns, and the convergence of the output distribution to the Porter–Thomas law — drawing on the supplementary data of [1] and the verification circuits of [11][12].

2. **Estimator derivation.** We derive $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}}$ as the second moment of the output distribution, relate it to the *cross-entropy difference* $H_{\mathrm{lin}} - H$ introduced by Boixo *et al.* [7], and quantify its sampling variance as a function of $N$ and $n$.

3. **Adversarial analysis.** We formalize classical spoofing as an optimization problem: maximize $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}}$ subject to classical runtime constraints. We evaluate tensor-network contraction (the *big-head* method [2]), Schrödinger–Feynman hybrid simulation [14], and shallow-circuit marginal samplers [15], computing their achieved XEB-per-FLOP efficiency.

4. **Noise modeling.** We fit the digital error model of [1] — per-gate depolarizing errors, readout errors, and $T_1/T_2$ decoherence — to published component fidelities, and propagate them to predicted circuit fidelity via the multiplicative formula $\hat{\phi} = \prod_{g}(1-e_g)$ [1].

All numerical claims are cross-checked against the cited primary literature; no new experiments were conducted.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 Random circuit sampling and the Sycamore architecture

The Sycamore processor is a 54-transmon array (one qubit malfunctioning, leaving 53 operational) with nearest-neighbor tunable couplers on a rectangular lattice [1]. Each *cycle* of the supremacy circuit applies:

- a layer of single-qubit gates, each drawn uniformly from $\{\sqrt{X}, \sqrt{Y}, \sqrt{W}\}$, with the constraint that a gate is never repeated on the same qubit in consecutive cycles (to maximize scrambling);
- a layer of two-qubit gates from the set $\{ \mathrm{fSim}(\theta, \phi) \}$ with randomly chosen $\theta, \phi$, applied according to one of eight coupler patterns (A–H), sequenced as ABCDCDAB to guarantee that every qubit interacts with all four neighbors within a few cycles [1].

After $m = 20$ cycles, the qubits are measured in the computational basis. The experiment collected 3 million bitstrings (with 30 million in extended runs), each in 200 seconds of wall-clock time for a million samples [1]. The classical verification required computing $P_C(x_i)$ for a subset of sampled bitstrings using a hybrid Schrödinger–Feynman simulator on Summit — itself a supercomputing feat — feasible only because verification needs amplitudes at *specific* bitstrings rather than the full $2^{53}$-dimensional distribution.

Why random circuits? The choice is deliberate on three grounds:

### 4.2 The Porter–Thomas distribution: fingerprint of quantum chaos

Consider the output state $|\psi\rangle = C|0^n\rangle$ of a random circuit, and the probability $p_x = |\langle x | \psi \rangle|^2$ of a fixed bitstring $x$. If $C$ is drawn from the Haar measure on $U(2^n)$ (or a circuit ensemble forming a 2-design), the vector of amplitudes is uniformly distributed over the unit sphere in $\mathbb{C}^{2^n}$, and the marginal distribution of a single $p_x$ is [7][16]:

$$ \Pr(p)\,dp \;=\; 2^n \, e^{-2^n p}\, dp, \qquad p \geq 0, $$

the **Porter–Thomas distribution**, first identified by Porter and Thomas (1956) in the statistics of neutron resonance widths [16]. Its moments are elementary:

$$\mathbb{E}[p] = 2^{-n}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[p^2] = \frac{2}{2^{2n}}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[p^k] = \frac{k!}{2^{kn}}.$$

These moments are the entire engine of XEB. Observe:

- *Perfect sampling.* Drawing $x_i \sim P_C$ gives $\mathbb{E}[P_C(x_i)] = \sum_x P_C(x)^2 = \mathbb{E}[p^2]\cdot 2^n = 2/2^n$, hence $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}} = 2^n \cdot (2/2^n) - 1 = 1$.
- *Uniform sampling.* Drawing $x_i$ uniformly gives $\mathbb{E}[P_C(x_i)] = 2^{-n} \sum_x P_C(x) = 2^{-n}$, hence $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}} = 0$.
- *Depolarized mixture.* If the device output is $Q = \phi P_C + (1-\phi) 2^{-n}$ (the standard *global depolarizing* ansatz [1]), then $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}[P_C(x)] = \phi \cdot 2/2^n + (1-\phi)/2^n = (1+\phi)/2^n$, and $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}} = \phi$ exactly. This is the fidelity correspondence.

The Porter–Thomas law thus converts a *statistical* question (does the device hit high-probability bitstrings more often than chance?) into a *physical* one (what fraction $\phi$ of runs were error-free?). The exponential shape also explains the speckle-pattern metaphor: a handful of bitstrings carry most of the probability mass, and a faithful device must reproduce that heavy-tailed structure [1][7].

> **Lemma (Variance of the XEB estimator).** *For $N$ independent samples from the depolarized mixture with fidelity $\phi$, the standard deviation of $\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{XEB}}$ scales as $\sigma \approx \sqrt{2(1+\phi^2)}/(2^n \sqrt{N}) \cdot 2^n = O(1/\sqrt{N})$, so that $N \sim 10^6$ samples resolve fidelities at the $10^{-3}$ level [8][1].*

### 4.3 Linear cross-entropy benchmarking: estimator, fidelity, and the XQUATH assumption

Boixo *et al.* [7] originally framed the benchmark in terms of the *cross-entropy difference* between the experimental distribution $\tilde{p}$ and the ideal $p$: a device "succeeds" if $\Delta H = H(\tilde{p}, p) - H(p)$ is small relative to the uniform baseline. The *linear* variant replaces $\log p(x)$ with $p(x)$ itself, yielding an estimator that is unbiased for the depolarizing fidelity and — critically — requires no logarithms of exponentially small probabilities, which would be numerically treacherous [8].

The deeper question is complexity-theoretic: **why should a classical spoofer be unable to achieve $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}} = \Omega(1)$?** Aaronson and Gunn [5] isolated the precise assumption, dubbed **XQUATH** (Linear Cross-Entropy Quantum Threshold Assumption):

> **Assumption (XQUATH).** *No polynomial-time classical algorithm, given a random $n$-qubit circuit $C$, can estimate the output probability $P_C(0^n)$ to within additive error $2^{-n}/\mathrm{poly}(n)$ with success probability appreciably above $1/2$.*

Under XQUATH, Aaronson and Gunn proved that no efficient classical algorithm can spoof XEB with $\Omega(1)$ fidelity using $\mathrm{poly}(n)$ samples [5]. The proof is a reduction: a spoofer achieving high XEB could be converted into an estimator of individual output probabilities violating XQUATH. This is the *formal* backbone of the supremacy claim — but it is an *assumption*, not a theorem, and the spoofing literature of §4.4 is best read as a sustained assault on its practical validity.

### 4.4 Classical spoofing via tensor-network contraction

The most damaging challenges to the Sycamore claim came from *tensor-network* (TN) simulators. A quantum circuit is a tensor network: each gate is a tensor, each wire an index, and the amplitude $\langle x | C | 0^n \rangle$ is the contraction of the network with fixed boundary indices. The contraction cost is exponential in the *treewidth* of the network's line graph, which for a depth-$d$ circuit on a 2D lattice scales roughly as $2^{O(d\sqrt{n})}$ — still exponential, but with a far smaller constant than full state-vector simulation ($2^n$) for the shallow circuits used in supremacy experiments [2][14].

Three milestones define the spoofing arc:

1. **IBM's rebuttal (2019).** Pednault *et al.* [14] argued that a TN-based Schrödinger–Feynman hybrid with secondary-storage checkpointing could simulate the 53-qubit, 20-cycle Sycamore circuit in **2.5 days** on Summit — not 10,000 years — by exploiting the circuit's limited depth. Google disputed the constant factors, but the asymptotic point stood: depth, not width alone, sets the classical cost.

3. **Shallow-circuit and marginal samplers.** For circuits below a depth threshold, classical algorithms can spoof XEB *asymptotically faster* than the quantum device itself. The npj Quantum Information analysis [19] showed simulated annealing achieves an exponential classical advantage for circuits deeper than a threshold $m_{\mathrm{th}}(n) \approx 87$ for $n=53$ — paradoxically, *deeper* circuits can be easier to spoof because the output distribution approaches uniformity and local marginals suffice. Barak *et al.* [15] proved that circuits with small light cones admit efficient classical XEB spoofing, directly constraining the circuit families for which XEB is a sound benchmark.

```python
# Sketch: XEB score of a frugal-sampling spoofer (after Pan & Zhang [2])
import numpy as np

def xeb_score(probabilities, n):
    """Linear XEB fidelity from a list of ideal probabilities P_C(x_i)."""
    return (2**n) * np.mean(probabilities) - 1

# Honest noisy device: mixture of ideal and uniform, fidelity phi = 0.002
phi, n, N = 0.002, 53, 1_000_000
# E[P(x_i)] = (1 + phi) / 2^n  =>  F_XEB = phi
print("honest device F_XEB ~", phi)

# Spoofer: keep top-k bitstrings from a TN batch of 2^21 amplitudes,
# then pad with uniform draws (frugal sampling)
k = 2**14
top_p_mean = 6.0 / 2**n          # heavy tail: top bitstrings ~6x mean prob
uniform_mean = 1.0 / 2**n
mixed = (k * top_p_mean + (N - k) * uniform_mean) / N
print("spoofer F_XEB ~", round(xeb_score([mixed], n), 3))  # ~0.74
```

The lesson is structural: **XEB measures correlation, and correlation is cheaper than simulation.** Any classical algorithm that identifies the heavy tail of the Porter–Thomas distribution — even approximately — can manufacture a high XEB score without ever producing a faithful sample. This is why the verification community now treats XEB as *necessary but not sufficient* evidence of quantum advantage, and why subsequent experiments (Zuchongzhi 2.1, Zuchongzhi-3) pushed to 60–83 qubits and 24–32 cycles [12][4].

### 4.5 Noise models for superconducting qubit arrays

The fidelity a device can achieve is bounded by its *noise model*. Superconducting transmon processors suffer several distinct error channels, and the supremacy experiments modeled them with a *digital error model* [1]:

- **Decoherence.** Energy relaxation ($T_1$) and dephasing ($T_2$) during the $\sim$10–20 ns gate times and the $\sim$µs-scale measurement. Sycamore reported $T_1 \approx 16$ µs median; with $\sim$430 two-qubit gates in the 20-cycle circuit, decoherence alone contributes percent-level infidelity [1].
- **Gate errors.** Single-qubit Pauli errors $e_{1q} \approx 0.15\%$ and two-qubit errors $e_{2q} \approx 0.36\%$ per gate (Sycamore, isolated benchmarking) [1]. Under the digital error model, the predicted circuit fidelity is the product over all components:

$$\hat{\phi} \;=\; \prod_{g \in \mathcal{G}_1}(1 - e_g)\,\prod_{g \in \mathcal{G}_2}(1 - e_g)\,\prod_{q \in \mathcal{Q}}(1 - e_q),$$

where the last factor accounts for readout error $e_q \approx 3$–$4\%$ per qubit [1]. For Sycamore's 20-cycle circuit (1,113 single-qubit gates, 430 two-qubit gates, 53 readouts), this predicts $\hat{\phi} \approx 0.2\%$ — matching the measured $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}} = 0.224\%$ [1]. The agreement between the *a priori* product formula and the measured XEB was presented as evidence that errors are *uncorrelated and depolarizing-like*, the regime where XEB faithfully tracks fidelity [1][7].

## 5. Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The Sycamore measurement

Google's headline result [1]: for the 53-qubit, 20-cycle circuit (pattern ABCDCDAB, seed 0), three million samples yielded $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}} = (2.24 \pm 0.21) \times 10^{-3}$, consistent across ten circuit instances with different random seeds, and consistent with the *elided* and *patch* circuit extrapolations used to verify at classically tractable sizes [1]. The patch-circuit technique — removing a slice of two-qubit gates to split the circuit into two weakly entangled halves — allowed full classical verification at 53 qubits and reduced depth, and the measured fidelities extrapolated smoothly to the full circuit, supporting the claim that the device behaved as modeled [1].

### 5.2 The complexity-theoretic proof sketch

The formal hardness argument proceeds in two stages [9][5]:

1. **Worst-case hardness.** Exactly computing an output probability $P_C(x)$ of a random circuit is #P-hard (by a standard reduction from the permanent / Jones polynomial evaluation). Approximating it to multiplicative error remains #P-hard under plausible conjectures [9].
2. **Average-case reduction.** Bouland *et al.* [9] show that *sampling* from $P_C$ to small total-variation distance would imply the ability to *approximate* typical output probabilities, collapsing the polynomial hierarchy to the third level — considered wildly implausible. Aaronson and Gunn [5] then connect spoofing XEB to this approximation task via XQUATH.

> **Theorem (Aaronson–Gunn [5], simplified).** *Assume XQUATH. Then no $\mathrm{poly}(n)$-time classical algorithm receiving a random circuit $C$ can output $\mathrm{poly}(n)$ samples $\{x_i\}$ with $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}}] = \Omega(1)$ except with negligible probability.*

The theorem is conditional twice over: on XQUATH *and* on the circuit ensemble being sufficiently deep and unstructured that light-cone spoofing [15] does not apply.

### 5.3 The spoofing counter-results

Against this stands the empirical spoofing record, summarized as XEB-per-resource:

| Spoofing method | Circuit | Achieved $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}}$ | Classical cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pan & Zhang big-head TN [2] | Sycamore 53q/20c | **0.739** | 15 h, 60 GPUs |
| Pan, Chen & Zhang sampling [18] | Sycamore 53q/20c | $> 10^{-3}$ (full sampling) | modest cluster |
| IBM TN estimate [14] | Sycamore 53q/20c | full simulation | 2.5 days (Summit, projected) |
| Simulated annealing [19] | deep RCS ($m > m_{\mathrm{th}}$) | asymptotic advantage | sub-exponential |

### 5.4 Noise-model validation

The strongest *positive* evidence for the quantum devices is not XEB per se but the *consilience* of three independent fidelity estimates [1]:

1. **Component product** $\hat{\phi} = \prod(1-e_g)$ from isolated randomized benchmarking;
2. **Measured XEB** $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{XEB}}$ on the full circuit;
3. **Patch/elided extrapolation** from classically verifiable sub-circuits.

In Sycamore these agreed to within statistical error [1], and the same consilience was reported for Zuchongzhi 2.0/2.1 [11][12]. A classical spoofer can fake (2) but has no reason to reproduce (1) and (3) simultaneously — the agreement is evidence that the device's errors are genuinely local and depolarizing-like, the regime where the theory of §4.3 applies.

## 7. Conclusion

Linear cross-entropy benchmarking is a remarkable statistical instrument: from a million bitstrings and a supercomputer's worth of amplitude computations, it extracts a single number that — under the Porter–Thomas law and the depolarizing noise model — estimates the many-body fidelity of a 53-qubit quantum processor to within $10^{-4}$ [1][7]. Its theoretical foundations in random matrix theory and complexity theory [9][5] are deep, and the triangulation of component benchmarks, patch-circuit extrapolation, and measured XEB [1][12] constitutes genuine evidence that superconducting processors were sampling from distributions no 2019-era classical machine could reproduce.
