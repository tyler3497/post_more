# Single-Cell RNA Sequencing: Dropout Statistics, Trajectory Inference, and the Mathematics of Transcriptional Heterogeneity

## Abstract

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) promises a census of transcriptional states across tissues, yet the data are dominated by zeros: for most gene–cell pairs no molecules are detected. Whether these zeros are biological, technical dropouts demanding zero-inflated models, or the natural consequence of shallow multinomial sampling has shaped a decade of statistical methodology. This thesis unifies three threads of the field: the count statistics of dropout and the zero-inflation debate, the geometry of trajectory inference that reconstructs differentiation from static snapshots, and the kinetic modeling of RNA velocity that predicts cellular futures from spliced and unspliced reads. We derive the negative binomial and zero-inflated negative binomial likelihoods, examine Townes et al.'s multinomial argument [1], survey pseudotime methods from Monocle's minimum spanning tree [4] to partition-based graph abstraction [5], and analyze the ordinary differential equations underlying RNA velocity [2] and scVelo's dynamical model [3]. We conclude that heterogeneity is best understood as a composition of sampling noise, bursting kinetics, and manifold structure — and we identify where current models provably break.

## 1 Intro

The central datum of single-cell transcriptomics is a count matrix **X** with genes in rows and cells in columns, where entry *x_{gc}* records how many molecules of gene *g* were captured in cell *c*. Everything else — cell-type atlases, developmental trajectories, regulatory networks — is inference built on this matrix. Yet **X** is pathological by classical standards: it is *sparse* (often 80–95% zeros), *overdispersed* (variance far exceeding the mean), *heteroscedastic* across genes, and confounded by cell-specific capture efficiency. The zeros are the crux: a zero can mean the gene is genuinely silent, its mRNA was never reverse-transcribed, or the cell's library was shallow. Distinguishing these cases is the dropout problem, foundational to every downstream analysis.

This thesis argues that three mathematical stories must be told together. **First**, the statistics of the counts themselves: the negative binomial (NB) family, its zero-inflated extension (ZINB), and the multinomial re-framing that questions whether zero inflation exists at all in UMI-based data [1][6]. **Second**, the geometry of *trajectory inference*: given a static snapshot of cells at unknown stages of differentiation, recover the temporal order — pseudotime — and the branching manifold on which it lives [4][5]. **Third**, the kinetics of *RNA velocity*: exploit the unspliced/spliced distinction to estimate the time derivative of expression and predict each cell's future state [2][3].

## 2 Background

### 2.1 From molecules to counts

In droplet-based protocols, individual cells are encapsulated with barcoded beads; mRNA is captured, reverse-transcribed, and tagged with a cell barcode and a *unique molecular identifier* (UMI). After sequencing, PCR duplicates are collapsed by UMI, so *x_{gc}* counts molecules rather than reads [6]. 

The loss is the dropout. If a transcript fails to convert to cDNA or is lost in amplification, the UMI count reads zero even when the molecule existed. Chen et al. showed that UMI counts and read counts have *distinct* distributional shapes, and that the negative binomial is a good approximation for UMI counts even in heterogeneous populations [6]. Grün and van Oudenaarden, using synthetic spike-in controls, validated the noise model directly: technical variation follows a Poisson law while biological variability is overdispersed, and the Gamma–Poisson mixture — exactly the negative binomial — captures the combination [7].

### 2.2 Transcriptional bursting

Behind the counts lies the two-state *telegraph model* of transcription. A promoter switches between ON and OFF states with rates *k_on* and *k_off*; while ON, transcripts are produced at rate *ν* and degrade at rate *δ*. Bursts of transcription — large *ν/k_off* burst sizes separated by silent intervals — generate the super-Poissonian variance observed in single cells. The NB distribution is not merely a convenient fit: it is the *marginal distribution* of a Gamma-distributed burst size composed with Poisson sampling. ### 2.3 The manifold hypothesis

A differentiating cell does not jump randomly through the 20,000-dimensional gene space; its state evolves along a low-dimensional *manifold* embedded in that space. Trajectory inference methods all instantiate this hypothesis differently: as a minimum spanning tree through cells [4], as diffusion distances along a Markov transition graph, or as an abstracted graph whose nodes are clusters and whose edges carry statistical evidence of connectivity [5]. 

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Count models: from Poisson to ZINB

For one gene–cell count *y* with mean *μ*, the candidate models form a hierarchy:

| Model | Parameters | Var(y) | Zero mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Poisson | *μ* | *μ* | sampling only |
| Negative binomial | *μ*, *θ* | *μ* + *μ*²/*θ* | sampling + bursting |
| Zero-inflated Poisson | *μ*, *π* | *μ*(1−*π*)(1+*πμ*) | structural zeros + sampling |
| ZINB | *μ*, *θ*, *π* | mixture | dropout zeros + NB zeros |
| Multinomial | **p**, *N_c* | *N_c p_g*(1−*p_g*) | shallow sampling |

The NB probability mass function is

```
NB(y; μ, θ) = Γ(y+θ) / (Γ(θ) · y!) · (θ/(θ+μ))^θ · (μ/(θ+μ))^y
```

and the zero-inflated negative binomial mixes a point mass at zero with weight *π*:

```
P(Y = 0) = π + (1 - π) · NB(0; μ, θ)
P(Y = y) = (1 - π) · NB(y; μ, θ),  y ≥ 1
```

Here *π* is the dropout probability — the excess zeros beyond what the NB predicts. The controversy is whether *π* is identifiable and nonzero in UMI data, or whether the zeros are fully explained by small *μ* and small library sizes *N_c* [1][6].

A minimal Python simulation makes the identifiability problem concrete:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import nbinom

def simulate_zinb(n_cells, mu, theta, pi, rng):
    """Draw n_cells counts from a ZINB(mu, theta, pi) law."""
    p = theta / (theta + mu)          # NB success prob.
    counts = nbinom.rvs(theta, p, size=n_cells, random_state=rng)
    dropout = rng.random(n_cells) < pi
    return np.where(dropout, 0, counts)

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
y = simulate_zinb(5000, mu=0.8, theta=1.5, pi=0.35, rng=rng)
zero_frac = (y == 0).mean()
# NB-implied zeros at pi=0 for these (mu, theta):
nb_zero = (theta / (theta + 0.8)) ** 1.5
print(f"observed zeros: {zero_frac:.3f}, NB-only zeros: {nb_zero:.3f}")
# The gap is the evidence for pi -- but a smaller mu explains it too.
```

The ambiguity is the point: a zero fraction near 0.6 arises from moderate dropout plus moderate expression *or* from low expression alone, so likelihood-ratio tests for *π* > 0 inherit all the fragility of the NB specification.

UMI deduplication must also collapse near-duplicate barcodes created by sequencing errors, typically within Hamming distance one:

```rust
fn collapse_umis(umis: &mut Vec<String>) -> usize {
    umis.sort_unstable();
    umis.dedup();
    let mut kept: Vec<&String> = Vec::with_capacity(umis.len());
    for u in umis.iter() {
        let dominated = kept.iter().any(|k| hamming(k, u) <= 1);
        if !dominated {
            kept.push(u);
        }
    }
    kept.len()
}

fn hamming(a: &str, b: &str) -> usize {
    a.bytes().zip(b.bytes()).filter(|(x, y)| x != y).count()
}
```

### 3.2 Trajectory inference: pseudotime geometry

Given the reduced-dimensional embedding of cells, trajectory methods differ in how they formalize "progress":

| Method | Core object | Pseudotime definition | Key assumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monocle [4] | MST over cells (ICA space) | position along longest path | tree topology |
| Diffusion pseudotime | Markov transition matrix | diffusion distance from root | ergodic random walk |
| PAGA [5] | cluster connectivity graph | random-walk distances on abstraction | partition captures manifold |
| Slingshot | cluster MST + principal curves | projection onto smooth curves | cluster lineages |
| scVelo [3] | velocity vector field | latent time from dynamical model | kinetic model correctness |

Monocle [4] selects ordering genes, reduces dimensions with independent component analysis, builds a minimum spanning tree over cells, and orders cells along the tree's longest path — its *diameter* — interpreting branches as alternative fates. PAGA [5] instead tests each pair of clusters for connectivity exceeding random expectation, building a coarse-grained graph that preserves global topology while denoising local noise.

### 3.3 RNA velocity: kinetics from a snapshot

Velocity exploits a biological clock hidden in every scRNA-seq library: unspliced (nascent) versus spliced (mature) transcripts. For one gene, let *u(t)* and *s(t)* be unspliced and spliced abundances. The standard kinetic model is

```
du/dt = α(t) - β · u        (transcription minus splicing)
ds/dt = β · u - γ · s   (splicing minus degradation)
```

RNA velocity is *v = ds/dt* [2]. In steady state, *u = (γ/β)·s* — a straight line through the origin of the (*s*, *u*) phase portrait. Cells *above* it are inducing the gene; cells *below* are repressing it. La Manno et al. estimated this line by extreme-quantile regression and extrapolated each cell's future state by aggregating velocities across genes [2].

The steady-state estimation pipeline is:

1. Quantify spliced and unspliced counts per gene and cell from intronic versus exonic reads.
2. Fit the steady-state ratio by regression restricted to extreme-quantile cells.
3. Compute per-gene velocities as signed residuals from the steady-state line.
4. Aggregate velocities across genes and project the vector field onto the low-dimensional embedding. Bergen et al. generalized this to transient dynamics: scVelo solves the full time-dependent system with an expectation–maximization scheme, infers gene-specific rates *(α, β, γ)*, and assigns each cell a *latent time* along the inferred dynamics [3].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Dropout or sampling? The zero-inflation debate

The most consequential statistical dispute in the field asks whether UMI counts need a zero-inflation component at all. Townes et al. mounted the strongest challenge: using *negative controls* — datasets in which no biological variation is expected — they showed that UMI counts follow multinomial sampling with *no* zero inflation [1]. Under the multinomial model, a cell with library size *N_c* distributes its reads across genes with probabilities **p**; zeros are simply the outcome of drawing few balls from many bins. What looks like excess zeros under a Poisson or NB null is fully explained once the library-size variation is modeled correctly.

The practical consequence is severe: log counts-per-million normalization plus highly-variable-gene selection *manufactures* false variability in dimensionality reduction [1]. Townes et al. proposed *generalized* PCA for non-normal likelihoods (GLM-PCA) and feature selection by *deviance* from a constant-expression null, both outperforming the standard workflow on ground-truth clustering benchmarks [1]. Chen et al. complement this from the NB side: even in heterogeneous populations, the negative binomial (without zero inflation) is a good approximation for UMI counts, and their NBID test — NB with independent dispersions per group — controls the false discovery rate while gaining power over hurdle-model competitors [6].

In short: read-count data genuinely exhibit bimodal dropout patterns justifying hurdle or ZINB treatment; UMI data largely do not [6]. For droplet UMI data the parsimonious account is shallow multinomial sampling plus NB overdispersion from bursting.

### 4.2 Dispersion, deviance, and the geometry of count noise

The Gamma–Poisson construction explains the NB's success: with true relative expression *λ* varying as *Gamma(θ, θ/μ)* across cells, Poisson sampling *y | λ ~ Poisson(λ)* marginalizes to *NB(μ, θ)*. Grün and van Oudenaarden validated this with spike-ins — technical noise alone is Poisson; the excess variance is biological [7]. The dispersion *θ* thus separates a technical floor from a biological ceiling.

Deviance-based feature selection exploits this decomposition. For gene *g* with total count *y_g* across cells of total library *N*, the multinomial deviance against a constant-expression null is

```
D_g = 2 · Σ_c  y_gc · log( y_gc / (N_c · y_g / N) )
```

Genes with large *D_g* vary beyond sampling noise and are retained before PCA. Because deviance is computed on *counts*, it avoids the variance-stabilization artifacts of the log-CPM workflow [1] — a rare case where the principled choice is also the cheaper one.

### 4.3 From pseudotime to graph abstraction

Monocle's MST [4] is elegant but brittle: one noisy edge can reroute the longest path, and the method commits to a tree topology a priori. Diffusion pseudotime softens this with a random walk — pseudotime as expected hitting time from a root cell — but requires the user to nominate the root. PAGA [5] *coarse-grains* first, testing cluster pairs for connectivity beyond chance under a configuration-model null; the abstracted graph preserves topology at the resolution the data support and scales to one million neurons [5].

A functional view of MST-based ordering clarifies what "longest path" means computationally:

```haskell
-- Longest path in a tree via two breadth-first searches.
-- For Monocle-style pseudotime, edge weights are Euclidean
distances in ICA space.
type Cell = Int
type Graph = [(Cell, Cell, Double)]

bfsFarthest :: Graph -> Cell -> (Cell, Double)
bfsFarthest g root = undefined  -- Dijkstra on tree = linear scan

treeDiameter :: Graph -> Cell -> (Cell, Cell, Double)
treeDiameter g seed =
  let (a, _)  = bfsFarthest g seed
      (b, d)  = bfsFarthest g a
  in (a, b, d)

-- Pseudotime of each cell: distance from endpoint a along the
-- diameter path, normalized to the unit interval.
pseudotime :: Graph -> Cell -> [(Cell, Double)]
pseudotime g seed =
  let (a, b, d) = treeDiameter g seed
  in [(c, dist g a c / d) | c <- cells g]
```

Normalizing to the unit interval is a convention: pseudotime has no units until anchored to real time.

### 4.4 RNA velocity and dynamical splicing kinetics

The steady-state velocity model [2] is a brilliant hack: it extracts dynamics from a single snapshot by reading the *phase portrait*. But its assumptions — a common splicing rate, observation of the full induction/repression cycle, steady-state extremes actually present in the data — are routinely violated. Genes with multiple kinetic regimes (*MURK* genes) produce distorted or even sign-flipped velocities. scVelo's dynamical model [3] addresses this by parametrizing the transcription rate as a switch: *α(t) = α_on* during induction, *α_off ≈ 0* during repression, with closed-form solutions for *u(t)* and *s(t)* in each regime:

```
u(t) = u_0·e^{-βτ} + (α/β)(1 - e^{-βτ})
s(t) = s_0·e^{-γτ} + (α/γ)(1 - e^{-γτ})
       + α(β-γ)^{-1}(e^{-γτ} - e^{-βτ})   [β≠γ]
```

An EM algorithm alternates between assigning cells to regimes and latent times (E-step) and maximizing the likelihood over *(α, β, γ)* (M-step), yielding a per-cell *latent time* and gene-specific kinetic rates [3]. The price is identifiability: latent time is defined only up to monotone reparametrization, and the absolute timescale must come from external calibration.

### 4.5 Normalization hazards and false variability

A recurring pathology corrupts atlases silently: the log-CPM transform *log(1 + 10^6 · y_gc / N_c)* plus highly-variable-gene selection and PCA is the field's default, yet under the null of no biological variation it still produces well-separated clusters [1]. The mechanism is Jensen's inequality amplified by library-size variation. Deviance residuals from the multinomial null, or Pearson residuals from an NB GLM, are the principled replacements [1][6].

## 5 Empirical/Proofs

We now state two results that sharpen the methodological discussion, then report the empirical record.

> Theorem: (Multinomial zero consistency.) Under multinomial sampling with cell library sizes *N_c* and gene probabilities *p_g*, the expected zero fraction for gene *g* is *E[z_g] = mean_c (1 − p_g)^{N_c}*, with no zero-inflation parameter. Any excess zeros beyond this expectation refute the pure-sampling model.
>
> Proof: Conditional on *N_c*, *y_gc ~ Binomial(N_c, p_g)* with zero probability *(1 − p_g)^{N_c}*; averaging over cells gives the expectation. Townes et al.'s negative controls fail to reject this null against a zero-inflated alternative [1].

> Theorem: (Velocity scale non-identifiability.) In the steady-state RNA velocity model, the degradation rate *γ* and the velocity *v = βu − γs* are identifiable only up to a per-gene positive scale factor; the *sign* of *v* is identifiable provided the extreme-quantile cells truly sample the steady states.
>
> Proof: Rescaling time *t → t/κ* multiplies *(α, β, γ)* by *κ* and *v* by *κ* while leaving all observed *(u, s)* pairs unchanged, so absolute rates are unidentifiable from a snapshot. The sign of *v* depends only on whether *(u, s)* lies above or below the steady-state line *u = (γ/β)s*, which is fixed by the extreme quantiles [2]. If those extremes are missing — e.g., a purely transient population — the line is misestimated and signs flip [3].

Empirically, the parsimonious models win. Chen et al.'s NBID test controlled FDR at nominal levels on UMI data where hurdle models were miscalibrated, gaining power via per-group dispersions [6]. In trajectory inference, PAGA-style graph abstraction dominates large branching datasets while MST methods stay competitive on small linear ones [4][5]. For velocity, scVelo's dynamical mode recovers correct directionality on pancreatic endocrinogenesis and neurogenesis where the steady-state model fails [3].

The EM iteration at the heart of scVelo can be specified rigorously. A TLA+ sketch of the convergence contract:

```tla+
----------------------------- MODULE ScVeloEM -----------------------------
EXTENDS Reals, Sequences
CONSTANTS Genes, Cells, MaxIter, Tol
VARIABLES rates, assignment, latentTime, iter, converged

TypeOK ==
  /\ rates \in [Genes -> [alpha: Real, beta: Real, gamma: Real]]
  /\ assignment \in [Cells -> [Genes -> {"on", "off"}]]
  /\ latentTime \in [Cells -> [Genes -> Real]]
  /\ iter \in 0..MaxIter

Init ==
  /\ rates \in [Genes -> [alpha: Real, beta: Real, gamma: Real]]
  /\ iter = 0 /\ converged = FALSE

EStep ==  \* assign regimes + latent times maximizing likelihood
  /\ assignment' \in [Cells -> [Genes -> {"on", "off"}]]
  /\ latentTime' \in [Cells -> [Genes -> Real]]
  /\ UNCHANGED <<rates, iter, converged>>

MStep ==   \* closed-form rate updates given assignments
  /\ rates' \in [Genes -> [alpha: Real, beta: Real, gamma: Real]]
  /\ iter' = iter + 1
  /\ UNCHANGED <<assignment, latentTime, converged>>

Next == EStep \/ MStep
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<rates, assignment, latentTime, iter, converged>>
=============================================================================
```

The specification exposes the real guarantee: EM monotonically increases the observed-data likelihood, so convergence is to a *local* optimum — initialization is load-bearing.

## 6 Limitations

No current framework escapes all of the following:

- **Dropout identifiability.** The ZINB parameter *π* is weakly identified at small *μ*; negative controls [1] suggest it is unnecessary for droplet UMI data, though the debate continues for full-length protocols.
- **Pseudotime directionality.** Tree- and graph-based methods order cells but cannot orient time without external anchors [4][5]; branch *existence* is usually inferred, not tested.
- **Velocity misspecification.** The steady-state model fails on transient populations and multi-rate genes; the dynamical model repairs this at the cost of local optima and uncalibrated timescales [2][3].
- **Ambient RNA and doublets.** Soup contamination and doublets create phantom intermediate states that trajectory methods wrongly order; neither count models nor velocity address them.
- **Batch as biology.** Integration can erase real differences or preserve technical ones; trajectory inference inherits whichever error was made.
- **Bursting versus regulation.** Differential dispersion is interpretable via the telegraph model, but DE tests overwhelmingly target the mean.

## 7 Conclusion

Transcriptional heterogeneity decomposes into three layers demanding three mathematics. *Sampling noise* is handled by count models, where evidence favors NB and multinomial formulations over zero-inflated ones for UMI data [1][6][7]. *Manifold structure* is recovered by trajectory inference, from Monocle's spanning trees [4] to PAGA's abstractions [5]. *Kinetics* is accessible through RNA velocity, with scVelo's dynamical model reaching the transient regimes where biology happens [2][3]. The open problems lie where layers interact: velocity-informed inference with calibrated timescales, dispersion-aware testing grounded in bursting models, and count models admitting ambient contamination explicitly. The zeros were never the enemy — only the beginning of the likelihood.
