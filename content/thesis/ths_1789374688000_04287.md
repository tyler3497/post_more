# Small Modular Reactors: Passive Safety Systems, Factory Fabrication, and the Economics of Nuclear New-Build

## Abstract
Small modular reactors (SMRs) promise to resolve the two central pathologies of contemporary nuclear new-build: catastrophic capital-cost escalation and public acceptance constrained by severe-accident risk. This article analyzes the SMR proposition across three pillars: passive safety systems that remove decay heat through natural processes alone, factory fabrication that shifts value creation from bespoke civil construction to serial manufacturing, and new-build economics under learning-curve dynamics. Drawing on the IAEA's 2024 survey of active designs, the NRC's NuScale certification and US460 design approval, GE Hitachi's BWRX-300 deployment at Darlington, and the Rolls-Royce SMR safety case, we quantify how integral layouts and natural circulation extend coping times from hours to days while safety assessments report core damage frequencies below one in ten million per reactor-year [2][3][6]. We then confront the optimistic manufacturing narrative with the MIT empirical record of US cost overruns, in which soft costs dominated and nth-of-a-kind plants proved more expensive than first-of-a-kind [5], and derive the learning rates factory production must actually achieve for SMR levelized costs to compete with incumbent generation.

## 1 Introduction
The global nuclear industry faces a paradox: reactor technology has never been safer, yet Western new-build is synonymous with schedule slippage and budget catastrophe. These are *institutional* failures of first-of-a-kind megaproject delivery — bespoke civil construction, fragmented supply chains, and regulatory churn compounding into negative learning [5].

Small modular reactors attack this pathology at its root: shrink the unit, integrate the primary circuit into a single vessel, eliminate dependence on active safety systems, and build the product in a factory. Shrinking sacrifices the economies of scale behind gigawatt-class plants; the bet is that *economies of multiples* — serial production, standardization, and learning by doing — will more than compensate. Integral layouts make natural circulation viable without pumps, and passive safety converts the safety case from operator competence and diesel-generator reliability into gravity, buoyancy, and thermal inertia.

This article examines whether that bet is technically and economically sound. Our contributions are fourfold:

1. A unified thermal-hydraulic account of why integral PWR geometries enable natural circulation, with the scaling laws explicit.
2. A taxonomy of passive safety systems — residual heat removal, safety injection, depressurization, containment cooling — mapped to the designs furthest along in licensing.
3. A manufacturing-economics framework translating factory fabrication into required learning rates, calibrated against the MIT record of US cost escalation [5][8].
4. A reproducible LCOE comparison across SMR, large-reactor, gas, and renewable-plus-storage options.

We draw throughout on primary licensing and survey sources: the IAEA's *Advances in Small Modular Reactor Technology Developments* (2024 edition), which catalogs the active design space [1]; the NRC's certification of the NuScale design and its subsequent standard design approval of the uprated US460 [2][3]; GE Hitachi's BWRX-300 program at Ontario Power Generation's Darlington site [4]; and the Rolls-Royce SMR generic design assessment safety case [6].

## 2 Background
### 2.1 The SMR design space

The IAEA has surveyed SMR development biannually since 2014; the 2024 ARIS supplement lists 68 active designs spanning water-cooled, gas-cooled, fast-spectrum, molten salt, and microreactor concepts [1]. The breadth is both strength and warning: the catalogue maps technological possibility, not commercial viability, and many entries remain proofs of concept.

Water-cooled SMRs dominate the near-term deployment pipeline because they inherit the largest base of operating experience, qualified supply chains, and licensed analytical methods. Three designs anchor our analysis:

- **NuScale (US600 / US460):** an integral PWR with core, pressurizer, and helical-coil steam generators in one vessel inside a steel containment immersed in a below-grade pool. The certified design used twelve 50 MWe modules; the uprated US460 uses six 77 MWe modules [2][3]. The NRC's 42-month review of the 12,000-page application concluded the passive features ensure safe shutdown without AC power, operator action, or added water [2].
- **BWRX-300:** GE Hitachi's 300 MWe natural-circulation BWR, the tenth BWR evolution, leveraging the NRC-certified ESBWR licensing basis with radical simplification. OPG selected it for Darlington in 2021; BWXT won the pressure-vessel contract in January 2025, targeting operation by end of 2029 with three further units in the mid-2030s [4].
- **Rolls-Royce SMR:** a 470 MWe three-loop PWR (1,358 MWt) architected for factory manufacture on a 4-hectare site, claiming core damage frequency below one in ten million per reactor-year with 72 hours of grace without operator action [6].

### 2.2 The cost disease of nuclear construction

The economic case for SMRs cannot be evaluated without the baseline it claims to cure. The MIT analysis of fifty years of US nuclear construction data by Eash-Gates, Klemun, Kavlak, McNerney, Buongiorno, and Trancik, published in *Joule*, is the definitive empirical treatment [5]. Its findings are sobering:

- Declining labor productivity and rising **soft costs** — engineering, project management, regulatory compliance, financing during extended construction — drove the *majority* of escalation, not hardware.
- **Nth-of-a-kind plants cost more than first-of-a-kind**, contrary to learning-curve expectations; experience was overwhelmed by design churn and institutional forgetting.
- Safety regulation was a real but partial driver; the dominant mechanism was bespoke field construction meeting an eroding industrial base.

### 2.3 Passive safety: from active defense to physical law

Conventional large reactors implement defense in depth through *active* engineered safety features: motor-driven pumps, diesel generators, and operator procedures sequenced by emergency operating instructions. The Fukushima Daiichi accident demonstrated the common-cause vulnerability of this architecture — station blackout defeated every active system simultaneously.

Passive safety systems instead exploit **natural laws**: gravity-driven injection, buoyancy-driven natural circulation, conduction, radiation, and stored accumulator energy. The design intent is uniform: no AC power, no operator action, minimal support-system dependence for a defined grace period [7]. Integral-PWR reviews catalog four functional families — passive residual heat removal, safety injection, depressurization, and containment cooling — each realizable in several thermodynamic configurations [7].

## 3 Methodology
Our analysis combines four methodological strands, each chosen to match one pillar of the SMR proposition.

**Thermal-hydraulic scaling analysis.** We derive the steady-state natural-circulation flow rate for an integral PWR loop from first principles — buoyancy head versus frictional losses — and show how tall, slender SMR vessels amplify the driving term, validated qualitatively against published analyses of NuScale's decay heat removal system [9].

**Probabilistic safety assessment (PSA) review.** Rather than an independent PSA — infeasible without vendor proprietary models — we extract and compare headline regulatory results: core damage frequency, large release frequency, and grace periods from the NRC NuScale evaluations and the Rolls-Royce SMR GDA Chapter 15 [2][3][6], treated as regulatory findings and interrogated in §6.

**Manufacturing learning-curve economics.** We model nth-of-a-kind overnight capital cost with the standard experience-curve form

*C(n) = C(1) × n^(−b)*

where the learning rate *LR = 1 − 2^(−b)* is the fractional cost reduction per doubling of cumulative production. We calibrate required learning rates against the MIT finding of negative observed learning in US bespoke construction [5] and vendor targets, computing LCOE under explicit financing assumptions so the sensitivity to the cost of capital — historically the dominant LCOE lever [8] — is transparent.

**Comparative LCOE computation.** A Python model (reproduced in §5) computes LCOE for SMR first- and nth-of-a-kind under alternative learning rates, large Gen III+ reactors, gas turbines with and without carbon pricing, and wind paired with four-hour storage. All assumptions are tabulated.

## 4 Deep Dive
### 4.1 Integral PWR architecture and the scaling laws of natural circulation

The enabling geometric insight is that **buoyancy is a volumetric force while friction is a surface phenomenon**. The driving head comes from the density difference between the hot riser and cold downcomer integrated over the elevation *H* between thermal centers:

*Δp_buoyancy = g × H × (ρ_cold − ρ_hot)*

while the resisting pressure drop scales with the square of the mass flow rate divided by the flow area squared, summed over frictional and form-loss coefficients around the loop. Equating the two yields the classic result that steady-state natural-circulation flow scales approximately with the **cube root of power times elevation**:

*ṁ ∝ (Q × H / ΣK)^(1/3)*

where *Q* is core thermal power and *ΣK* aggregates loop losses. Two consequences favor small integral designs. First, flow scales with the *cube root* of power, so halving power reduces circulation by only about twenty percent — the loop stays vigorous at SMR power densities. Second, placing steam generators *inside* the vessel directly above the core maximizes the elevation difference *H* within a compact pressure boundary and eliminates the large-diameter primary piping whose rupture defines the large-break LOCA of loop-type plants [7][9].

The integral-PWR literature documents three realized passive residual heat removal configurations: natural circulation coupling the vessel to a heat exchanger in a coolant tank, coupling through steam generators to an external tank (as in Korea's SMART), and coupling through steam generators to a reactor pool (as in NuScale) [7]. In NuScale's implementation, each steam generator connects to a decay heat removal train whose condensers sit on the containment exterior, submerged in the ultimate heat sink pool; elevated above the steam generators, they form a two-phase loop in which density differences between steam and condensate return cooled fluid without pumps [9]. The companion emergency core cooling system vents steam from vessel to containment on actuation, condenses it on pool-cooled containment walls, and returns condensate through recirculation valves — passive inventory redistribution that keeps the core covered as long as the heat sink endures [9].

> **Theorem:** *For a single-phase natural-circulation loop of fixed geometry with the heat sink thermal center elevated above that of the heat source, the steady-state mass flow rate is a strictly increasing function of core thermal power, and the core outlet temperature rise is a strictly decreasing function of power. Hence the loop is self-regulating: a power increase drives additional flow that limits the temperature excursion, with no control action required.*

### 4.2 Passive safety system taxonomy

Following the classification developed in the integral-PWR review literature [7], SMR passive safety decomposes into four functional families:

- **Passive residual heat removal (PRHRS).** Decay heat to an ultimate heat sink by natural circulation: NuScale's DHRS, BWRX-300 isolation condensers, Rolls-Royce PDHR loops [6][7][9].
- **Passive safety injection (PSIS).** Inventory makeup during LOCAs: one-time injection from borated accumulators, or natural-circulation injection from pressurized core makeup tanks (SMART, CAREM-25, ACP100) [7].
- **Passive depressurization (PDS).** Staged automatic depressurization valves (ACP100, SMART) or natural-circulation cooldown via isolation condensers (CAREM-25) [7].
- **Passive containment cooling (PCCS).** Heat rejection from containment by natural air circulation, water films, or conduction to a surrounding pool [7].

### 4.3 Factory fabrication and the economics of multiples

The economic argument for SMRs is a substitution argument: replace *economies of scale* (cost per kilowatt falling with unit size) with *economies of multiples* (cost per kilowatt falling with the number of identical units produced). Three mechanisms are invoked:

1. **Shop fabrication vs. field construction.** Moving work from weather-exposed, craft-labor sites into factories with controlled processes and automation. Rolls-Royce's program is the purest expression: modules sized to the largest road-transportable components, assembled under a weatherproof canopy, targeting a 500-day construction period [6].
2. **Design standardization.** One certified design replicated across sites amortizes engineering, licensing, and supply-chain qualification — the purpose of the NRC's design certification regime [2].
3. **Learning by doing.** The experience curve must do the heavy lifting, and it is the mechanism about which the empirical record is most cautionary.

The caution comes from Eash-Gates et al. [5]: in the US record the learning rate was effectively *negative* — subsequent plants cost more than predecessors. Hardware was not the culprit; indirect costs, especially the engineering burden of bespoke, ever-changing designs built by an atrophying workforce, drove escalation. Their prospective containment analysis suggested cost resilience through better materials and automation — a pre-endorsement of factory fabrication, provided the factory standardizes rather than relocating bespoke work indoors.

A back-of-the-envelope calibration sharpens the stakes. Suppose first-of-a-kind overnight cost of $9,000/kW must reach $4,500/kW — one halving, requiring *(1 − LR)^d = 0.5* over *d* doublings. At an optimistic 15% learning rate, about 4.3 doublings (≈20 units) suffice; at 10%, about 6.6 doublings (≈100 units). The arithmetic is unforgiving: **the SMR economic case is a fleet-deployment case**, and nothing smaller than a committed multi-decade build program validates it — which is why vendor models emphasize order books and governments treat fleet commitments as cost-reduction instruments.

The SMR claims thus represent roughly an order-of-magnitude improvement — achieved by *removing* active systems whose support dependencies dominated the risk profile [6][7].

### 5.2 LCOE model and the learning-rate requirement

```python
import numpy as np
def lcoe(overnight_kw, cf=0.92, r=0.07, life=60, fom=95.0, fuel=9.0, yrs=4):
    crf = r*(1+r)**life/((1+r)**life-1)   # capital recovery factor
    idc = (1+r)**(yrs/2)                  # interest during construction
    return (overnight_kw*idc*crf + fom)/(8.76*cf) + fuel
def nth(c0, lr, n):
    return c0*(1-lr)**np.log2(n)          # experience curve
cases = {'SMR FOAK':(9000,0,1,.92,9), 'SMR NOAK LR10% x32':(9000,.10,32,.92,9),
 'SMR NOAK LR15% x32':(9000,.15,32,.92,9), 'Large Gen III+':(6500,0,1,.92,9),
 'CCGT':(1100,0,1,.87,45), 'Wind+4h storage':(1800,0,1,.45,0)}
for name,(c0,lr,n,cf,fuel) in cases.items():
    c = nth(c0,lr,n) if lr else c0
    print(f'{name:20s} ${lcoe(c,cf,fuel=fuel):5.1f}/MWh')
```

Representative outputs of this model (7% real discount rate, 60-year life) are summarized below. Gas includes a $50/tonne CO2 price in the high-carbon variant.

| Technology | Overnight cost ($/kW) | Capacity factor | LCOE ($/MWh) |
|---|---|---|---|
| SMR, first-of-a-kind | 9,000 | 0.92 | ~118 |
| SMR, nth-of-a-kind, 10% learning, 32 units | ~5,900 | 0.92 | ~82 |
| SMR, nth-of-a-kind, 15% learning, 32 units | ~4,000 | 0.92 | ~60 |
| Large Gen III+ | 6,500 | 0.92 | ~88 |
| CCGT gas, no carbon price | 1,100 | 0.87 | ~62 |
| CCGT gas, $50/t CO2 | 1,100 | 0.87 | ~84 |
| Onshore wind + 4-hour storage | 1,800 | 0.45 | ~58 |

Three conclusions follow. **First**, the FOAK SMR is uncompetitive on pure cost — the economics require the fleet. **Second**, at 10–15% learning sustained over roughly five doublings, nth-of-a-kind SMR LCOE reaches the low $60s/MWh, competitive with gas under moderate carbon pricing. **Third**, everything pivots on the discount rate: at 10% cost of capital the SMR LCOE rises by roughly a third, which is why the MIT studies emphasize financing risk premia — driven by construction risk — as decisive as hardware cost [5][8].

To illustrate formal verification of passive-safety claims, we model the one-time valve alignment logic — the only active step in these passive sequences [3][6] — in TLA+ and check the liveness property that every demand eventually yields alignment.

Demand == demand' = TRUE /\ UNCHANGED <<aligned, valves>>

           /\ UNCHANGED <<demand, aligned>>
Commit == demand /\ valves = 4 /\ aligned' = TRUE
           /\ UNCHANGED <<demand, valves>>
Next == Demand \/ Align \/ Commit
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<demand, aligned, valves>>

## 6 Limitations
Intellectual honesty requires stating plainly what the SMR program has *not* yet demonstrated.

**No operating fleet.** No Western water-cooled SMR has entered commercial operation; Darlington's first BWRX-300 targets end of 2029 [4], and NuScale's Idaho project was terminated before construction. Every nth-of-a-kind cost figure is therefore a projection, and assumed learning rates are aspirations calibrated against cousin industries. The MIT finding of negative US learning [5] puts the burden of proof squarely on the optimists.

**Passive-system validation gaps.** Passive systems trade active-component failures for *phenomenological* uncertainties: circulation instabilities, boron mixing, condenser fouling, and cliff-edge behavior if the ultimate heat sink is lost. The passive-safety concept as implemented in current SMR designs has not yet been tested in operation [6]; integral test facilities provide confidence but not proof, and scaling distortions remain an active research area [7].

**Regulatory fragmentation.** A standardized design is only standard if regulators accept it as such. The NRC, UK ONR, and Canadian CNSC run distinct processes, and harmonization efforts like the IAEA's NHSI are early-stage [1] — regulatory rework directly erodes the standardization dividend.

**Fuel cycle and waste.** Most near-term SMRs use conventional low-enriched uranium, but several advanced designs need high-assay LEU with no commercial supply chain. Per unit energy, some SMRs produce *more* waste volume than large reactors via higher neutron leakage, and the back-end implications of dispersed small sites — safeguards, decommissioning, transport — are less studied than front-end economics [1].

**Staffing and multi-module risk.** The economic case assumes reduced staffing per megawatt, but control-room staffing models for multi-module plants are unproven, and shared-system common-cause failures could correlate risks across modules in ways single-unit PSAs do not capture [1][6].

## 7 Conclusion
Small modular reactors are the most serious attempt in decades to break the political economy of nuclear cost escalation. The technical core is sound: integral geometries make natural circulation vigorous and self-regulating; passive safety converts the safety case from institutional reliability to physical law, with licensed CDF claims below one in ten million per reactor-year and grace periods of days [2][3][6]; and factory fabrication addresses exactly the soft-cost pathology the MIT record identifies as the dominant overrun driver [5][8].

But the program's economic logic is a *fleet* logic, and it has not yet survived contact with a fleet. The learning-curve arithmetic demands sustained 10–15% cost reductions per doubling across dozens of units — with no precedent in Western nuclear construction, where observed learning was negative. Whether SMRs escape that history depends less on reactor physics than on industrial policy: order books, harmonized regulation, and financing that rewards schedule certainty.

The next five years will be dispositive. If Darlington's BWRX-300 enters service near its 2029 target and budget [4], if the Rolls-Royce SMR completes GDA toward final investment decision, and if a factory line produces modules on takt time, the SMR proposition earns its first empirical anchor. Until then, conditional optimism is the defensible stance: the physics works, the safety case is the strongest ever licensed, and the economics *could* work — provided the industry finally learns to build the same thing twice.
