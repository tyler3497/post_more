# Persistent Homology and the Geometry of Data: Filtrations, Stability, Mapper, and High-Dimensional Scientific Applications

## Abstract

Topological data analysis (TDA) extracts shape — the qualitative, multi-scale organization of data — from point clouds and scalar fields via algebraic topology. This thesis develops **persistent homology**: filtrations from data (sub-level sets and Vietoris–Rips complexes), persistence modules and their decomposition into interval summands, and the encoding of persistence as barcodes and diagrams. It treats the **stability theorem** of Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, and Harer [2], bounding bottleneck distance between diagrams by the sup-norm of the underlying functions, with Vietoris–Rips stability under Gromov–Hausdorff perturbations [6]. The thesis presents the **Mapper** construction as a discrete approximation to the Reeb space, explains the persistence algorithm via boundary-matrix reduction, and surveys validated scientific applications — notably the Mapper analysis of breast-cancer expression, voting, and NBA data [4] — alongside the computational landscape of Otter et al. [5]. Limitations are discussed honestly: parameter sensitivity, the incomplete multiparameter theory, and the cost of exact computation.

## 1 Introduction

Modern scientific measurement produces data whose geometry is high-dimensional, noisy, and far from the tidy manifolds of classical statistics. Gene-expression profiles, voting records, sensor arrays, neural recordings — each arrives as a cloud of points in a space of hundreds or thousands of dimensions, and the analyst is asked to find *shape*: clusters that merge, loops that enclose, voids that partition, and the scales at which each of these features is real rather than artifactual.

Topological data analysis (TDA) answers this demand with tools that are at once classical and computational: classical, because they descend from the homology theory of Poincaré, Čech, and Vietoris; computational, because the decisive innovation was to make homology *algorithmic* and *stable*. The founding step was taken by Edelsbrunner, Letscher, and Zomorodian in 2002, who formalized **topological persistence** as a notion of simplification within a filtration: a growing complex whose topological changes are classified as feature or noise according to their *lifetime* [1].

This thesis reconstructs the theory from the ground up. Section 2 fixes the algebraic background — simplicial complexes, homology over a field, and the structure theorem for persistence modules. Section 3 describes the methodology: how filtrations are built from data, how persistence is computed, and how the Mapper algorithm compresses data into a shape-respecting graph. Section 4 dives deeply into four pillars: the persistence algorithm, the stability theorem, the Mapper/Reeb construction, and statistical summaries of persistence. Section 5 presents empirical validation — both computational benchmarks and the landmark applied studies. Section 6 confronts limitations. The thesis is written for a reader with graduate-level mathematics who wants not merely the vocabulary of TDA but its proofs, its costs, and its honest domain of applicability.

> **Thesis claim:** Persistence diagrams are not heuristic summaries. Under the stability theorem, they are *Lipschitz summaries* of data, and the Mapper graph is a principled discretization of the Reeb space. This mathematical grounding is what separates TDA from ad hoc clustering — and it is what makes the method's empirical successes reproducible rather than decorative.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Simplicial complexes and homology

A **simplicial complex** *K* on a vertex set *V* is a collection of finite subsets of *V* closed under taking subsets. A *k*-simplex σ = [v₀, …, vₖ] has *k* + 1 vertices. The chain group Cₖ(K) over a field 𝔽 (usually ℤ/2 for computation) is the 𝔽-vector space spanned by the *k*-simplices, and the boundary operator ∂ₖ: Cₖ → Cₖ₋₁ sends [v₀, …, vₖ] to the alternating sum of its faces. The **homology groups** Hₖ(K) = ker ∂ₖ / im ∂ₖ₊₁ measure *k*-dimensional holes:

| Hₖ | Detects | Example |
|----|---------|---------|
| H₀ | Connected components | β₀ = number of components |
| H₁ | Loops / tunnels | β₁ = 1 for a circle |
| H₂ | Voids / cavities | β₂ = 1 for a hollow sphere |

The Betti numbers βₖ = dim Hₖ are the classical shape invariants. The problem they pose for data analysis is their *brittleness*: a single noisy point can change β₀, and a slightly different distance threshold can create or destroy every βₖ. Persistence was invented precisely to cure this brittleness.

### 2.2 Filtrations

A **filtration** is a nested sequence of complexes indexed by the real line:

```
K₁ ⊆ K₂ ⊆ ⋯ ⊆ Kₙ = K
```

or, more generally, a functor K: (ℝ, ≤) → **Simp** from the poset ℝ to simplicial complexes. Two constructions dominate applications:

1. **Sub-level set filtrations.** Given a function *f*: X → ℝ on a space X, the sub-level sets Xₐ = {x : f(x) ≤ a} grow with *a*, and the homology of Xₐ tracks how features of *f* appear and merge. This is the Morse-theoretic picture: critical points of *f* birth and kill homology classes.

2. **Vietoris–Rips filtrations.** Given a finite metric point cloud P ⊆ ℝᵈ, the Vietoris–Rips complex VR(P, ε) contains a *k*-simplex for every (*k*+1)-tuple of points of pairwise distance ≤ ε. As ε grows from 0 to ∞, simplices enter in order of their *diameter*, and the homology of VR(P, ε) sweeps through every scale of the data.

The Rips complex is attractive because it is a **flag complex** — determined entirely by its 1-skeleton — and can be built from the pairwise distance matrix alone. Its cost is its size: the full Rips complex on *n* points has 2ⁿ simplices. Practical computation relies on *skeleton truncation* (computing only up to dimension *k* for Hₖ), *sparsification*, and the algorithmic advances surveyed in [5].

### 2.3 Persistence modules and the structure theorem

Applying Hₖ to a filtration yields a **persistence module**: a sequence of vector spaces with linear maps

```
Hₖ(K₁) → Hₖ(K₂) → ⋯ → Hₖ(Kₙ).
```

A class is **born** at the first index where it appears and **dies** where it maps to zero (or merges into an older class, following the *elder rule*). The multiset of birth–death pairs (b, d) is the **persistence diagram** Dgmₖ; drawing each pair as a horizontal interval [b, d) gives the **barcode**.

> **Theorem: (Structure theorem for persistence modules).** *A persistence module over a field that is pointwise finite-dimensional decomposes uniquely (up to order) as a direct sum of interval modules* [6].

This is the algebraic miracle on which all of TDA rests: persistence modules over ℝ, despite being infinite families of vector spaces, admit a *complete discrete invariant* — the barcode. The diagonal {(a, a)} is adjoined with infinite multiplicity so that diagrams of different cardinalities can be matched.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 The standard persistence algorithm

Computing persistence reduces to a single matrix computation. Order all simplices σ₁, …, σₘ of the filtration compatibly with their entrance times (ties broken by dimension, then arbitrarily). Build the **boundary matrix** D with D[i, j] = 1 iff σᵢ is a codimension-1 face of σⱼ. The algorithm performs left-to-right column reduction:

```python
def persistence_pairs(boundary):
    """Reduce the boundary matrix; return birth-death pairs."""
    n = len(boundary)
    R = [set(col) for col in boundary]   # columns as index sets
    low = {}                              # low[j] = lowest 1 in column j
    pairs = []
    for j in range(n):
        while R[j] and max(R[j]) in low:
            k = low[max(R[j])]
            R[j] ^= R[k]                  # symmetric difference over F2
        if R[j]:
            low[max(R[j])] = j
            pairs.append((max(R[j]), j))  # birth -> death
        else:
            pass                          # creator: birth, unpaired for now
    return pairs
```

The procedure is *O(m³)* worst-case but near-linear in practice, because reduced columns stay sparse [1]. Modern libraries (GUDHI, Ripser, Eirene, giotto-tda) implement variants — Ripser's implicit matrix reduction and cohomology computation made million-point Rips persistence routine [5].

### 3.2 Bottleneck and Wasserstein distances

To compare diagrams, fix a metric. The **bottleneck distance** between diagrams D₁, D₂ is

```
d_B(D₁, D₂) = inf_γ sup_{p ∈ D₁} ‖p − γ(p)‖_∞,
```

the infimum over all bijections γ: D₁ → D₂ (points may be matched to the diagonal), of the worst matched displacement. Points near the diagonal — short-lived features — can be matched to the diagonal at cost (d−b)/2, so noise contributes little. The **p-Wasserstein** distances d_{W,p} replace the supremum by an ℓ^p sum; the bottleneck distance is the *p* → ∞ limit [2].

### 3.3 The Mapper construction

Mapper, introduced by Singh, Mémoli, and Carlsson and popularized in [4], discretizes the **Reeb space** of a map *f*: X → Z. The pipeline:

1. Choose a **filter function** *f*: X → ℝᵈ (e.g., PCA projection, density estimate, eccentricity).
2. Cover *f*(X) ⊆ ℝᵈ with overlapping bins {U_α} (parameters: *resolution* N = number of intervals, *gain* = percent overlap).
3. For each bin, cluster the preimage *f*⁻¹(U_α) (e.g., single-linkage), producing clusters C_{α,i}.
4. Build the **nerve**: a vertex per cluster, an edge (or higher simplex) when clusters share points.

The output is a simplicial complex — in practice a graph — that summarizes the data's shape *as seen through f*. Mapper combines the coordinate-free character of clustering with the multi-scale discipline of covers, and it famously recovers stratifications invisible to PCA or k-means [3][4].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The stability theorem

The single most important theorem in TDA is the stability of persistence diagrams under perturbations of the defining function.

> **Theorem: (Stability of persistence diagrams; Cohen–Steiner, Edelsbrunner, Harer 2007).** *Let f, g: X → ℝ be tame functions on a triangulable space X. Then*
>
> ```
> d_B(Dgm(f), Dgm(g)) ≤ ‖f − g‖_∞.
> ```
> [2]

The proof proceeds by interpolating between *f* and *g*, tracking how the diagram changes as critical values cross, and showing that each crossing moves diagram points by at most the sup-norm shift — the celebrated *bottleneck stability* argument. A consequence for point clouds, standard in the applied literature, is Vietoris–Rips stability: for finite metric spaces X, Y,

```
d_B(Dgm_k(VR(X)), Dgm_k(VR(Y))) ≤ 2 · d_GH(X, Y),
```

where d_GH is the Gromov–Hausdorff distance; the factor 2 is tight for Rips, and 1 for Čech [6]. Stability is what makes persistence diagrams *statistical objects*: without it, one could not speak of convergence of empirical diagrams, confidence bands, or hypothesis tests on topological summaries.

### 4.2 Extended persistence and the elder rule

Ordinary persistence pairs each birth with a death, but classes that never die (essential classes) need the diagonal trick. **Extended persistence** refines this: it concatenates the sub-level filtration of *f* with the super-level filtration, producing a single sequence in which *every* class dies, and essential classes acquire finite coordinates encoding the global topology. The bottleneck distance extends to this setting, and the universality results of Bauer, Botnan, and Fluhr show it is, in a precise sense, the *largest* stable distance on extended diagrams — no strictly more discriminative stable metric exists.

### 4.3 Zigzag persistence and multiparameter persistence

Real data rarely arrives as a single monotone filtration. **Zigzag persistence** allows complexes to both grow and shrink,

```
K₁ ↔ K₂ ↔ ⋯ ↔ Kₙ,
```

with arrows in either direction; the interval decomposition survives, enabling the analysis of time-varying data and level-set zigzags. **Multiparameter persistence** goes further: a bifiltration indexed by ℝ² (e.g., scale × density) yields a persistence module over a 2D poset. Here the structure theorem *fails* — there is no barcode for two parameters — and the field studies coarser invariants: the rank invariant, multigraded Betti numbers, and fibered barcodes. The absence of a complete discrete invariant in the multiparameter setting is the deepest open structural problem in applied TDA.

### 4.4 Statistical summaries: landscapes, images, and kernels

A persistence diagram is a multiset, not a vector, so it cannot be fed directly into most machine-learning pipelines. Three vectorizations dominate:

- **Persistence landscapes** (Bubenik): rotate the diagram 45°, take the *k*-th largest tent function λₖ(t); the result is a function in a Banach space, with means, variances, and a CLT.
- **Persistence images**: place Gaussians on diagram points, integrate over a pixel grid — a stable, differentiable vectorization compatible with CNNs.
- **Kernels**: the persistence weighted Gaussian kernel and sliced Wasserstein kernel embed diagrams in an RKHS.

Each construction is *stable* with respect to the underlying diagram metric, so the Lipschitz chain — data → diagram → vector — is preserved end to end. This is the formal content of "topological features for machine learning," and it is why persistence summaries appear as layers in deep networks rather than as post hoc decorations.

### 4.5 What the barcode actually measures: a worked example

Consider *n* points sampled from a circle of radius 1 with Gaussian noise of σ = 0.1. In the Rips filtration:

1. At ε ≈ 0, VR has *n* components; β₀ = *n*.
2. Edges form as ε passes typical nearest-neighbor distances; components merge, each merger killing one H₀ class. By ε ≈ 0.5, a single component remains: one H₀ bar persists to ∞.
3. Triangles fill in, but a 1-cycle encircling the annulus is born when the polygon closes — H₁ gains a class with birth ≈ chord length, death ≈ the *diameter* at which the disk fills (ε ≈ 2 for the unit circle… in practice death ≈ 1.7 for dense samples).
4. Short H₁ bars from noise triangles die almost immediately.

The diagram therefore shows **one long H₁ point far from the diagonal** — the signal — amid a cloud of near-diagonal noise. This separation of scales is the entire inferential content of persistence: *persistence = significance*, made quantitative by stability.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Computational benchmarks

Otter, Porter, Tillmann, Grindrod, and Harrington benchmarked the state-of-the-art PH implementations on synthetic and real data sets, navigating the full pipeline from point cloud to diagram [5]. Their findings, which remain the standard reference:

- **Ripser** dominates Vietoris–Rips computation via implicit matrix reduction and cohomology, handling complexes with hundreds of millions of simplices.
- **GUDHI** provides the broadest construction library (Rips, Čech, alpha, witness complexes) with competitive performance.
- **DIPHA** and **Eirene** target distributed and GPU settings.
- The practical frontier is not the cubic worst case but *memory*: the number of simplices in dimension *k*+1 bounds Hₖ computation, so skeleton truncation and approximation (e.g., sparse Rips) are essential above a few thousand points.

### 5.2 Mapper on breast cancer, voting, and NBA data

The landmark applied study of Lum et al. applied Mapper (with PCA/SVD filters) to three high-dimensional data sets [4]:

| Data set | Finding beyond standard methods |
|----------|---------------------------------|
| Breast-tumor gene expression | A subgroup with *100%* survival, distinguished by a c-MYB-centered expression signature, missed by clustering |
| U.S. House voting records | Polarization dynamics and a "fragmentation index" tracking party cohesion across decades |
| NBA player performance | A refined player-role stratification (beyond positions) recovering specialists like defensive rebounders |

These results established the template for applied TDA: *the shape of the Mapper graph is the hypothesis*, and its branches, flares, and loops correspond to scientifically meaningful stratifications.

### 5.3 Proof sketch: why the Rips stability bound holds

We sketch the metric stability bound d_B(Dgm(VR(X)), Dgm(VR(Y))) ≤ 2·d_GH(X,Y) [6]:

1. A correspondence between metric spaces X, Y with distortion < δ induces maps between their Rips complexes shifting the filtration parameter by < δ (each direction), because an edge of length ≤ ε in X maps to a chain of edges of total length ≤ ε + δ in Y — the factor 2 arises from the triangle inequality applied twice, once per direction of the correspondence.
2. These shifted maps give a **δ-interleaving** of the persistence modules: V_ε → W_{ε+2δ} → V_{ε+4δ} commuting with structure maps.
3. The *isometry theorem* (persistence modules, algebraic stability) converts a δ-interleaving into a bound d_B ≤ 2δ on diagrams — matching points across the interleaving, with unmatched short bars sent to the diagonal.

The tightness of the constant 2 is witnessed by explicit two-point configurations; the Čech complex, being the nerve of balls rather than a flag complex, achieves constant 1 [6].

## 6 Limitations

Honest accounting requires stating where the theory strains:

1. **Parameter dependence.** Mapper's output depends on the filter, cover resolution, gain, and clustering. The same data yields different graphs under different lenses; the Reeb-space convergence theorems require dense sampling and Lipschitz filters, conditions rarely verifiable in practice.
2. **No complete multiparameter invariant.** As noted in §4.3, bifiltrations admit no barcode. Multiparameter persistence is an active research frontier, not a settled toolkit.
3. **Computational cost.** Exact Rips persistence is exponential in the worst case (simplex count), and even the practical near-linear regime breaks down for dense high-dimensional clouds; approximation introduces its own unquantified error.
4. **Interpretability gap.** A long bar proves a feature persists across scales — it does not prove the feature is *scientifically* meaningful. The Lum et al. breast-cancer subgroup required domain validation; topology proposes, biology disposes.
5. **Metric sensitivity.** Persistence sees only the metric. Two data sets with identical Rips diagrams can differ arbitrarily in non-metric structure, and the choice of distance (Euclidean, correlation, cosine) dominates the output more than any topological parameter.
6. **Statistical inference is young.** Confidence bands and hypothesis tests for diagrams exist, but the sampling theory of persistence — rates of convergence, minimax optimality — remains far less developed than classical nonparametric statistics.

## 7 Conclusion

Persistent homology earns its place in the scientific toolkit through a rare combination: it is **functorial** (maps of data induce maps of summaries), **stable** (small perturbations move diagrams only slightly, in bottleneck distance), **coordinate-free** (depending only on the metric, not the embedding), and **computable** (a single matrix reduction, scaled by a decade of algorithmic engineering [5]). The Mapper construction extends these virtues to exploratory analysis, producing Reeb-space approximations whose empirical record — from oncology to political science — shows it finds structure that linear and cluster-based methods miss [4].

The open frontiers are clear: a usable multiparameter theory, approximation algorithms with guarantees, and a mature inferential statistics of diagrams. But the core message of this thesis is already secure. Where classical statistics asks *what values* the data take, topological data analysis asks *what shape* the data have — and, thanks to the stability theorem, that question now admits answers that are theorems rather than impressions.
