# Double Descent and Benign Overfitting: The Neural Tangent Kernel, Ridgeless Regression, and the Failure of Classical Bias-Variance Wisdom

## Abstract

*See abstract field. (The standalone abstract of this work is stored in the post metadata and reads:)* Double descent and benign overfitting have forced a fundamental revision of the classical bias-variance trade-off. We give a unified account of interpolating learning: the double-descent risk curve of Belkin et al., the effective-rank characterization of benign overfitting in linear regression by Bartlett et al., the Neural Tangent Kernel theory of Jacot et al. that linearizes wide networks into kernel machines, and the beyond-NTK world of grokking, feature learning, and implicit regularization. We derive the ridgeless estimator as a minimum-norm interpolator, state the spectral conditions under which interpolation is provably harmless, analyze kernel-gradient-flow dynamics of infinitely wide networks, and connect epoch-wise double descent to the same spectral mechanism. We close with practical consequences for scaling laws, early stopping, and regularization, and identify the open problems that remain before theory can claim to explain deep learning as practiced.

## 1 Introduction

For the better part of a century, the **bias–variance trade-off** was the central organizing principle of statistical learning: a hypothesis class must be rich enough to express the data's structure yet restrained enough not to fit its noise. Expected risk, plotted against model complexity, was believed to trace a **U-shaped curve** — bias falling, variance rising — and the textbook corollary was blunt: *"a model with zero training error is overfit to the training data and will typically generalize poorly"* [1].

Modern practice reads like a deliberate refutation. Deep networks with millions of parameters are trained on tens of thousands of examples to **zero training error** — interpolating even deliberately corrupted labels — yet achieve state-of-the-art generalization [1][2]. The contradiction raised an uncomfortable question: *was the classical understanding wrong, or merely incomplete?*

The answer, given decisively by Belkin, Hsu, Ma, and Mandal, is that the classical picture is a **truncation** of a larger truth [1]. The U-shaped curve describes only capacities below the point at which the training data can be fit exactly. Beyond the **interpolation threshold**, a second regime opens — the *modern interpolating regime* — where risk can descend again. The unified curve is the celebrated **double-descent risk curve** [1][2]: classical descent, a sharp peak at the interpolation threshold, and a second descent into the overparameterized wilderness.

The recurring theme of this thesis: *interpolation itself is never the pathology*. The pathology, when it exists, lies in **which interpolator** the algorithm finds. Minimum-norm interpolation under a favorable data spectrum is benign; the classical fear of zero training error was a confusion between interpolation and *bad* interpolation.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 The classical bias–variance decomposition

For data $(x_i, y_i)_{i=1}^n$ drawn i.i.d. from $P$ and squared loss, the excess risk of an estimator $\hat{f}$ decomposes as

$$\mathbb{E}[(\hat{f}(x) - f^*(x))^2] = \underbrace{(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f^*(x))^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[(\hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)])^2]}_{\text{variance}},$$

with $f^*(x) = \mathbb{E}[y \mid x]$. Classical doctrine held bias monotone-decreasing and variance monotone-increasing in capacity — hence the U-shape — with complexity controlled by explicit knobs (VC dimension, ridge penalties, early stopping) and uniform convergence guaranteeing that training risk tracked test risk.

> **Theorem: (Classical uniform convergence, informal).** If $\mathcal{H}$ has Rademacher complexity $\mathcal{R}_n(\mathcal{H})$, then with high probability $\sup_{h \in \mathcal{H}} |R(h) - \hat{R}_n(h)| = O(\mathcal{R}_n(\mathcal{H}))$.

The catch: this bound is vacuous exactly where modern learning operates — when $\mathcal{H}$ interpolates, $\mathcal{R}_n(\mathcal{H})$ is order one and the bound says nothing. Worse, the *monotone variance* claim is empirically false. Neal et al. showed variance is often **unimodal** — peaking near the interpolation threshold, then falling — while bias decreases monotonically, so double descent, monotone descent, or unimodal risk all arise from the same decomposition depending on relative scales [6].

### 2.2 The ridgeless estimator and minimum-norm interpolation

In the overparameterized linear setting ($p > n$), $\min_w \|Xw - y\|_2^2$ has infinitely many solutions, and gradient descent from zero converges to the **minimum-$\ell_2$-norm interpolator** [3]:

$$\hat{w} = \arg\min_w \|w\|_2 \ \text{s.t.}\ Xw = y = X^\top (XX^\top)^{-1} y,$$

the *ridgeless* estimator — the $\lambda \downarrow 0$ limit of ridge regression. This one object is the Rosetta Stone of the modern regime: it is what gradient methods find, it admits exact risk analysis, and it generalizes verbatim to kernel machines and (via the NTK) to wide networks.

### 2.3 Interpolation is not new — but benign interpolation is

Interpolation *per se* was never absent from respectable statistics: 1-nearest-neighbors interpolates; kernel smoothers with vanishing bandwidth interpolate. What was missing was proof that interpolation could be **benign** — near-Bayes-optimal — in high-dimensional parametric models. That required two ingredients classical theory lacked: (i) *overparameterization* as enabler rather than curse, and (ii) the *implicit bias* of the optimizer selecting one interpolator among infinitely many.

## 3 Methodology

We assemble the three analytical frameworks that together explain benign interpolation, in consistent notation, and verify the central prediction numerically (§5).

**Framework A — Spectral analysis of ridgeless regression.** Gaussian linear regression $y = x^\top w^* + \varepsilon$, $\mathbb{E}[xx^\top] = \Sigma$, noise variance $\sigma^2$. Risk splits into a bias term (alignment of $w^*$ with top eigenvectors of $\Sigma$) and a variance term (spectrum $\{\lambda_i\}$). The operative quantities are the **effective ranks** [3]:

$$r_0(\Sigma) = \frac{\sum_i \lambda_i}{\lambda_1}, \qquad R_0(\Sigma) = \frac{(\sum_i \lambda_i)^2}{\sum_i \lambda_i^2}.$$

Benignity requires the *tail* spectrum to carry enough variance across enough directions — both tail effective ranks large relative to $n$ [3].

**Framework B — Kernel gradient flow and the NTK.** For $f_\theta$ trained by gradient flow on squared loss, Jacot et al. [4] showed function-space dynamics follow the **Neural Tangent Kernel** $\Theta_t(x,x') = \langle \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_t}(x), \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_t}(x') \rangle$. At infinite width (NTK parameterization), $\Theta_t \to \Theta$, deterministic and *time-independent* — training becomes kernel gradient descent. Convergence is fastest along top kernel principal components, motivating early stopping [4].

**Framework C — Empirical phenomenology.** We survey the documented signatures — the double-descent curve [1][2], epoch-wise double descent, grokking [5] — and test Frameworks A–B against them. Where they fail (grokking's delayed generalization), the missing ingredient is *feature learning*: evolution of the kernel itself.

Citations [1]–[6] are the verified sources in References: Belkin et al. (PNAS 2019) [1] and its arXiv version [2], Bartlett et al. [3], Jacot et al. [4], Power et al. [5], Neal et al. [6].

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The classical U-shaped risk curve and the interpolation threshold

The double-descent curve [1][2] *completes* rather than refutes the classical picture. Indexing models by capacity:

| Regime | Capacity vs. data | Training risk | Test risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classical | capacity $< n$ | decreasing, positive | U-shaped: bias ↓, variance ↑ |
| Interpolation threshold | capacity $\approx n$ | hits zero | **sharp peak** |
| Modern interpolating | capacity $> n$ | zero | **second descent** |

The peak is a *norm* phenomenon. Just below the threshold, the model barely fits the data — contorting to satisfy nearly as many constraints as degrees of freedom, producing wild high-norm predictors hypersensitive to noise. Just above, spare degrees of freedom permit interpolation at *small norm*, and small norm means small variance. At the threshold itself, the minimum-norm interpolant's norm explodes.

Neal et al. [6] sharpened this empirically: **bias falls monotonically** while **variance is unimodal**, peaking at the threshold. Monotone descent, double descent, and unimodal risk are one decomposition with different amplitudes — large label noise inflates the variance hump and reveals double descent; clean data submerges it [6]. No paradox: only a variance term classical theory wrongly assumed monotone.

A second axis is **epoch-wise** double descent: fixed overparameterized capacity, risk versus training time — descent, ascent as noise is fit, second descent as training converges toward the min-norm interpolator. Early stopping takes the first valley; patience reaches the better interpolator.

### 4.2 Benign overfitting in linear models: effective rank and eigenvalue decay

The sharpest results live in Gaussian linear regression [3]. With $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)$, $y = x^\top w^* + \varepsilon$, the min-norm interpolator's excess risk is

$$R(\hat{w}) - \sigma^2 = \underbrace{\|\Sigma^{1/2}(w^* - \Pi\hat{w}_{\text{bias}})\|^2}_{\text{unlearnable-tail bias}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2\,\mathbb{E}\,\mathrm{tr}[(XX^\top)^{-1}X\Sigma X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}]}_{\text{noise-fitting variance}}.$$

Bartlett et al.'s insight is a *tale of two spectra* [3]: split $\Sigma$ at cutoff $k$; the top-$k$ directions carry signal while the remaining $p-k$ must absorb label noise without contaminating prediction. This works — interpolation is *benign* — when the tail is **high-dimensional and diffuse**: many small eigenvalues, none dominant. Formally, for some $k \ll n$ [3]:

1. $r_k(\Sigma) := (\sum_{i>k}\lambda_i)/\lambda_{k+1} \gg n$ — tail effective rank far exceeds sample size, so noise spreads thinly;
2. $R_k(\Sigma) := (\sum_{i>k}\lambda_i)^2/(\sum_{i>k}\lambda_i^2) \gg n$ — no single tail direction concentrates noise.

Then the variance term vanishes *despite exact noise fitting*, and the bias term vanishes when $w^*$ aligns with top eigenvectors. **Overparameterization is essential**: unimportant directions must significantly exceed $n$ [3]. Finite-dimensional settings with $p$ growing faster than $n$ and suitable decay (e.g., $\lambda_i \asymp i^{-1}(\log i)^{-\beta}$, $\beta>1$) admit benignity; genuinely infinite-dimensional settings admit a much narrower range — an important qualification [3].

> **Theorem: (Benign overfitting, Bartlett et al. [3], informal).** In Gaussian linear regression, the minimum-norm interpolator has excess risk $o(1)$ iff (up to constants) the tail effective ranks $r_k(\Sigma)$, $R_k(\Sigma)$ both outgrow $n$ for some $k = o(n)$, with negligible signal energy outside the top-$k$ eigenspace. Isotropic covariates ($\Sigma = I$) are *not* benign without $p/n \to \infty$ fast enough.

The moral: benignity is a property of *data geometry*, not of the algorithm. Spiked spectra with thin tails overfit catastrophically; diffuse tails interpolate benignly. Representations with gently decaying spectra are precisely those that tolerate interpolation.

### 4.3 The NTK regime: linearization, kernel gradient descent, lazy training

Neural networks seem far from linear regression — until Jacot, Gabriel, and Hongler proved that, in a precise limit, they *are* linear regression [4]. For $f_\theta$ under gradient flow $\dot{\theta}_t = -\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}$ on squared loss, the *function* evolves as

$$\dot{f}_t(x) = -\sum_{i=1}^n \Theta_t(x,x_i)(f_t(x_i) - y_i), \quad \Theta_t(x,x') := \langle \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_t}(x), \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_t}(x') \rangle,$$

the **Neural Tangent Kernel** [4]. The functional cost is *convex* though the parametric cost is not — gradient flow in function space is kernel gradient descent. And in the **infinite-width limit** (NTK parameterization), $\Theta_t$ converges at initialization to a deterministic $\Theta$ and **stays constant during training** [4]: parameters barely move ("lazy training"); all action is linear combination of fixed gradient features.

Consequences:

- **Convergence.** Training converges iff the limiting NTK Gram matrix is positive definite (proven for non-polynomial activations, data on the sphere [4]). Dynamics decouple along kernel principal components — fastest along top eigenfunctions — giving a *theoretical motivation for early stopping* [4].
- **Generalization as kernel generalization.** At convergence the network equals the kernel-ridgeless solution with kernel $\Theta$; Framework A applies verbatim, with benignity governed by the *NTK spectrum* relative to the target. Double descent in networks is, in the NTK regime, double descent in kernel ridgeless regression.
- **A bridge, not the destination.** The NTK freezes features; real networks learn them. It is the *null model* of deep learning: every phenomenon it cannot explain (grokking, depth's benefits, transfer) is, by elimination, a signature of feature learning.

### 4.4 Beyond the NTK: grokking, feature learning, implicit bias

The frozen-kernel picture cracks on **grokking** [5]: small transformers on algorithmic tasks (e.g., modular division mod 97) hit ~100% training accuracy within $10^3$ steps while validation sits at chance — then, near $10^5$–$10^6$ steps, validation rockets to perfect generalization [5]. Generalization emerges *long after* interpolation.

Grokking is inexplicable lazily: fixed-kernel gradient descent converges monotonically to the ridgeless solution — no delayed phase transition exists. The leading explanations invoke **feature learning**: the network first memorizes with unstructured features, then slowly reorganizes representations (embeddings revealing the task's algebraic structure [5]) into a generalizing circuit. Weight decay accelerates grokking dramatically [5] — a competition between a fast memorizing solution and a slow, simpler generalizing one that wins under the optimizer's implicit regularization.

The broader principle is **implicit bias**: gradient methods select *specific* interpolators — min-$\ell_2$-norm for squared loss from zero init (§2.2), max-margin direction for separable logistic loss, sparsity-like biases for diagonal linear nets. Grokking is implicit bias made visible over time: the optimizer's preference ordering over interpolators is *dynamical*.

The synthesis distinguishes **lazy/kernel** training (very wide, small steps: features frozen, NTK exact) from **rich/feature-learning** training (moderate width, large steps, long horizons: kernel evolves, grokking appears). Real deep learning lives in the rich regime — which is why the NTK, for all its beauty, underpredicts practical generalization. The frontier is a dynamical theory of kernel evolution: how $\Theta_t$ learns task structure.

### 4.5 Practical consequences: scaling laws, early stopping, regularization

1. **Bigger is safer (past the threshold).** The second descent is the theoretical shadow of **scaling laws**: test loss falls as a power law in parameters, data, compute, with no U-turn. *Train the largest model affordable; regularize the optimizer, not the architecture.*
2. **Early stopping, reconsidered.** In the NTK view [4], early stopping is *spectral filtering*: flow fits top kernel components first, so stopping early discards the noisy tail. But epoch-wise double descent warns the first valley isn't optimal — with a benign spectrum, *training to convergence* beats early stopping. The spectrum, estimable from the empirical NTK, decides.
3. **Regularization design.** Uniform ridge shrinkage is crude; Bartlett [3] suggests *spectral* regularization — penalize by inverse data spectrum, or choose architectures/initializations whose NTK spectrum aligns with the task. Weight decay's role in grokking [5] shows even simple penalties have rich dynamical effects.
4. **Data geometry beats model size.** Benignity is spectral [3], so the highest-leverage intervention is often the representation: pretraining, augmentation, and architectures producing diffuse heavy-tailed spectra make interpolation safe. Foundation models are *spectrum engineers*.
5. **Label noise is the stress test.** Noise inflates the variance hump and reveals double descent; clean data hides it [6]. Noise-robust losses and sample reweighting are the operational counterparts of the theory.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 Reproducing double descent in ridgeless regression

We verify the peak at $p \approx n$ and the second descent. Setup: $n=200$, $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)$ with decay spectrum $\lambda_i = i^{-1.5}$, signal $w^*$ on top-10 eigenvectors, noise $\sigma=0.5$; min-norm interpolator for $p>n$, OLS for $p<n$.

```python
import numpy as np

def ridgeless_risk(n=200, p_grid=None, trials=50, sigma=0.5, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    risks = {}
    for p in p_grid:
        lambdas = np.arange(1, p + 1) ** -1.5   # benign decaying tail
        rp = []
        for t in range(trials):
            X = rng.normal(size=(n, p)) * np.sqrt(lambdas)
            w_star = np.zeros(p); w_star[:10] = rng.normal(size=10) / np.sqrt(lambdas[:10])
            y = X @ w_star + sigma * rng.normal(size=n)
            if p <= n:
                w_hat = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
            else:
                w_hat = X.T @ np.linalg.solve(X @ X.T, y)  # min-norm interpolator
            rp.append(float(((w_hat - w_star) ** 2 * lambdas).sum()))
        risks[p] = float(np.mean(rp))
    return risks

grid = [20, 50, 100, 150, 190, 200, 210, 250, 400, 800, 2000]
for p, r in ridgeless_risk(p_grid=grid).items():
    print(f"p={p:5d}  excess risk={r:.4f}")
```

*Expected:* risk falls to $p=100$, spikes at **$p \approx n = 200$** ($(XX^\top)^{-1}$ ill-conditioned at the threshold), then **descends monotonically** — the second descent. An isotropic spectrum weakens the second descent dramatically, confirming the *spectrum*, not mere overparameterization, drives benignity [3].

### 5.2 Proof sketch: why the peak collapses

> **Theorem: (Variance peak at the interpolation threshold, informal).** For $p>n$, variance $= \sigma^2\,\mathrm{tr}[(XX^\top)^{-1}X\Sigma X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}]$. As $p \downarrow n$, the smallest eigenvalue of $XX^\top$ → 0 (Marchenko–Pastur edge): variance explodes. As $p \to \infty$ under a benign spectrum, noise energy spreads over $p-n$ null directions and its $\Sigma$-norm vanishes.

*Sketch.* $\hat{w} = w^* + X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}\varepsilon + \text{bias}$; the noise component's $\Sigma$-norm is $\varepsilon^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}X\Sigma X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}\varepsilon$. Near $p=n$, near-singular $XX^\top$ forces large coefficients — the peak. For $p \gg n$, the min-norm solution distributes fitting load across all $p$ coordinates; with a diffuse tail, the $\Sigma$-weighted norm of the distributed fit is small [3]. The peak is a finite-size resonance; the second descent is the thermodynamic limit.

### 5.3 NTK convergence: the linearization proof in miniature

> **Theorem: (Jacot et al. [4], informal).** Under the NTK parameterization, as widths → ∞, the empirical NTK $\Theta_t^{(m)}$ converges to a deterministic $\Theta$ *uniformly in training time*, and $f_t$ follows kernel gradient flow $\dot{f}_t = -\Theta(\cdot,X)(f_t(X)-y)$.

*Sketch.* Layer preactivations are sums of $m$ weakly dependent terms; building blocks concentrate by the LLN. Parameter motion is $O(1/\sqrt{m})$ per neuron while the function moves $O(1)$ — so $\Theta_t$ varies by $O(1/\sqrt{m}) \to 0$. With $\Theta$ fixed, $f_t - y = e^{-\eta\Theta(X,X)t}(f_0 - y)$, converging iff $\Theta(X,X) \succ 0$; the limit is exactly kernel ridgeless regression — closing the loop with §4.2.

## 6 Limitations and Open Problems

1. **The finite-width gap.** The NTK is exact at infinite width; real networks are finite, and kernel evolution — where the interesting phenomena live — lacks a quantitative theory in width, depth, and learning rate.
2. **Beyond Gaussianity.** The Bartlett characterization [3] assumes (sub-)Gaussian covariates; real data have manifold structure. Benign-overfitting theory for realistic geometries is largely open.
3. **Classification.** Sharp results concern squared loss; for 0–1 loss the implicit bias is max-margin, the relevant "spectrum" is murkier, and the theory is still being written.
4. **Grokking has no complete theory.** Phenomenology [5] and partial explanations exist, but no theorem of the form "grokking occurs iff …". The dynamical competition between memorizing and generalizing solutions is not understood.
5. **Shift and adversarial fragility.** Benignity is in-distribution; min-norm interpolators can be fragile under shift or attack, where "harmless" tail directions become vulnerabilities. Depth's qualitative role [4][5] and compute-optimal allocation at the threshold remain open.

## 7 Conclusion

The classical bias–variance trade-off was not wrong; it was *myopic* — precise below the interpolation threshold, mistaking the boundary of its vision for the boundary of the world. Beyond lie three insights:

- **Double descent** [1][2][6]: risk need not rise past interpolation; the threshold peak is a norm resonance, the second descent the generic fate of min-norm interpolation.
- **Benign overfitting** [3]: interpolation is harmless exactly when the data spectrum is diffuse enough to absorb noise — a checkable property of *effective rank*.
- **The Neural Tangent Kernel** [4]: wide networks are kernel machines in disguise; their interpolation inherits linear-model benignity, while their failures (grokking [5], feature learning) mark where linearization ends and true deep learning begins.

The practical reversal of twentieth-century caution: *do not fear interpolation — fear the wrong interpolator*. Choose implicit bias deliberately, engineer the data spectrum, regularize spectrally, and let capacity grow. The U-shaped curve hangs in the museum of ideas, intact but retired — subsumed, as Belkin et al. promised [1], into a larger curve matching what practitioners always knew: the biggest models, trained longest, generalize best.
