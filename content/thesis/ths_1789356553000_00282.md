# Data Assimilation for Numerical Weather Prediction: 4D-Var, Ensemble Kalman Filters, and the Bayesian Geometry of Observations

## Abstract

Numerical weather prediction is an initial-value problem posed against a chaotic atmosphere, and its skill is bounded by the fidelity with which the initial state is estimated from billions of noisy, heterogeneous observations. This article develops the mathematical machinery of modern data assimilation as a Bayesian inverse problem: four-dimensional variational assimilation (4D-Var), which solves a large-scale optimal-control problem via the adjoint of the forecast model, and the ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF), which represents the prior distribution with an ensemble and updates it with a stochastic or deterministic analysis step. We derive the cost functions, the adjoint gradient, the Kalman gain as a minimum-variance estimator, and the covariance localization and inflation machinery that makes ensemble methods viable at operational scale. Empirical evidence from the ECMWF operational transition and from canonical low-order experiments is reviewed, and the assumptions of Gaussianity, linearized dynamics, and perfect models are examined as the principal remaining limits. The treatment closes on hybrid ensemble-variational schemes and weak-constraint formulations as the current frontier.

##1 Intro

Every weather forecast begins as a statement of uncertainty. The atmosphere is a chaotic dynamical system with a Lyapunov time of order days, so a forecast is never a single trajectory: it is an evolving probability distribution over the state space, conditioned on everything we have ever measured. **Data assimilation** is the formal procedure that performs this conditioning, and it is the single most consequential algorithmic component of any operational numerical weather prediction (NWP) centre [1].

The problem is deceptively simple to state. Let the true atmospheric state at time $t_i$ be a vector $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with $n \sim 10^8$ for a modern global model. Observations $y_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$ arrive irregularly in space and time and are related to the state by

$$y_i = \mathcal{H}_i(x_i) + \epsilon_i^o, \qquad \epsilon_i^o \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_i)$$

where $\mathcal{H}_i$ is the observation operator (which may include radiative transfer for satellite radiances, radar forward models, or simple interpolation) and $R_i$ is the observation-error covariance. A background, or prior, estimate $x^b$ with error covariance $B$ arrives from the previous forecast cycle. The assimilation task is to combine prior and observations into an *analysis* $x^a$ that optimally initializes the next forecast.

Bayes' theorem frames the entire enterprise:

$$p(x \mid y) \propto p(y \mid x)\, p(x)$$

The posterior density $p(x \mid y)$ is what we want. Under Gaussian errors, maximizing the posterior is equivalent to minimizing a weighted least-squares cost function. All of 3D-Var, 4D-Var, and the Kalman family are algorithms for computing either the maximizer of this density or an approximation to the density itself. Where they differ is in how they handle the *time dimension* — whether observations are used at a single instant (3D-Var), across a window with the full nonlinear model as a constraint (4D-Var), or sequentially with an ensemble-propagated covariance (EnKF) [6].

Two facts make this problem one of the hardest inverse problems in applied mathematics. First, the state dimension exceeds the number of observations by orders of magnitude, so the posterior lives in a vast space constrained only weakly by data. Second, the dynamics are nonlinear and chaotic, so Gaussian approximations degrade precisely in the situations — rapidly developing cyclones, convection — where forecasts matter most [2].

##2 Background

### 2.1 From optimal interpolation to variational methods

Early objective analysis used *optimal interpolation* (OI), a local statistical interpolation that computed weights from assumed error statistics. OI was superseded by three-dimensional variational assimilation (3D-Var), which posed analysis as a global minimization problem at a single time:

$$J(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}(x - x^b)^\top B^{-1}(x - x^b) + \tfrac{1}{2}(y - \mathcal{H}(x))^\top R^{-1}(y - \mathcal{H}(x))$$

The minimizer $x^a = \arg\min J$ is the maximum *a posteriori* estimate under Gaussian assumptions. 3D-Var's weakness is temporal: it ignores the model dynamics within the assimilation window, treating observations at different times as if they were simultaneous, which squanders the dynamical consistency that is the atmosphere's most informative constraint [1].

### 2.2 The Kalman filter and the Riccati equation

For linear dynamics $x_{i+1} = M_i x_i + \eta_i$ with model error $\eta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q_i)$ and linear observations, the Kalman filter gives the *exact* Bayesian solution recursively. The forecast step propagates the mean and covariance:

$$x_i^f = M_{i-1} x_{i-1}^a, \qquad P_i^f = M_{i-1} P_{i-1}^a M_{i-1}^\top + Q_{i-1}$$

and the analysis step applies the Kalman gain $K_i = P_i^f H_i^\top (H_i P_i^f H_i^\top + R_i)^{-1}$:

$$x_i^a = x_i^f + K_i (y_i - H_i x_i^f), \qquad P_i^a = (I - K_i H_i) P_i^f$$

The covariance propagation is a discrete Riccati equation. The fatal scaling is that $P$ is an $n \times n$ matrix with $n \sim 10^8$ — a hundred million squared is not storable, let alone propagatable. This single computational fact is the reason operational NWP never implemented the extended Kalman filter, and the reason both 4D-Var and EnKF were invented: they are two different strategies for avoiding explicit representation of the full covariance [5].

### 2.3 Predictability and the Lyapunov horizon

Lorenz's 1963 experiments established that infinitesimally close atmospheric states diverge exponentially, with error doubling times of roughly two days for synoptic scales. Data assimilation therefore has a sharply defined mission: produce initial conditions whose error is small enough, and whose error structure lies in directions that grow slowly enough, that the forecast remains useful out to the Lyapunov horizon. Remarkably, the observations themselves define which error directions matter: the analysis update preferentially corrects the unstable subspace, which is why *flow-dependent* error statistics — covariances that know where today's instabilities are — outperform static ones [4].

##3 Methodology

### 3.1 Four-dimensional variational assimilation

Strong-constraint 4D-Var treats the forecast model as exact and controls only the initial condition $x_0$. Given observations $y_0, \dots, y_N$ distributed over an assimilation window (typically 6 to 12 hours operationally), the cost function is [1]

$$J(x_0) = \tfrac{1}{2}(x_0 - x^b)^\top B^{-1}(x_0 - x^b) + \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{N} \big(\mathcal{H}_i(x_i) - y_i\big)^\top R_i^{-1} \big(\mathcal{H}_i(x_i) - y_i\big)$$

with the dynamical constraint $x_i = \mathcal{M}_{0 \to i}(x_0)$ imposed by the nonlinear model. Substituting the constraint converts this to an unconstrained optimization over the initial state — a problem with $10^8$ control variables.

The gradient is obtained by the **adjoint method**. Define the tangent linear model $M_{i}$ as the linearization of $\mathcal{M}$ about the trajectory, and its adjoint $M_i^\top$. Introducing Lagrange multipliers $\lambda_i$ for the constraints and eliminating them yields the backward-in-time adjoint recursion:

$$\lambda_{N+1} = 0, \qquad \lambda_i = M_i^\top \lambda_{i+1} + H_i^\top R_i^{-1}\big(\mathcal{H}_i(x_i) - y_i\big)$$

and the gradient

$$\nabla_{x_0} J = B^{-1}(x_0 - x^b) + \lambda_0$$

A single adjoint integration, costing roughly two to three times a forward run, supplies the exact gradient of a function of a hundred million variables. This is the computational miracle on which 4D-Var rests: gradient-based minimization (conjugate gradient, quasi-Newton) becomes feasible because the adjoint collapses the sensitivity of every observation to the initial condition into one backward sweep [2].

Operational 4D-Var uses the *incremental formulation*: the nonlinear trajectory is recomputed in a few *outer loops* at high resolution, while each inner-loop minimization solves a quadratic approximation with the tangent linear and adjoint models at reduced resolution and with simplified physics. This nesting, pioneered in the operational ECMWF implementation described by Rabier and colleagues, is what made 4D-Var affordable [3].

### 3.2 The ensemble Kalman filter

The EnKF sidesteps the Riccati equation by representing the prior with an ensemble of $m$ states, $m \ll n$ (typically 50 to 200 operationally). Forecast statistics are Monte Carlo estimates:

$$\bar{x}^f = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m} x_k^f, \qquad P^f \approx \frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{k=1}^{m}(x_k^f - \bar{x}^f)(x_k^f - \bar{x}^f)^\top$$

Each member is updated with perturbed observations (stochastic EnKF) or with a deterministic square-root transformation of the ensemble (ETKF, EAKF, LETKF), which avoids the extra sampling noise of perturbed observations [5]:

$$x_k^a = x_k^f + K\big(y + \epsilon_k - \mathcal{H}(x_k^f)\big), \qquad \epsilon_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R)$$

Because the covariance is carried by the ensemble, it is *flow-dependent by construction*: the ensemble naturally spans the unstable subspace of the dynamics, concentrating the analysis correction exactly where errors grow. This is the EnKF's decisive conceptual advantage over 3D-Var's static $B$.

Two practical corrections are mandatory. **Localization** damps spurious long-range correlations that arise from sampling a hundred-million-dimensional covariance with a hundred members, typically via a Schur (elementwise) product with a compactly supported correlation function such as the Gaspari–Cohn function. **Inflation** counteracts the systematic underestimation of variance caused by finite sampling and unrepresented model error, multiplicatively or additively widening the ensemble spread [4].

### 3.3 Hybrid and weak-constraint extensions

Operational centres increasingly blend the two philosophies. In hybrid ensemble-variational schemes (e.g., 4DEnVar), the static $B$ of 4D-Var is replaced or augmented by an ensemble-derived, flow-dependent covariance, so that the variational machinery inherits the ensemble's knowledge of the day's instabilities without the ensemble filter's sequential update. Weak-constraint 4D-Var relaxes the perfect-model assumption, adding a model-error term to the control vector so that the assimilation estimates a bias or forcing correction alongside the initial state [7].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 Adjoint sensitivity: seeing the atmosphere backwards

The adjoint model is more than a gradient device; it is an instrument for *sensitivity analysis*. The adjoint variable $\lambda_i$ at time $t_i$ is the sensitivity of the cost function — the total misfit to all observations in the window — to a perturbation of the state at $t_i$. Running the adjoint therefore answers the question: which features of the initial condition did the observations actually constrain?

> **Theorem:** For the quadratic incremental cost function with linearized operators, the adjoint recursion computes the exact gradient $\nabla_{x_0} J$ in one backward integration, independent of the state dimension $n$. The cost of the gradient is bounded by a small constant multiple of the cost of the forward model.

This dimension-independence is what separates 4D-Var from any method requiring explicit covariances. It is also the source of a subtle limitation: the gradient is exact only for the *linearized* problem, so the validity of 4D-Var's inner loop degrades as the assimilation window lengthens and nonlinearity accumulates. Studies of the tangent-linear approximation conclude it holds for large-scale flow over two to three days but only about 36 hours for mesoscale flow [1].

### 4.2 The geometry of the background covariance

$B$ encodes the prior's ignorance, and its structure determines what the analysis is allowed to learn. A diagonal $B$ would permit observations to correct each grid point independently, producing noisy, unbalanced analyses that the model immediately rejects as gravity-wave noise. Operational $B$ models therefore impose *balance constraints* — geostrophic and hydrostatic relationships — and spatial correlations via control-variable transforms (e.g., spectral or wavelet representations where covariances are nearly diagonal) [6].

The variational cost function has a revealing geometric reading. Each term is a squared Mahalanobis distance: the background term penalizes distance from $x^b$ in the metric defined by $B^{-1}$, and each observation term penalizes distance from $y_i$ in the metric defined by $R_i^{-1}$. The analysis is the point in state space minimizing the sum of these distances — the intersection of uncertainty ellipsoids. 4D-Var extends this geometry across time: the model dynamics transport the ellipsoids, and the adjoint transports the misfit gradients backward, so that an observation at the end of the window constrains the initial state along the unstable manifold that connects them [2].

### 4.3 Localization and the rank problem in ensemble methods

An ensemble of $m$ members spans at most an $(m-1)$-dimensional subspace. The sample covariance therefore has rank at most $m-1$, while the true forecast-error covariance is full rank. In observation space, this rank deficiency is harmless; in state space, it is catastrophic, because the analysis update can only correct errors within the ensemble subspace, and spurious correlations between distant locations — pure sampling noise — generate false corrections.

Localization restores effective rank by tapering correlations with distance.

### 4.4 Bayesian non-Gaussianity and the limits of the Gaussian ansatz

Both 4D-Var and the EnKF are, at bottom, Gaussian machines: 4D-Var finds the posterior mode under a Gaussian approximation, and the EnKF propagates Gaussianity through a linear analysis update even as the forecast ensemble explores nonlinear dynamics.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

### 5.1 The ECMWF operational transition

The canonical empirical test of 4D-Var is the ECMWF transition from 3D-Var to operational 4D-Var in November 1997. Twelve weeks of experimentation with incremental 4D-Var, using simplified physics in the inner loops, showed consistent improvement over 3D-Var; the difference in forecast scores was statistically significant, and the fast-growing components of the 4D-Var analysis errors were demonstrably smaller than their 3D-Var counterparts. In the short range, every two-to-three-week verification period was neutral or positive, with the largest gains over the Southern Hemisphere and northern midlatitude oceans — precisely the data-sparse regions where the dynamical propagation of information within the window matters most. During the FASTEX campaign, 4D-Var showed a clear advantage in cases of rapid cyclogenesis, behaving better on the unstable manifold than 3D-Var: the observations were used not merely to correct the state but to correct it *along the directions that grow* [3].

### 5.2 Optimality of the Kalman gain

> **Theorem:** Let the forecast error $e^f = x^f - x^t$ have covariance $P^f$ and the observation error $\epsilon^o$ have covariance $R$, with the two errors uncorrelated. Among all linear unbiased estimators $x^a = x^f + K(y - Hx^f)$, the choice $K = P^f H^\top(HP^fH^\top + R)^{-1}$ minimizes the trace of the analysis-error covariance $P^a$.

*Proof sketch.* Write $P^a(K) = (I - KH)P^f(I - KH)^\top + KRK^\top$. Differentiating $\mathrm{tr}\,P^a$ with respect to $K$ and setting the derivative to zero gives $-2(I - KH)P^fH^\top + 2KR = 0$, whence $K(HP^fH^\top + R) = P^fH^\top$. The Hessian is positive definite in $K$, so this stationary point is the global minimizer. The resulting minimum-variance analysis has covariance $P^a = (I - KH)P^f$, and the identity $P^a = \big((P^f)^{-1} + H^\top R^{-1} H\big)^{-1}$ exhibits the analysis precision as the sum of background and observation precisions — the information form of Bayes' rule for Gaussians [5].

### 5.3 Low-order laboratory: the Lorenz-96 experiments

Because operational systems are too expensive for controlled experiments, the field standardizes on the 40-variable Lorenz-96 model, a chaotic surrogate for midlatitude dynamics. Canonical results include:

| Experiment | Method | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Lorenz-96, 40 vars, sparse obs | Stochastic EnKF | Analysis RMSE below observation error with 20 members; diverges without inflation |
| Lorenz-96, dense obs | LETKF | Local patches of radius ~4 grid points optimal; beats global EnKF at fixed ensemble size |
| Lorenz-96, long window | 4D-Var (twin) | Converges to true trajectory when window shorter than predictability limit; multiple minima appear beyond it |
| Lorenz-63, non-Gaussian obs | Particle filter vs EnKF | EnKF biased for strongly nonlinear observation operators; particle filter exact but needs exponentially more particles |

These experiments reproduce, in miniature, every operational pathology: filter divergence from under-dispersive ensembles, the localization-radius trade-off, and the breakdown of Gaussian updates under strong nonlinearity [5][6].

### 5.4 A worked miniature: EnKF on Lorenz-96

The following Python sketch implements the stochastic EnKF analysis for a Lorenz-96 twin experiment. Note how the Kalman gain is never formed as a full matrix — it is applied through ensemble-space operations:

```python
import numpy as np

def lorenz96(x, F=8.0):
    n = x.size
    dx = np.empty(n)
    for i in range(n):
        im1 = (i - 1) % n
        im2 = (i - 2) % n
        ip1 = (i + 1) % n
        dx[i] = (x[ip1] - x[im2]) * x[im1] - x[i] + F
    return dx

def enkf_analysis(ensemble, y_obs, H, R):
    m, n = ensemble.shape
    mean = ensemble.mean(axis=0)
    X = ensemble - mean                      # anomalies, shape (m, n)
    HX = X @ H.T
    S = HX @ HX.T / (m - 1) + R              # innovation covariance
    K = (X.T @ HX.T / (m - 1)) @ np.linalg.inv(S)
    perturbed = y_obs + np.random.multivariate_normal(
        np.zeros(y_obs.size), R, size=m)
    innovation = perturbed - ensemble @ H.T
    return ensemble + innovation @ K.T
```

The same update expressed functionally, emphasizing the Bayesian structure rather than the linear algebra:

```haskell
-- | One EnKF analysis step: prior ensemble -> posterior ensemble
enkfStep :: Matrix -> Vector -> Matrix -> Matrix -> [Vector] -> [Vector]
enkfStep h r priorCov obsEns prior =
  let k = kalmanGain priorCov h r
      upd member noise =
        let innov = (obsVec noise) `sub` (h `applyTo` member)
        in member `add` (k `applyTo` innov)
  in zipWith upd prior (sampleNoises r)
```

And a TLA+ sketch of the forecast–analysis cycle, capturing the temporal logic that 4D-Var and the EnKF both implement — observations constrain the state, the model propagates it, and the cycle repeats:

```tla+
------------------------------ MODULE Assimilation ------------------------------
EXTENDS Reals, Sequences
CONSTANTS N, StateSpace, ObsSpace
VARIABLES x, cycle

Init == x \in StateSpace /\ cycle = 0

Forecast(x) == { s \in StateSpace : Dist(s, Model(x)) < epsModel }

Analyze(xf, y) ==
  LET K == KalmanGain(PriorCov(xf), H, R)
  IN  xf + K * (y - H(xf))

Next == \E y \in ObsSpace :
          /\ x' \in Forecast(Analyze(x, y))
          /\ cycle' = cycle + 1

Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<x, cycle>>
=================================================================================
```

A Rust fragment shows how the adjoint backward sweep — the heart of 4D-Var — composes tangent-linear operators in reverse:

```rust
/// Adjoint (backward) sweep: lambda accumulates observation misfit
/// sensitivities from the end of the window back to t0.
fn adjoint_sweep(
    trajectory: &[State],
    innovations: &[Vector],
    r_inv: &[Matrix],
) -> Vector {
    let mut lambda = Vector::zeros(DIM);
    for t in (0..trajectory.len()).rev() {
        let h_t = tangent_linear_obs(&trajectory[t]);
        lambda = tangent_linear_model_adjoint(&trajectory[t], lambda)
            + h_t.transpose() * (r_inv[t] * &innovations[t]);
    }
    lambda // == B^{-1}(x0 - xb) contribution added by caller
}
```

##6 Limitations

1. **Gaussianity.** Both methods assume Gaussian errors at the update step. For bounded, skewed variables (humidity, hydrometeors) and for convective scales, this assumption is structurally wrong and produces analyses that violate physical bounds [6].
2. **Linearity of the inner loop.** 4D-Var's adjoint gradient is exact only for the linearized problem; beyond the tangent-linear validity horizon (two to three days for synoptic scales, far less for mesoscale), the minimization can converge to dynamically inconsistent states [1].
3. **The perfect-model fiction.** Strong-constraint 4D-Var assumes the model is exact. Model error — from unresolved physics, discretization, and parameter error — is often the dominant error source, and weak-constraint formulations that estimate it remain expensive and underdetermined [7].
4. **Observation-error correlations.** Operational systems routinely assume $R$ is diagonal for computational convenience, discarding known spatial correlations in satellite radiances. This misspecification is partially compensated by inflating variances or thinning observations, both of which discard information [3].
5. **Rank and sampling.** The EnKF's $(m-1)$-dimensional subspace cannot represent the full error covariance; localization and inflation are effective but theoretically inelegant patches, and their tuning interacts with model error in ways that are still poorly understood [4][5].
6. **Cost.** A 4D-Var cycle requires multiple high-resolution nonlinear trajectories plus tangent-linear and adjoint integrations at reduced resolution; ensemble systems require $m$ full nonlinear forecasts. Operational assimilation consumes a substantial fraction of the world's weather-computing budget.

##7 Conclusion

Data assimilation is Bayesian inference executed at planetary scale under chaotic dynamics. 4D-Var and the ensemble Kalman filter represent the two great answers to the covariance problem: avoid the covariance entirely by exploiting the adjoint structure of the dynamics, or approximate it stochastically with an ensemble that discovers the unstable subspace on its own. The ECMWF operational record shows that the variational route, implemented incrementally with careful attention to the inner-loop physics, delivered a step change in forecast skill; the ensemble route delivers flow-dependent uncertainty estimates that variational methods must borrow through hybridization [3][4].

The frontier is defined by the assumptions both methods share. Relaxing Gaussianity without succumbing to the curse of dimensionality, accounting for model error inside the assimilation rather than outside it, and correctly specifying correlated observation errors are the problems on which the next decade of progress depends. The Bayesian geometry is clear — posterior precision is the sum of background and observation precisions, transported by the dynamics. The challenge is computational: to realize that geometry faithfully for a hundred million variables, every six hours, forever [6][7].
