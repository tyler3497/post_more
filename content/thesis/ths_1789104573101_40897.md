# Provenance Semirings and the Algebraic Theory of Databases: From Relational Algebra to Why-Provenance

## Abstract

The question *where did this answer come from* sits at the center of data provenance: when a query returns a tuple, which input tuples justify its presence, and in what combination? Early answers — *lineage* in data warehousing [7], and the *why-* and *where-provenance* of Buneman, Khanna, and Tan [2] — shared an uncomfortable amount of machinery with the annotation bookkeeping already needed for *incomplete* databases (c-tables), *probabilistic* databases, and plain *bag semantics*. Green, Karvounarakis, and Tannen's 2007 "Provenance Semirings" paper [1] observed that these four computations are literally the same algorithm, provided the annotations live in a *commutative semiring* (K, +, ·, 0, 1). Addition of annotations models *alternative* derivations (union, projection); multiplication models *joint* derivations (join); the expected relational-algebra identities *force* the semiring laws as a theorem, not a design choice. From this flows a comprehensive theory: *K-relations*, the *provenance polynomial* semiring N[X] as the most general annotation, the universal property that every valuation v: X → K extends to a unique homomorphism Eval_v: N[X] → K (so provenance computed once symbolically specializes to lineage, bag multiplicity, or probability "for free"), formal power series N∞[[X]] for recursive datalog [4], and implemented systems such as ProvSQL [3]. This thesis reconstructs the theory from first principles, works a full example from annotated input to evaluated output, sketches the key proofs, and maps the framework's boundaries: negation, where-provenance at cell granularity, aggregates, and the cost of symbolic annotation.

## 1. Introduction

Relational databases were designed to forget. The relational algebra maps input relations to output relations, and the classical semantics records only the *extents*: which tuples are in, which are out. But modern database practice keeps asking for the *history*: a data analyst debugging an unexpected aggregate, a security auditor tracing a leaked value, a scientist reproducing a curated result all need to know not just *that* a tuple appears in the answer, but *why*. This is **data provenance** — metadata describing data derivation through a query — and by 2007 the literature had four distinct answers to the same question.

The first answer was **lineage** [7]: Cui, Widom, and Wiener's technique for tracing view tuples in a data warehousing environment back to the source tuples that contributed to them. The second was **why-provenance** [2]: Buneman, Khanna, and Tan defined, for a query *Q* and output tuple *t*, the set of *witnesses* — subsets of the input database sufficient to guarantee that *t* appears in *Q(D)* — and distinguished *minimal* witnesses as the most informative explanation. They also introduced **where-provenance** [2]: not which tuples justify an output, but which *cells* (attribute values) an output value was copied from, at the finer granularity of attribute positions.

Meanwhile, entirely separately, the communities studying **incomplete information** and **probabilistic databases** had developed their own annotation machinery. Imieliński and Lipski's *c-tables* annotate tuples with Boolean conditions describing the possible worlds in which the tuple exists; probabilistic databases annotate tuples with probabilities or event expressions and compute output probabilities from input ones [5]. Even mundane **bag (multiset) semantics** annotates every tuple with a natural-number multiplicity. Green and Tannen [5] had already shown that query answering over incomplete and probabilistic databases shares fundamental algebraic structure.

The decisive move of Green, Karvounarakis, and Tannen [1] was to notice that the *calculations with annotations* in all four settings are "strikingly similar," and to ask what algebraic structure makes them identical. The answer: a **commutative semiring**. Union and projection combine annotations of *alternative* derivations — *addition*; join combines annotations of inputs used *jointly* — *multiplication*. A decade of follow-up work surveyed by Senellart [3], from query optimization to security lattices to the ProvSQL system, is the working-out of this single idea.

> **Thesis statement.** Commutative semirings are the canonical algebraic structure for tuple annotations in positive relational algebra: the semiring laws are forced by the expected query identities; the polynomial semiring N[X] is the universal, most-informative annotation; and every concrete provenance application — lineage, why-provenance, probabilities, multiplicities, access control — is obtained by homomorphic evaluation of N[X] into a specific semiring.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The provenance landscape before 2007

Three independent traditions converged on the annotation idea. **Lineage** [7] records, for each output tuple of a view, the source tuples it was derived from — developed for data warehousing, where explaining a materialized view matters operationally. **Why- and where-provenance** [2] gave the first formal *characterization*: why-provenance is a set of *witness* input subsets, each sufficient to reproduce the output tuple; where-provenance is the set of input *cells* an output value was copied from, including indirect copying through join equalities [7]. **Incomplete and probabilistic models** [5] annotate tuples with conditions or probabilities and propagate them through queries.

What all three traditions lacked was a shared *algebra*: each propagated its annotations with ad hoc rules, and relationships between the models were proved one pair at a time.

### 2.2 Semirings, briefly

A **commutative semiring** is a structure (K, +, ·, 0, 1) such that (K, +, 0) is a commutative monoid, (K, ·, 1) is a commutative monoid, multiplication distributes over addition, and 0 annihilates: 0 · a = 0 for all a [1]. Familiar examples include the naturals (N, +, ·, 0, 1), the Booleans (B, ∨, ∧, false, true), and the *tropical* semiring (N ∪ {∞}, min, +, ∞, 0) used for shortest paths. The definition is deliberately weak — no subtraction — because positive-query provenance never subtracts: derivations only accumulate.

### 2.3 Annotated relations and the propagation intuition

A **K-relation** is a function from the set of all tuples to K with finite support: each tuple *t* carries an annotation *R(t) ∈ K*, and only finitely many tuples carry nonzero annotations [1]. Set semantics is K = B; bag semantics is K = N (annotation = multiplicity); c-tables are K = PosBool(X), positive Boolean formulas.

The propagation rules are then:

| RA operator | Annotation rule | Provenance reading |
|---|---|---|
| Selection σ_P(R) | keep R(t) if P(t), else 0 | filtering preserves annotations |
| Projection π_A(R) | Σ_{t: π_A(t)=u} R(t) | *alternative* derivations add |
| Union R ∪ S | R(t) + S(t) | *alternative* sources add |
| Join R ⋈ S | Σ_{t1⋈t2=t} R(t1) · S(t2) | *joint* use multiplies |
| Difference R − S | needs negation (see §4.4) | not positive — needs m-semirings |

The table tells the story: `+` is *or*, `·` is *and*.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 K-relations and positive relational algebra

The methodology of [1] is to *parameterize* the semantics of the positive relational algebra RA⁺ (selection, projection, union, join — no difference) by a commutative semiring K, defining the K-relations semantics as in the table above. The first technical result, Proposition 3.4 of [1], is that the laws of commutative semirings are **forced** by expected RA identities: union identities force `+` to be associative, commutative, with identity 0; join identities force the same for `·` with identity 1; distributivity of join over union forces the distributive law. If you want union to behave like union and join to behave like join, your annotations *must* form a commutative semiring — no alternative algebra is hiding in the wings.

### 3.2 The provenance polynomials N[X]

The **provenance polynomial semiring** N[X] consists of polynomials with natural-number coefficients in the variables X (one variable per input tuple), with ordinary polynomial addition and multiplication [1]. This is the *symbolic* representation: instead of computing a concrete annotation during query evaluation, compute a polynomial recording *how* the annotation was built. The paper's running example: a tuple annotated `2s² + rs` was "computed three ways: two use input `s` twice, one uses `r` and `s`" [1].

> **Theorem (Universal property of N[X], Prop. 4.2 of [1]).** *For any commutative semiring K and any valuation v: X → K, there exists a unique semiring homomorphism Eval_v: N[X] → K extending v.*

*Proof sketch.* Define Eval_v on monomials by Eval_v(∏ x_i^{e_i}) = ∏ v(x_i)^{e_i} and extend linearly, interpreting n ∈ N in K by repeated addition of 1_K. Polynomial representations are unique, so this is well-defined; it preserves + and · by construction, and uniqueness follows because any homomorphism extending v is forced on monomials. ∎

This is the load-bearing theorem of the whole theory [3]. Its operational meaning: **compute provenance once, as a polynomial, and specialize for free.** Bag semantics? Evaluate in N with each variable mapped to its multiplicity. Lineage? Evaluate in B. Probabilities? Map each variable to its marginal probability and evaluate — with the standard independence caveat (§6). Trust or clearance level? Evaluate in a security lattice semiring. The engine implements one symbolic algorithm; the applications are just homomorphisms.

### 3.3 From polynomials to datalog: formal power series

Recursive queries (datalog) can generate *infinitely many* distinct derivation trees for one output tuple, so finite polynomials no longer suffice. The fix [1], revisited in [4], is the semiring N∞[[X]] of **formal power series**: possibly infinite sums over monomials with coefficients in N∞ = N ∪ {∞}. N∞[[X]] is ω-continuous — limits of increasing chains exist — which is exactly what least-fixpoint datalog semantics needs. The same pattern recurs: B[X] (Boolean polynomials) and PosBool(X) (positive Boolean expressions) appear as natural specializations for datalog provenance [4].

### 3.4 Systems methodology: provenance by rewriting

The theory suggests a systems architecture: provenance by *query rewriting*. ProvSQL [3], a PostgreSQL extension, rewrites queries over an augmented schema carrying annotations, so the engine computes the semiring operations; parametricity in the semiring lets one implementation serve lineage, why-provenance, probabilities, and more.

## 4. Deep Dive

### 4.1 The anatomy of N[X]: why polynomials are the most general annotation

Why polynomials, rather than some other symbolic structure? The universal property gives the precise answer: N[X] is the **free commutative semiring** on the generators X — it imposes *no* relations beyond the semiring laws themselves, so every other annotation scheme respecting the RA identities is a *quotient* (homomorphic image) of N[X]:

- **Bag semantics** N: identify each variable with a natural number — coefficients count derivations, like monomials merge.
- **Why-provenance**: quotient by idempotence (x² = x, x + x = x); duplicate uses and duplicate derivations collapse, leaving exactly the *sets of input tuples* that suffice — the witnesses of [2]. Formally the semiring B[X] of Boolean polynomials [4].
- **Lineage** [7]: a further coarsening — the *union* of all witnesses, the inputs appearing in *some* derivation.

Each coarsening forgets information, and the homomorphism theorem says exactly what is forgotten at each step. N[X] is the *most informative* annotation: a system storing N[X] annotations answers lineage, why-provenance, and multiplicity queries without recomputation.

### 4.2 Why-provenance, recovered algebraically

The semiring framework recovers Buneman–Khanna–Tan why-provenance [2] *syntactically*. Evaluate the N[X] provenance polynomial in the semiring whose elements are sets of *sets* of variables, with union as + and pairwise union as ·, then quotient by absorption (W₁ ∪ W₂ with W₁ ⊆ W₂ absorbs W₂) [1]. The surviving monomials are precisely the minimal witnesses. The algebraic view adds what the semantic definition lacks: a *compositional* computation, operator-by-operator during query evaluation, with absorption explaining why minimality is the natural normal form.

### 4.3 Probabilistic databases: from provenance to probabilities

The probabilistic application is where the homomorphism theorem pays off [5]. Annotate each input tuple with its marginal probability and evaluate: independent events multiply, *disjoint* alternatives add. The subtlety is that query-derived events are rarely independent or disjoint, so naive evaluation in ([0,1], +, ·) is *wrong* in general. The correct methodology [1, 5] is two-stage: compute the symbolic polynomial in N[X], then compute the probability of the *Boolean formula* it denotes (#P-hard in general, hence the literature on safe plans and read-once formulas). The semiring framework does not make probability computation easy; it *isolates* the hardness in formula evaluation while keeping query evaluation uniform.

### 4.4 Negation and the limits of positivity: m-semirings

Difference R − S breaks the framework: "t is in R and not in S" needs to *subtract* annotations, and semirings have no subtraction. Green, Karvounarakis, and Tannen [1] propose **m-semirings** — semirings with a *monus* operation ⊖ satisfying a ⊖ a = 0 and a ⊖ 0 = a — as the algebraic home of difference. The cost is honest: the universal property of N[X] does not survive negation unscathed, and non-monotone provenance [3] remains the theory's roughest edge. Where-provenance [2], tracking value-copying at cell granularity rather than tuple derivation, likewise falls outside the pure semiring picture [7].

### 4.5 Aggregates: semimodules over semirings

Aggregation is the other classical hard case: an aggregate's value depends on a *multiset* of inputs, and its provenance must explain the *value*, not just existence. The algebraic answer [3] is **semimodules**: the aggregate's domain forms a commutative monoid M on which the provenance semiring K acts (k ⊗ m scales a value by an annotation), and group-by collects (annotation, value) pairs. With the right monoid — e.g., (N, +, 0) with the evident K-action for SUM — aggregates compose with the rest of the positive algebra. The pattern is consistent: find the algebraic structure the query identities force, then compute symbolically.

## 5. Empirical Results / Proofs

### 5.1 Worked example: from annotated input to evaluated output

A tiny database with tuple identifiers as variables.

```python
# K-relation semantics over N[X]: annotations are polynomials.
from collections import defaultdict

R = {('Alice','Sales'): 'e1', ('Bob','Eng'): 'e2', ('Carol','Sales'): 'e3'}
S = {('Sales',): 'd1', ('Eng',): 'd2'}

def join(r1, r2, on):
    out = defaultdict(list)
    for t1, a1 in r1.items():
        for t2, a2 in r2.items():
            if on(t1, t2):
                out[t1 + t2].append(f"{a1}*{a2}")  # joint use: multiply
    return out

def project(rel, cols):
    out = defaultdict(list)
    for t, mons in rel.items():
        u = tuple(t[c] for c in cols)
        out[u].extend(mons)                        # alternatives: add
    return out

p = project(join(R, S, on=lambda t1, t2: t1[1] == t2[0]), cols=[0])
for u, mons in p.items():
    print(u, " <- ", " + ".join(mons))
# ('Alice',)  <-  e1*d1
# ('Carol',)  <-  e3*d1
```

Now specialize by homomorphism — one symbolic computation, three applications:

```python
val_mult = {'e1':2,'e2':1,'e3':1,'d1':3,'d2':1}            # K = N (bags)
val_bool = {k: True for k in ['e1','e2','e3','d1','d2']}   # K = B (sets)
val_prob = {'e1':0.9,'e2':0.8,'e3':0.7,'d1':1.0,'d2':1.0}  # independent probs

def ev(mons, val, add, mul):
    tot = None
    for m in mons:
        v = None
        for var in m.split('*'):
            v = val[var] if v is None else mul(v, val[var])
        tot = v if tot is None else add(tot, v)
    return tot

for u, mons in p.items():
    print(u, "bag:",      ev(mons, val_mult, lambda a,b: a+b,   lambda a,b: a*b),
             "exists:",   ev(mons, val_bool, lambda a,b: a or b, lambda a,b: a and b),
             "prob:",     ev(mons, val_prob, lambda a,b: a+b,   lambda a,b: a*b))
# ('Alice',)  bag: 6  exists: True  prob: 0.9
# ('Carol',)  bag: 6  exists: True  prob: 0.7
```

This is the homomorphism theorem as an engineering pattern — exactly how ProvSQL-style rewriting systems [3] are architected.

### 5.2 Proof sketch: query identities force the semiring laws

We sketch Proposition 3.4 of [1]: ∪ forces (a + b) + c = a + (b + c), a + b = b + a, a + 0 = a. Join's associativity/commutativity force the same for · with 1 the annotation of {⟨⟩}. Distributivity R ⋈ (S ∪ T) = (R ⋈ S) ∪ (R ⋈ T) forces a · (b + c) = a · b + a · c, and R ⋈ ∅ = ∅ forces a · 0 = 0. Each law corresponds to a query identity: any annotation algebra satisfying the identities *is* a commutative semiring. ∎

### 5.3 The semiring zoo: one algorithm, many instantiations

| Semiring K | +, ·, 0, 1 | What Eval_v computes | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| N[X] provenance polynomials | poly +, · | full derivation history | [1] |
| B[X] Boolean polynomials | ∨-like, ∧-like | why-provenance witnesses | [1], [4] |
| PosBool(X) | ∨, ∧ | lineage / c-table conditions | [4], [5] |
| N (bags) | +, · | derivation multiplicities | [1] |
| B (sets) | ∨, ∧ | tuple existence | [1] |
| Tropical (min,+) | min, + | cheapest derivation | [1] |
| Security lattice | join, meet | clearance of derived data | [3] |
| N∞[[X]] power series | series +, · | datalog provenance | [1], [4] |

In Haskell, the pattern is a one-line abstraction:

### 5.4 What the theory predicts, and what systems confirm

The theory predicts that *any* provenance-aware engine for positive RA factors into (i) a semiring-parametric evaluator and (ii) per-application homomorphisms. ProvSQL [3] confirms it: one PostgreSQL extension supports why-provenance, probabilistic evaluation, and access-control semirings by swapping the annotation domain. The KR 2022 revisiting of datalog provenance [4] confirms the infinite case, and causality-oriented work [6] — Halpern's program of defining *cause* and *responsibility* for database answers — builds on the same lineage substrate, extending the framework's reach into explanation and accountability.

## 6. Limitations

**Negation is second-class.** The semiring laws are forced by *positive* RA identities; difference needs m-semirings [1], whose monus is far less canonical, and recursive queries with negation remain largely open. Real SQL's NOT EXISTS is handled by ad hoc extensions, not the clean theory.

**Where-provenance escapes the tuple level.** The framework annotates *tuples*; where-provenance [2] tracks *cells* — which input attribute values an output value was copied from, including indirect copying through join equalities [7]. Cell-level tracking needs labeled values rather than labeled tuples, which K-relations do not provide.

**Symbolic blowup.** N[X] polynomials can grow exponentially in query size: a chain of n joins over binary relations yields up to 2ⁿ monomials. Systems cope by evaluating eagerly into coarser semirings — but the informativeness-for-tractability trade is fundamental, not an implementation artifact.

**Probabilities need more than the semiring.** Evaluating provenance polynomials in ([0,1], +, ·) is unsound without independence/disjointness; the honest pipeline keeps the symbolic formula and computes its probability separately (#P-hard in general) [5]. The semiring tells you *how to combine*; it does not tell you *when combining is valid*.

**Aggregates are partial.** The semimodule treatment [3] handles distributive aggregates (SUM, COUNT, MIN, MAX) but struggles with holistic ones (MEDIAN, MODE) and nested aggregation, where the value domain resists the K-action.

**Complexity of the infinite.** N∞[[X]] power series are elegant, but computing with infinite series requires truncation or symbolic cycle analysis [4]; the ω-continuity that makes the theory work is what makes naive evaluation diverge.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Provenance semirings did for data provenance what the relational model did for data: they replaced a zoo of special-purpose mechanisms with a single algebraic abstraction and showed the abstraction was *forced*, not chosen. The chain is tight — identities force semiring laws, polynomials are the free structure, the universal property makes N[X] the annotation from which all others derive [1] — and extensions to datalog [4], probabilistic databases [5], aggregates, and systems [3] confirm its predictive power.

The open frontier is where the algebra breaks: negation, cell-level where-provenance, holistic aggregates, and the complexity cliffs of symbolic evaluation. Each is a request for a *larger* algebra — and the methodology of [1] suggests how to look: write down the query identities you need, and read off the laws they force.
