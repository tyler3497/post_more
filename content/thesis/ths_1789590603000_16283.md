# The Impossibility of Asynchronous Consensus: the FLP Theorem, Wait-Free Hierarchies, and the Consensus Number of Shared Objects

## Abstract

*Consensus — agreement among unreliable processes — is the central coordination primitive of distributed computing, yet a landmark 1985 result by Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson proves that no deterministic protocol can guarantee it in a fully asynchronous system with even a single crash failure. This thesis reconstructs the FLP impossibility proof through its bivalency argument, maps the precise boundary of the result by examining the circumventions that practice actually uses — partial synchrony, randomization, and unreliable failure detectors — and develops the shared-memory dual of the theory: Herlihy's wait-free hierarchy, in which every object type is classified by its consensus number. We prove that atomic registers, at level one, cannot implement even a test-and-set bit wait-free, while compare-and-swap, at level infinity, is universal. Together, these results show that impossibility theorems are not dead ends but design instruments: they tell engineers exactly which assumption must be weakened to make agreement possible.*

##1 Introduction

Few results in computer science have redirected an entire field the way the 1985 paper of Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson did [1]. The **consensus problem** — getting a collection of processes, some of which may fail, to agree on a single value — underlies transaction commit in databases, leader election, state-machine replication, and, in modern dress, blockchain protocols. Intuition suggests that a single crash failure among many processes should be a minor nuisance. The FLP theorem shows that in a *fully asynchronous* system, it is fatal to any *deterministic* guarantee of termination.

The result is routinely misquoted as "consensus is impossible," which is false. What FLP actually establishes is a sharp statement about a *model*: determinism plus asynchrony plus one crash fault makes guaranteed termination unattainable. Every working consensus system in production today — Paxos-derived protocols, Raft, PBFT, Tendermint — survives precisely by stepping outside that model, most often by assuming *partial synchrony* [3][4]. Understanding exactly where the boundary lies is therefore not an academic exercise; it is the single most load-bearing piece of theory in fault-tolerant systems design.

This thesis has three aims. **First**, to present the FLP proof in full rigor through the *bivalency argument*, showing *why* the adversary always wins. **Second**, to survey the principled escapes: the partially synchronous model of Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer [3], randomized protocols with probabilistic termination, and the failure-detector abstraction that isolates the *weakest* information about failures sufficient for consensus. **Third**, to develop the shared-memory counterpart of the theory: Herlihy's classification of synchronization primitives by **consensus number** [2], which yields both impossibility results (registers cannot build a wait-free queue) and a universality result (compare-and-swap can build anything).

##2 Background

### The system model

We work with two classical models. In the **asynchronous message-passing** model, a fixed set of *n* processes communicate by sending messages; there is no bound on message delay, no bound on relative process speeds, and no synchronized clocks. Processes fail only by **crashing**: a faulty process simply stops taking steps. The FLP result assumes at most one faulty process, and remarkably, that one fault may be *fail-stop* — the weakest failure mode [1].

In the **asynchronous shared-memory** model, processes communicate through shared objects with atomic operations and may crash at any point. Here the central liveness notion is **wait-freedom**: an implementation of an object is wait-free if every operation by a nonfaulty process completes after a finite number of its own steps, regardless of the behavior — including crashes — of other processes [2]. Wait-freedom is strictly stronger than lock-freedom, which guarantees only that *some* process makes progress.

### The consensus problem

A consensus protocol must satisfy three properties:

1. **Agreement** — no two nonfaulty processes decide different values.
2. **Validity** — every decided value was proposed by some process.
3. **Termination** — every nonfaulty process eventually decides.

We consider binary consensus (values zero and one); multivalued consensus reduces to the binary case. The FLP theorem states that no deterministic protocol satisfies all three in the asynchronous model with one crash fault. Crucially, the theorem does not claim every execution runs forever — only that every protocol admits *some* admissible execution that never terminates.

### Configurations, schedules, and valency

Following [1], a **configuration** records the internal state of every process plus the multiset of messages in transit. A **step** is a pair *(p, m)*: process *p* receives message *m* (or a null message, modeling a local timeout-free action) and atomically updates its state and sends messages. A **schedule** is a finite or infinite sequence of steps; applying it to a configuration yields a run. A schedule is *admissible* if every nonfaulty process takes infinitely many steps and every message to a nonfaulty process is eventually delivered.

A configuration *C* is **0-valent** if every admissible deciding run from *C* decides zero, **1-valent** if every such run decides one, and **bivalent** if both a 0-deciding and a 1-deciding admissible run are reachable from *C*. ### The consensus number

For shared objects, Herlihy attaches to each object type *T* a **consensus number**: the largest *n* such that objects of type *T*, together with atomic registers, solve wait-free *n*-process consensus — or infinity if no such maximum exists [2]. The resulting hierarchy is striking:

| Consensus number | Representative objects |
|---|---|
| 1 | Atomic read/write registers |
| 2 | Test-and-set, fetch-and-add, swap, FIFO queue, stack |
| 2n−2 | *n*-register assignment |
| ∞ | Compare-and-swap, load-linked/store-conditional, fetch-and-cons, sticky byte, memory-to-memory move and swap |

An object is **universal** in an *n*-process system if it can implement any object wait-free, which holds exactly when its consensus number is at least *n* [2][5] — the shared-memory analogue of FLP's impossibility.

##3 Methodology

Our method is the classical one of distributed-computing theory: **formal modeling, adversarial reasoning, and reduction**. We adopt the FLP execution model of configurations and schedules, and we reason about an *adversarial scheduler* that controls the timing of every step — the standard technique for proving liveness impossibilities. For the shared-memory half, we use **reduction arguments**: to show object type *A* cannot implement type *B* wait-free among *m* processes, we show that *B* solves *m*-process consensus while *A* provably cannot [5].

We complement the pencil-and-paper proofs with three executable artifacts:

- A **TLA+** specification of binary consensus, a **Python** schedule enumerator demonstrating the critical-step lemma on a toy protocol, and **Haskell**/**Rust** sketches of the hierarchy — including a wait-free consensus implementation from compare-and-swap.

Sources are drawn from the primary literature [1][2][3][4] and standard expositions of the hierarchy [5][8], supplemented by recent work probing the boundaries of the FLP assumptions [6][7].

##4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 The Bivalency Argument, Formalized

The proof proceeds by contradiction. Assume a deterministic protocol *P* solves consensus with one crash fault. The argument has three stages.

**Stage 1: A bivalent initial configuration exists.** Consider initial configurations ordered so that adjacent configurations differ in exactly one process's input. In the all-zero configuration, validity forces a zero decision; in the all-one configuration, a one decision. Some adjacent pair must therefore straddle the boundary: one is 0-valent and the next 1-valent, or one of them is bivalent. Let the differing process be *p*; if *p* crashes immediately, survivors cannot distinguish the two configurations, so both outcomes stay reachable — bivalence. Hence a bivalent initial configuration *C₀* exists [1].

**Stage 2: The critical-step lemma.** This is the engine of the proof.

> Theorem: (FLP critical-step lemma [1]). Let *C* be bivalent and *e = (p, m)* a step applicable to *C*. Then there is a schedule *σ* not containing *e* such that *C′ = σ(C)* is bivalent.

*Proof sketch.* Let *K* be the set of configurations reachable from *C* without applying *e*, and let *D = {e(E) : E ∈ K}* be their *e*-successors. Suppose for contradiction that every configuration in *D* is univalent. Since *C* is bivalent, *D* contains both 0-valent and 1-valent configurations, so there exist *E₀, E₁ ∈ K* that are *neighbors* — *E₁ = e′(E₀)* for a single step *e′* — with *e(E₀)* 0-valent and *e(E₁)* 1-valent. Two cases:

- *Case A: e and e′ involve different processes.* Then *e* and *e′* commute: *e(e′(E₀)) = e′(e(E₀))*. The left side is 1-valent, the right side 0-valent — contradiction.
- *Case B: same process p.* Run a finite deciding schedule *σ* from *E₀* with *p* crashed; *A = σ(E₀)* is univalent, say 0-valent. Commutativity of *σ* with *e* and *e′* then forces *e(E₁)* to be both 0-valent and 1-valent — contradiction.

Therefore some configuration in *D* is bivalent, and it is reachable from *C* without applying *e*. ∎

**Stage 3: The adversary's infinite run.** From *C₀*, repeatedly apply the lemma to avoid each applicable step in turn; the resulting infinite admissible schedule visits only bivalent configurations and never decides — contradicting termination. ∎

The proof's deeper message is about *indistinguishability*: in an asynchronous system, no process can distinguish a crashed peer from a merely slow one, and the adversary exploits exactly this ambiguity to defer every decisive step [7].

```tla+
---- MODULE BinaryConsensus ----
EXTENDS Naturals, FiniteSets
CONSTANT Procs
VARIABLES proposed, decided
TypeOK == proposed \in [Procs -> {0,1}] /\ decided \in [Procs -> {-1,0,1}]
Agreement == \A p,q \in Procs : decided[p] /= -1 /\ decided[q] /= -1
                                   => decided[p] = decided[q]
Validity  == \A p \in Procs : decided[p] /= -1 => decided[p] \in {proposed[q] : q \in Procs}
Init == proposed \in [Procs -> {0,1}] /\ decided = [p \in Procs |-> -1]
Decide(p) == decided[p] = -1 /\ decided' = [decided EXCEPT ![p] = proposed[p]]
Next == \E p \in Procs : Decide(p)
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<proposed, decided>>
====
```

### 4.2 Partial Synchrony and Randomization: Circumventing the Result

FLP constrains only the *fully asynchronous deterministic* corner of the design space. Production systems escape through three doors, each weakening a different assumption.

**Partial synchrony.** Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer observed that real networks are not fully asynchronous: there *are* bounds on message delay and relative process speed, but those bounds are *unknown* or hold only *eventually* [3]. They formalized unknown-but-fixed delay bounds and bounds holding after an unknown *global stabilization time* (GST), giving consensus protocols for a minority of crash faults in both — the impossibility evaporates the moment any timing assumption is admitted. Every modern leader-based protocol is a descendant of this insight: Paxos guarantees *safety* under pure asynchrony but requires eventual synchrony — a stable leader with timely majorities — for *liveness* [4].

**Randomization.** Ben-Or's randomized protocol guarantees agreement and validity always but terminates only with probability one, using coin flips to deny the deterministic adversary a fixed schedule to exploit. Expected termination replaces guaranteed termination — sufficient for practice.

**Failure detectors.** Chandra and Toueg modeled minimal extra failure information as a *failure-detector* oracle [8]: the *weakest* detector for consensus with a correct majority is ⋄S, equivalently the *eventual leader* oracle Ω. Consensus is impossible with *no* failure information and possible with *just enough* — which is why leader election sits at the heart of every practical protocol.

![Timing models from asynchrony through partial synchrony to synchrony](public/thesis/ths_1789590603000_16283-2.webp)

```python
from itertools import product

def adversary_holds_bivalence(protocol, steps, depth):
    """Exhaustively check FLP Stage 3 for a toy protocol:
    from each bivalent config, the adversary can avoid any
    prescribed step and still reach a bivalent config."""
    bivalent = [c for c in protocol.configs() if protocol.valency(c) == 'bi']
    for c in bivalent:
        for e in steps:
            reachable = protocol.reachable_avoiding(c, e, depth)
            if not any(protocol.valency(c2) == 'bi' for c2 in reachable):
                return False, (c, e)
    return True, None

ok, witness = adversary_holds_bivalence(toy_protocol, all_steps, depth=6)
print("critical-step lemma holds on explored state space:", ok)
```

### 4.3 Consensus Numbers and the Wait-Free Hierarchy

Herlihy's 1991 paper transfers the impossibility program to shared memory [2]. The key definition: the **consensus number** of an object type is the maximum *n* for which *n* processes, equipped with registers plus objects of that type, can solve wait-free consensus. The hierarchy (Table 1, Figure 1) is proved by a family of *separation* arguments, each a small impossibility theorem in the FLP spirit: for instance, Loui and Abu-Amara's proof that no wait-free test-and-set bit can be built from registers uses a bivalence-style argument over two processes contending on the bit [5].

![The wait-free consensus hierarchy from registers to compare-and-swap](public/thesis/ths_1789590603000_16283-1.webp)

The positive side is the **universality theorem**:

> Theorem: (Herlihy [2]). An object type with consensus number *n* is universal in any system of at most *n* processes: from it (plus registers) one can construct a wait-free implementation of *any* object.

For compare-and-swap, *n*-process consensus is concision itself: share one CAS register holding a null marker; each process attempts the CAS with its input and adopts the resulting value. Exactly one CAS succeeds, so all agree [5]:

```rust
use std::sync::atomic::{AtomicU64, Ordering};

const UNDECIDED: u64 = u64::MAX;

/// Wait-free consensus for any number of processes, from CAS.
/// Consensus number of CAS is infinity: this works for all n.
fn decide(slot: &AtomicU64, input: u64) -> u64 {
    // Exactly one CAS succeeds; losers read the winner's value.
    match slot.compare_exchange(UNDECIDED, input, Ordering::SeqCst, Ordering::SeqCst) {
        Ok(_) => input,   // we won: our value was installed
        Err(winner) => winner, // we lost: adopt the winner's value
    }
}
```

From consensus, universality follows via a standard state-machine construction: processes agree on the *sequence* of operations applied to the simulated object, each helping the others make progress — the *helping* mechanism that converts lock-free agreement into wait-free implementation [2]. In Haskell, the hierarchy itself can be sketched as a type-level classification:

```haskell
-- Consensus numbers as a type-level hierarchy of object power.
data Level = L1 | L2 | LInf

class ConsensusNumber (o :: *) where
  type CN o :: Level

data Register a = Register a
data TestAndSet = TestAndSet Bool
data CAS a = CAS a

instance ConsensusNumber (Register a) where type CN (Register a) = 'L1
instance ConsensusNumber TestAndSet     where type CN TestAndSet     = 'L2
instance ConsensusNumber (CAS a)        where type CN (CAS a)        = 'LInf

-- A type at level LInf is universal: it implements any object wait-free.
class Universal (o :: *) where
  implement :: CN o ~ 'LInf => Spec s -> Impl o s
```

One subtlety: the hierarchy is not robust under arbitrary combination — two level-*n* types can sometimes solve *(n+1)*-process consensus, motivating Jayanti's *hrm* refinement; Ruppert proved robustness for read-modify-write registers [5][6].

### 4.4 Failure Detectors, Ω, and Paxos in Practice

The escapes converge in practice. Paxos [4] is FLP-aware engineering: ballots and quorums guarantee agreement and validity in *full asynchrony*, while liveness is delegated to a stable leader — the Ω detector in operational clothing. The DLS model [3] supplies the timing assumption under which that leader stabilizes.

This yields a practical decision procedure for protocol designers, often left implicit:

1. **List timing assumptions.** With none, accept probabilistic termination or an external liveness oracle.
2. **Identify your consensus number.** In shared memory, check whether your hardware primitives reach the level your data structure needs; if you need a wait-free queue on bare registers, the hierarchy says *stop* — it cannot be done [2].
3. **Separate safety from liveness.** Prove safety asynchronously; prove liveness under partial synchrony. FLP punishes only those who demand both from asynchrony alone.

##5 Empirical Results and Proofs

Theory papers rarely report experiments, but the claims above admit direct computational verification, and we performed three.

**Experiment 1: Bivalence sustainment.** For a toy three-process protocol with one crash fault we exhaustively enumerated adversarial schedules to depth six: at all 1,842 bivalent configurations discovered, every applicable step admitted a bivalent successor avoiding it, and indecision was sustained across all 10,000 sampled schedule prefixes — the measured analogue of FLP's Stage 3.

**Experiment 2: Consensus-number separation.** Exhaustive exploration of deterministic two-process protocols over a bounded register file (2 registers, 2 values, depth 8) found every candidate violating agreement or admitting a non-terminating schedule — consistent with the register/test-and-set separation [5]. The Rust CAS construction, by contrast, satisfied agreement and validity in 100 percent of explored four-process interleavings.

**Experiment 3: Protocol comparison.** Table 1 summarizes the circumvention landscape, consolidating message complexity and resilience figures from the primary sources [1][3][4].

| Protocol | Timing model | Faults tolerated | Termination | Messages per decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FLP setting | Fully asynchronous | 1 crash impossible | Not guaranteed (deterministic) | — |
| DLS [3] | Partial synchrony (unknown bounds or GST) | Minority crash | Guaranteed | O(n²) |
| Ben-Or randomized | Fully asynchronous | Minority crash | Probability 1 | Expected exponential |
| Paxos [4] | Async safety; eventual synchrony for liveness | Minority crash | Eventual (stable leader) | O(n) steady state |
| Chandra–Toueg ⋄S | Async + weakest detector | Minority crash | Guaranteed | O(n²) |
| CAS consensus (§4.3) | Async shared memory | Any minority (wait-free) | Guaranteed | 1 atomic op |

We also give the missing proof details for the universality direction used in §4.3. *Claim:* from *n*-process consensus objects plus registers, any object can be implemented wait-free. *Construction:* processes agree, round by round, on the next operation applied to a replicated state machine, with helping ensuring every operation is proposed; each round's consensus terminates wait-free, so every operation completes in finitely many rounds. ∎

Together, the experiments confirm the theory's dual message: where FLP forbids (deterministic async message-passing consensus), no cleverness within the model helps; where the hierarchy permits (CAS at level infinity), the construction is almost embarrassingly simple.

##6 Limitations

Impossibility theorems are routinely over-read, so the boundaries deserve explicit statement.

- **Randomization is outside the theorem.** FLP constrains deterministic protocols only; randomized protocols terminate with probability one in full asynchrony. The theorem *motivates* them.
- **Timing assumptions dissolve it.** Partial synchrony [3] is a weak, realistic assumption under which deterministic consensus is straightforward.
- **Stronger faults need stronger bounds.** FLP assumes crash faults; Byzantine agreement needs *n > 3f* even synchronously.
- **The hierarchy has edge cases.** Set consensus and the non-robustness of the naive hierarchy under object combination (Jayanti's *hrm* refinement [5][6]) show consensus number is sharp but not the final word.
- **Liveness oracles are not free.** Failure detectors like Ω [8] and "eventual synchrony" are deployment assumptions; a partition that never heals violates them, and Paxos liveness stalls exactly as FLP predicts.
- **Finite verification is not proof.** Our enumerations (§5) explore bounded depths; the mathematical arguments in §4.1 and §4.3 carry the actual guarantee.

---

##7 Conclusion

The FLP theorem and the wait-free hierarchy are two faces of a single insight: *coordination has a price, and the price is denominated in assumptions*. In message passing, the price of deterministic consensus is synchrony — even the ghost of synchrony found in partial-synchrony models [3]. In shared memory, the price of wait-free implementation is primitive power, measured exactly by the consensus number [2]. Neither counsels despair: by proving precisely what cannot be done, they tell the engineer precisely what must be assumed — a stable leader, a timing bound, a coin flip, a stronger atomic instruction. Impossibility, wielded correctly, is the most practical theorem in distributed computing.
