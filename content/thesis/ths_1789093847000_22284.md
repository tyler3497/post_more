# Reinforcement Learning Theory: Bellman Optimality, Optimism in the Face of Uncertainty, and Regret Bounds

## Abstract

This thesis develops the theoretical foundations of reinforcement learning (RL) in Markov decision processes (MDPs), centering on the Bellman optimality equations as the fixed point of a contractive operator, the *exploration–exploitation dilemma*, and the *optimism in the face of uncertainty* (OFU/UCB) principle that resolves it. We begin with finite-horizon and discounted MDPs, the contraction property of the Bellman operator, and the convergence of tabular Q-learning as stochastic approximation. We then turn to exploration theory: the regret formalism, the diameter of an MDP, and the UCRL2 algorithm with its near-optimal Õ(*DS*√*AT*) regret bound, together with the matching Ω(√*DSAT*) lower bound. We treat the minimax-optimal UCBVI algorithm for episodic MDPs, which sharpens the bound to Õ(√*H*³*SAT*) via Bernstein-type bonuses, and survey modern extensions to linear function approximation — LSVI-UCB and the eluder dimension framework. Finally, we expose the "deadly triad" of bootstrapping, off-policy learning, and function approximation that defeats naive theory, and we state precise open problems at the frontier of RL theory.

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning differs from supervised learning in one decisive respect: *the learner's actions determine the data it observes*. An agent interacts with an environment whose dynamics are unknown, seeks to maximize cumulative reward, and must therefore *explore* — taking actions whose value is uncertain in order to learn about them — while simultaneously *exploiting* the knowledge already gained to accumulate reward. This tension, the exploration–exploitation dilemma, is the central algorithmic and statistical problem of reinforcement learning.

Two mathematical ideas organize everything that follows. The first is *dynamic programming* and its stochastic descendants: the Bellman equations, which characterize optimal behavior as the fixed point of a contraction mapping, and which justify every value-based algorithm from tabular Q-learning to deep Q-networks. The second is *optimism in the face of uncertainty* (OFU), the principle that an agent should act as if the unknown world were the most favorable among all worlds consistent with its observations. OFU converts a statistical confidence set into a planning problem and is the engine behind the first algorithms with *provable* regret bounds in unknown MDPs.

> **Thesis claim.** The trajectory of modern RL theory is a single arc: the Bellman optimality equations furnish a contractive target; Q-learning approximates that target stochastically; and OFU-style algorithms such as UCRL2, UCBVI, and LSVI-UCB solve the exploration problem by planning optimistically inside confidence regions, achieving regret bounds Õ(√*HSAT*), Õ(√*H*³*SAT*), and Õ(√*d*³*H*³*T*) respectively — each matched, up to logarithmic factors, by an information-theoretic lower bound.

This thesis presents that arc rigorously, while remaining honest about its limits: the deadly triad, the dependence of bounds on strong structural assumptions, and the gap between provable algorithms and the deep RL methods used in practice.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Markov Decision Processes

A finite-horizon *Markov decision process* (MDP) is a tuple *M* = (𝒮, 𝒜, *P*, *r*, *H*) where 𝒮 is a finite state space with |𝒮| = *S*, 𝒜 is a finite action space with |𝒜| = *A*, *P* = {*P*ₕ}ₕ₌₁ᴴ are transition kernels with *P*ₕ(·|*s*,*a*) a distribution over 𝒮, *r* = {*r*ₕ} are (deterministic) reward functions *r*ₕ: 𝒮 × 𝒜 → [0, 1], and *H* is the horizon [1][2]. A *policy* π = {πₕ}ₕ₌₁ᴴ maps states to distributions over actions. The value function of π is

$$V^\pi_h(s) = \mathbb{E}^\pi\left[\sum_{t=h}^{H} r_t(s_t, a_t) \,\middle|\, s_h = s\right],$$

with *Q*^πₕ(*s*,*a*) defined analogously for a first action *a*. The *regret* after *K* episodes (total steps *T* = *KH*) is the performance gap relative to an optimal policy π\*:

$$\mathrm{Regret}(K) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \left(V^*_1(s^k_1) - V^{\pi_k}_1(s^k_1)\right),$$

where *V*\*ₕ is the optimal value function. An algorithm is *no-regret* if Regret(*K*) = *o*(*T*), i.e., the per-step deficit vanishes asymptotically. Regret, unlike sample complexity, measures performance *during* learning — the economically relevant quantity [2][4].

### 2.2 The Bellman Optimality Equations and Contraction

The optimal value functions satisfy the *Bellman optimality equations* [1]:

$$V^*_h(s) = \max_a \left[ r_h(s,a) + \sum_{s'} P_h(s'|s,a)\, V^*_{h+1}(s') \right], \quad V^*_{H+1} \equiv 0,$$

$$Q^*_h(s,a) = r_h(s,a) + \sum_{s'} P_h(s'|s,a)\,\max_{a'} Q^*_{h+1}(s',a').$$

In the discounted infinite-horizon setting with discount γ < 1, the Bellman optimality operator (𝒯\*V*)(*s*) = maxₐ [*r*(*s*,*a*) + γ Σₛ′ *P*(*s*′|*s*,*a*)*V*(*s*′)] is a **γ-contraction** in the sup norm:

> **Theorem: (Contraction and unique fixed point).** The Bellman optimality operator 𝒯\* is a γ-contraction on the Banach space of bounded functions on 𝒮 with the sup norm. Hence, by the Banach fixed-point theorem, 𝒯\* has a unique fixed point *V*\*, and the iterates *V*ₖ₊₁ = 𝒯\*V*ₖ converge to *V*\* geometrically, ‖*V*ₖ − *V*\*‖∞ ≤ γᵏ ‖*V*₀ − *V*\*‖∞.

> **Proof.** For any *U*, *V*, the max-norm difference satisfies |maxₐ *f*(*a*) − maxₐ *g*(*a*)| ≤ maxₐ |*f*(*a*) − *g*(*a*)|, whence |(𝒯\*U)(*s*) − (𝒯\*V*)(*s*)| ≤ γ maxₛ′,ₐ Σₛ′ *P*(*s*′|*s*,*a*) |*U*(*s*′) − *V*(*s*′)| ≤ γ ‖*U* − *V*‖∞. Completeness of the sup-norm space yields the unique fixed point and convergence. ∎

This single result justifies value iteration, and via stochastic approximation, Q-learning: every value-based method is an attempt to approach this fixed point under sampling noise.

### 2.3 Tabular Q-learning as Stochastic Approximation

Q-learning [5] updates, upon observing (*s*, *a*, *r*, *s*′),

$$Q_{t+1}(s,a) \leftarrow Q_t(s,a) + \alpha_t(s,a)\left[r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_t(s',a') - Q_t(s,a)\right].$$

Watkins and Dayan proved that under Robbins–Monro learning rates (Σₜ αₜ = ∞, Σₜ αₜ² < ∞) and infinite visitation of every state–action pair, *Q*ₜ → *Q*\* almost surely [5]. The proof decomposes the update into a contraction plus a martingale-difference noise term; the contraction absorbs the noise at a vanishing rate. Crucially, the convergence is *asymptotic* and requires infinite exploration — it says nothing about how much exploration costs, which is precisely the regret question.

## 3 Methodology

Our analysis proceeds through four methodological stages, each corresponding to a generation of RL theory:

1. **Exact dynamic programming.** We establish the Bellman optimality fixed point and its contraction properties (Section 2.2), giving the *target* every algorithm aims at.
2. **Stochastic approximation.** We analyze tabular Q-learning's convergence via Robbins–Monro theory, exposing the gap between asymptotic consistency and finite-sample efficiency [5].
3. **Regret analysis via optimism.** For unknown MDPs we adopt the regret formalism and the OFU principle: at each episode, construct a confidence set ℳ of MDPs consistent with past data, select the *most optimistic* MDP *M̃* ∈ ℳ and its optimal policy, and execute. The key technical step is a regret decomposition into (i) a term measuring deviation from optimism (nonpositive, by construction) and (ii) a martingale term controlled by concentration.
4. **Function approximation.** We generalize OFU to linear MDPs, where uncertainty is measured by ellipsoidal confidence sets over feature space and the relevant complexity measure is the *eluder dimension* [6][3].

Throughout, our yardsticks are *upper bounds* (what some algorithm achieves) and *lower bounds* (what no algorithm can avoid): a theory is mature when the two match up to logarithmic factors.

## 4 Deep Dive

### 4.1 UCRL2 and the Diameter of an MDP

For the undiscounted (average-reward) setting, Jaksch, Ortner, and Auer introduced the *diameter* of an MDP — the maximal expected hitting time between any two states under the fastest policy — as the parameter governing exploration difficulty [4]. An MDP has diameter *D* if for every pair *s*, *s*′ there exists a policy reaching *s*′ from *s* in at most *D* steps on average. The diameter captures the "navigability" of the environment: an MDP can be huge yet easy to explore if it is easy to move around.

**UCRL2** [4] operates in *episodes* with doubling criteria: at the start of each episode it computes empirical transition estimates and confidence intervals (via Hoeffding-type bounds) defining a set of plausible MDPs, then runs extended value iteration on the *optimistic* MDP — the plausible MDP with the largest optimal average reward — and executes its policy until some state–action count doubles.

> **Theorem: (UCRL2 regret bound, Jaksch–Ortner–Auer 2010).** With probability at least 1 − δ, for any initial state and any *T* > 1, the regret of UCRL2 in an MDP with *S* states, *A* actions, and diameter *D* satisfies
>
> $$\Delta(M, \mathrm{UCRL2}, T) \;\le\; C \cdot DS\sqrt{AT\log(T/\delta)},$$
>
> i.e., regret Õ(*DS*√*AT*), where *C* is a universal constant [4].

The proof's core insight: with high probability the true MDP lies in the confidence set, so the optimistic policy's value overestimates the optimal value, and per-step regret is bounded by the *width* of the confidence interval at the visited pair. A *span lemma* — the optimistic value function's span is bounded by *D* — connects estimation error to regret.

Matching this is a lower bound of the same era:

> **Theorem: (Lower bound).** For some *c* > 0 and any algorithm, any *S*, *A* ≥ 10, *D* ≥ 20 log *S*, and *T* ≥ *DSA*, there exists an MDP with *S* states, *A* actions, and diameter *D* such that expected regret is at least *c*√*DSAT* [4].

The construction is a two-armed-bandit-like "combination lock" MDP: the learner must identify the correct action sequence, and information-theoretic arguments (KL-divergence between hypotheses) force Ω(√*T*) regret. UCRL2 is thus *near-minimax-optimal* up to the *DS* factor — a gap that took seven years to close.

### 4.2 UCBVI: Bernstein Bonuses and Minimax Optimality in Episodic RL

Azar, Osband, and Munos attacked the *finite-horizon episodic* setting, where regret admits a cleaner dependence on the horizon *H* [2]. Their algorithm, **UCBVI**, is an optimistic modification of value iteration: it maintains empirical models, then adds *exploration bonuses* to the reward before performing backward induction.

Two insights made UCBVI the first near-minimax-optimal episodic algorithm:

1. **Concentration on values, not transitions.** Prior analyses applied Hoeffding bounds to each transition probability separately, incurring an extra √*S* factor. UCBVI instead applies concentration directly to the *optimal value function* as a whole, improving the dependence on *S*.
2. **Bernstein bonuses.** Instead of Hoeffding-style bonuses scaling with *H*/√*n*, UCBVI uses bonuses based on the *empirical variance* of the estimated values at successor states, scaling like √(Var·log/*n*) + *H*·log/*n*. Where the value function is nearly deterministic, exploration costs far less [2].

> **Theorem: (UCBVI regret bound, Azar–Osband–Munos 2017).** UCBVI achieves regret Õ(√*HSAT* + *H*²*S*²*A* + *H*√*T*), where *H* is the horizon, *S* the number of states, *A* the number of actions, and *T* = *KH* the total steps [2].

When *T* ≥ *H*³*S*³*A* — i.e., in the regime of many episodes — the leading term is Õ(√*H*³*SAT*), which matches the Ω(√*H*³*SAT*) lower bound (due to Jin et al. and Domingues et al.) up to logarithmic factors. The remaining gap of √*H* between UCRL2's *H*-dependence and UCBVI's was closed only later, by algorithms using *reference-advantage decompositions* (Zhang et al., 2020; 2021).

### 4.3 Linear Function Approximation: LSVI-UCB and the Eluder Dimension

Tabular bounds scale with *S* and *A* — hopeless when states number in the billions. The modern program replaces the state space by a *d*-dimensional feature representation. In a *linear MDP*, transitions and rewards are linear in a known feature map φ: 𝒮 × 𝒜 → ℝᵈ:

$$P_h(\cdot|s,a) = \langle \phi(s,a), \mu_h(\cdot)\rangle, \qquad r_h(s,a) = \langle \phi(s,a), \theta_h\rangle.$$

Jin, Yang, Wang, and Jordan's **LSVI-UCB** [3] performs least-squares value iteration with an optimism bonus derived from an ellipsoidal confidence set: the bonus at (*s*,*a*) is β·‖φ(*s*,*a*)‖_{Λ⁻¹}, where Λ is the regularized design matrix of visited features — the classic linear-bandit UCB transported into Bellman backup.

> **Theorem: (LSVI-UCB regret bound, Jin et al. 2020).** Under the linear MDP assumption, LSVI-UCB achieves regret Õ(√*d*³*H*³*T*), polynomial in the feature dimension *d* and horizon *H*, and *independent of S and A* [3].

This was the first provably efficient algorithm with both polynomial runtime and polynomial sample complexity under function approximation, without a simulator. The quantity √(*d*³) was later improved to √(*d*)·*H*² dependencies by follow-ups, but the conceptual contribution endures: *optimism generalizes beyond tables via geometry*.

The right abstraction for "what function classes admit optimistic exploration" is the **eluder dimension** of Russo and Van Roy [6]: the length of the longest sequence of points *x*₁, …, *x*_m such that each *x*ᵢ is "independent" of its predecessors — i.e., two functions can agree on all previous points yet disagree at *x*ᵢ. It measures the sequential information content of a class, and regret of OFU algorithms scales as Õ(√(dim_E · *T*)).

| Algorithm | Setting | Regret bound | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| UCRL2 [4] | Average reward, diameter *D* | Õ(*DS*√*AT*) | Near-optimal; √*DS* gap |
| UCBVI [2] | Episodic, horizon *H* | Õ(√*H*³*SAT*) (large *T*) | Minimax-optimal |
| LSVI-UCB [3] | Linear MDP, dim *d* | Õ(√*d*³*H*³*T*) | Polynomial; improvables |
| Lower bound [4][2] | — | Ω(√*DSAT*) / Ω(√*H*³*SAT*) | Information-theoretic |

### 4.4 The Deadly Triad

Not everything composes. Sutton and Barto's *deadly triad* [1] names the combination of (i) **function approximation**, (ii) **bootstrapping** (updating estimates from other estimates, as in TD), and (iii) **off-policy learning** (learning about a policy different from the one generating data) as a recipe for divergence. Classic counterexamples (Baird; Tsitsiklis–Van Roy) show linear TD can diverge to infinity off-policy, even though each ingredient is benign in isolation:

- Tabular TD + on-policy: convergent (contraction preserved).
- Linear TD + on-policy: convergent (to the *projected* fixed point; the composition Π𝒯^π is a contraction in a weighted norm).
- Tabular Q-learning + off-policy: convergent (the max operator keeps 𝒯\* a contraction).
- **All three together: no contraction, possible divergence.** The projected Bellman operator need not be a contraction in any norm, and semi-gradient updates follow the gradient of a *moving* target.

The triad explains the brittleness of deep Q-learning and motivates the theoretical program's central tension: LSVI-UCB dodges the triad because its least-squares step is a *full* projection (not a semi-gradient) and its optimism keeps the analysis on-policy enough; deep RL has no such guarantees. Gradient TD, emphatic TD, and target networks are engineering responses to the same mathematical wound.

## 5 Empirical Results and Proofs

While this thesis is theoretical, the algorithms above are not armchair constructs — their predicted scaling laws have been stress-tested computationally.

**Regret scaling verification.** In the canonical "river swim" and "combination lock" MDPs used by the UCRL2 authors, empirical regret grows as √*T* with the predicted dependence on diameter: environments with larger hitting times incur proportionally larger regret, matching the *D* factor in Theorem 4.1 [4]. For UCBVI, simulated episodic MDPs show the Bernstein bonus dramatically reducing exploration in low-variance regions — in near-deterministic chains, empirical regret tracks the variance-weighted bound rather than the looser Hoeffding bound, confirming that the improvement is not merely an artifact of analysis [2].

**Pseudocode: the OFU skeleton shared by all three algorithms.** The following captures the common algorithmic anatomy:

```python
def optimistic_rl(env, K, H, beta):
    """Generic optimism-in-the-face-of-uncertainty loop."""
    dataset = []
    for episode in range(K):
        # 1. Estimate: fit model/value from data
        model = fit_model(dataset)
        # 2. Confidence set: plausible worlds (ellipsoid / intervals)
        confidence_set = build_confidence_set(model, dataset, delta)
        # 3. Optimism: plan in the most favorable plausible world
        #    equivalently: add bonus b(s,a) ~ beta * uncertainty(s,a)
        Q_optimistic = value_iteration(model, bonus=bonus_fn)
        policy = greedy(Q_optimistic)
        # 4. Execute and collect data
        trajectory = env.rollout(policy, H)
        dataset.append(trajectory)
```

> **Lemma: (Optimism implies near-zero "optimism gap").** If the true MDP lies in the confidence set with probability 1 − δ, then the optimistic value overestimates the optimal value, *Ṽ* ≥ *V*\*, so that per-episode regret satisfies *V*\* − *V*^π ≤ *Ṽ* − *V*^π, i.e., regret is controlled by the *estimation error of the optimistic value along the executed trajectory*.

> **Proof.** By construction *Ṽ* = max_{M̃∈ℳ} *V*\*_{M̃} ≥ *V*\*_{M} = *V*\* whenever *M* ∈ ℳ. Subtracting the executed policy's value and applying the *simulation lemma* — which bounds |*V*^π_{M̃} − *V*^π_M| by the expected one-step model errors under π's visitation — reduces regret to a sum of confidence widths, each shrinking as 1/√*n*(*s*,*a*) with visit counts. A pigeonhole ("doubling") argument sums these to Õ(√*T*). ∎

This single proof skeleton, instantiated with Hoeffding intervals (UCRL2), Bernstein bonuses on values (UCBVI), or ellipsoidal bonuses on features (LSVI-UCB), generates all the bounds in the table of Section 4.3.

**Lower-bound proof technique.** The Ω(√*HSAT*) lower bound reduces RL to *HSA* independent bandit problems embedded in a "combination-lock" MDP: only one action sequence yields reward, and the learner must identify it. Standard bandit lower bounds (KL-divergence / Bretagnolle–Huber) force any algorithm to suffer Ω(√*T*) regret on at least one instance [4][2]. The construction shows the √*T* rate is *unavoidable* — no cleverness escapes it.

## 6 Limitations

The theory surveyed here is powerful but circumscribed, and a PhD-level account must state the boundaries precisely:

1. **Structural assumptions are strong.** LSVI-UCB requires a *linear MDP* — features such that transitions and rewards are exactly linear. Misspecification breaks the analysis, and the real world rarely cooperates. Weaker assumptions (low Bellman rank, bilinear classes, coverability) extend the frontier but with worse rates and heavier computation.
2. **Bounds are worst-case and often vacuous in practice.** A regret bound of Õ(√*d*³*H*³*T*) with large constants says little about why DQN masters Atari. The constants hidden in Õ(·) can exceed the horizon itself; practitioners rightly treat these bounds as *qualitative* guides (e.g., "use variance-aware bonuses") rather than performance certificates.
3. **The deadly triad is unresolved in full generality.** Convergent off-policy algorithms with nonlinear approximation remain elusive; target networks and replay buffers are heuristics without finite-sample guarantees. The gap between provable algorithms (tabular/linear) and deployed algorithms (deep, off-policy, bootstrapped) is the field's central embarrassment.
4. **Computational cost.** UCBVI-style planning is polynomial but often impractical: solving an optimistic MDP per episode, or inverting *d* × *d* matrices per step, does not scale to deep networks. Provable efficiency in *samples* does not imply efficiency in *wall-clock*.
5. **Regret is not the only objective.** The regret formalism assumes the learning phase itself is costly (every mistake counts). In sim-to-real or pure-exploration settings, *PAC* (probably approximately correct) bounds or reward-free exploration may be the right criterion, and optimistic regret-minimizers can be needlessly conservative.

## 7 Conclusion

The Bellman optimality equations give reinforcement learning its target — a unique fixed point of a contraction, approachable by dynamic programming and, stochastically, by Q-learning. But knowing the target is not the same as reaching it efficiently: the exploration–exploitation dilemma means that data collection and optimization are inseparable. The principle of *optimism in the face of uncertainty* resolves the dilemma with remarkable generality: maintain a confidence set over plausible worlds, plan in the best of them, and let the width of your uncertainty bound your regret.

From UCRL2's Õ(*DS*√*AT*) through UCBVI's minimax-optimal Õ(√*H*³*SAT*) to LSVI-UCB's Õ(√*d*³*H*³*T*) — each step preserved the OFU skeleton while refining *where* the concentration is applied: from transitions, to value functions, to feature geometry. The matching lower bounds certify that the √*T* rate and the parameter dependencies are essentially unavoidable. And the eluder dimension distills the lesson into a single complexity measure for optimistic exploration.

Yet the edifice rests on tabular and linear foundations. The deadly triad — bootstrapping plus off-policy learning plus function approximation — still defeats the contraction arguments that make everything else work, and it is precisely this triad that powers modern deep RL. Closing the gap between what we can prove and what we can deploy, extending optimism to general function classes with practical computation, and understanding exploration in the presence of misspecification remain the defining open problems of RL theory. The arc from Bellman to Bernstein is complete; the arc from Bernstein to deep networks has barely begun.
